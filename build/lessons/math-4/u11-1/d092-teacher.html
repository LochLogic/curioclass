Before class

Print what the header lists. Have the 36-foot string loop and grid paper out, the display on the projector, and the two warm-up problems on the board.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: two perimeters

Alone, on the back of the grid paper. A rectangle is 264 feet long and 129 feet wide. Find its perimeter. [786 feet] and A rectangle is 2 3/5 inches long and 1 4/5 inches wide. Find its perimeter. [8 4/5 inches; 6 14/5, 7 9/5 and 44/5 are also right]. Students add the length and the width, then add the sum to itself, as on day 91. Go over both at 5 minutes. A sum not doubled (393, 4 2/5 or 3 7/5) is error B: trace the four sides on the board. If day 88's item 8 showed many with no estimate: write "about " before each sum.

If many made B on day 91's exit (383 or 5 5/10): Ben's wrong answer from day 91's display first, one minute. E (240 feet): the opener's eight answers with no units replace A rectangle is 2 3/5 inches long and 1 4/5 inches wide. Find its perimeter. [8 4/5 inches; 6 14/5, 7 9/5 and 44/5 are also right]. A (92 for the hall): open the dog pen with Lia's wrong answer: "Does the floor cover, or go around?"

20 min. The dog pen, then teaching first

10 minutes: the task, in pairs (A dog pen is a rectangle. It uses all 36 feet of fence. Which pen gives the dog the most room? [9 feet by 9 feet: 81 square feet. Every pen's length plus width is 18]). Read the task aloud once. Four volunteers hold the corners of the string loop as a long thin pen, then a square one: same string, which dog has more room? Pairs sketch two pens on the grid side, the long thin one and the square, and fill the rest of the table on side 1 by numbers alone: length + width must be 18. Watch for pairs who change the fence while changing the pen (a 10 by 10 pen): count its sides with them.

Ask. "Every pen used 36 feet of fence. What is the same about the length plus the width of each? Why?" Expect: "they all make 18"; then "the fence is two lengths and two widths, so one of each is half". Take the first, then press for the second.

Ask. "Two rectangles have the same perimeter. Must they have the same area?" Expect "no": point at 1 by 17 (17 square feet) and 9 by 9 (81).

Ask first, from the pairs' tables. Then close it, smallest pen first:

  1. Every pen uses all 36 feet: two lengths and two widths.
  2. So one length and one width use half the fence: 18 feet.
  3. Every pen has length + width = 18: 1 and 17, 2 and 16, and so on up to 9 and 9.
  4. Room for the dog is the inside: the area, length times width.
  5. 1 × 17 = 17, 5 × 13 = 65, 8 × 10 = 80, 9 × 9 = 81.
  6. The 9 by 9 pen gives the most room: 81 square feet.

10 minutes: two worked examples, whole class. Students fill the frames on side 1 as you go.

Example 1, times as long (A rug is 7 feet wide. Its length is 2 times its width. How much floor is under the rug? [Length 2 × 7 = 14 feet; area 14 × 7 = 98 square feet]).

  1. Sketch the rug: 7 feet wide, the length unknown.
  2. Draw the width as 1 bar of 7. The length is 2 bars of 7.
  3. "2 times its width" means 2 × 7, not 7 + 2. The rug is 14 feet long.
  4. The floor under the rug is the inside of the rectangle: the area.
  5. Area is length times width: 14 × 7 = 98.
  6. The rug is on 98 square feet of floor. Square feet, because it is area.
  7. Check: 14 is 2 sevens, and 98 is 14 rows of 7.

"2 times as long" is 2 × 7, not 7 + 2: the same as stretch 10's comparing. A child who writes 9 is adding.

Example 2, two steps (A chicken yard has 72 square feet of ground. It is 8 feet wide. How much fence does it need? [8 × ? = 72, so 9 feet long; fence 9 + 8 + 9 + 8 = 34 feet]).

  1. Sketch the yard: 8 feet wide, the length unknown, 72 square feet inside.
  2. Step 1: the 72 square feet is the inside, so 8 × ? = 72. 8 × 9 = 72: the yard is 9 feet long.
  3. Step 2: fence goes along the edge of the yard. That is the perimeter.
  4. 9 + 8 + 9 + 8 = 34, or 2 × (9 + 8) = 34.
  5. The yard needs 34 feet of fence. Feet, because it is a length.

In each step ask: "Is this number the inside or the edge?"

20 min. Six problems by hand

Alone, side 2. Read all six aloud once at the start. For each, students write "area" or "perimeter" first, sketch on the back of the grid paper (a plain rectangle with its sides labelled; do not count squares), then the answer with its unit. None of the six says "covers" or "around": the story tells which. A kitchen floor is 9 feet long and 6 feet wide. Each tile is 1 foot by 1 foot. How many tiles does the floor need? [9 × 6 = 54 tiles (area, 54 square feet)] Zoe makes a rectangle out of tape on the gym floor. It is 14 feet long and 9 feet wide. How much tape does she use? [14 + 9 + 14 + 9 = 46 feet (perimeter)] A bench seat is 2 feet wide. It is 6 times as long as it is wide. Mom paints the top of the seat. How much does she paint? [Length 6 × 2 = 12 feet; 12 × 2 = 24 square feet (area)] A dog yard is 6 feet wide. It has 42 square feet of ground. How much fence closes it in? [6 × ? = 42, so 7 feet long; fence 7 + 6 + 7 + 6 = 26 feet] A flag is 9 inches wide. It is 2 times as long as it is wide. Gold trim makes a border on the flag. How much trim is used? [Length 2 × 9 = 18 inches; 18 + 9 + 18 + 9 = 54 inches (perimeter)] Kai bends 24 inches of wire into a rectangle. It is 7 inches long. How wide is it? [7 + ? + 7 + ? = 24; 5 inches (the wire is the perimeter)]

After 15 minutes, pairs swap and check each answer against the story.

Check: Is your number the inside or the edge? Is the unit right? If not, put a question mark.

Watch for: a "times as long" length found by adding (8 for the bench, 11 for the flag); the wrong measure (30 tiles, 126 feet of tape, 162 inches of trim: error A); "54 feet" of tiles (error E); stopping at the side (7 for the dog yard). If many choose the wrong measure: each pair sketches problem 1 and shades what the story asks for, the inside or the edge. If many add for "times as long": draw 2 bars of 9 for the flag.

5 min. Written task

Alone, the box at the foot of side 2: Look at problem 2. Did it need area or perimeter? How did you know? [Perimeter, 46 feet: the tape makes the sides, not the floor inside]. Students may answer aloud to you instead (a scribe is allowed). Collect the student pages with the exit slips at the end; read after class.

Tick each look-for on the key alone; the key gives samples that meet, partly meet and do not (ours, untested). A child who writes "tape, lines, 46 feet" with no full sentence has look-fors 1 to 3: that is language, not math.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Which problem was hardest to decide? What in the story told you?" Expect the dog yard or the flag. Show one decided from the story, then one from a word; connect them: "inside or edge?" before any number.

5 min. Exit: the slip

Alone, on paper: A patio is 4 yards wide. Its length is 3 times its width. (a) Ana paints the patio. How much does she paint? (b) She fences in the patio. How much fence does she need? Write the units. [48 square yards; 32 yards]. Read it aloud once. Write the results on the record sheet under E92.

Other rooms

  • No string loop. Draw a 17 by 1 pen and a 9 by 9 pen on the board, each with 36 feet of edge.
  • One child at home. Make the 36-foot pens from a 36-inch string on a table, with paper squares for the room inside.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: stop the task's table at 6 pens and close it from there. Never drop the written task or the reading aloud. Longer: the challenge (A dog pen is 10 feet long and 2 feet wide. Draw a pen with more room but less fence. [For example 5 by 5: 25 square feet and 20 feet of fence. The 10 by 2 pen has 20 square feet and 24 feet of fence]), on the back of the grid paper.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: area, perimeter, length, width, square foot, times as long. Frames on the display: "This problem needs because the story asks about the ." "The length is times , so it is feet."
