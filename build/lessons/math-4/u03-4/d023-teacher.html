Before class

Print what the header lists. Stack the pairs' keys and cut out the eight card rows (s03-P23-2 to s03-P23-9, together in the table): that strip is the card key, and the rest stays hidden. Put the display page on the projector. Have day 22's written tasks ready to hand back, look-fors ticked.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: one benchmark

Alone, no line given. Hand back day 22's written tasks as students start. Use 1/2 to decide. Write >, = or <. 45/100 9/12 [<: 45/100 is less than 1/2 and 9/12 is more] Students write the sign and one sentence. Go over it at 5 minutes: half of 100 is 50, half of 12 is 6. A student who wrote > read 45 and 100 as big numbers (error D).

If day 22's tickets showed many D ((a) <, (b) > or (c) <): before the example, two minutes from the cards. Hold up a strip cut in tenths and one in twelfths, and say four pairs aloud as "parts of size": "7 parts of size one twelfth". If many G (signs turned round): read six comparisons from day 22 aloud as sentences; the open side faces the greater number. Both: D first, G in the stories' pull-together.

5 min. Teaching first: one pair, three ways

Whole class. Students fill the three lines of the frame on side 1 as you go. The display shows each step on its own screen; sketch one line from 0 to 1 on the board for the first way.

Example (Compare 2/10 and 3/4 three ways: with 1/2, with the same parts, and with the same count. [2/10 < 3/4: 2/10 is under 1/2 and 3/4 is over; 4/20 < 15/20; 6/30 < 6/8]).

  1. With 1/2: half of 10 is 5, and 2 is less, so 2/10 is under 1/2.
  2. Half of 4 is 2, and 3 is more, so 3/4 is over 1/2. One under, one over: 2/10 < 3/4.
  3. Same parts: 20 is a multiple of 10 and of 4. 2/10 = 4/20 and 3/4 = 15/20.
  4. The parts are the same size now, and 4 parts are fewer than 15. So 4/20 < 15/20, and 2/10 < 3/4.
  5. Same count: make both tops 6. 2/10 = 6/30 and 3/4 = 6/8.
  6. 6 parts each. Eighths are bigger than thirtieths, because the whole is cut into fewer parts. So 6/30 < 6/8, and 2/10 < 3/4.
  7. Three ways, one answer. Here 1/2 was quickest, because one is under and one is over. When both are under or both over, 1/2 cannot settle it.

Say it plainly: every way gives the same sign, so pick the one that is quickest for this pair, then prove it.

25 min. Cards, with a partner holding the key

Pairs. Fraction Wall Duel may take this slot once it is built and played; until it exists, the day runs on the eight cards on side 1 (5/10 7/12 [<: 5/10 is 1/2, and 7/12 is more than 1/2], 2/8 3/12 [=: 2/8 = 6/24 and 3/12 = 6/24 (both are 1/4)], Jay read 1/2 of a short book. Kim read 1/3 of a long book. Who read more pages? [Cannot tell: the books are different wholes. 1/2 > 1/3 is true only of the same whole], 8/12 4/5 [<: 4/5 = 8/10, and tenths are bigger than twelfths (or 40/60 < 48/60)], 9/8 5/4 [<: 5/4 = 10/8, and 9/8 < 10/8], 6/8 8/10 [<: 30/40 < 32/40; or 6/8 is 1/4 short of 1 and 8/10 is only 1/5 short], Mo drank 4/8 of a small carton of milk. Lu drank 1/6 of a big carton. Who drank more milk? [Cannot tell: the cartons are different wholes. 4/8 > 1/6 is true only of the same whole], 1/5 2/12 [>: 1/5 = 2/10, and tenths are bigger than twelfths (or 12/60 > 10/60)]). Partner A takes card 1 and says the sign and the way before writing anything; then writes the proof in the row. Partner B holds the card key and says only "yes" or "try again". Swap for card 2, and so on.

Two cards are traps (the book and the milk): the two fractions are of different wholes, so the answer is "cannot tell". Read both aloud to the class at the start. Warn pairs that a trap is only a trap when the story names two different things.

Watch for:

  • A > on 8/12 and 4/5, > on 9/8 and 5/4, < on 1/5 and 2/12 or < on 2/8 and 3/12: bigger numbers read as a bigger fraction (error D). Ask "how many parts, and of what size?"
  • An = on 6/8 and 8/10, "each is two parts short" (error I): ask which missing piece is longer, 2/8 or 2/10.
  • "Jay" or "Mo" (error H): ask "half of what?"
  • 1/2 tried on 8/12 and 4/5, both over: send them to same parts or same count.

If many miss the traps, hold two strips of different length and fold each in half. Stop at 25 minutes; collect the card keys.

20 min. Two stories, the written task, the planned question

Stories, 8 minutes, alone (Ivy's ribbon is 13/10 meters long. Max's ribbon is 5/3 meters long. Whose ribbon is longer? Write the comparison with >, = or <. [Max's: 13/10 < 5/3 (39/30 < 50/30; or 13/10 is 3/10 past 1, 5/3 is 2/3 past 1, and 3/10 < 2/3)], Noor ate 4/6 of a small sandwich. Sam ate 3/5 of a big sandwich. Noor says, "I ate more, because 4/6 > 3/5." Can she be sure? Tell why. [No. 4/6 > 3/5 is true (20/30 > 18/30), but the sandwiches are different wholes, so the fractions cannot tell who ate more]). Read both aloud once. The ribbons are past 1; the sandwiches are different wholes. Then 2 minutes: partners compare answers, and each mismatch gets a question mark.

Check: Max's ribbon is longer, 13/10 < 5/3. Noor cannot be sure: the sandwiches are different wholes.

Watch for "Ivy's" (D) and "Max's" with 13/10 > 5/3 written (G). For Noor, a "yes" is H; a "no" with no reason gets "why not?".

Written task, 6 minutes, alone, the box at the foot of side 2: Which is greater, 5/6 or 9/10? Prove it two ways. One way may be a number line. [9/10: 25/30 < 27/30; or each is one part short of 1 and a tenth is smaller than a sixth; or both on one line. 5/6 < 9/10]. A student may answer aloud to you instead (a scribe is allowed). Collect the pages at the end; read after class against the key's look-fors and samples.

Pull it together, 4 minutes, whole class.

Ask. "Which way did you pick for 6/8 and 8/10, and why not another?" Expect: same parts or same count, "1/2 can't settle it: both are over". Take one of each, then: "For 1/5 and 2/12?" Expect same count, one rename.

Ask. "When does 'cannot tell' fit?" Expect: only when the wholes are different things.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper: No line given. "Ana and Ben walk the same trail. Ana has walked 2/5 of it. Ben has walked 3/8 of it. Who has walked farther? Write the comparison with >, = or <, and show or tell how you know." [Ana: 2/5 > 3/8 (16/40 > 15/40, or 6/15 > 6/16)]. Read it aloud once. Write the results on the record sheet under E23.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Write the eight cards on the board, numbered; pairs work in notebooks, and the partner reads the key from your copy, card by card.
  • One projector. One card at a time on the screen. Every student writes the sign and the reason, then holds up a sign card, or "cannot compare" for a trap.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop cards 1 and 8 and the partner swap after the stories. Never drop the traps, the written task or the proof on each card. Longer: "Find a fraction between 2/3 and 3/4. Prove it.", on the back of side 2.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: greater than, less than, common denominator, common numerator, same whole. Each card sits beside a marked line. Frame on the display: " and are both ths now, and is more than , so > ." Read every sign aloud as words. A student may place sign cards instead of writing a sign, and may say a proof aloud.

Tomorrow

Day 24 is the end-of-unit assessment; mark E23 tonight. "Ben" is error D. Ana's answer with 2/5 < 3/8 written is error G. "Cannot tell" takes the same-whole rule too far: the trail is one whole. Hand back the E23 tickets and the written tasks, look-fors ticked, when pencils go down on day 24, so their D, G and "cannot tell" join the respond groups.
