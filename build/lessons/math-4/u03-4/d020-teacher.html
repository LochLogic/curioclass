Day 20: Rename both fractions

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Mark the E19 tickets and sort them by error: D (the bigger numbers called the bigger fraction), A (one number changed, as 1/2 = 1/12), G (a right renaming, the sign turned round). Put the display page on the projector. Draw Example 1's lines large on the board. If many showed D or A on E19, also draw a 0 to 1 line in twelfths.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: common multiples and one benchmark

Alone, side 1: List the multiples of 6 and of 9, up to 40. Circle the numbers in both lists. [18 and 36], then Use the line. Write >, = or <: 2/5 7/8. Is each more or less than 1/2? [2/5 is less than 1/2 and 7/8 is more, so 2/5 < 7/8] on the printed line from 0 to 1. Go over both at 4 minutes: 18 and 36 are in both lists; 2/5 is under 1/2 and 7/8 is over it.

5 min. Task: can both be cut into the same parts?

Hand back the E19 tickets. If many missed E19, two minutes of this part go to its error first:

If day 19's tickets showed many D (a > in (a) or a < in (b)): day 19's wrong example again, "7/12 is more than 2/3, because 7 and 12 are bigger", beside both on the twelfths line: 8/12 sits right of 7/12.

If many A (1/2 = 1/12): cut the twelfths line at its half: 6 twelfths, so 1/2 = 6/12.

If many G: read E19's two answers aloud as sentences, "5 twelfths is less than 6 twelfths". The open side faces the greater number.

Then pairs. Ask. "Can 2/3 and 4/5 be put in the same parts? Which parts?" Expect, in this order: "no, 3 does not go into 5" (yesterday only one fraction changed; today both may); "cut each third into 5"; "fifteenths, because thirds and fifths both fit into 15". Note any pair that says 2/15 and 4/15 for Teaching first.

10 min. Teaching first: rename both

Whole class. Students fill the frames on side 1 as you go, not ahead of you. The display shows each step on its own screen; point to the board line as each cut is made.

Example 1 (Compare 2/3 and 4/5. Rename both so the parts are the same size. [10/15 < 12/15, so 2/3 < 4/5]).

  1. Thirds and fifths are not the same size, so we cannot compare the 2 and the 4 yet.
  2. List multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. List multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15. 15 is in both lists.
  3. Cut each third into 5 equal parts. Now the whole has 15 parts. 2 thirds are 2 × 5 = 10 fifteenths, so 2/3 = 10/15.
  4. Cut each fifth into 3 equal parts. The whole has 15 parts again. 4 fifths are 4 × 3 = 12 fifteenths, so 4/5 = 12/15.
  5. Now the parts are the same size, so we compare the counts: 10 fifteenths is less than 12 fifteenths.
  6. Write it: 2/3 = 10/15 and 4/5 = 12/15, and 10/15 < 12/15, so 2/3 < 4/5. On the line, 2/3 is left of 4/5.

Then the task's wrong example (error A): "2/3 = 2/15 and 4/5 = 4/15, so 2/3 < 4/5." The sign is right; the names are wrong. On the board line, cut each third into 5 and count the parts in 2/3: 10, not 2. A right sign from wrong names is still wrong.

Example 2 (Compare 5/8 and 7/12. Rename both so the parts are the same size. [15/24 > 14/24, so 5/8 > 7/12]).

  1. List multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24. List multiples of 12: 12, 24. 24 is in both lists. 8 × 12 = 96 works too, but 24 comes first.
  2. Cut each eighth into 3 equal parts. 5 eighths are 5 × 3 = 15 twenty-fourths, so 5/8 = 15/24.
  3. Cut each twelfth into 2 equal parts. 7 twelfths are 7 × 2 = 14 twenty-fourths, so 7/12 = 14/24.
  4. 15 twenty-fourths is more than 14 twenty-fourths: 15/24 > 14/24, so 5/8 > 7/12.
  5. Check with 1/2: both are more than 1/2, so 1/2 does not settle this pair. The renamed parts do.

In Example 1 the bigger numbers won; in Example 2 they lost. The numbers alone tell us nothing: only the renamed parts settle it.

8 min. Fix it: Ivy's working

Side 2 (Ivy says: "4/5 = 4/30 and 5/6 = 5/30. So 4/5 < 5/6." [4/5 = 24/30 and 5/6 = 25/30, so 4/5 < 5/6. Ivy changed the bottom number only]). Two minutes alone, two minutes of turn-and-talk in pairs, then the class: the display shows Ivy's working beside the right working. Ask. "Ivy's sign is right. Is her working right?" Expect: no; 4/30 is a tiny piece near 0, and 4/5 is near 1; each fifth cut into 6 gives 24 thirtieths.

If many still change one number: count the parts in 4/5 aloud on the board, "one fifth is 6 thirtieths, two fifths are 12", up to 24.

22 min. On your own: four pairs, then a partner check

Alone, side 2, no line given, 17 minutes. For each pair: list multiples of both denominators until one number is in both lists, rename both, write the sign. Then check with 1/2 or 1 where that settles it. Do not say which.

Walk with one question: "What size are the parts now, and how many of each?" Watch for:

  • a sign that follows the bigger numbers, as 5/8 > 4/5 or 2/3 < 3/10 (error D). Ask how many fortieths each is.
  • one number changed, as 5/40 and 4/40 (error A). Ask where 5/40 would sit on a line: near 0.
  • a list that runs long: the two denominators multiplied always work (8 × 5 = 40). Any number in both lists works.

Then 5 minutes in pairs: partners swap and check each renamed fraction by multiplying back ("5 × 5 = 25 and 8 × 5 = 40, so 5/8 = 25/40"). A mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer. If many miss item 2 (5/8 and 4/5), list the multiples of 8 and 5 together on the board.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Is 15 the only number of parts that works for thirds and fifths?" Expect: no; 30 and 45 work too; any number in both lists; 3 × 5 always works. Show 2/3 = 20/30 and 4/5 = 24/30: the same sign.

Ask. "When did 1/2 or 1 save you the renaming?" Expect: when one fraction is under it and one is over (items 3 and 4: 2/3 and 3/10, 9/10 and 4/3).

5 min. Exit: the ticket

Alone: the exit ticket (Compare 3/4 and 7/10. Rename both so the parts are the same size. Write the comparison with >, = or <. [15/20 > 14/20, so 3/4 > 7/10; fortieths are also right]), no line given. Write results on the record sheet under E20.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Copy the warm-up and the pairs from the board. Students rule their own lines: 15 centimetres with a mark every centimetre for Example 1 (each mark is a fifteenth), 12 centimetres with a mark every half centimetre for Example 2 (twenty-fourths). Thirds and fifths, or eighths and twelfths, are then counted off. The key is read aloud for the partner check.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop item 5, the partner check (students check with 1/2 or 1 alone) and the second Ask. Never drop the fix-it or the listed multiples. Longer: the challenge (Find three numbers of parts that work for 3/8 and 5/12. Use one to compare them. [24, 48 and 72 (any three common multiples); 9/24 < 10/24, so 3/8 < 5/12]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: multiple, common multiple, common denominator, greater than, less than. Read every sign as words. Frame on the display: " and are both ths now, and is more than , so > ." A sign card may be placed instead of written.

Tomorrow

On the ticket, 3/4 < 7/10 (error D): after day 21's warm-up, the tenths and twelfths strips are held up and four pairs said aloud as "parts of size". 3/20 and 7/20 (error A): before day 21's Example 2, a line in fourths with each fourth cut into 5, 3/4 = 15/20. The sign turned round (error G): six comparisons read aloud as sentences after day 21's warm-up.
