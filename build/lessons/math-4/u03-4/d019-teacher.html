Day 19: Rename one, then compare

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Put the display page on the projector. Cut a thirds strip and a twelfths strip of one length from card, big enough for the room to see. Strip envelopes from section 3.1 go on desks.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: a common multiple and one half

Alone, top of side 1 (Write a number that is a multiple of both 3 and 4. [12 (24 or 36 also work)], Use 1/2 to compare. Write >, = or <. 2/12 4/6 [<: 2/12 is less than 1/2 and 4/6 is more]). Hand back day 18's written task (Dee's claim) as they work. Read the answers aloud at 4 minutes. Take 12, 24 or 36 for the first, then say "12 is 3 times 4": Example 1 needs it. For the second, each fraction goes on its own side of the 1/2 mark on the printed line. A ">" there is error D.

If day 18's tickets showed many D (9/10 > 7/6): the D students sit near you, and in Example 1 give Kai's claim a full minute of turn-and-talk before step 2. If many I (7/8 = 11/12, "each is one part short"): after Example 2, do Pair 1 together: 3/4 and 7/8 are each one part short, yet 6/8 < 7/8. If many J ("eighths are bigger, so 7/8 > 11/12"): in both examples, say each fraction as "how many parts, of what size" before you rename.

2 min. Two pairs from grade 3

Alone, side 1 (Circle the larger: 3/8 or 5/8. [5/8], Circle the larger: 1/4 or 1/6. [1/4]): readiness item 3 again, with new numbers. Hands up for each answer. A child who circles 1/6 (error D) lays a fourths strip beside a sixths strip: more parts, each smaller. They sit near you for the examples.

13 min. Teaching first: two worked examples

Whole class. Students fill the frames on side 1 as you go and cut and mark on the printed lines. The display shows each step on its own screen. At step 4 of Example 1, hold the large thirds strip on the twelfths strip.

Example 1 (Compare 2/3 and 7/12. Write >, = or <. [2/3 = 8/12 and 8/12 > 7/12, so 2/3 > 7/12]).

  1. Kai says 7/12 is more than 2/3, because 7 and 12 are bigger. Is he right?
  2. Thirds and twelfths are parts of different sizes. We cannot compare the 2 and the 7 yet.
  3. Does 3 fit into 12? 12 = 3 × 4. So cut each third into 4 equal parts.
  4. Cut each third on the line into 4. The whole now has 12 parts. Lay the thirds strip on the twelfths strip: one third is 4 twelfths.
  5. 2/3 = (4 × 2)/(4 × 3) = 8/12. The point did not move; it has a new name.
  6. Mark 7/12 on the same line: count 7 twelfths from 0.
  7. 8/12 and 7/12 have parts of one size. 8 is more than 7, so 8/12 > 7/12.
  8. So 2/3 > 7/12: two thirds is greater than seven twelfths. Kai is not right.

Ask. "Why can't we just compare the 2 and the 7 in 2/3 and 7/12?" Expect, in this order: "they are different parts"; "a third is bigger than a twelfth"; "a third is 4 twelfths, so 2 thirds is 8 twelfths". Finish on the last: it names the size.

Example 2 (Compare 5/4 and 9/8. Write >, = or <. [5/4 = 10/8 and 10/8 > 9/8, so 5/4 > 9/8]).

  1. Fourths and eighths. Which fits into the other? 8 = 4 × 2.
  2. Cut each fourth on the line into 2 equal parts, all the way from 0 to 2.
  3. 5/4 = (2 × 5)/(2 × 4) = 10/8.
  4. Mark 9/8: count 9 eighths from 0. Eight eighths make 1, so 9/8 is one eighth past 1.
  5. 10/8 and 9/8 have parts of one size. 10 is more than 9, so 10/8 > 9/8.
  6. So 5/4 > 9/8: five fourths is greater than nine eighths. Check: 5/4 is to the right of 9/8 on the line.

Write it the long way every time: 2/3 = 8/12 and 8/12 > 7/12, so 2/3 > 7/12. Read every sign as words.

8 min. Fix it: two wrong answers

Side 2 (Rae says: "9/12 is more than 5/6, because 9 and 12 are bigger." [5/6 = 10/12, and 10/12 > 9/12, so 5/6 > 9/12. Rae read the numbers as whole numbers], Ivy says: "4/5 = 4/10, so 4/5 < 7/10." [4/5 = 8/10, and 8/10 > 7/10, so 4/5 > 7/10. Ivy changed the bottom number only]). Pairs for 5 minutes, then the class; the display shows each wrong working beside the right one.

Rae read 9 and 12 as whole numbers (error D): ask "what size are the parts?", then cut each sixth into 2. Ivy changed the bottom number only (error A): ask "how many tenths are in one fifth?" and count them on a line.

If many back Rae: one strip in tenths and one in twelfths side by side, then four pairs said aloud as "parts of size", as in "7 parts of size one tenth". If many back Ivy: fold a fifths strip into tenths and count the shaded parts aloud.

20 min. Four more pairs

Alone for 15 minutes, side 2 (3/4 7/8 [3/4 = 6/8 and 6/8 < 7/8, so 3/4 < 7/8], 2/4 3/8 [2/4 = 4/8 and 4/8 > 3/8, so 2/4 > 3/8], 3/12 1/4 [1/4 = 3/12, so 3/12 = 1/4], 13/10 7/5 [7/5 = 14/10 and 13/10 < 14/10, so 13/10 < 7/5]): rename one fraction, mark both on the printed line, write the comparison the long way, then the sign. Each line is printed in the parts of the fraction to rename; the child cuts each part. Strips are allowed.

Then 5 minutes in pairs: partners swap and read each comparison aloud as a sentence. A mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer.

Check: Read it aloud. Is the greater one to the right on the line? Does the open side face it?

Watch for renaming the wrong fraction (fourths from eighths), a name with one number changed (A), and the right renaming with the sign turned round (error G, first met today): have the child read it aloud. Pair 3 is equal, one point with two names. Pair 4 runs past 1.

7 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "How did you choose which fraction to rename?" Expect: "the one whose bottom number fits into the other's"; "8 is 4 times 2, so fourths cut into eighths".

Ask. "Pair 3 came out equal. What does that look like on the line?" Expect: one point with two names.

Three children read a comparison aloud; the class checks the sign.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone. The ticket (Rename one fraction in each pair so the parts are the same size. Then write >, = or <. (a) 5/12 1/2 (b) 3/2 11/8. [(a) 5/12 < 6/12, so <; (b) 12/8 > 11/8, so >]), read aloud once, signs included. Collect it, and write the results under E19 on the record sheet.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Copy the pairs on the board; students rule a line for each, mark the parts as well as they can, and you read the key aloud.
  • No strips. Fold paper strips of one length in fourths, eighths and twelfths.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop the second Ask. in "Pull it together" and Pair 2. Never drop the fixes, the printed lines or the comparison written the long way. Longer: the challenge (Write a fraction in twelfths that is between 3/4 and 1. [10/12 or 11/12 (3/4 = 9/12)]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: greater than, less than, equal to, twelfths, rename, each beside a marked line. Read every sign as words. Frame on the display and the student page: " and are both ths now, and is more than , so > ." Access: a sign card (>, =, <) may be placed instead of written, and a comparison may be said aloud.

Tomorrow

On the ticket, (a) > or (b) < with no renaming (error D): day 20's task opens with the wrong example on 9/12 and 5/6 and the tenths and twelfths strips. 1/2 = 1/12, or 3/2 = 3/8 (error A): day 20 opens with the one on 4/5 and 7/10, folded into tenths, before its own A example. The right renaming with the sign turned round (error G): day 20's warm-up reads six comparisons aloud as sentences, the open side to the greater.
