Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Mark the E20 tickets and sort them by error: A (3/20 and 7/20), D (3/4 < 7/10), G (sign turned round). Put the display page on the projector. Cut two long paper strips of one length; mark one in tenths and one in twelfths, to hold up.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: an equal fraction and a benchmark

Alone, side 1: 5/6 = /12 [10] and Mark 9/10 and 1/2 on the tenths line. Write >, = or <: 9/10 1/2. [>: 9/10 is past 5/10, which is 1/2], on the tenths line. Then hand back the E20 tickets.

If day 20's tickets showed many A (3/20 and 7/20, one number kept): before Example 2, cut one fourth of the tenths line's whole into 5 on the board and count: 3 fourths is 15 twentieths, not 3. Take the minute from Pull it together.

If many D (3/4 < 7/10): hold up the two strips, tenths over twelfths, and say four pairs aloud as "parts of size": "4 parts of size one tenth", "4 parts of size one twelfth".

If many G (the sign turned round): read six comparisons aloud as sentences; the open side faces the greater number. Take the minutes from Pull it together.

5 min. Teaching first: same count, different parts

Whole class. Students fill the frames on side 1 as you go. The display shows each step on its own screen.

Example 1 (Which is greater, 1/10 or 1/12? [1/10 > 1/12: a tenth is longer than a twelfth]). Hold the two strips up, ends together.

  1. Look at the two lines on side 1. Both go from 0 to 1: the same whole.
  2. The top line is cut into 10 parts. The bottom line is cut into 12 parts.
  3. More parts in the same whole means each part is shorter.
  4. One tenth is longer than one twelfth. So 1/10 > 1/12.

Example 2 (Compare 2/5 and 4/12. Make the numerators the same. [2/5 = 4/10, and 4/10 > 4/12, so 2/5 > 4/12]).

  1. The numerators are 2 and 4. Make 2/5 have 4 parts, like 4/12.
  2. Cut each fifth into 2 equal parts: 2/5 = (2 × 2)/(2 × 5) = 4/10.
  3. Now both have 4 parts: 4/10 and 4/12. Find both on the lines on side 1.
  4. Tenths are longer than twelfths. 4 longer parts are more than 4 shorter parts.
  5. 4/10 > 4/12, so 2/5 > 4/12.
  6. Check with 1/2: both are under 1/2, so 1/2 does not settle it. The renaming does.

Ask. "Both have 4 parts. Which parts are longer, tenths or twelfths?" Expect: tenths, because the same whole is cut into fewer parts. Take "4 and 4 are the same, so they are equal" first, then point at the two lines: the counts match, the lengths do not.

10 min. Two ways, one pair

Whole class, then pairs. Students fill the frame for Example 3.

Example 3 (Compare 1/3 and 2/8 two ways. Which way is quicker? [1/3 = 2/6, and 2/6 > 2/8; or 8/24 > 6/24. So 1/3 > 2/8. Same numerators is quicker here]).

  1. Way 1, same numerators. 2/8 has 2 parts. Cut each third into 2: 1/3 = 2/6.
  2. Sixths are longer than eighths: fewer parts in the same whole. So 2/6 > 2/8, and 1/3 > 2/8.
  3. Way 2, same denominators. 24 is a multiple of 3 and of 8. 1/3 = 8/24 and 2/8 = 6/24.
  4. 8/24 > 6/24, so 1/3 > 2/8. The same sign both ways.
  5. Which was quicker? Same numerators: one fraction renamed, by 2. Same denominators: both renamed, by 8 and by 3.

Ask. "Which way is quicker for this pair?" One minute of turn-and-talk in pairs, then the class. Expect: same numerators, because only 1/3 needed renaming, by 2. Then: "When would the other way be quicker?" Expect: when one denominator fits into the other, as 3 into 12. Do not settle on one way for every pair.

7 min. Fix it: Mia's claim

Side 1 (Mia says 3/12 is more than 3/10, because 12 is more than 10. Is she right? [No. Both have 3 parts, and twelfths are shorter than tenths, so 3/12 < 3/10]). Two minutes alone, two in pairs, then the class: the display shows Mia's working beside the right working (error D). If many still agree with Mia: on the two lines, count 3 tenths from 0 and 3 twelfths from 0, and compare where each stops.

20 min. On your own: six pairs

Alone, side 2 (4/5 4/6 [>: fifths are longer than sixths] to 1/3 5/12 [1/3 = 4/12, and 4/12 < 5/12, so < (or 5/15 < 5/12). Same denominators is quicker here]). Each pair gets renamed fractions, a sign, and the word for the larger parts. Pairs 1 and 2 already have the same numerators; 3 and 4 need one fraction renamed; 5 is equal; for 6 the student says which way was quicker. A student may sketch a line in the box.

Walk with one question: "How many parts, and of what size?" Watch for:

  • < on 1 or > on 2: the bigger denominator read as the bigger fraction (error D). Point to the two lines on side 1.
  • 3/4 for 1/4 on 3, or 8/6 for 4/6 on 4: one number changed (error A). Ask what each fourth was cut into.
  • The right renaming, then the sign turned round (G): ask them to read it aloud as a sentence.
  • On 6, "thirds are bigger pieces" (error J): ask "how many parts, of what size?" for each.

At 14 minutes, partners swap pages and read each comparison aloud as a sentence ("4 fifths is greater than 4 sixths"). Check: Partners read each comparison aloud and agree on the sign. A mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer. If many miss 3 or 4, redo Example 2 together with 1/4 and 3/10.

8 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Pair 1 had the same numerators. Pair 6 was quicker in twelfths. How do you choose?" Expect: "look for a denominator or a numerator that fits the other"; "either way gives the same sign". Show Example 3's two ways side by side.

Ask. "Why is 4 tenths more than 4 twelfths, when 12 is more than 10?" Expect: twelfths are shorter, because the same whole is cut into more parts.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper ((a) 5/12 5/8. (b) Make the numerators the same, then compare: 2/3 6/10. Which parts are bigger, and how do you know? [(a) <; (b) 2/3 = 6/9, ninths are bigger than tenths because the whole is cut into fewer parts, so 2/3 > 6/10]). Read it aloud once, signs as words. Collect at the door; write results on the record sheet under E21.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Draw the two lines on the board, the same length, one in tenths and one in twelfths; students copy the pairs and rule their own two lines of one length.
  • One projector. Show one pair at a time; each student writes the renamed fractions and holds up a sign card before the line is shown.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop pair 5 and the partner reading (students read their own aloud quietly). Never drop the fix or the renamed fractions written beside each sign. Longer: the challenge (Find a fraction with numerator 4. It must be greater than 2/5 and less than 1/2. [4/9: 2/5 = 4/10 and 1/2 = 4/8, and ninths are between]): the answer is a ninth, off the list, found only by making the numerators the same.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: numerator, denominator, greater than, less than, common numerator. Read every sign as words. Frame on the display: " and both have parts. are longer than , so > ."

Tomorrow

On the ticket, (a) > or (b) < (error D, the bigger numbers read as the bigger fraction): day 22's teaching first puts the tenths and twelfths lines up again and says the pair as "parts of size" before the two ways. 2/3 = 6/3 (error A, one number kept): day 22 has those students cut each third into 3 on a line and count. Students who wrote a sign with no renamed fractions sit near you for day 22's first ten minutes.
