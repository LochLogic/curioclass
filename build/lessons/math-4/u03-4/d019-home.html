Day 19: Rename one, then compare

At home

GoalI can compare two fractions when one can be renamed in the other's parts
Time35 minutes, one parent and one child (a teaching day)
Printthe student page and one exit ticket; this page and its answers are for you

Swap. No projector: point at side 1. No strip envelope: fold one paper strip in fourths and one in eighths.

Skip. The warm-up, the grade 3 pairs, Pairs 2 and 4, and the challenge.

  • 10 min. Two examples. Read Example 1 (side 1) together; the child cuts each third on the line into 4 and fills the frame. Then Example 2 (side 1), with the folded strips.

    Say. "Why can't we just compare the 2 and the 7 in 2/3 and 7/12?" Expect: thirds and twelfths are different sizes; 2 thirds is 8 twelfths.

  • 8 min. Fix it. Fix it 1 (side 2), then 2. The child says what went wrong, then does it right.

  • 12 min. Two pairs. Pair 1 (side 2) and 3: rename one, mark both, write the sign.

    Say. "Read it to me as a sentence. Which point is on the right?" Expect: "three fourths is less than seven eighths"; 7/8 is on the right.

  • 5 min. Exit ticket. The child answers the ticket question alone.

Example 1 (side 1)Compare 23 and 712. Write >, = or <.2/3 = 8/12 and 8/12 > 7/12, so 2/3 > 7/12
  1. Kai says 7/12 is more than 2/3, because 7 and 12 are bigger. Is he right?
  2. Thirds and twelfths are parts of different sizes. Mark 2/3 on the line: two thirds from 0.
  3. Does 3 fit into 12? 12 = 3 × 4. So cut each third into 4 equal parts.
  4. Cut each third on the line into 4. The whole now has 12 parts. Lay the thirds strip on the twelfths strip: one third is 4 twelfths.
  5. 2/3 = (4 × 2)/(4 × 3) = 8/12. The point did not move; it has a new name.
  6. Mark 7/12 on the same line: count 7 twelfths from 0.
  7. 8/12 and 7/12 have parts of one size. 8 is more than 7, so 8/12 > 7/12.
  8. So 2/3 > 7/12: two thirds is greater than seven twelfths. Kai is not right.
Example 2 (side 1)Compare 54 and 98. Write >, = or <.5/4 = 10/8 and 10/8 > 9/8, so 5/4 > 9/8
  1. Mark 5/4 on the line: one fourth past 1. Fourths and eighths: which fits into the other? 8 = 4 × 2.
  2. Cut each fourth on the line into 2 equal parts, all the way from 0 to 2.
  3. 5/4 = (2 × 5)/(2 × 4) = 10/8: the same point, a new name.
  4. Mark 9/8: count 9 eighths from 0. Eight eighths make 1, so 9/8 is one eighth past 1.
  5. 10/8 and 9/8 have parts of one size. 10 is more than 9, so 10/8 > 9/8.
  6. So 5/4 > 9/8: five fourths is greater than nine eighths. Check: 5/4 is to the right of 9/8 on the line.
