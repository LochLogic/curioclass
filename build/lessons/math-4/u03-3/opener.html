Section 3.3: Comparing to one half and one; the same whole (days 17 to 18)

The math, for the adult

Standard 4.NF.A.2: "Compare two fractions with different numerators and different denominators, e.g., by creating common denominators or numerators, or by comparing to a benchmark fraction such as 1/2. Recognize that comparisons are valid only when the two fractions refer to the same whole. Record the results of comparisons with symbols >, =, or <, and justify the conclusions, e.g., by using a visual fraction model." This section's share: comparing to 1/2 and to 1, the same whole, and justifying. Common denominators and numerators are section 3.4's, and nothing here may lean on them.

A fraction is of a whole. Half of a small pizza and half of a large pizza are both 1/2, but not the same amount of food. A fraction on its own is a number, a point on the line, where the whole is the fixed length from 0 to 1. A fraction of a thing is an amount, and two amounts can be compared by their fractions only when the things are the same size. So "Jo read 1/3 of a long book and Max read 1/2 of a short one, and Jo read more pages" says nothing about 1/3 and 1/2 unless the books match. From day 17 every story says whether the whole is the same, or leaves it out on purpose, and the student says which.

Comparing to 1/2 without common denominators. Three ways, all from what students already have:

  • A name for 1/2 in the fraction's own parts (section 3.2): 1/2 = 5/10, so 4/10 is less than 1/2.
  • Half of the denominator: half of 5 fifths is two and a half fifths, so 2/5 is less than 1/2.
  • The printed line, from 0 to 2 with 1/2 and 1 marked: "more than half" becomes "to the right of 1/2".

When one fraction is below 1/2 and the other above, the comparison is settled: 2/5 < 1/2 and 1/2 < 5/8, so 2/5 < 5/8. Two fractions on the same side of 1/2 are not compared here, with one exception, next.

Comparing to 1. A fraction with a top larger than its bottom is past 1: 6/5 is more than 1 and 5/6 less, so 6/5 > 5/6 at once. Two fractions that are each one part short of 1, such as 4/5 and 5/6, are compared by the part that is missing. 4/5 is 1/5 short of 1 and 5/6 is 1/6 short. A sixth is smaller than a fifth, so 5/6 is closer to 1, and 4/5 < 5/6. The count of missing parts is the same (one each); their size is not. That is the reasoning in the Progressions, and the source of two errors (I and J below).

Limits. Denominators from the grade's list. Benchmarks 1/2 and 1 only. No common denominators or numerators.

The line from 0 to 2

  • Day 17. Two paper strips of different length, each folded in half, the halves laid together. Then the line from 0 to 2 with 1/2 and 1 marked. The comparison is written under the line: 2/5 < 1/2 and 1/2 < 5/8, so 2/5 < 5/8. This is the day the benchmark is tied to the written form.
  • Day 18. The same printed line, now used for 1: pairs on each side of 1, then pairs each one part short.

The number lines sheet from section 3.1 (u03-1/number-lines.md) has the line from 0 to 2 with 1/2 and 1 marked on side 2. The line is withdrawn on day 22, in section 3.4; benchmark comparisons return in the warm-ups of days 19 to 23. A sketched line is always one way to justify.

Why this model

A benchmark turns a hard comparison into two easy ones. It also catches a wrong renaming later: in section 3.4, "one is under a half and one is over, so the sign is right" is the check on the sign. Two real wholes of different size are the only way to make "the same whole" felt rather than told, and two paper strips do it in a minute. The line from 0 to 2 makes 1/2 and 1 places, so "closer to 1" is a distance a student can see. No tool is needed; the section runs on print.

The errors to expect, in plain words

What students do What it shows What to do when many do it
D Think bigger numbers make a bigger fraction: "4/10 is more than 1/2, because 4 and 10 are bigger" They read the numerals as whole numbers Find 1/2 on the line as 5/10 too: more parts, each smaller
H Compare fractions of different wholes: "half of the large pizza is more, so this 1/2 is greater than that 1/2" The fraction is taken as an amount of stuff, not a number Fold two strips of different length in half and lay the halves together; ask "half of what?"
I Say two fractions each one part short of 1 are equal: "5/6 = 7/8" They count the missing parts, not their size Mark both on one line and shade the missing parts: 1/6 is longer than 1/8
J Judge by the size of the parts alone: "2/3 is more than 5/6, thirds are bigger pieces" They look at the denominator and not at how many parts there are Say each fraction as "how many parts, of what size"; then mark both on one line

The day each wrong example is shown: D and H on day 17, I and J on day 18. Each day's teacher page names the wrong answer that signals each error on that day's exit. J sounds like good reasoning: thirds are bigger, true, but 2 thirds is 4 sixths, fewer than 5 sixths.

The question asked later (readiness)

Day 17 opens, after the warm-up, with the question held back from the readiness check: "Is half of a small pizza equal to half of a large one?" Ask it aloud and listen for five minutes; do not settle it. The two strips settle it in the teaching that follows. Note who says "yes, a half is a half": they are the students to watch for error H on day 17's exit.

Supplies for the section

  • Per student: two paper strips of different length (day 17), cut from scrap; the number lines sheet (side 2, days 17 and 18); a pencil.
  • Per class: the projector for the display pages; a board; a short and a long strip for the teacher to fold where all can see. There is no document camera: fold them at the front, then draw the two halves on the board.
  • A room with nothing: two strips torn from scrap paper; a line ruled by hand with 1/2 and 1 marked.

Words

Benchmark, the same whole, closer to, greater than, less than, each put on the marked line or the two strips. Signs are read as words. Frame, to take or leave: " is less than 1/2 and __ is more than 1/2, so __ < ." For day 18: " is missing __ and __ is missing . A __ is smaller, so __ is closer to 1."

How the section is checked

Exits on days 17 and 18, on paper, written on the unit record sheet by error letter (courses/math-4/u03-1/ record.md). Day 17's exit has a sentence in part (a): a tick needs the reason, not "no" alone. Day 18's written task (Dee's claim) is judged with three look-fors on that day's key and handed back on day 19. End-of-unit assessment items 3.3a and 3.3b, on day 24, are this section's.
