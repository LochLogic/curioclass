Day 18: Closer to 1

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Sort day 17's tickets by error. Draw a long line from 0 to 2 on the board, with 1/2 and 1 marked and room under it to write. Put the display page on the projector.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: one equal fraction

Alone, top of side 1 (7/3 = /12 [28]). Show the answer at 4 minutes. 7 is one number kept (A); 16 is 9 added to both (B).

If day 17's tickets showed many H (yes in part a, or a no with no word on the bottles): before Example 1, fold your two strips from day 17 in half and lay the halves together. Ask "half of what?" Write under the board line: "On one line, 1 is one length."

If day 17's tickets showed many D (3/8 > 2/3, or 3/8 > 1/2 in the working): before Example 1, write "4/10 is more than 1/2, because 4 and 10 are bigger" beside the board line, where 1/2 is also 5/10. Either branch takes its 3 minutes from the partner check. With both, H goes first and D waits for Each side of 1, in place of its two strips.

5 min. Teaching first: 6/5 and 5/6

Whole class, at the board line. Students fill the frame under Example 1.

Example 1 (Mark 5/6 and 6/5 on the line from 0 to 2. Write >, = or <: 5/6 6/5. [<: 5/6 is less than 1 and 6/5 is more than 1]).

  1. The top line is in sixths: 1 is 6 sixths. The lower line is in fifths: 1 is 5 fifths.
  2. 5/6 is 5 of the 6 parts from 0 to 1. It stops one sixth before 1.
  3. 6/5 is 5 fifths, which make 1, and 1 fifth more. It is past 1.
  4. 5/6 is left of 1 and 6/5 is right of 1. So 5/6 < 1 and 1 < 6/5.
  5. So 5/6 < 6/5. The top number tells us: less than the bottom number, less than 1; more than the bottom number, more than 1.

45 min. By hand: each side of 1, then one part short

Each side of 1, 8 minutes: 5 alone, 3 in pairs. Side 1, Each side of 1 (7/5 9/12 [>: 7/5 is more than 1 and 9/12 is less], 6/8 5/3 [<: 6/8 is less than 1 and 5/3 is more], 8/8 10/10 [=: each is 1], 9/5 9/12 [>: 9/5 is more than 1 and 9/12 is less]). Pairs: swap pages; a mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer.

Check: Is the top number less than, equal to, or more than the bottom number?

Watch for the sign that follows the bigger numbers (D): all four are built so D gives the wrong sign; on 3 it gives < where both are 1. If many make it, two strips from the envelopes side by side: more parts, each smaller. Then say each fraction as "how many parts, of what size".

Example 2, 6 minutes. Whole class. Students fill the frame under the printed lines.

Example 2 (4/5 and 5/6 are each one part short of 1. Which is closer to 1? Write 4/5 5/6. [5/6, because a sixth is smaller than a fifth; <]).

  1. 4/5 is on the fifths line, one fifth short of 1. 5/6 is on the sixths line, one sixth short of 1.
  2. Look at the gap from each point up to 1: a fifth for 4/5, a sixth for 5/6.
  3. A sixth is smaller than a fifth: the whole is cut into more parts.
  4. 5/6 is missing the smaller part, so it is closer to 1.
  5. So 4/5 < 5/6.

The planned question, 3 minutes. Pairs, one minute, then the class.

Ask. "5/6 and 7/8 are each one part short of 1. Are they equal?" Expect, in this order: "yes, each is one part short"; "no, an eighth is smaller, so 7/8 is closer to 1". Take the yes first and write it up as Rio's claim.

Fix it, 9 minutes. Side 2 (Rio says 5/6 = 7/8, because each is one part short of 1. [Not equal: 5/6 is 1/6 short and 7/8 is 1/8 short; an eighth is smaller, so 5/6 < 7/8], Ivy says 2/3 is more than 5/6, because thirds are bigger pieces. [Not right: 2/3 is 1/3 short of 1 and 5/6 only 1/6 short, so 2/3 < 5/6]). For each, one minute of turn-and-talk in pairs, then the class. The display shows their working first and the right working after the pair minute. Rio counted the missing parts and not their size (I): shade both missing parts on the board line, 1/6 is longer than 1/8. Ivy looked at the size of the parts and not how many (J): say each as "how many parts, of what size", then shade both missing parts on the board line: 1/3 is longer than 1/6.

One part short, or one part more, 8 minutes. Alone, side 2 (3/4 2/3. Each is one part short of 1. [>: 3/4 is missing a fourth, 2/3 a third; a fourth is smaller], 1/2 99/100. Each is one part short of 1. [<: 1/2 is missing a half, 99/100 only a hundredth], 5/4 and 13/12 are each one part more than 1. Which is closer to 1? Write 5/4 13/12. [13/12, because a twelfth is smaller than a fourth; >]). Each needs the sign and the missing or extra part of each. Watch for = (I) and for the sign turned by part size (J). A right sign on 3 and 4 is not enough; ask what each is missing. Item 5 shows who is using bigger numbers (D). If many miss the first, mark 2/3 and 3/4 on the board line and shade both missing parts together.

Dee's claim, 8 minutes. Alone, side 2 (Dee says 3/4 and 5/6 are the same distance from 1, because each is one part short. Is Dee right? Mark both on the line and explain. [Dee is not right: 3/4 is 1/4 short of 1 and 5/6 is 1/6 short; a sixth is smaller than a fourth, so 5/6 is closer to 1, and 3/4 < 5/6]). Students mark both on the printed lines and write. Walk and read. Ask: "What is each one missing? Which missing part is longer?" Collect these with the tickets; they are judged against the look-fors on the key and handed back in day 19's first five minutes.

Pull it together, 3 minutes. Whole class.

Ask. "How can you tell a fraction is more than 1 without the line?" Expect: the top number is more than the bottom number.

Ask. "Two fractions are each one part short of 1. How do you tell which is closer?" Expect: the one missing the smaller part; more parts in the whole means each part is smaller.

Ask. "Someone says the fraction with bigger numbers is always closer to 1. Test it on 5/4 and 13/12." Expect: "no: 13/12 is closer, and it is the smaller one; it has the smaller extra part".

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, no line given ((a) 9/10 7/6. How do you know without renaming? (b) 7/8 and 11/12 are each one part short of 1. Which is closer to 1, and why? So 7/8 11/12. [(a) <: 9/10 is less than 1 and 7/6 is more; (b) 11/12, because a twelfth is smaller than an eighth (a tick needs the reason); <]). Read it aloud once. Collect at 5 minutes.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Draw each example's lines on the board.
  • No projector. Write each fix on the board, the wrong working on the left and the right working beside it.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop the partner check (students check their own against the display) and the challenge. Never drop the fixes or Dee's claim. Longer: the challenge (Put 5/12, 9/8 and 3/5 in order. Say which benchmark you used for each. [5/12, 3/5, 9/8: 5/12 is less than 1/2; 3/5 is more than 1/2 and less than 1; 9/8 is more than 1]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: benchmark, the same whole, closer to, one part short. Frames on the display: " is missing 1/. is missing 1/. A is smaller, so is closer to 1." " is less than 1 and is more than 1, so < ." Large-print line on request.
