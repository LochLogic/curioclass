Day 17: Half of what?

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Cut your two strips, 10 inches and 6 inches. Cut about 7 sheets into long strips and 5 into short ones. Draw a line from 0 to 2 on the board with 1/2 and 1 marked, long enough to write under. Put the display page on the projector.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: a name for 1/2

Alone: Fill in the blank: 1/2 = 6/. [12]. Hand back day 16's written task as they work, and read one "meets" answer aloud. If many said 10/6 is 2 times as big (error F), mark 5/3 and 10/6 on the board line: one point. Then ask for other names for 1/2 and write them on the board: 2/4, 3/6, 4/8, 5/10, 6/12, 50/100. Leave them up all day. A student who wrote 7 added 5 to both numbers (error B): ask "6 is how many times 1?"

5 min. Question aloud: two pizzas

Whole class. Ask. "Is half of a small pizza equal to half of a large one?" Listen; do not settle it yet. Note who says "the same, both are a half" and who says "the big half is more food".

14 min. Teaching first: two worked examples

Whole class. Hand out the strips first; students fold their own as you fold yours, and fill the frames on side 1. The display shows each step on its own screen.

If day 16's tickets showed many A, B or E: that error's wrong example from the error register opens with its "if many" action ("1/3 = 1/6" beside two strips for A; Jo's 3/4 = 5/6 on one line for B; Lena's 6/4 and 9/6 each given a name with smaller numbers for E). Many who left out 4/3: write 8/6 = 4/3 with both numbers divided by 2. Either takes its 3 minutes from the sort's partner check.

Ask. After step 3, pairs, 1 minute: "Half of this strip and half of that one: are they the same length? Are they both one half?" Expect: not the same length; both are 1/2, each of its own strip. Push for "half of what?"

Example 1 (Ms. Ruiz has a 10-inch strip and a 6-inch strip. She folds each in half. How long is each half? Is each one 1/2? [5 inches and 3 inches; each is 1/2 of its own strip, so the halves are not the same amount]).

  1. Fold the 10-inch strip in half: each half is 5 inches.
  2. Fold the 6-inch strip in half: each half is 3 inches.
  3. Lay the two halves together. They are not the same length.
  4. Are both one half? Yes. Each is 1/2 of its own strip.
  5. So "1/2" alone does not tell how much. Ask: half of what?
  6. Two fractions can be compared only when they are parts of the same whole.

Come back to the pizzas: the big half is more food, and both are 1/2. "1/2" says how the whole is cut, not how much stuff. On the number line the whole is always the length from 0 to 1, so it never changes: any two fractions on one line can be compared.

Example 2 (Compare 2/5 and 5/8. Use 1/2. [2/5 < 1/2 and 1/2 < 5/8, so 2/5 < 5/8]). On the board line.

  1. Draw a line from 0 to 2. Mark 1/2 and 1. The whole is the length from 0 to 1.
  2. Is 2/5 more or less than 1/2? Half of 5 parts is two and a half parts. 2 parts is less, so 2/5 is left of 1/2. Write 2/5 < 1/2.
  3. Is 5/8 more or less than 1/2? In eighths, 1/2 is 4/8. 5/8 is one eighth more.
  4. So 5/8 is right of 1/2. Write 1/2 < 5/8.
  5. 2/5 is left of 1/2 and 5/8 is right of it, so 2/5 < 5/8.
  6. We never compared 2 with 5 or 5 with 8. We compared each fraction with 1/2.

Say: "1/2 is a benchmark: a point we compare to." Name the two ways to set a fraction against 1/2: half of its bottom number, or a name for 1/2 in its own parts (the warm-up list). Say aloud that 5 and 8 being bigger than 2 and 5 is not the reason.

10 min. Fix it: two wrong answers

"Fix it", side 1 (Rio says: "Half a large pizza is more food than half a small pizza. So this 1/2 is greater than that 1/2." [Each is 1/2 of its own pizza, so 1/2 = 1/2. The large half is more food because its whole is bigger. Compare fractions only of the same whole], Kim says: "4/10 is more than 1/2, because 4 and 10 are bigger." [4/10 < 1/2: in tenths 1/2 is 5/10, and 4/10 is one tenth less. Kim read the numbers as whole numbers]). One minute in pairs on each, then the class; the display shows their working first and the right working after the pair minute.

Rio (error H) is right that there is more food and wrong that 1/2 > 1/2. If many agree with Rio: fold two strips of different length again and ask "half of what?" Kim (error D) read 4 and 10 as whole numbers. Point to 5/10 under 1/2 on the line: 4/10 is one tenth to its left. If many agree with Kim: students lay fifths and tenths from their envelopes side by side; more parts, each smaller.

9 min. Sort ten fractions

Alone, side 2 (Sort each fraction: less than 1/2, equal to 1/2, or more than 1/2. 1/10, 2/12, 30/100, 4/8, 3/6, 7/12, 70/100, 6/8, 7/5, 3/3 [Less: 1/10, 2/12, 30/100. Equal: 4/8, 3/6. More: 7/12, 70/100, 6/8, 7/5, 3/3]), 6 minutes; students may mark them on the printed line. Then 3 minutes in pairs: partners settle any fraction they placed differently by naming its half. Walk the room for 30/100 or 2/12 under "more than" (error D): ask "what is 1/2 in hundredths?" Watch 7/5 and 3/3; both are past 1/2 on the line.

Check: Less than 1/2: 1/10, 2/12, 30/100. Equal: 4/8, 3/6. More: 7/12, 70/100, 6/8, 7/5, 3/3.

8 min. On your own: compare across 1/2

Alone, side 2 (Write >, = or <: 35/100 4/5. [<: 35/100 < 1/2 (1/2 is 50/100) and 1/2 < 4/5 (half of 5 is two and a half)], Write >, = or <: 9/12 45/100. [>: 9/12 > 1/2 (1/2 is 6/12) and 1/2 > 45/100 (1/2 is 50/100)]). Each is two comparisons with 1/2, then the answer. In both, the fraction with bigger numbers is the smaller one: > in the first or < in the second compared the numbers (error D). Send the student to the warm-up list for a name for 1/2.

4 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Rio and Kim were both wrong. What do you check before you compare two fractions?" Expect: "that they are of the same whole", then "where each one is beside 1/2".

Ask. "How can you tell a fraction is less than 1/2 without a picture?" Expect: "the top is less than half the bottom"; "find 1/2 in its own parts".

5 min. Exit: the ticket

Alone. Read part (a) aloud once ((a) Sam drank 1/3 of a small bottle of water. Tia drank 1/4 of a large bottle. Tia drank more water. Does that show 1/4 > 1/3? Explain in one sentence. (b) Write >, = or <: 3/8 2/3. Show how you know. [(a) No: the bottles are different wholes, so Tia's 1/4 can be more water than Sam's 1/3. A "no" that only says 1/4 < 1/3 is true but does not say why Tia drank more: not a tick; (b) <: 3/8 < 1/2 (1/2 is 4/8) and 2/3 > 1/2 (half of 3 is one and a half)]). Collect them; the results go on the record sheet under E17.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Write the sort and the two comparisons on the board; students write in notebooks and rule their own line from 0 to 2.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop the partner check on the sort and the challenge. Never drop the strips, the two fixes or the written comparisons with 1/2. Longer: the challenge (Write every fraction in tenths that is more than 0 and less than 1/2. How do you know you have them all? [1/10, 2/10, 3/10, 4/10; the next one, 5/10, is 1/2, so it is not less]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: half, the same whole, benchmark, more than, less than. Frame on the display: " is less than 1/2 and is more than 1/2, so < ." Wide strips and a large-print line for those who need them; a scribe or an oral answer for part (a).

Tomorrow

On the ticket, "yes" in (a), or a "no" with no word on the bottles (error H): day 18 opens with the two strips again, folded and laid together, and "half of what?". In (b), 3/8 > 2/3 (error D): day 18 opens with "4/10 is more than 1/2" beside the line where 1/2 is also 5/10.
