Day 7: Follow the rule, then notice more

At home

GoalI can follow a rule to make a number pattern, notice something the rule did not say, and say why it keeps going
Time38 minutes, one parent and one child (a teaching day)
Printthe student page, the recording page and one exit ticket; this page and its answers are for you

Swap. No counters: coins or dried beans, laid in pairs. No projector: point at side 1.

Skip. The warm-up, items 5 and 6, tape 5, and the challenge.

  • 10 min. The example. Read Example 1 (side 1) together, with coins in pairs for 3 and 7. The child fills the frame. Then Fix it 1 (side 1).

    Say. "The rule says add 4. Did it say anything about odd numbers? So why is every number odd?" Expect: no; 3 is odd, and 4 is two pairs, so the one left over stays alone.

  • 7 min. Tape 1. Tape 1 (side 1): write 1, 4, 7 on the recording page; the child writes the next five, marks one feature and writes why.

  • 6 min. Fix two. Fix it 2 (side 2), then 3.

    Say. "Lena showed three more numbers. How do you know about the ones not written?" Expect: a jump of 3 is a pair and one more, so it turns odd to even.

  • 10 min. On your own. Pattern 3 (side 2), then 4: eight numbers first, then a feature and why.

  • 5 min. Exit ticket. The child answers the ticket question alone.

The questions

The child works on the printed page; the answers are for you.

WhereQuestionAnswer
Example 1 (side 1)Start at 3. The rule is add 4. Write the first eight numbers. What is true that the rule did not say? Why will it keep going?3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, 31. Every number is odd: 3 is odd, and 4 is two pairs, so the one left over never gets a partner
  1. Write the start first. 3 is the 1st number. 3 is one pair and 1 left over, so 3 is odd.
  2. Jump by the rule: 3 + 4 = 7, the 2nd number. Draw 7 in pairs: the 4 added two more pairs, and the 1 left over is still alone. 7 is odd.
  3. Keep jumping by 4, an arrow between neighbours: 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, 31. That is eight numbers, the start counted as the 1st.
  4. Look at the numbers, not the rule. Every one is odd. The rule said add 4. It said nothing about odd.
  5. Why it keeps going: 4 is two pairs. Adding pairs never gives the one left over a partner. Odd plus even is odd, every jump, for ever.

Pattern 4 (side 2)Start at 3. The rule is multiply by 2. Write the first eight numbers. Write one thing the rule did not say. Why will it keep going?3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192, 384. Every number after the first is even: each is 2 times a number, so it is all pairs. Also right: every number is a multiple of 3
the ticket questionStart at 29. The rule is add 6. Write the first six numbers. Write one thing you notice that the rule did not say. Why will it stay true for every number?29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59. All odd: an odd number plus 6 is odd. "The ones digits go 9, 5, 1, 7, 3" is also right
Look-fors:
  1. writes 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59
  2. names a feature true of every number that is not the rule
  3. says what one jump of 6 does to it
Samples (ours, untested):
  • meets: 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59. All odd. 6 is three pairs, so the one left over never gets a partner.
  • partly: 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59. All odd, because it adds 6 each time. (Look-fors 1 and 2 only: ask what one jump of 6 does to the one left over.)
  • not: 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59. It adds 6. 65 is odd too. (Errors C and E.)
