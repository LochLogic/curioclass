Day 9: Show what I know

At home

GoalI can show what I know and fix what I missed
Time45 minutes, one parent and one child (an assessment day)
Printthe student page; this page and its answers are for you

Swap. No projector: mark from the key. No paper squares: coins or squares torn from scrap.

Skip. The groups, the challenges, and unit 3's readiness check (give it the next morning, before day 10).

  • 30 min. End-of-unit assessment. Items 1 to 6, alone, with no squares or coins. Read each item aloud once. Not reached is not wrong.

  • 15 min. Go over it, one to one. Mark 1, 2, 4 and 6 together from the key; read 3 and 5 against its look-fors. Fix each missed one in pen beside the first try: item 1 (side 1) by circling the start and writing it first; item 4 (side 1) by building step 1 with coins, one on a row of 5.

    Say. For item 2 (side 1): "Cover the rule. Is each number odd or even?" Expect: even, odd, even, odd, even; they take turns.

The questions

The child works on the printed page; the answers are for you.

WhereQuestionAnswer
item 1 (side 1)Start at 8. The rule is add 9. Write the first six numbers.8, 17, 26, 35, 44, 53
item 2 (side 1)Start at 12, add 11: 12, 23, 34, 45, 56. Write two things you notice that the rule did not tell you.Any two: even and odd take turns; the ones digit goes up by 1 each time; the tens digit goes up by 1 each time; in each number the ones digit is 1 more than the tens digit; or any other true thing about all five numbers that is not the rule (if yours is not listed, ask your teacher). Only even and odd taking turns lasts for ever: the digit features stop at the 9th number, 100
item 3 (side 1)Nia starts at 61 and adds 8 each time: 61, 69, 77, 85, 93. She says every number in her pattern will be odd, for ever. Is Nia right? Explain why.Right: 61 is odd, and adding 8, an even number, to an odd number always gives an odd number
Look-fors:
  1. says Nia is right
  2. says 8 is even or 61 is odd
  3. says why odd plus even stays odd
Samples (ours, untested):
  • meets: Yes. 61 is odd. 8 is even, so it adds pairs and the one left over is still there.
  • partly: Nia is right. 61 is odd and 8 is even. (Look-fors 1 and 2 only: ask what adding 8 does to the one left over.)
  • not: Yes, I checked up to 125. (Error E: more numbers, not a reason.)
item 4 (side 1)Step 1 is one square sitting on a row of 5 squares. The rule: each step adds one more row of 5 underneath. Draw steps 2 and 3. Write the number of squares in steps 1 to 4. How many squares are in step 9?Step 2 has two rows of 5, step 3 has three, each with the one square on top; 6, 11, 16, 21; step 9 is 6 + 8 × 5 = 46 (or 1 + 9 × 5 = 46)
item 5 (side 2)Look at your numbers for item 4. Write one thing you notice that the rule did not tell you. Explain why it will keep going.Even and odd take turns, because adding 5 to an even number gives an odd one and to an odd number an even one; or the ones digits go 6, 1, 6, 1, because two rows of 5 add 10. Judge it on the student's own item 4 numbers: 5, 10, 15 also take turns and can meet both look-fors. No item 4 numbers: read the reason only
Look-fors:
  1. the feature is not the rule
  2. the reason is about what adding 5 does, not more numbers
Samples (ours, untested):
  • meets: They go even, odd, even, odd. Adding 5 changes even to odd and odd to even every time.
  • partly: They go even, odd, even, odd. (Look-for 1 only: ask what adding 5 does to an even number.)
  • not: It goes even, odd. 36 is even and 41 is odd, so it keeps going. (Error E: more numbers, not a reason.)
item 6 (side 2)Start at 14. The rule is multiply by 3. Write the first four numbers. What do you notice about every number?14, 42, 126, 378; each is even (also right: each is a multiple of 14, or of 7; every number after the first is a multiple of 3)
