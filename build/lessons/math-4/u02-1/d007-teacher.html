Day 7: Follow the rule, then notice more

Teacher page

Day7, section 2.1, understanding day
GoalI can follow a rule to make a number pattern, notice something the rule did not say, and say why it keeps going
Standard parts4.OA.B.4 (a) all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1 to 100; 4.OA.B.4 (c) whether a whole number in the range 1 to 100 is a multiple of a given one-digit number; 4.OA.C.5 (a) a number pattern that follows a given rule; 4.OA.C.5 (c) features of the pattern that were not in the rule itself; 4.OA.C.5 (d) informally why a feature will keep going (the standard's own example: "explain informally why the numbers will continue to alternate")
ToolRule Machine (light), 10 min

Before class

Print everything on the print list. Put the display page on the projector. Write the two tapes on a covered part of the board: "Start at 1. Add 3. 1, 4, 7" and "Start at 3. Add 5. 3, 8, 13". Have the readiness results on unit 2's record sheet beside you, and day 6's marked items 5 and 6 ready to hand back.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: factor pairs and multiples

Alone, then the answers on the display: Find all the factor pairs of 65. [1 × 65, 5 × 13], Is 74 a multiple of 4? Show how you know. [No: 4 × 18 = 72 and 4 × 19 = 76, so 74 falls between them], Is 95 a multiple of 5? Show how you know. [Yes: 5 × 19 = 95 (5 × 20 = 100, and 5 less is 95)]. Students below the facts mark keep the 9 by 9 table out.

If the readiness check showed gaps: nothing moves today; the reteach is day 8's warm-up and first five minutes. Students who missed item 3 get counters now and keep them. Students who missed item 1 are the ones to watch on the first jump. Students who gave examples on item 2 get the second planned question.

5 min. Unit 1's items 5 and 6 back

Alone. Hand back the day 6 assessments. Show the "meets" answer of item 5, then of item 6, from the key. Students fix their own in pen beside the first try. Collect them again for the record.

12 min. Teaching first: start at 3, add 4, with dot pairs

Whole class. You draw dots under the camera; pairs build each number with counters. Students fill the frame for Example 1 on side 1.

Example 1 (Start at 3. The rule is add 4. Write the first eight numbers. What is true that the rule did not say? Why will it keep going? [3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, 31. Every number is odd: 3 is odd, and 4 is two pairs, so the one left over never gets a partner]).

  1. Write the start first. 3 is the 1st number. 3 is one pair and 1 left over, so 3 is odd.
  2. Jump by the rule: 3 + 4 = 7, the 2nd number. Draw 7 in pairs: the 4 added two more pairs, and the 1 left over is still alone. 7 is odd.
  3. Keep jumping by 4, an arrow between neighbours: 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, 31. That is eight numbers, the start counted as the 1st.
  4. Look at the numbers, not the rule. Every one is odd. The rule said add 4. It said nothing about odd.
  5. Why it keeps going: 4 is two pairs. Adding pairs never gives the one left over a partner. Odd plus even is odd, every jump, for ever.

Ask (after step 4). "The rule says add 4. Did it say anything about odd numbers? So how did they get there?" Expect: "no"; then "3 is odd, and 4 is two pairs". Take "no" first, then the pairs.

Then the wrong example, Fix it 1 (Rosa followed the rule start at 3, add 4. She wrote 4, 8, 12, 16. [3, 7, 11, 15. Rosa left out the start: the start, 3, is the 1st number]), whole class.

  1. Rosa's working: 4, 8, 12, 16. She began with the 4 from the rule and counted by 4s, as if from 0.
  2. Right working: the start is the 1st number, so write 3 first. Then jump by 4: 3 + 4 = 7, 7 + 4 = 11, 11 + 4 = 15.

The start is a number in the list, not a number in the rule. If many wrote 4 first in the frame: five rules on the board, circle each start, write it first, then jump.

10 min. Rule Machine, on paper: two tapes

Alone, on the recording page, patterns 1 and 2 (Start at 1. Add 3. The tape shows 1, 4, 7. Write the next five numbers. Mark one thing the rule did not say. Why will it keep going? [10, 13, 16, 19, 22. Odd and even take turns: 3 is a pair and one more, so each jump turns odd to even and even to odd], Start at 3. Add 5. The tape shows 3, 8, 13. Write the next five numbers. Mark one thing the rule did not say. Why will it keep going? [18, 23, 28, 33, 38. The ones digits go 3, 8, 3, 8: two jumps of 5 are one jump of 10, and adding 10 leaves the ones digit alone. Also right: odd and even take turns]). When it exists: Rule Machine, number tapes 1 and 5, practise with feedback. Until then, uncover tape 1 on the board. Students copy the start, the rule and the three numbers, write the next five, mark one feature and write why. At 5 minutes show the full list; each checks their own numbers in pen. Then tape 5 the same way.

Watch for the rule given as the feature (error C) and more numbers as the reason (error E): the next fixes take both. On tape 5, a student who saw only odd and even is right; ask "what do the ones digits do?"

Stop at 10 minutes.

23 min. By hand: two fixes, then two rules

Fix it, 8 minutes. Side 2 (Theo made start at 1, add 3: 1, 4, 7, 10. He says: "I notice it adds 3." [Adding 3 is the rule, not something new. Something the rule did not say: odd and even take turns (1, 4, 7, 10 go odd, even, odd, even)], Lena made start at 1, add 3. She noticed odd and even take turns. She says: "It keeps going because 13, 16 and 19 do it too." [More numbers are examples, not a reason. A reason: 3 is a pair and one more, so every jump gives the one left over a partner or leaves one alone; odd turns even and even turns odd]). Pairs, two minutes each to say what went wrong, then the class. The display shows each working beside the right one.

For Theo:

  1. Theo's working: the numbers are right, but the thing he noticed, add 3, is the rule itself.
  2. Right working: cover the rule and look at the numbers alone. 1 is odd, 4 is even, 7 is odd, 10 is even. Odd and even take turns. The rule never said that.

If many named the rule as the feature: cover the rule on the board and list what is true of the numbers themselves.

For Lena:

  1. Lena's working: 13, 16 and 19 take turns, so it keeps going. Three more numbers say nothing about the ones not written yet.
  2. Right working: 13 is pairs and 1 left over. Add 3: the 1 left over gets a partner from it, and 2 more make a pair. Draw 16 in pairs: none left over. Add 3 again and 1 is left over. Every jump of 3 does this, so odd and even take turns for ever.

Ask. "Lena showed me three more numbers. How do you know about the ones she has not written?" Expect: "every jump adds 3, a pair and one more"; take any answer about the jump, not about more numbers. If many give numbers: dot pairs for one jump, then finish "It must keep going because " together.

On your own, 12 minutes. Side 2 (Start at 2. The rule is add 3. Write the first eight numbers. Write one thing the rule did not say. Why will it keep going? [2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23. Even and odd take turns, starting with even: each jump of 3 turns even to odd and odd to even], Start at 3. The rule is multiply by 2. Write the first eight numbers. Write one thing the rule did not say. Why will it keep going? [3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192, 384. Every number after the first is even: each is 2 times a number, so it is all pairs. Also right: every number is a multiple of 3]), alone, on the recording page, patterns 3 and 4. Counters stay on the desk. Eight numbers each before anything is written about them. Watch for 3, 6, 9 on the first (error A), and for 3, 5, 7, 9 on the second: that student added 2. Ask "what does multiply by 2 do to 3?"

Partner check, 3 minutes. Partners swap recording pages. Each reads the other's reason and asks "does it say what one jump does?" A reason that is only more numbers gets a question mark.

Check: Does the reason say what every jump does?

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper (Start at 29. The rule is add 6. Write the first six numbers. Write one thing you notice that the rule did not say. Will it keep going? Why? [29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59. All odd: an odd number plus 6 is odd. "The ones digits go 9, 5, 1, 7, 3" is also right]). Read it aloud once. A tick needs the six numbers, a feature that is not the rule, and a reason about the jump. Record results on the record sheet under E7. Send the family note home.

Other rooms

  • No counters. Dots drawn in pairs, two rows, in pencil; a short row shows the one left over.
  • No printer. Write the tapes and the two rules on the board; students rule eight boxes in a notebook.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop tape 5 and the challenge. Never drop the two fixes or the reasons; every pattern today ends with "why will it keep going". Longer: the challenge (Make a rule whose numbers take turns ending in 4 and 9. Write the start, the rule and six numbers. Why does it keep going? [Start at 4, add 5: 4, 9, 14, 19, 24, 29 (any start ending in 4 or 9 with add 5 works). Two jumps of 5 are one jump of 10, which leaves the ones digit alone]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: rule, pattern, term, odd, even, take turns. Say "feature" as "something true about the numbers". Frames, on side 1: "I notice ." "It will keep going because ." Access: large counters; a student who cannot draw directs a partner; reasons may be said aloud to you or a scribe.

Tomorrow

On the ticket, 6 or 35 first (error A): these students build step 1 first on day 8 and write its count before any jump. "It adds 6" (error C), or more numbers as the reason, such as 65 or "it did it for 59" (error E): day 8's first five minutes, when the readiness reteach is not needed by many, go to these tickets: cover the rule and list what is true of 29, 35, 41, then dot pairs for one jump of 6. Keep the recording pages: day 8 uses side 2, and its wrong example D sits on today's doubling list, 3, 6, 12, 24.
