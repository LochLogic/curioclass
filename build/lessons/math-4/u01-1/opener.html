Section 1.1: Rectangles and factor pairs (days 1 to 3)

The math, for the adult

Standard 4.OA.B.4, the first of its four parts: "Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100." Section 1.2 teaches the other three (multiples, and prime and composite).

A factor pair is a rectangle. In grade 3, students found area by counting squares in rows and columns (3.MD.C.7). Turn that round: take 12 squares and ask which rectangles they make. 1 row of 12, 2 rows of 6, 3 rows of 4. Each rectangle is a multiplication, 3 × 4 = 12, and its sides are a factor pair of 12. Some counts of rows do not work: 12 squares in 5 rows leave a short last row, so 5 is not a factor of 12. A factor is a number of rows that comes out equal, with none left over.

A turned rectangle is the same pair. A 3 by 4 rectangle turned on its side is a 4 by 3 rectangle. It is the same rectangle and the same factor pair, so 3 × 4 and 4 × 3 are listed once. This is the commutative property students met in grade 3 (3.OA.B.5), now used to decide when a list is finished.

The search in order, and the turn. To find every pair, try 1 row, 2 rows, 3 rows and on, in order, and write "fits, with __" or "no" for each. For 36: 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 5 no, 6 × 6. At 6 the two factors meet. Any number of rows past 6 would need a partner smaller than 6, and every number below 6 has already been tried. One stop rule, taught on day 2 and used on days 3 and 5: when rows × rows is more than the number, stop. So no search up to 100 needs more than 10 tries: 10 × 10 is 100. Students say why they stopped; they are not given a rule to recite.

Pairs outside the times tables. The tables stop at 9 × 9 (or at ten times). The search does not. 3 is a factor of 42 because 3 × 14 = 42, and 14 is in no table students learned. Students find it by building from ten times: 3 × 10 = 30, 12 more is 3 × 4, so 3 × 14 = 42. When what is left is still past the table, take another ten: 3 × 20 = 60, 21 more is 3 × 7, so 3 × 27 = 81. This is division as a missing factor (3.OA.B.6): "3 rows of how many make 42?"

Limits. Whole numbers 1 to 100. No factor trees, no greatest common factor (grade 6). The Progressions say students "need not be fluent" in finding all pairs, so nothing in the section is done for speed.

The rows table

The model the section carries. A table with three columns: rows, "fits, with __", or no. Each number of rows from 1 is tried in order; each fit is written as an equation; a line goes under the last pair.

  • Day 1. Square tiles, then centimetre grid paper. Every rectangle for 12 built at the front, shown on the display; pairs build 18, 14, 16 and 15. An equation inside each rectangle.
  • Day 2. The rows table on paper (rows-table.md), checked by the fact or on grid paper.
  • Day 3. Lists only, no tiles or grid. Side 1 of the student page carries one table for 72 and 90; after that, lists.

The rows table page holds six tables: four for day 2 and two spare for the fixes and 40.

Why this model

Tiles make "a factor" something a hand does: deal the squares into rows and look at the last row. The rows table keeps the same question ("how many rows, and does it fit?") with the tiles gone, and it puts the tries in order, which is what lets a student say the list is finished. Egg Carton Factory's Pack Station deals a count into any number of rows and shows what is left over; it is the planned tool for day 2, and its cards called Every shape are planned for day 3. The tool is not live, so both days run their paper path in the same minutes: the rows table, with each fit checked by the fact or on grid paper.

The reteach (readiness items 2 and 3)

Readiness items are marked after day 1 (readiness.md, or the start-of-year check once it exists). If more than a third of the class missed item 2 or item 3, day 2's first five minutes after the warm-up are this reteach for the whole class. Otherwise run it with the students who missed it during day 2's warm-up.

Item 2, division as a missing factor.

  1. Draw 3 rows of 8 dots on the board.
  2. Ask: "How many rows? How many in each row? How many dots?" Write 3 × 8 = 24.
  3. Cover one row's count with your hand: "3 rows of how many make 24?" Write 3 × ? = 24, then 24 ÷ 3 = 8.
  4. Pairs draw 4 rows of 7 dots and write both equations, the multiplication first.

Item 3, every rectangle for an area. Only for students who also missed E1, since day 1 built rectangles with tiles. Each student takes 14 paper squares and builds every rectangle, one at a time, writing its equation. Turn each one: "Is this a new rectangle, or the same one turned?"

Item 1 (facts) has no reteach slot: it is the first reading for the facts checks on days 3 and 4.

Supplies for the section

  • Per pair: 24 square tiles or paper squares (day 1, and day 2's worked search).
  • Per student: centimetre grid paper, the rows table page (day 2), the day 3 practice sheet, a pencil.
  • Per class: the projector; a board; the 9 by 9 table (table-9x9.md), about 6, from day 3.
  • A room with nothing: squares cut from paper, and tables ruled by hand.

Words

Factor, factor pair, row, column, product, rectangle, turn, each taught on a labelled rectangle. Frame: " and __ are a factor pair of , because __ × __ = __." A student may say a pair in a home language first, then in the frame.

How the unit is checked

Readiness: three items, day 1 (readiness.md), marked after class. Exits: one each on days 1 to 5, on paper. Day 2's exit and the written tasks of days 3 and 5 are judged with look-fors. End-of-unit assessment: day 6, six items, unaided; items 1 and 6 are this section's. All of it goes on one record sheet for the unit (record.md), by error letter. The family note (family.md) goes home on day 1.
