Section 10.1: Times as many (days 80 to 82)

The math, for the adult

Standard 4.OA.A.1: "Interpret a multiplication equation as a comparison, e.g., interpret 35 = 5 × 7 as a statement that 35 is 5 times as many as 7 and 7 times as many as 5. Represent verbal statements of multiplicative comparisons as multiplication equations."

A comparison has two amounts. In grade 3, 5 × 7 meant 5 groups of 7: one amount, built from equal groups (3.OA.A.1). A comparison puts two amounts side by side and measures the larger in copies of the smaller. "35 is 5 times as many as 7" says: take the 7, make 5 copies of it, and you have 35. Nothing is added on top of the 7; the 7 is the first copy.

Times as many is not more than. "5 more than 7" is 12: the 7 with a piece of 5 added to its end. "5 times as many as 7" is 35: five 7s. The only comparison students have met before is grade 2's "how many more" (2.OA.A.1), so reading "times as many" as "more than" is the first error to expect.

One equation reads two ways. 35 = 5 × 7 says 35 is 5 times as many as 7, and also 7 times as many as 5, because 5 × 7 = 7 × 5 (3.OA.B.5). On day 81 students see why: an array of 12 squares cut into 3 strips of 4, then into 4 strips of 3. In the equation the larger amount stands alone on one side; the two factors are "how many copies" and "how big each copy is", and either can play either part.

Ten times. Day 82 ties this to place value (4.NBT.A.1): a ten is 10 times as much as a one.

Words we use, and one we avoid. "Times as many as" for things counted; "times as much as", "times as long as", "times as tall as" for measures. Never "times more than": in everyday English "3 times more than 4" is read as 12 by some people and 16 by others (Progressions for the Common Core State Standards, Operations and Algebraic Thinking). Day 83 talks about it once and it is never asked. If a student says it, accept the idea and restate it: "You mean 3 times as many as." Also say once, on day 80, that "times" means occasions too ("three times a day").

Limits. Whole numbers. Comparisons use one-digit facts; a one-digit number times a multiple of 10 up to 90; or 10 times a one- or two-digit number. No word problems to solve yet: that is section 10.2.

The strip picture

One strip for the smaller amount. Under it, lined up at the left, a row of equal copies of that strip for the larger amount. Each row is labelled with whose it is; each copy has its amount written inside; a brace at the right gives the total. "More than" looks different: one strip with a short extra piece on its end.

  • Day 80. Cubes snapped into strips, then drawn. The cubes are put away at the end of the day.
  • Day 81. The strip is tied to the equation: the number of copies is one factor, the amount in each copy is the other, the whole row is the product.
  • Day 82. Drawn only when a sentence is hard.

The strip stays all unit; from day 86 a student draws it only when stuck, and may in the end-of-unit assessment. The strip sheet (strip-sheet.md) has six frames, three rows of ten boxes each, one box a copy. Print one a student, both sides, for each of days 80, 81 and 83 to 85; plain or grid paper does the same.

Why this model

Cubes make a copy something a hand does: build the small strip, build it again and again, count the copies. The drawn strip is the same picture with the cubes gone, and it carries every comparison problem to the end-of-unit assessment, in both kinds. A student who can point to the copies can say which number counts what, which is what the standard asks. Growth Spurt (Read it both ways) is a spec only, so day 81 runs its paper path.

The reteach (readiness item 1)

Readiness item 1 (chairs in equal rows) asks for the equal groups this section compares. If more than a third of the class missed it, day 81's warm-up and first five minutes are this reteach for the whole class. Otherwise run it with the students who missed it during day 81's warm-up, and the five minutes go to day 80's exit.

  1. Each pair has 15 cubes or counters. Deal them into 3 equal rows, one at a time round the rows.
  2. Count one row: 5. Say it: "3 rows of 5 is 15." Write 3 × ? = 15, then 3 × 5 = 15.
  3. Ask: "What does the 3 count? What does the 5 count?" (The rows; the counters in each row.)
  4. Write 15 ÷ 3 = 5 beside it: dividing finds the missing factor.
  5. Pairs deal 24 counters into 3 equal rows, write the equation with a ?, then solve it. Swap roles and check by counting.

With no counters, draw dots in rows. The numbers are new on purpose.

Supplies for the section

  • Per pair: 30 connecting cubes or counters (day 80, and the reteach on day 81).
  • Per student: strip sheets, or paper strips or grid paper; a ruler; a pencil.
  • Per class: the projector for the display pages; a board; the frame " is times as many as " on the wall over a strip picture, all unit.
  • A room with nothing: squares drawn on paper stand in for cubes, and hand-drawn boxes for the strip sheet.

Words

Times as many as, times as much as, times as long as, comparison, equation, factor, product, strip, copy, each taught on a strip picture. A student may say a comparison in a home language first, then in the frame.

How the unit is checked

Readiness check: day 79, three items, one for each section (readiness.md). Exits: one each on days 80 to 87, on paper. Day 82's written task is judged with look-fors on its key. End-of-unit assessment: day 88, eight items, unaided; items 1 and 2 are this section's. All of it goes on one record sheet for the unit (record.md), by error letter. The family note (family.md) goes home on day 80; its Spanish needs a qualified reader first.
