Before class

Print the student page both sides, the strip sheets, the exit slips and the key. Count readiness item 1 from day 79 and sort day 80's slips by error. Put the display page on the projector.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: two divisions and a difference

Alone, top of side 1 (64 ÷ 8 = ? [8], 81 ÷ 9 = ? [9], 503 − 168 = ? [335]). Answers on the screen at 4 minutes. 72 or 56 for the first is adding or taking away, not a missing factor.

Students who missed readiness item 1, if they are not many, skip the warm-up and sit with you: deal 30 counters into 5 rows, then write 5 × ? = 30 and read it as "5 rows of what make 30?".

5 min. Reteach, or day 80's error

If many missed readiness item 1 (more than a third): the whole class does the counters, as above, with you dealing on the board.

Otherwise, show day 80's commonest error on its slips. A (7, or "5 more"): "8 times as many as 3 is 11", a 3-strip and a strip of 3 and 8, beside eight copies of the 3-strip, 24. D (12, or six copies): "4 times as many as 3" drawn as a 3-strip with four more after it, 15, beside four copies, 12. Count the copies aloud, the first strip as copy one.

If both are needed, the counters go here and day 80's error is talked through in the notice task.

8 min. Notice: 3 strips of 4 and 4 strips of 3

Pairs. Each partner cuts a rectangle 3 squares by 4 from grid paper: one cuts it into 3 strips of 4, the other into 4 strips of 3. They lay their strips side by side.

Ask. "What do you notice about 3 strips of 4 and 4 strips of 3?" Expect, and show in this order: "both are 12 squares"; "the 3 and the 4 swap jobs"; "one is 3 copies, the other is 4 copies". Take a sentence with "copies" last.

If day 80's error rides here, a pair whose strips show it counts its copies aloud.

3 min. Tie the strips to 12 = 3 × 4

Whole class. Write 12 = 3 × 4 on the board. Students fill the two frames under Example 1 as you go.

Example 1 (12 = 3 × 4. What two comparisons does it show? [12 is 3 times as many as 4; 12 is 4 times as many as 3]).

  1. Lay the 3 strips of 4 beside the 4 strips of 3. Both make 12 squares.
  2. Look at the 3 strips of 4. That is 3 copies of a 4-strip, so 12 is 3 times as many as 4.
  3. Look at the 4 strips of 3. That is 4 copies of a 3-strip, so 12 is 4 times as many as 3.
  4. The 12 stands alone in the equation. It is the bigger amount: all the copies together.
  5. Either factor can be the number of copies. So 12 = 3 × 4 says two things.

8 min. Growth Spurt, Read it both ways: on paper

Growth Spurt is not built. When it exists: "Read it both ways", three cards, 8 minutes, stopped at the third card. Until it exists, the same 8 minutes run on side 1's equation cards.

Example 2 (42 = 6 × 7. Finish both sentences. [42 is 6 times as many as 7; 42 is 7 times as many as 6]), whole class, read aloud as the model:

  1. The 42 stands alone, so 42 is the bigger amount.
  2. 6 copies of a 7-strip make 42. So 42 is 6 times as many as 7.
  3. 7 copies of a 6-strip make 42. So 42 is 7 times as many as 6.

Then alone, cards 1 and 2 (45 = 5 × 9 [45 is 5 times as many as 9; 45 is 9 times as many as 5], 7 × 8 = 56 [56 is 7 times as many as 8; 56 is 8 times as many as 7]). At 6 minutes, read the four sentences aloud from the key; students check their own and fix in pen.

Check: Listen to each sentence. Is the number that stands alone the bigger amount?

Watch for one sentence only, and for the smaller amount first (C). Ask: "Which number stands alone? Is it the biggest?"

15 min. Six equations

Alone, side 2 (48 = 6 × 8 [48 is 6 times as many as 8; 48 is 8 times as many as 6], 2 × 9 = 18 [18 is 2 times as many as 9; 18 is 9 times as many as 2], 35 = 7 × 5 [35 is 7 times as many as 5; 35 is 5 times as many as 7], 8 × 9 = 72 [72 is 8 times as many as 9; 72 is 9 times as many as 8], 36 = 4 × 9 [36 is 4 times as many as 9; 36 is 9 times as many as 4], 3 × 8 = 24 [24 is 3 times as many as 8; 24 is 8 times as many as 3]). For each: both sentences, then one strip picture on the strip sheet, either way round. Read the first two aloud first: the product sits on either side.

Walk and ask: "Where is the 9 in your strips? Where is the 2?" Watch for a strip picture of two strips with no copies, or a 9-strip beside a 2-strip (A), and for sentences that say "more". At 12 minutes, partners check one equation: both sentences against the strips.

Check: Do both sentences match the strips? Is the bigger amount first?

If many write one sentence only, stop and read 12 = 3 × 4 both ways again from the cut strips.

6 min. Fix it: Ben's equation

Ben reads "12 is 4 times as many as 3." He writes 3 = 4 × 12. [12 = 4 × 3. Ben put the smaller amount alone]. One minute of turn-and-talk in pairs, then the class. The display shows Ben's working beside the right working. Point to the longer strip first and ask "Which amount is bigger?": the bigger amount stands alone. Students write what went wrong and the right equation.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Why does 12 = 3 × 4 tell us two things?" Expect: "12 is 3 fours and 4 threes"; "you can cut 12 squares both ways". Show the strips first, then the sentences.

5 min. Exit: the slip

Alone. The exit slip (28 = 4 × 7. Write the two comparisons this equation shows. Draw a strip picture for one of them. [28 is 4 times as many as 7; 28 is 7 times as many as 4; four copies of a 7-strip, or seven copies of a 4-strip]). Collect at 5 minutes.

Other rooms

  • No devices, or the tool not yet built. The 8 minutes are the equation cards on side 1, as written, checked against the key read aloud.
  • No scissors. Pairs draw the two rectangles and ring each strip in pencil.
  • One child at home. Twelve coins in 3 rows of 4; the child reads it both ways, then makes 4 rows of 3.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop the partner check and the challenge, and cut the six equations to four (the first four). Never drop the second sentence or the fix. Longer: the challenge (49 = 7 × 7. How many comparisons does it show? Why? [One: 49 is 7 times as many as 7. Both factors are the same number]), then the extension (Write every comparison 36 can show. Use one-digit numbers. [36 is 4 times as many as 9; 36 is 9 times as many as 4; 36 is 6 times as many as 6]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: times as many as, comparison, equation, factor, product, copy. The frame stays on the wall over a strip picture: " is times as many as ." A student may say each sentence in a home language first.
