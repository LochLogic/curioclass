Before class

Print the student page both sides, the strip sheets, the exit tickets and the key. Count readiness item 1 from day 79 and sort day 80's tickets by error. Put the display page on the projector.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: two divisions and a difference

Alone, top of side 1 (64 ÷ 8 = ? [8], 81 ÷ 9 = ? [9], 503 − 168 = ? [335]). Answers on the screen at 4 minutes. 72 or 56 for the first is adding or taking away, not a missing factor.

Students who missed readiness item 1, if they are not many, skip the warm-up and sit with you: in pairs, deal 15 counters into 3 rows, then 24 into 3 rows, as in the section opener, and write 3 × ? = 15 and 3 × ? = 24.

5 min. Reteach, or day 80's error

If many missed readiness item 1 (more than a third): the warm-up and these 5 minutes are the reteach, whole class, as above; the warm-up sums move to day 82's warm-up.

Otherwise, show day 80's commonest error beside the right one. A: "8 times as many as 3 is 11" beside eight copies of the 3-strip, 24. D: a 3-strip with four more after it, 15, beside four copies, 12. Count the copies aloud, the first strip as copy one.

If both are needed, the counters go here and day 80's error is talked through in the notice task.

8 min. Notice: 3 strips of 4 and 4 strips of 3

Pairs. Each partner cuts a rectangle 3 squares by 4 from the grid paper: one cuts it into 3 strips of 4, the other into 4 strips of 3. Each also cuts one extra strip of their size, "the one copy", and lays it above their row.

Ask. "What do you notice about 3 strips of 4 and 4 strips of 3?" Expect, and show in this order: "both are 12 squares"; "the 3 and the 4 swap jobs"; "one is 3 copies, the other is 4 copies". Take a sentence with "copies" last.

If day 80's error rides here, a pair counts its copies aloud.

3 min. Tie the strips to 12 = 3 × 4

Whole class. Write 12 = 3 × 4 on the board. Say: "Equal groups is one amount. A comparison is two amounts: the 12 and one 4. The 12 is measured in 4s." Students fill the two frames under Example 1 as you go.

Example 1 (12 = 3 × 4. What two comparisons does it show? [12 is 3 times as many as 4; 12 is 4 times as many as 3]).

  1. Lay one 4-strip on its own above the 3 strips of 4, and one 3-strip above the 4 strips of 3. Both rows make 12 squares.
  2. Look at the 3 strips of 4. That is 3 copies of a 4-strip, so 12 is 3 times as many as 4.
  3. Look at the 4 strips of 3. That is 4 copies of a 3-strip, so 12 is 4 times as many as 3.
  4. The 12 stands alone in the equation. It is the bigger amount: all the copies together.
  5. Either factor can be the number of copies. So 12 = 3 × 4 says two things.

8 min. Growth Spurt, Read it both ways: on paper

When Growth Spurt exists: "Read it both ways", three cards, stopped at the third. Until then, side 1's equation cards.

Example 2 (42 = 6 × 7. Finish both sentences. [42 is 6 times as many as 7; 42 is 7 times as many as 6]), whole class, read aloud as the model:

  1. The 42 stands alone, so 42 is the bigger amount.
  2. 6 copies of a 7-strip make 42. So 42 is 6 times as many as 7.
  3. 7 copies of a 6-strip make 42. So 42 is 7 times as many as 6.

Then alone, cards 1 and 2 (45 = 5 × 9 [45 is 5 times as many as 9; 45 is 9 times as many as 5], 7 × 8 = 56 [56 is 7 times as many as 8; 56 is 8 times as many as 7]). At 6 minutes, read the four sentences aloud from the key; students check their own and fix in pen.

Check: Does each sentence start with the bigger amount?

Watch for one sentence only, and for the smaller amount first (C). Ask: "Which number stands alone? Is it the biggest?"

15 min. Six equations

Alone, side 2 (48 = 6 × 8 [48 is 6 times as many as 8; 48 is 8 times as many as 6], 2 × 9 = 18 [18 is 2 times as many as 9; 18 is 9 times as many as 2], 35 = 7 × 5 [35 is 7 times as many as 5; 35 is 5 times as many as 7], 8 × 9 = 72 [72 is 8 times as many as 9; 72 is 9 times as many as 8], 36 = 4 × 9 [36 is 4 times as many as 9; 36 is 9 times as many as 4], 3 × 8 = 24 [24 is 3 times as many as 8; 24 is 8 times as many as 3]). For each: both sentences, then one strip picture on the strip sheet, either way round, the equation on its letter line. Read equations 1 and 2 aloud first: the product can be on either side of the equals sign. Early finishers: the challenge at the foot of side 2.

Walk and ask: "Where is the 9 in your strips? Where is the 2?" Watch for a 9-strip beside a 2-strip with no copies (A), and for "more". At 12 minutes, partners check one equation against the strips; then show the answers screen.

Check: Do both sentences match the strips? Is the bigger amount first?

If many write one sentence only, stop and read 12 = 3 × 4 both ways again from the cut strips.

6 min. Fix it: Ben's sentence

Ben reads 12 = 4 × 3. He says: "3 is 4 times as many as 12." [12 is 4 times as many as 3; 12 is 3 times as many as 4. Ben put the smaller amount first], side 2. Pairs talk one minute, then the class. The display shows Ben's working beside the right working. Point to the longer strip first and ask "Which amount is bigger?": the bigger amount comes first. Students write what went wrong and both sentences.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Why does 12 = 3 × 4 tell us two things?" Expect: "you can cut 12 squares both ways"; last, "12 is 3 times as many as 4, and 4 times as many as 3, because the 4 and the 3 can swap jobs". Show the strips first.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone. The exit ticket (28 = 4 × 7. Write the two comparisons this equation shows. Draw a strip picture for one of them. [28 is 4 times as many as 7; 28 is 7 times as many as 4; four copies of a 7-strip under one 7-strip, or seven copies of a 4-strip under one 4-strip]). Collect at 5 minutes.

Other rooms

  • No devices, or no tool yet. Side 1's equation cards, as written.
  • No scissors. Pairs draw the two rectangles and ring each strip in pencil.
  • One child at home. Twelve coins in 3 rows of 4; the child reads it both ways, then makes 4 rows of 3.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop the partner check and cut the six equations to four (the first four). Never drop the second sentence or the fix. Longer: the challenge (49 = 7 × 7. How many comparisons does it show? Why? [One: 49 is 7 times as many as 7. Both factors are the same number]), then the extension (Write every comparison 36 can show. Use one-digit numbers. [36 is 4 times as many as 9; 36 is 9 times as many as 4; 36 is 6 times as many as 6]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: times as many as, comparison, equation, factor, product, copy. The frame stays on the wall over a strip picture: " is times as many as ." A student may say each sentence in a home language first.
