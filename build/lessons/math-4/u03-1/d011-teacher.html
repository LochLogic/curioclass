Before class

Print the student page both sides, the line pages, the exit tickets and the key. Sort day 9's readiness checks by item and day 10's tickets by error. Draw a line from 0 to 1 on the board, as long as the large strip, and mark it in fourths. Put the display page on the projector.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: two sums and a fact

Alone, top of side 1 (465 + 278 = ? [743], 802 − 357 = ? [445], 8 × 7 = ? [56]). Read the answers aloud at 4 minutes; students mark their own.

Students who missed a readiness item, if they are not many, skip the warm-up and sit with you: item 1 or 4, lay the one-fourth strip from 0 five times, counting "one fourth, two fourths..."; item 2, fold a half strip again and count the shaded fourths; item 3, a thirds strip beside an eighths strip.

5 min. Reteach, or day 10's error

If many missed a readiness item (more than a third): the warm-up and these 5 minutes are that item's fix, whole class; drop the warm-up sums.

Otherwise, if day 10's tickets showed many A (2/6 or 4/3): show "1/3 = 1/6" beside the two strips; fold thirds into sixths again and count the shaded parts aloud. Many B (4/5): a 1/3 strip beside a 2/4 strip. Adding 1 to both numbers changed the length.

If neither, everyone does item 1's fix: four fourths land on 1.

5 min. Teaching first: the strip on the line

Whole class. Hold the large strip against the board line, its left end at 0. Students lay their own 1/4 strip on line 1 of the line page and copy each move. The display shows each step on its own screen.

Example 1 (The line from 0 to 1 is marked in fourths. A strip 1/4 long lies on it from 0. Cut each fourth into 2 equal parts. What is the new name for the strip's end? [2/8; the point did not move]).

  1. Lay the 1/4 strip on the line, its left end at 0. Its right end is the point 1/4.
  2. Fold the strip in half and mark the fold on the line. Cut every other fourth in half the same way.
  3. Now 8 equal parts fill the line from 0 to 1. Each part is an eighth.
  4. Count the parts from 0 to the strip's end, not the marks: 1, 2. The point is 2/8.
  5. The strip did not change, so the point did not move. Each fourth was cut into 2, so 1/4 = 2/8.

Every time: the point is where the strip ends; count parts, not marks.

10 min. Cut and rename, up to 1

Alone, side 1 (A line from 0 to 1 is in fourths. A point is at 2/4. Cut each fourth into 2. New name? [4/8], A line from 0 to 1 is in fifths. A point is at 4/5. Cut each fifth into 2. New name? [8/10], On the fifths line, a point is at 1. Name it in fifths, then in tenths. [5/5; 10/10]), 7 minutes, one line each on the line page: mark it with the matching strip, then fold to cut each part in 2.

Watch for one part too many, 5/8 or 9/10 (error C: the mark at 0 counted), and only the denominator changed, 2/8 or 4/10 (error A). Ask: "Where does your strip end? How many parts from 0?"

Then 3 minutes in pairs: partners swap and lay a strip on each point. A mismatch gets a question mark.

Check: Does your strip end at the point? Did you count parts, not marks?

5 min. Fix it: Ana's count

Ana marked sixths on a line from 0 to 1. She counted the marks from 0: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. She named the point 6/6. [5/6. Ana counted the mark at 0 as one], side 1. One minute of turn-and-talk in pairs, then the class. The display shows Ana's working beside the right working. Ask: "Where would 6/6 be?" Lay the strip from 0 again: the first part ends at the first mark after 0.

If many still count marks, every student lays the sixths strip on the line and touches each part, saying "one sixth, two sixths..."

5 min. Past 1: the second whole

Whole class. Lay a second strip after the first on the board: the line now runs to 2. Students fill the frame under Example 2.

Example 2 (The line from 0 to 2 is marked in halves. A point is at 3/2. Cut each half into 4 equal parts. What is the point's new name? [12/8; the point did not move]).

  1. Lay the 1/2 strip from 0 three times: one half, two halves, three halves. Two halves reach 1, so the point is one half past 1.
  2. Cut each half into 4 equal parts: fold the half strip in half, then in half again, and mark the folds on every half.
  3. 8 parts now fill 0 to 1, so each part is an eighth.
  4. Count the parts from 0 to the point: 12. The point is 12/8.
  5. The length from 0 did not change, so the point stayed put. Each half was cut into 4, so 3/2 = 12/8.
  6. Check: eight eighths reach 1, and four more eighths reach the point.

10 min. Past 1, alone

Alone, side 2 (A line from 0 to 2 is in fifths. A point is at 7/5. Cut each fifth into 2. New name? [14/10], A line from 0 to 2 is in fourths. A point is at 2. Name it in fourths. Then cut each fourth into 2 and name it again. [8/4; 16/8]), on the line page's 0 to 2 lines. Early finishers: the challenge.

Watch for 7/10 and 14/20 for the first (error A, then error K: the whole line taken as the whole), and 8/8 for the point at 2 (error K). Ask: "Where is 1 on your line? How many parts make one whole?"

5 min. Fix it: Max's line

Max's line from 0 to 2 is marked in fourths. His point is on the fifth mark after 0. He counted all 8 parts and named it 5/8. [5/4. Max took 0 to 2 as the whole], side 2. Pairs, one minute, then the class. The display shows Max's working beside the right working. Cover the line past 1 with a sheet of paper: four parts make 1. Uncover it and count on past 1: "five fourths."

If many said 8/8 for the point at 2, cover that line the same way.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "When we cut each fourth into two parts, what changed and what stayed the same?" Expect, in this order: "there are more parts"; "each part is smaller"; last, "the point stayed in the same place, so it is the same amount with a new name". Students finish the frame on side 2.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone. The exit ticket (The line from 0 to 1 is marked in fourths. Put a point at 3/4. Cut each fourth into 2 equal parts. What is the point's new name? Did the point move? [6/8; no, the point did not move]). The line is printed on the ticket. Collect at 5 minutes.

Other rooms

  • No line page yet. Until the line page exists, students lay a whole strip along a line of lined paper and mark 0 and 1 at its ends; for 0 to 2, two strips end to end.
  • No strip sheet yet. Cut strips 10 inches long from scrap paper: rule fifths every 2 inches, tenths every inch; fold the rest.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop questions 6 and 9 and the partner check (students lay their own strip on each point). Never drop the two fixes or the planned question. Longer: the challenge (Can 3/8 be written in fourths? Why or why not? [No: 3/8 is between 2/8 and 4/8, that is 1/4 and 2/4, so no mark in fourths lands on it]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: number line, point, equivalent, numerator, denominator. Read fractions as words: "three halves", not "three over two". Frame on side 2 and on the display: "Each part was cut into , so there are times as many parts and each part is smaller. The length is the same, so = ." A cut may be ruled, not folded.

Tomorrow

Many 3/8 (error A): fold fourths into eighths and count the shaded parts aloud before the task. "Yes, it moved": lay a 1/4 strip on the line before and after the cut. Results go on the record sheet under E11.
