Day 12: The same amount, or not? Prove it

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Put the display page on the projector. Sort day 11's exit tickets: 5/8 or 7/8 (error C), 3/8 (error A), "yes, it moved", and the rest. Strip envelopes stay closed in desks.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: multiples

Alone, on the board: Write the first five multiples of 6. [6, 12, 18, 24, 30], Write the first five multiples of 5. [5, 10, 15, 20, 25], Is 12 a multiple of 6? Is 12 a multiple of 5? [Yes, 6 × 2 = 12; no]. Answers on the display at 4 minutes. Leave the fives up: Example 2 uses them.

8 min. The question: one half as tenths, as thirds

If day 11's tickets showed many C (5/8 or 7/8): first show a line in fourths with 0 counted as "one", then lay a fourths strip from 0 again; the point is the strip's end, not a tick. If day 11's tickets showed many A (3/8): show "1/3 = 1/6" beside two strips, 1/6 plainly shorter, and count the shaded parts aloud. Either takes 3 minutes from the pairs' talk below. Students who wrote "yes, it moved" sit near you.

Alone, 3 minutes, on the line stack, side 1 (Can 1/2 be written as tenths? As thirds? How do you know? [Tenths: yes, 1/2 = 5/10, one point on the lines. Thirds: no, 1/2 falls between 1/3 and 2/3]); a strip only to check. Then pairs, 2 minutes, then the class.

Ask. "Can 1/2 be written as tenths? As thirds? How do you know?" Expect, in this order: "yes, 5/10: the 1/2 point is on a tenths mark"; "no: 1/2 falls between 1/3 and 2/3". Take the thirds answer from a student who used the lines. Listen for 2/3 "because you add one to each" (error B): leave it on the board for Jo's claim.

10 min. Teaching first: two worked examples

Whole class. Students mark the line stack and fill the frames on side 1 as you go. The display shows each step on its own screen; hold the long strip up for each cut.

Example 1 (Are 3/4 and 9/12 the same amount? Prove it on the lines. [Yes: each fourth cut into 3 makes twelfths, and 3/4 = 9/12, one point]).

  1. Mark 3/4 on the fourths line: 3 fourths from 0.
  2. Cut each fourth into 3 equal parts. 4 × 3 = 12, so the whole now has 12 parts: twelfths.
  3. The 3 fourths are now 3 × 3 = 9 twelfths. The same point is named 9/12.
  4. Check on the twelfths line, right below: 9/12 lines up with 3/4.
  5. 3 times as many parts, each a third the size, and the length did not change. 3/4 = 9/12.

Example 2 (Are 2/5 and 5/12 the same amount? Prove it on the lines. [No: 2/5 is between 4/12 and 5/12, so the points do not line up]).

  1. Mark 5/12 on the twelfths line: 5 twelfths from 0.
  2. Mark 2/5 on the fifths line, right below: 2 fifths from 0.
  3. Do the points line up? No. 2/5 is a little closer to 0 than 5/12.
  4. Why: 5 × 2 = 10 and 5 × 3 = 15, so a fifth is a bit more than 2 twelfths. 2 fifths are a bit more than 4 twelfths, not 5.
  5. The points are not lined up, so 2/5 and 5/12 are not the same amount.

Tie both to the question: each half cut into 5 makes tenths, so 1/2 = 5/10. No thirds mark sits at 1/2, and the lines show it. Say every new name with its cut: "each part was cut into 3".

12 min. Same or not? Four pairs

Alone, side 2, 8 minutes (Are 5/6 and 10/12 the same amount? [The same: one point], Are 3/10 and 5/12 the same amount? [Not the same: 3/10 is closer to 0], Are 3/6 and 5/10 the same amount? [The same: one point, at 1/2], Are 1/3 and 2/4 the same amount? [Not the same: 1/3 is closer to 0]): mark both fractions on their two lines, then circle. Then 4 minutes in pairs: partners swap and check each pair by laying a pencil straight down from one point. A mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer.

Check: Did you count from 0? Does the pencil touch both points?

Watch for "the same" on pairs 2 or 4 (error B: the same number added to the top and the bottom), and "not the same, it has bigger numbers" on pairs 1 or 3 (error D). If many show B, mark 3/10 and 5/12 on one line: adding moved the point. If many show D, lay two strips side by side: more parts, each smaller.

5 min. Jo's claim

Whole class. Read it aloud (Jo says 3/4 = 5/6 because she added 2 to the top and the bottom. Do the lines agree? [No: 3/4 and 5/6 are at two points; 5/6 is farther from 0. Adding moved the point]); the display shows Jo's working beside the right working.

Ask. "Jo says 3/4 = 5/6 because she added 2 to the top and the bottom. Do the lines agree?" Expect: no, the points do not line up; 5/6 is farther from 0. Then say it once: adding moves the point; cutting each part does not. Students write what went wrong.

6 min. Kai's claim: a talk

Read it aloud (Kai says 2/5 and 4/10 are different amounts, because 4 and 10 are bigger. [Not right: each fifth cut into 2 makes tenths, and 2/5 = 4/10, one point]). Pairs, 2 minutes, then the class.

Ask. "Is Kai right? Prove it with the fifths and tenths lines." Expect: no; each fifth cut into 2 makes tenths, twice as many parts, each half the size, so 2/5 and 4/10 are one point. Hold the long strip in fifths up and mark each fifth in half. Students write what went wrong.

9 min. Written task

Alone, side 2 (Explain why 3/4 and 6/8 are the same amount. You may draw. [Each fourth is cut into 2: twice as many parts in the whole and in the shaded piece, each half the size, so the length is the same]). Students may draw on the line stack. Read after class with the tickets. Look-fors, each ticked alone:

  1. says how each part was cut
  2. says there are more parts and each is smaller
  3. says the length or the point is the same

Watch for "6/8 is more, 6 and 8 are bigger" (error D): ask "how long is one eighth? one fourth?"

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, envelopes closed, read aloud once (No strips. "The line from 0 to 1 is marked in twelfths. Mark 2/3 and 4/5 as well as you can. Are they the same amount? Then write another name for 2/3." [No: 2/3 is at 8/12 and 4/5 falls between 9/12 and 10/12; 4/6 or 8/12]). Students mark the line printed on the ticket and answer there; collect pages and tickets together. A mark for 4/5 past 8/12 and short of 1 is "as well as you can". Write the results on the record sheet under E12.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Draw the stack on the board, every line 0 to 1 the same length; students judge from it and write on lined paper. For the exit, each student rules a line 12 centimeters long and marks every centimeter: twelfths.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop pair 4 and the partner check (students check their own with a pencil). Never drop Jo's claim, the written task or the exit. Longer: the challenge (Which of these can name 1/2: thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths, eighths, tenths, twelfths? Write each name. [Fourths, sixths, eighths, tenths and twelfths: 2/4, 3/6, 4/8, 5/10, 6/12. Not thirds or fifths]), with a strip folded in eighths.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: equivalent, numerator, denominator, multiple. Frames on the display: "Each part was cut into , so there are times as many parts and each part is smaller. The length is the same, so = ." " and are not the same amount: the points do not line up." Jo's and Kai's claims are read aloud; the line stack prints large for students who need it.

Tomorrow
