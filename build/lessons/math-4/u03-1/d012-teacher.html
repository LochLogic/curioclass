Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Put the display page on the projector. Sort day 11's exit tickets: 7/8 (error C), 3/8 (error A), "yes, it moved", 8/10 on part (b) (error K), and the rest. Strip envelopes stay in desks, closed for the exit.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: multiples

Alone, on the board: Write the first five multiples of 6. [6, 12, 18, 24, 30], Write the first five multiples of 5. [5, 10, 15, 20, 25], Is 12 a multiple of 6? Is 12 a multiple of 5? [Yes, 6 × 2 = 12; no]. Answers on the display at 4 minutes. Leave the fives up: the question uses them (5 × 2 = 10).

8 min. The question: one half as tenths, as thirds

If day 11's tickets showed many C (7/8): on a board line in fourths, count 0 as "one", then lay a fourths strip from 0; the point is the strip's end. Many A (3/8): fold fourths into eighths; count the shaded parts aloud. "Yes, it moved": lay the fourths strip on the line, cut each fourth in 2, lay it again; its end stays. Many K (8/10): cover the line past 1, count one whole's parts, then count on. Any of these takes the pairs' 2 minutes and 1 from the class talk.

Alone, 3 minutes, on the line stack, side 1 (Can 1/2 be written as tenths? As thirds? How do you know? [Tenths: yes, 1/2 = 5/10, one point on the lines. Thirds: no, 1/2 falls between 1/3 and 2/3]); a strip only to check. Then pairs, 2 minutes, then the class.

Ask. "Can 1/2 be written as tenths? As thirds? How do you know?" Expect, in this order: "yes, 5/10: the 1/2 point is on a tenths mark"; "no: 1/2 falls between 1/3 and 2/3". Take the thirds answer from a student who used the lines. Listen for 2/3 "because you add one to each" (error B): leave it on the board for Jo's claim.

10 min. Teaching first: two worked examples

Whole class. Students mark the line stack and fill the frames on side 1 as you go. In Example 1 they draw 2 marks inside each fourth on the fourths line before looking at the twelfths line, the check. The display shows each step on its own screen.

Example 1 (Are 3/4 and 9/12 the same amount? Prove it on the lines. [Yes: each fourth cut into 3 makes twelfths, and 3/4 = 9/12, one point]).

  1. Mark 3/4 on the fourths line: 3 fourths from 0.
  2. Cut each fourth into 3 equal parts. 4 × 3 = 12, so the whole now has 12 parts: twelfths.
  3. The 3 fourths are now 3 × 3 = 9 twelfths. The same point is named 9/12.
  4. Check on the twelfths line, right below: 9/12 lines up with 3/4.
  5. 3 times as many parts, each a third the size, and the length did not change. 3/4 = 9/12.

Example 2 (Are 2/5 and 6/12 the same amount? Prove it on the lines. [No: 6/12 is halfway and 2/5 is short of halfway, so the points do not line up]).

  1. Mark 2/5 on the fifths line: 2 fifths from 0.
  2. Mark 6/12 on the twelfths line, right below: 6 twelfths from 0. 6 is half of 12, so 6/12 is halfway.
  3. Lay a pencil straight down from 2/5. Do the points line up? No: 2/5 is closer to 0.
  4. Why: half of 5 fifths is 2 and a half fifths. 2 fifths is short of halfway.
  5. The points do not line up, so 2/5 and 6/12 are not the same amount.

Tie both to the question: each half cut into 5 makes 1/2 = 5/10; no thirds mark sits at 1/2. Say every new name with its cut.

12 min. Same or not? Four pairs

Alone, side 1 (pair 1) and side 2 (pairs 2 to 4), 8 minutes (Are 5/6 and 10/12 the same amount? [The same: 5/6 and 10/12 are one point, on the fifth sixths mark and the tenth twelfths mark], Are 3/10 and 5/12 the same amount? [Not the same: 3/10 (third tenths mark) is closer to 0 than 5/12 (fifth twelfths mark)], Are 1/3 and 2/4 the same amount? [Not the same: 1/3 (first thirds mark) is closer to 0 than 2/4 (the middle, 1/2)], Are 3/6 and 5/10 the same amount? [The same: 3/6 and 5/10 are one point, at 1/2]): cut the top line first, then mark and circle. Pair 1: mark the middle of each sixth, then 10/12; the twelfths line checks the cuts. Then 4 minutes in pairs: partners swap and lay a pencil straight down from one point. A mismatch gets a question mark.

Check: Did you count from 0? Does the pencil touch both points?

Watch for "the same" on pairs 2 or 3 (error B: the same number added to the top and the bottom), and "not the same, it has bigger numbers" on pairs 1 or 4 (error D). If many show B, mark 3/10 and 5/12 on the board line: adding moved the point. If many show D, lay two strips side by side: more parts, each smaller. On pair 4, "not the same, nothing times 6 is 10" is not D: no cut joins sixths and tenths, but both are 1/2 (the halves line). Day 16 meets this again.

5 min. Jo's claim

Whole class. Read it aloud (Jo says 3/4 = 5/6 because she added 2 to the top and the bottom. Do the lines agree? [No: 3/4 and 5/6 are at two points; 5/6 is farther from 0. Adding moved the point]); the display shows Jo's working. Show the right working after the class has answered.

Ask. "Jo says 3/4 = 5/6 because she added 2 to the top and the bottom. Do the lines agree?" Expect: no, the points do not line up; 5/6 is farther from 0. Then say it once: adding moves the point; cutting each part does not. Students write what went wrong.

6 min. Kai's claim: a talk

Read it aloud (Kai says 2/5 and 4/10 are different amounts, because 4 and 10 are bigger. [Not right: each fifth cut into 2 makes tenths, and 2/5 = 4/10, one point]); the display shows Kai's working. Pairs, 2 minutes, then the class; show the right working after the class has answered.

Ask. "Is Kai right? Prove it with the fifths and tenths lines." Expect: no; each fifth cut into 2 makes tenths, twice as many parts, each half the size: one point. Students mark the middle of each fifth on the stack's fifths line and check on the tenths line; you mark the display's (or the board). Then they write.

9 min. Written task

Alone, side 2 (Explain why 3/4 and 6/8 are the same amount. You may draw. [Each fourth is cut into 2: twice as many parts in the whole and in the shaded piece, each half the size, so the length is the same]). There is no eighths line: cut each fourth in 2 on the fourths line. Read after class with the tickets; write "One point?" beside any 6/8 called more. Look-fors, each ticked alone:

  1. says how each part was cut
  2. says there are more parts and each is smaller
  3. says the length or the point is the same

Watch for "6/8 is more, 6 and 8 are bigger" (error D): ask "how long is one eighth? one fourth?"

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, envelopes closed, read aloud once (The line from 0 to 1 is marked in twelfths. Mark 2/3 and 4/5 on it as well as you can. Are they the same amount? Write another name for 2/3. [No: 2/3 is on 8/12, and 4/5 is past 8/12 and short of 1 (it falls between 9/12 and 10/12); 4/6 or 8/12]). Students mark and answer on the ticket; collect pages and tickets together. A mark for 4/5 past 8/12 and short of 1 is "as well as you can". Write the results on the record sheet under E12.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Draw the stack on the board, every line 0 to 1 one length; students write on lined paper. For the exit, each rules a 12 centimeter line and marks every centimeter: twelfths.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop pair 4 and the partner check. Never drop Jo's claim, the written task or the exit. Longer: the challenge (Which of these can name 5/6: halves, thirds, fourths, fifths, tenths, twelfths? Write each name you find. [Twelfths only: 5/6 = 10/12. On the other lines no mark lands on the 5/6 point (it falls between 3/4 and 1, between 4/5 and 1, and between 8/10 and 9/10)]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: equivalent, numerator, denominator, multiple. The frames are on side 1 and the display. Jo's and Kai's claims are read aloud; the line stack prints large for students who need it.
