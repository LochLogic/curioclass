Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Display page on the projector. A long paper strip and a marker for a line from 0 to 2.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: factor blanks

Alone, in notebooks, untimed: the three blanks on the display (Fill in each blank: 3 × = 21. 2 × = 14. 3 × = 27. [7; 7; 9]), the groupings the sort needs. At 5 minutes one student reads the answers.

If day 15's tickets showed many A (20, 9 or 4: one number kept): the warm-up becomes "9/10 = 9/100" beside a line from 0 to 1 in tenths. Cut the first tenth into 10: 9 hundredths sit inside the first tenth, far from 9/10. The blanks move to the sort.

If day 15's tickets showed many B (11, 99 or 14): the warm-up becomes "20/12 = 11/3". 11/3 is more than 3; 20/12 is less than 2: adding moved the point. With both, B first; A takes 2 minutes of the sort.

Seat near you for the sort the students day 15 flagged (A or B in two sets) and any whose ticket showed A or B.

5 min. Teaching first: Lena's claim

Whole class. Read Lena's claim aloud (Lena says 6/4 and 9/6 cannot be equal. She says nothing times 4 is 6. [Lena is not right: 6/4 = 3/2 and 9/6 = 3/2, so 6/4 = 9/6]); students follow on side 1.

Ask. "What would you show Lena?" Take answers before showing the fix. Expect first: "6/4 is 3/2 and 9/6 is 3/2"; then "on a line from 0 to 2, both land halfway between 1 and 2". If no one finds a third name, ask: "Can you give each one a name with smaller numbers?"

  1. Lena's working: she looked for one number that turns the fourths into sixths. Nothing times 4 is 6, so she said they are not equal.
  2. Right working: give each one a name with smaller numbers. Group the fourths in twos: 6/4 = (6 ÷ 2)/(4 ÷ 2) = 3/2. Group the sixths in threes: 9/6 = (9 ÷ 3)/(6 ÷ 3) = 3/2. Both are 3/2, the same name, so 6/4 = 9/6. On a line from 0 to 2 both are at one point, halfway from 1 to 2.

After the talk, students fill 6/4 = , 9/6 = , both are . Error E: a student looks for one multiplier, finds none, and stops. The fix is the third name.

8 min. Worked example: circle every equal fraction

Students fill the frame on side 1 as you go.

Example (Circle every fraction equal to 22/10: 11/5, 24/12, 220/100, 22/5. [11/5 and 220/100]).

  1. Take each fraction in turn. Can I cut or group the tenths to make it?
  2. 11/5: group the tenths in twos. 22/10 = (22 ÷ 2)/(10 ÷ 2) = 11/5. Equal: circle it.
  3. 24/12: 2 was added to the top and the bottom. Adding is not cutting. Put both in fifths: 24/12 is 2, which is 10/5, and 10/5 is not 11/5. Not equal.
  4. 220/100: group the hundredths in tens. 220/100 = (220 ÷ 10)/(100 ÷ 10) = 22/10. Equal: circle it. Bigger numbers, the same amount.
  5. 22/5: only the bottom changed. Fifths are bigger than tenths, so 22/5 is far more. Not equal.
  6. Circled: 11/5 and 220/100. One has smaller numbers and one has bigger numbers.

Say: "Check every one. Some equal names have bigger numbers, some have smaller." Watch for a student who skips 11/5 (the rule used one way only), circles 24/12 (B) or 22/5 (A), or says 220/100 is more because its numbers are bigger (D).

22 min. Equal or not: eight pairs in three short sets

Alone, sides 1 and 2. Each set is marked at once against the display; students tick each pair right on the first try and write the count. No clock. Two minutes are left for a branch or a reteach.

Set 1 (Are 2/4 and 4/6 equal? [Not equal: 2/4 = 1/2 = 3/6, and 3/6 is not 4/6], Are 3/6 and 5/10 equal? [Equal: 3/6 = 1/2 and 5/10 = 1/2], Are 7/10 and 70/100 equal? [Equal: cut each tenth into 10, and 7/10 = 70/100]), 6 minutes, then 1 to mark.

Check: Right on the first try? Tick it. If not, fix it beside your first try.

Set 2 (Are 9/4 and 9/8 equal? [Not equal: only the bottom changed; eighths are smaller than fourths], Are 14/4 and 21/6 equal? [Equal: 14/4 = 7/2 and 21/6 = 7/2], Are 18/8 and 27/12 equal? [Equal: 18/8 = 9/4 and 27/12 = 9/4]), 6 minutes, then 1 to mark.

Check: Right on the first try? Tick it. Then add your ticks so far.

Set 3 (Are 12/8 and 18/12 equal? [Equal: grouped in twos they are 6/4 and 9/6, two names; group again, both are 3/2], Are 15/10 and 20/12 equal? [Not equal: 15/10 = 3/2 = 9/6 and 20/12 = 5/3 = 10/6; 9 sixths is not 10 sixths]), 5 minutes, then 1 to mark.

Check: Right on the first try? Tick it. Write your count of 8.

Walk and ask: "Can you group both to smaller numbers? If the names differ, can you put them in the same parts?" Pairs 2, 5, 6 and 7 need a third name. Watch for "equal" on 2/4 and 4/6 (B) or 9/4 and 9/8 (A), "not equal" on 7/10 and 70/100 (D) or a third-name pair (E). A sketch may explain, never find, the answer.

If many miss a third-name pair: do 14/4 and 21/6 together, Lena's way. If many call 15/10 and 20/12 equal: put both in sixths, 9/6 and 10/6. If many stop at 6/4 and 9/6 on pair 7: group again.

10 min. Written task: Mo's 2s

Alone, side 2 (Mo wrote 5/3 = (2 × 5)/(2 × 3) = 10/6. What does each 2 mean on the number line? [Each third was cut into 2: twice as many parts in the whole and in the fraction, and the point did not move]). Read it aloud once; a sketch is allowed. Collect it with the exit tickets. Look for: each third cut into 2; twice as many parts in the whole and in the fraction; the point did not move.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Lena looked for one number to multiply by. What do you do when there is none?" Expect: "give both a name with smaller numbers, then compare the names."

Ask. "How do you know two fractions are not equal?" Expect first: "in the same parts the counts differ: 3/6 and 4/6"; then "only one number changed."

5 min. Exit: the ticket

Alone ((a) Circle every fraction equal to 8/6: 8/12, 16/12, 10/8, 4/3. (b) Are 2/8 and 3/12 equal? Show how you know. [(a) 16/12 and 4/3; (b) yes, both are 1/4]), read aloud once. Results go under E16 on the record sheet.

Other rooms

  • No projector. Write each set's answers on a covered part of the board and uncover them to mark. Lena's claim and the example go on the board, one step at a time.
  • No printer. Write the eight pairs on the board, one set at a time. Students write "equal" or "not equal" and the names they found in notebooks. The written task goes on the board.
  • A room with nothing. Lines ruled by hand; the key read aloud.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: set 3 becomes pair 7 alone, and the pull-together takes one question. Never drop Lena's claim, the third-name pairs or the written task. Longer: the challenge (6/8 = /12. Nothing times 8 is 12. Find the missing number through a third name. [9: 6/8 = 3/4 = 9/12]).

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: equal, group, numerator, denominator. Frames on the display: "I grouped the in s, so = ." "Both are , so they are equal." "In the same parts they are and , so they are not equal." Lena and the written task are read aloud.

Tomorrow

On the ticket: circles a name with the same number added (error B); circles a name with one number changed (error A); leaves out an equal name (the rule used one way only); "not equal" in (b) (error E). If many show one, day 17's teaching opens with it: A, "1/3 = 1/6" beside two strips; B, Jo's 3/4 = 5/6 on one line; E, Lena's 6/4 and 9/6 again, then in the same parts; a left-out name, one written with both numbers divided. Judge the written tasks after class, each look-for alone; they go back in day 17's first five minutes with the "meets" answer.
