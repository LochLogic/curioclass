Day 13: Cut each part, multiply both numbers

Before class

Print the student page both sides, the exit tickets and the key. Mark and sort the E12 tickets: "yes, the same amount" is error B. Put the display page on the projector. Draw a long line from 0 to 1 on the board, marked in fifths, with a point at 2/5. It stays up all lesson.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: multiples and factor pairs

Alone, side 1 (Write the first six multiples of 7. [7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42], Find all the factor pairs of 18. [1 × 18, 2 × 9, 3 × 6]). Hand back the E12 tickets and day 12's written tasks as they work. On a written task that called 6/8 more (error D), say: "Watch Example 1: one point gets two names." Return to it after step 5. At 4 minutes, the answers on the display; students fix in pen. Today these products count parts.

Check: Do the multiples run 7 to 42? Does 18 have three pairs?

15 min. Teaching first: the rule under the line

Whole class. Students draw the cuts and fill the frames on side 1 as you go. You cut the board line; the display shows each step on its own screen.

If day 12's tickets showed many B (2/3 and 4/5 called the same amount): read Tam's claim (Tam says 2/5 = 4/7, because he added 2 to the top and the bottom. [Not equal: 4/7 is past 2/5 on the line. Adding moves the point; cutting does not]), 3 minutes. Mark 4/7 on the board line, a little past the 1/2 point. Two points: adding moved the point. Then Example 1 and Example 2 as written. Take the 3 minutes from Cuts on printed lines: drop problem 5 there.

Example 1 (The line from 0 to 1 is marked in fifths, with a point at 2/5. Cut each fifth into 2 equal parts. What is the new name? [2/5 = (2 × 2)/(2 × 5) = 4/10]).

  1. The old name is above the point: 2/5. The whole, 0 to 1, is cut into 5 parts. The point is 2 parts from 0.
  2. Cut each fifth into 2 equal parts. Draw the new marks.
  3. Count the parts in the whole now. 5 parts, each cut into 2: 2 × 5 = 10.
  4. Count the parts from 0 to the point. 2 parts, each cut into 2: 2 × 2 = 4.
  5. Write the new name below the point: 4/10. The point did not move.
  6. Write the rule under the line: 2/5 = (2 × 2)/(2 × 5) = 4/10. Each 2 in front is the cut: each fifth cut into 2.

Point at each number as you say it. The 2 in front is the cut. The cut is the number in front, not the top number: here both happen to be 2. The 5 and the 10 count the parts in one whole. The 2 and the 4 count the parts from 0 to the point.

Example 2 (The line from 0 to 2 is marked in halves, with a point at 3/2. Cut each half into 5 equal parts. What is the new name? [3/2 = (5 × 3)/(5 × 2) = 15/10]). Past 1, the whole is still 0 to 1.

  1. The old name is above the point: 3/2. One whole is 0 to 1: 2 halves. The point is 3 halves from 0.
  2. Cut each half into 5 equal parts, past 1 too. Draw the new marks.
  3. Count the parts in one whole, 0 to 1. 2 halves, each cut into 5: 5 × 2 = 10.
  4. Count the parts from 0 to the point. 3 halves, each cut into 5: 5 × 3 = 15.
  5. Write the new name below the point: 15/10. The point did not move.
  6. Write the rule: 3/2 = (5 × 3)/(5 × 2) = 15/10. The 5 in front is the cut: each half cut into 5.

Ask. "What did the cut change, and what did it not change?" Expect: "the numbers: more parts, each one smaller", then "the point". Take "more parts" first and "the point" last.

15 min. Cuts on printed lines

Alone, 12 minutes (Two lines from 0 to 1 are marked in halves, with a point at 1/2. Cut each half into 3 on the first line. Cut each half into 5 on the second. Write each rule. [1/2 = (3 × 1)/(3 × 2) = 3/6; 1/2 = (5 × 1)/(5 × 2) = 5/10], The line from 0 to 2 is marked in thirds, with a point at 5/3. Cut each third into 2 equal parts. Write the rule. [5/3 = (2 × 5)/(2 × 3) = 10/6], The line from 0 to 2 is marked in fourths, with a point at 5/4. Cut each fourth into 2 equal parts. Write the rule. [5/4 = (2 × 5)/(2 × 4) = 10/8]). On each line: the cuts, the old name above, the new name below, the rule under it. Then 3 minutes in pairs: partners check each new name by counting on the line, the parts from 0 to the point and the parts in one whole. A mismatch gets a question mark, not the answer.

Check: Are the new names 3/6, 5/10, 10/6 and 10/8? Is the cut in front, on the top and on the bottom?

On 0 to 2 lines, students may mark only the cuts they need. Watch for the top left alone, 1/6, 5/6 or 5/8 (error A): "Were the parts up to the point cut too?" For 7/5 or 7/6 (error B): "Where on the line do you see adding?" For 10/12 or 10/16 (error K): cover the line past 1 and count one whole. If many write A: count the parts up to the point aloud on the board line, before the fix.

6 min. Fix it: 2/5 = 2/10

Side 2 (Ria cut each fifth into 2 equal parts. She wrote 2/5 = 2/10. Her line shows the point at 4/10. [2/5 = (2 × 2)/(2 × 5) = 4/10. Ria changed the bottom number only]). Two minutes alone, one in pairs, then the class. The display shows Ria's working beside the right working. Ria counted the new parts in the whole and forgot the parts up to the point. On the board line, 2/10 is another point, nearer 0. If many keep the 2: count the parts up to the point aloud, one by one.

9 min. Is it twice as much?

Pairs, then alone.

Ask. "We multiplied both numbers by 2. Is 4/10 twice as much as 2/5?" One minute in pairs, then the class. Expect "yes, it is times 2" first, then "no, it is the same point". Let the board line settle it: one point, so one amount. Twice as many parts, each half as big.

Then the fix for Sam, side 2 (Sam says 3/6 is 3 times as much as 1/2, because both numbers were multiplied by 3. [Not 3 times: 1/2 and 3/6 are at one point. There are 3 times as many parts, each a third as big]), 3 minutes alone: his claim is about 1/2 and 3/6, on the line from problem 3. The display shows his working beside the right one. If many still write "3 times" (error F): mark 1/2 and 3/6 on one line; one point.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "In 3/2 = (5 × 3)/(5 × 2), which number tells the cut? Which numbers count parts?" Expect: "the 5 is the cut"; "10 is the parts in one whole, 15 the parts up to the point". The rule is the cut, written down.

5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper (A line from 0 to 2 is marked in fourths, with a point at 7/4. Each fourth is cut into 3 equal parts. Write the rule and the new name: 7/4 = . Say in a sentence what the cut did to the 7 and the 4. [7/4 = (3 × 7)/(3 × 4) = 21/12; each fourth was cut into 3, so there are 3 times as many parts in one whole (12) and up to the point (21), each a third as big]), read aloud once. Collect at the door; results go on the record sheet under E13.

Other rooms

  • No printer. Rule each line on the board with its marks and point; students copy it with a ruler, then cut. The key is read aloud.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop problem 5 and the pairs check (students count their own). Never drop the rule under each line, the fix for 2/5 = 2/10 or the twice question. Longer: the challenge (A point is at 5/3. Each third is cut into equal parts. The new name is /12. Into how many parts was each third cut? Finish the name. [Each third cut into 4: 5/3 = (4 × 5)/(4 × 3) = 20/12]), then the extension (Write 3/4 as hundredths. Into how many parts is each fourth cut? [Each fourth cut into 25: 3/4 = (25 × 3)/(25 × 4) = 75/100]), for the few who are ready: hundredths come on day 15.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: multiply, equal, cut, parts in one whole, each shown on a cut line. A student may say the cut in a home language first. Frame on the display: "Each part was cut into , so I multiply both numbers by ." Lines may be printed large; a student may say the cuts and you draw them.
