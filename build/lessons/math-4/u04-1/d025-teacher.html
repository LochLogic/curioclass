Before class

Print the student page, line pages, tickets, key and family note. As morning work, students cut the thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths and tenths strips into single pieces; the whole, eighths, twelfths and blank strips go in the envelope uncut. Have day 24's assessments and the readiness marks beside you. Seat those who stopped at 4/4 on readiness item 1 near the camera.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: count by halves

Whole class, aloud. Count together by halves from one half to eight halves (Count by halves from 1/2 to 8/2. Write the counts that are whole numbers. [2/2 = 1, 4/2 = 2, 6/2 = 3, 8/2 = 4]); write each count on the board as it is said. Then, alone, students write the counts that are whole numbers.

Ask. "Which counts are whole numbers? Why every second one?" Expect: 2/2, 4/2, 6/2, 8/2 are 1, 2, 3, 4; two halves make each new whole. Then: "Did the count stop at 1?" Expect: no, it went on to 8/2.

5 min. Day 24's items 2 and 5 back

Alone, in pen. Hand back day 24's assessments, look-fors ticked; students fix items 2 and 5 beside the first try. Agreed with Rosa (unit 3's D): lay two sixth-pieces beside the twelfths strip, still whole, and count four twelfths. Part size alone (unit 3's J): say each as "how many parts, of what size". Item 5 with unit 3's D or G: one line from 0 to 1, both points, read the sign aloud.

15 min. Teaching first: two worked examples

Whole class. You lay pieces under the camera; students lay their own in a row on the desk and fill the frames on side 1. The line only counts and marks the end. Read every fraction as words: "three fifths", never "three over five".

Example 1 (Lay fifth-pieces in a row to make 3/5. Write 3/5 as a sum of unit fractions. [1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 3/5]).

  1. A fifth-piece is one of 5 equal parts of a whole strip. Its name is 1/5, one fifth.
  2. Lay one piece. Write 1/5. Say: one fifth.
  3. Lay a second piece touching it. Write + 1/5. Say: two fifths.
  4. Lay a third piece. Write + 1/5. Say: three fifths.
  5. Three pieces, each of size one fifth: 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 3/5.
  6. The bottom number names the size of each piece. The top number counts the pieces. Laying pieces together does not change their size, so the bottom stays 5.

Ask. After step 4: "How many pieces, and how big is each one?" Expect: three, each one fifth.

Example 2 (Lay fourth-pieces in a row, then mark 7/4 on the line from 0 to 2. Write 7/4 as a sum of unit fractions. [1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 7/4; 7/4 is more than 1]). Pairs pool their fourth-pieces: one envelope holds only four.

  1. Each piece is one fourth, 1/4. Lay the first piece on the desk. Say: one fourth.
  2. Lay pieces end to end in a row: two fourths, three fourths, four fourths. Four fourths make 1, one whole strip long.
  3. The count does not stop at 1. Keep going: five fourths, six fourths, seven fourths. Stop at 7/4.
  4. Write one 1/4 for each piece: 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 7/4.
  5. On the line from 0 to 2, count seven fourths from 0 and mark the end: 7/4.
  6. Is 7/4 more or less than 1? More. Four fourths make 1, and there are 3 fourths more.

Ask. After step 5: "Is 7/4 more or less than 1? How do you know?" Expect: more; four fourths make 1, and three more are past it.

14 min. Board paths

Pairs, pieces pooled. "Board paths", sides 1 and 2 (Path 1 is 4/6 meter long. Each board is 1/6 meter. Write the path as a sum of boards. Is it more or less than 1 meter? [1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 4/6; less than 1 meter: six sixths make 1, and there are only 4], Path 2 is 5/3 meters long. Each board is 1/3 meter. Write the path as a sum of boards. Is it more or less than 1 meter? [1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 5/3; more than 1 meter: three thirds make 1, and there are 2 thirds more], Path 3 is 7/6 meters long. Each board is 1/6 meter. Write the path as a sum of boards. Is it more or less than 1 meter? [1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 7/6; more than 1 meter: six sixths make 1, and there is 1 sixth more]). For each path, students lay one piece a board in a row, write one unit fraction a piece, then answer more or less than 1 with a reason. Then they mark the path's end on the line page: line 3 (sixths) for paths 1 and 3; line 1 for path 2, after marking thirds by eye, two marks in each whole.

Watch for a sum with the bottoms added (5/15 on path 2: error A) and a count that stops at 6/6 on path 3 (error B). Ask as you walk: "How does your row of pieces show the sum? Where is 1 on your row?"

8 min. Fix it: two wrong answers

Pairs, two minutes each to say what went wrong, then the class. "Fix it", side 2 (Bo laid 4 tenth-pieces in a row. He wrote 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 = 4/40. [4/10. Each piece is a tenth, and laying pieces together does not change their size. Bo added the bottoms too], Ada counted third-pieces: 1/3, 2/3, 3/3. She says there is no 4/3, because 3/3 is all of it. [There is a 4/3: 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 4/3. The count goes on past 1]). The display shows the wrong picture; the right one comes after the Ask.

Ask. After step 1: "Bo's row is 4 tenth-pieces. Is 4/40 that long?" Expect: no; 4/40 is 4 tiny parts.

  1. Bo's working: 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 = 4/40. He added the tops to get 4 and the bottoms to get 40.
  2. Right working: each piece is one tenth. Four pieces of size one tenth are four tenths: 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 = 4/10.
  3. 4/40 is 4 parts of a whole cut into 40: far shorter than Bo's row of 4 tenth-pieces. The size of the pieces did not change, so the bottom stays 10.

Ask. After step 1: "Ada says 3/3 is all of it. Lay a fourth third-piece. Where does it end?" Expect: past 1, at 4/3.

  1. Ada's working: 1/3, 2/3, 3/3, then stop. She takes 3/3, one whole, as the end of the numbers.
  2. Right working: lay a fourth third-piece after the first three. It ends past 1. The count is four thirds: 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 4/3.
  3. 1 is one point on the line, not its end. The count goes on past 1, and past 2 too.

If many still add the bottoms: say each sum in words, "four tenths", and lay the row again. If many still stop at 1: lay third-pieces in a row past one whole strip, then count thirds aloud on line 1, marked by eye.

6 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Five thirds, path 2: how many pieces, and how big is each one?" Expect: five pieces, each one third; it is past 1 because three thirds make 1. Take "five pieces" first, then the size.

Ask. "In 5/3, which number tells the size of a piece, and which counts them?" Expect: the 3 names the size, thirds; the 5 counts the pieces.

7 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper (Write 6/5 as a sum of unit fractions. Is 6/5 more or less than 1? How do you know? [1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 6/5; more: five fifths make 1, and there is one fifth more. A reason says five fifths make 1 and there is one fifth more, or points past 1 on a line; "the top is bigger" alone is not a reason]). Pieces stay in the envelope. A tick needs the sum and "more" with a reason: five fifths make 1 and one fifth more (or past 1 on a line); "the top is bigger" is not one. Record under E25 on the record sheet. Send the family note home.

Other rooms

  • No strip sheet. Rule strips 30 cm long: thirds every 10 cm, fourths every 7.5 cm, fifths every 6 cm, sixths every 5 cm, tenths every 3 cm. Cut them.
  • No printer. Write the three paths and the two fixes on the board; students answer in notebooks and draw a line from 0 to 2 by hand.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop path 1 and the challenge. Never drop the fixes, path 3 or the exit. Longer: the challenge (Ren also has long boards. Each is 3/6 meter. Make a 7/6 meter path with long and short boards. Write the sum. [3/6 + 3/6 + 1/6 = 7/6, or 3/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 7/6]). Then: how many different paths can Ren make from the two kinds of boards? A new order is not a new way. The search ends at two: one or two long boards; three would pass 7/6. A long board is three sixth-pieces pushed together.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: unit fraction, sum, equation, whole, each on a labelled piece. Frame, on side 1 under Example 1: " is pieces of size ." Access: wide pre-cut pieces; a helper lays pieces as the student points.

Tomorrow

On the ticket, five 1/5s or "less, it is a fraction" (error B): day 26 shows "There is no 6/4: 4/4 is all of it" beside fourth-pieces in a row past a whole strip, in its first five minutes. A sum = 6/30 (error A): day 26 shows "1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 3/15" beside three fifth-pieces. Five 1/6s (top and bottom read the other way): in the same minutes, those students read 6/5 aloud as "six fifths" and lay six fifth-pieces with a partner. Readiness gaps go to day 26, as the section opener says. Pieces stay in envelopes for day 26's rings.
