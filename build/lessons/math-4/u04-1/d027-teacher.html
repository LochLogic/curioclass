Day 27: Wholes are parts too

Teacher page

GoalI can take apart a fraction bigger than 1 using wholes, and show why it is right

Before class

Print the student page, tickets and key. Students take three blank whole strips from the envelope. Have day 26's tickets and the facts group's names beside you.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: groups of twelfths

Whole class, in their heads (Find each in your head. (a) 3/12 + 3/12 (b) 3/12 + 3/12 + 3/12 + 3/12 (c) 6/12 + 6/12 + 6/12 [(a) 6/12 (b) 12/12, which is 1 (c) 18/12, which is 1 and 6/12 more]). Write one sum at a time on the board; hands up when ready; write each answer and one student's way on the board. The facts group (readiness item 4) does Find each: 7 + 5, 16 − 8, 8 + 9, 14 − 6, 5 + 9, 13 − 4 [12, 8, 17, 8, 14, 9] alone first, then joins for (c).

Ask. After (c): "Which sums made a whole? How do you know?" Expect: (b), 12/12, twelve twelfths cover one strip; (c), 18/12 is one whole, 12/12, and 6/12 more.

3 min. Day 26's tickets

Whole class for what more than a third showed; otherwise the students named, at your table, while the rest lay out their whole strips and sixth-pieces.

  • If day 26's tickets showed many A (3/6 + 4/6 for 7/12): lay seven twelfth-pieces, ring 3 and 4, and write "7/12 = 3/6 + 4/6?" beside them. The class says each ring: "three twelfths and four twelfths". The rings hold twelfths, so the bottom stays 12.
  • If many D (the same two parts swapped): show "4/10 + 3/10 and 3/10 + 4/10" beside seven tenth-pieces ringed 1, 2 and 4: a new way needs new ring sizes.
  • A sum making 6/12 or 8/12: those students recount seven twelfth-pieces with a partner.

15 min. Teaching first: whole strips under the pieces

Whole class. You lay strips and pieces under the camera; students lay theirs and fill the frames on side 1. Pairs pool pieces for the 6/5 test and Example 3; threes for Example 2. Read every fraction as words.

Example 1 (Lay sixth-pieces on a whole strip until it is covered. Write 1 as sixths. [1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 6/6, so 1 = 6/6]).

  1. Lay a whole strip on the table. It is one whole, 1.
  2. Lay sixth-pieces on it end to end. Count: one sixth, two sixths, up to six sixths.
  3. Six sixth-pieces cover the strip: no gap, and nothing past the end. So 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 6/6.
  4. 6/6 covers the same strip as 1, so 1 = 6/6. A whole cut in 6 equal parts has 6 of them.

Ask. "Why can we write 1 as 6/6 but not as 6/5?" Students lay six fifth-pieces on a whole strip to test it. Expect: six sixths cover the strip exactly; five fifths already cover it, so the sixth fifth-piece runs past the end: 6/5 is more than 1.

Readiness item 2 (2 written 2/4): those students, pairs pooled, cover a second strip: 2 = 12/6.

Example 2 (Write 2 1/6 as a sum that uses 6/6 for each whole. Then write it as one fraction. [6/6 + 6/6 + 1/6 = 13/6, so 2 1/6 = 13/6]).

  1. 2 1/6 is 2 wholes and 1 sixth more.
  2. Lay two whole strips end to end. Cover each with six sixth-pieces. Each whole is 6/6.
  3. Lay one more sixth-piece past the second strip. Write one whole at a time: 6/6 + 6/6 + 1/6.
  4. Count the sixths: 6 and 6 is 12, and 1 more is 13. So 2 1/6 = 13/6. The bottom stays 6: every piece is a sixth.
  5. On line 9, count sixths from 0 and mark the end: six reach 1, six more reach 2, and 13/6 is one more, past 2.

Ask. After step 4: "On line 9, how many sixths from 0 to 1? From 0 to 2?" Expect: six; twelve. Each space is one sixth, so 13/6 is one space past 2.

Example 3 (Make 10/6 with sixth-pieces. Fill a whole strip first. Write 10/6 as a whole and sixths. [6/6 + 4/6 = 10/6, so 10/6 = 1 + 4/6, one and four sixths]). This one starts from the fraction.

  1. Lay ten sixth-pieces in a row. Count: one sixth, up to ten sixths.
  2. Lay a whole strip under the row. Six of the pieces cover it: 6/6, one whole.
  3. Four pieces are left past the whole: 4/6.
  4. So 10/6 = 6/6 + 4/6. And 6/6 is 1, so 10/6 = 1 + 4/6. Say it: one and four sixths.

Ask. After step 3: "Rui wrote 10/6 = 6/6 + 4/6. Bea wrote 10/6 = 1 + 4/6. Who is right?" Expect: both; 6/6 and 1 cover the same whole strip. Take "Rui, the bottoms match" and ask what 6/6 covers.

6 min. Fix it: the whole on top

Pairs, two minutes, then the class. "Fix it", side 1 (Jo wrote 2 1/3 as one fraction. Jo put the 2 and the 1 on top: 2 1/3 = 3/3. [7/3. Each whole is 3/3, so 2 1/3 = 3/3 + 3/3 + 1/3 = 7/3. Jo's 3/3 is only one whole]).

Ask. "How many whole strips does Jo's 3/3 cover? How many does 2 1/3 need?" Expect: one; two, and a third more.

  1. Jo's working: 2 1/3 = 3/3. Jo added the 2 and the 1 to make the top.
  2. Right working: each whole is three thirds, 3/3. Two wholes and one third more: 3/3 + 3/3 + 1/3.
  3. Count the thirds: 3 and 3 is 6, and 1 more is 7. So 2 1/3 = 7/3.
  4. Jo's 3/3 covers one whole strip. 2 1/3 is more than 2 wholes, so 3/3 is far too small.

If many still put the whole number on top: write the whole as a sum first, 2 = 1 + 1 = 3/3 + 3/3, lay a whole strip of thirds for each 1, then count the thirds.

12 min. Wholes and parts, then the line

Alone. "Wholes and parts", side 2 (Lay fourth-pieces on a whole strip, then 3 more. Write 1 3/4 as a sum that uses 4/4 for the whole. Then write it as one fraction. [4/4 + 3/4 = 7/4], Write 2 2/6 as a sum that uses 6/6 for each whole. Then write it as one fraction. Mark it on line 9. [6/6 + 6/6 + 2/6 = 14/6, marked past 2 on line 9], On line 7, mark the halves by eye. Write 3 1/2 as a sum that uses 2/2 for each whole. Then write it as one fraction. [2/2 + 2/2 + 2/2 + 1/2 = 7/2], (a) Write 10/4 as one whole and some fourths. (b) Write 10/4 as two wholes and some fourths. [(a) 10/4 = 4/4 + 6/4, or 1 + 6/4 (b) 10/4 = 4/4 + 4/4 + 2/4, or 2 + 2/4. Parts in any order; (b) also accepts 8/4 + 2/4]). Question 2 with pieces on a whole strip, in pairs; question 3 with strips, in threes, then on line 9; question 4 on line 7 only, halves marked by eye; question 5 from the fraction.

Watch for 2 2/6 written 4/6 (error C), a sum with one 6/6 where two are needed (one whole counted), and a bottom that grows (error A). Ask as you walk: "How many sixths fill one whole?" For 5 (a), 1 + 6/4 is right: a true sum whose parts still hold a whole's worth.

If many miss 3 1/2 on the line: count halves aloud from 0, two to each whole, then write 2/2 under each 1.

8 min. Written task: Lee's sum

Alone (Lee says 1 2/4 = 1/4 + 2/4. Is Lee right? Show with a drawing. [Not right: the 1 is a whole, four fourths, so 1 2/4 = 4/4 + 2/4 = 6/4; Lee's sum is only 3/4]). Read Lee's claim aloud once. Pieces and strips may be used; the drawing goes in the box. Collect; judge after class with the three look-fors and record under W27, one tick a look-for.

4 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "10/4 had two right answers, 1 + 6/4 and 2 + 2/4. Which fills every whole it can? How do you know?" Expect: 2 + 2/4; 6/4 still holds four fourths, a whole, and 2/4 cannot fill one.

Ask. "Why does the bottom stay 6 when a whole is written as 6/6?" Expect: the whole is cut into sixths, so we count sixth-pieces; counting does not change their size. Sketch a strip in tenths, parts roughly equal, and write 10/10 on it: the exit's strips are drawn this way.

7 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper (Write 2 3/10 as a sum that uses 10/10 for each whole. Then write it as one fraction. Draw strips to show it. [10/10 + 10/10 + 3/10 = 23/10, with two whole strips in tenths and 3 tenths more]). Read it aloud once. Pieces stay in the envelope; strips are drawn. A tick needs 10/10 + 10/10 + 3/10, then 23/10, and two whole strips in tenths with 3 tenths more; roughly equal tenths, or a strip labelled 10/10, counts. Record under E27.

Other rooms

  • No blank whole strips. Cut scrap paper to the length of six sixth-pieces laid end to end.
  • No printer. The examples, the fix and the four questions on the board; notebooks; lines from 0 to 5 ruled by hand, sixths and halves marked by eye.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: drop question 4 and the second Ask in Pull it together; never the fix, question 3, Lee's sum or the exit. Longer: the challenge (Write 9/4 as a whole number and fourths, like 1 + , in every way you can. How do you know you have them all? [1 + 5/4 (4/4 + 5/4) and 2 + 1/4 (4/4 + 4/4 + 1/4). Each whole uses 4 fourths; a third whole needs 12/4, more than 9/4, so 2 wholes is the most]). The search ends: each whole uses 4 fourths, and a third whole needs 12/4, more than 9/4.

Words and supports

Word Wall cards: whole, sum, equation, unit fraction. Frame, to take or leave, on side 1: "One whole is pieces of size , so 1 = ." Access: tape the whole strip to the desk so pieces do not slide; a helper lays pieces as the student points.

Tomorrow

On the ticket, 5/10 (error C, the 2 and the 3 put on top): day 28 opens with C's wrong example, three minutes. 13/10 (one whole counted): those students sketch two whole strips in tenths with a partner and count. Lee's sums go back in day 28's first minutes, look-fors ticked; a "yes" there is C too. Pieces are put away after today: day 28 sketches only.
