P1. Ibn Battuta, Taghaza, 1352. Ibn Battuta crossed the Sahara from Sijilmasa (in today's Morocco) to Mali and dictated his travels in 1355, for readers in Morocco; translated by H. A. R. Gibb (1929), pp. 317 to 323. Public domain.

After twenty-five days [from Sijilmasa] we reached Taghaza, an unattractive village, with the curious feature that its houses and mosques are built of blocks of salt, roofed with camel skins. ... No one lives at Taghaza except the slaves of the Massufa tribe, who dig for the salt; they subsist on dates imported from Dar'a and Sijilmasa, camels' flesh, and millet imported from the Negrolands. The negroes come up from their country and take away the salt from there. At Iwalatan a load of salt brings eight to ten mithqals; in the town of Malli [Mali] it sells for twenty to thirty, and sometimes as much as forty. The negroes use salt as a medium of exchange, just as gold and silver is used [elsewhere]; they cut it up into pieces and buy and sell with it.

Gloss: subsist on, live on; Massufa, an Amazigh (Berber) people of the desert who guided caravans; the salt diggers were people the Massufa held in slavery; mithqal, a weight of gold used as money; Iwalatan, Walata; medium of exchange, money.

P2. The Catalan Atlas, Majorca, 1375. A map of the known world attributed to Abraham Cresques, a Jewish mapmaker who worked in Majorca, an island in the Mediterranean, far from West Africa; possibly commissioned by the King of Aragon for King Charles V of France (Northwestern University, Caravans of Gold record). Paris, Bibliotheque nationale de France. The picture, in words: over West Africa, a Black king sits on a throne, wearing a gold crown, holding a sceptre and a round gold object, perhaps an orb, a coin or a nugget. The caption beside him, translated by the British Library (CC BY):

This Black ruler is named Musse Melly (Musa of Mali), lord of Guinea. This king is the richest and noblest ruler of this whole region because of the abundance of gold that is found in his lands.

Gloss: Musse Melly, Mansa Musa; Guinea, the European name for West Africa; abundance, a great amount.

S. A statement written for this assessment (ours). Saharan wealth depended on connections between places, and distant mapmakers associated Mali with gold.

F1 (cut out and fold shut). Ibn Battuta, arriving at Walata, 1352. Same book and translation, pp. 317 to 323. Public domain.

Iwalatan is the northernmost province of the negroes, and the sultan's representative there was one Farba Husayn, "farba" meaning deputy [in their language]. When we arrived there, the merchants deposited their goods in an open square, where the blacks undertook to guard them, and went to the farba. He was sitting on a carpet under an archway, with his guards before him carrying lances and bows in their hands, and the headmen of the Massufa behind him.

F2 (cut out and fold shut). Ibn Battuta, a festival at the court of Mali, 1352 to 1353. Same book and translation, pp. 323 to 335. Public domain.

A chair is placed for Dugha to sit on. He plays on an instrument made of reeds, with some small calabashes at its lower end, and chants a poem in praise of the sultan, recalling his battles and deeds of valour. The women and girls sing along with him and play with bows. ... Thereupon the sultan orders a gift to be presented to Dugha and he is given a purse containing two hundred mithqals of gold dust and is informed of the contents of the purse before all the people.

Gloss: Dugha was the sultan's interpreter and a griot, a keeper and singer of history; calabashes, dried gourds.

Goods cards for A3 (ours).

  • Silk: woven in China; carried west through Central Asian markets toward Persia and the Mediterranean.
  • Horses: bred on the grasslands of the steppe; sold toward China and to the armies of Persia and India.
  • Furs: sable and squirrel trapped in the northern forests of Rus and Siberia; carried south to Central Asian and Persian markets.
  • Spices: pepper from southern India, cloves and nutmeg from the islands of Southeast Asia; carried west and north by sea and land through Indian Ocean and Persian ports.
  • Grain: wheat and millet grown near oasis towns; sold in the same region to feed towns and caravans.
  • Gold and salt: gold from West African fields carried north across the Sahara to North Africa and the Mediterranean; salt dug in the desert carried south to the towns of Mali.
