Day 102 source context, teacher only

Teacher only. Hold all selections until day 102. Read each source context inside its question's minutes. Repeat neutrally when requested. Never explain an inference during the check. The printed response sheet carries only source labels and short questions. The map sheets are separate sources, not extra response pages.

A: Use the new outline arrangement, not the teaching locator. Dated place labels: Louisiana 1803, Oregon 1846, Alaska 1867, Puerto Rico 1898. Mixed method cards may repeat: purchase; agreement; war and treaty cession. Students supply the national parties. The border overlay separates modern outlines from historical claims and the later southern strip. Do not name the events or dates on that overlay.

Q8 reserved context, read only in Q8's four minutes: Farmers west of the Appalachians shipped goods along the Mississippi River. New Orleans linked river goods to ocean ships. Leaders sought control of this route. The national claim reached west toward the Rocky Mountains. Settlers gained access, while Native nations faced land loss and forced relocation over time. The Osage Nation records land treaties beginning after the purchase. A national purchase alone does not establish Native consent. These effects unfolded over time; the purchase did not instantly transfer Native ownership.

B, original explanatory diagram, not a historical map: A road lets wagons cross mountain passes toward inland places. A canal connects inland farms with a river and port. A railway links eastern and western settlements, carrying passengers and freight. Each mode offers access; no arrow represents land acquisition.

C, original factual summaries: Narcissa Whitman traveled for missionary work. Her August 27, 1836 letter describes noon heat without a house for shelter. The page's historical introduction records building a mission after arrival. The California Trail guide records Catherine Haun and her husband's hope for gold. They planned to repay debts and return home. Describe religious purpose as a historical motive only.

D, Polk's message to Congress, May 11, 1846, exact excerpt: “shed the blood of our fellow-citizens on our own soil.” Later historian Ramón A. Gutiérrez describes United States troops entering disputed territory between the Nueces and Rio Grande. The new locator shows both national claims. This is a government argument compared with later scholarship, not two eyewitnesses. The 1848 treaty ended the war after Mexican defeat and transferred land. Payment under those conditions does not establish a freely chosen sale.

E: The Kansas-Nebraska Act reopened conflict over whether slavery could spread into western territories. This intensified political division. Conflict over slavery contributed to secession and the Civil War, 1861 to 1865. Dates alone do not explain this causal connection.

F: Irish immigrants faced unstable employment. Many Germans sought greater freedom from government control. Chinese immigrants came for gold-mining work. Choices could be difficult and constrained. Africans were captured, forcibly transported and enslaved. They did not choose that journey or enslavement. Free Black Boston communities also existed, with homes and public institutions. These records do not describe every person in any group.

G1: Hui Aloha ʻĀina's 1897 petition against annexation carried 21,269 signatures. It records opposition rather than agreement. G2: Congress's July 7, 1898 joint resolution provided for annexing Hawaii. These are independently made records, not two excerpts counted as two sources from one author. Background: sugar interests favored annexation. Read no model argument.

Teacher background after collection only: Public Law 103-150, November 23, 1993, acknowledged the overthrow and apologized for United States participation. It did not settle claims. This is not a third assessment demand.
