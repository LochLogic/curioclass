Before class

Print the listed pieces. Fold Sources D and E under so they stay covered. Sort day 78's exit tickets by look-for, and count how many missed the place the Proclamation did not reach.

The day

5 min. Warm-up

Return day 78's practice, s05-P78-1 and s05-P78-2. Read the right order aloud, Sources A, D, B, C, with the aim beside each; students correct their own timelines, then put them in folders. An aim that never changes is error J. Return day 78's exit. Then students answer Ask. Name one place where the Emancipation Proclamation freed no one at once, and say why. [A loyal slave state (Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri, Delaware), Tennessee, which the Proclamation never named, or a named exception (13 Louisiana parishes, West Virginia, 7 Virginia counties): it applied only to areas in rebellion.] alone. If more than a third of the exits missed the reach (error C), read the list from day 78 Source C's gloss now: the loyal slave states, Tennessee, never named, and the excepted parishes and counties, Fortress Monroe among them. Otherwise take that list to a small group during the organizer.

10 min. Teaching first

Three offices: Davis, Confederate president; Lee, commander of the Army of Northern Virginia; Grant, Union general in the West, later of all Union armies. The Confederacy was founded to protect slavery, and its armies fought for that. Read Sources A and B aloud with their source lines. Davis signed the 1862 draft law to keep armies full; Lee asked Davis to let him enter Maryland.

Read the facts card, Source F, row by row. Antietam turned Lee back in September 1862 and gave Lincoln the moment for the preliminary proclamation. Gettysburg turned back a second invasion; Lee never invaded the North again. Battles cost thousands of lives; none alone ended the war.

Source G: a photograph of Lincoln and McClellan at Antietam, no dead shown. Read its description: Lincoln came to press McClellan to attack, and McClellan's slowness soon cost him his command. The picture shows a meeting, not what either man thought.

10 min. Worked example

Read Source C aloud. Students find Vicksburg on the Mississippi in Source F and attempt Ask. Worked example: Grant at Vicksburg. Use Source C and Source F to name Grant's decision, its immediate effect and its lasting effect. [Decision: move his army down the river and inland, away from his supply line. Immediate: the city and its army surrendered on July 4, 1863. Lasting: with Port Hudson's surrender on July 9, 1863, the Union held the Mississippi and split the Confederacy.] before the model:

  1. Find Vicksburg on the Mississippi in Source F
  2. Read Grant's sentence and its date: he wrote it in 1885
  3. Name the decision
  4. Name the same-month effect
  5. Name the effect that lasted to the end of the war
  6. Limit: a memoir written years later is the general's own view

Grant cut loose from his supply line; Vicksburg surrendered on July 4, 1863. Port Hudson surrendered on July 9, and only then did the Union hold the whole river, splitting the Confederacy. As Union armies moved through Mississippi, thousands of enslaved people fled to their lines. Grant wrote twenty-two years later, so this is his memory. Students fill the frame on side 1.

15 min. Alone: Component M

Hand out the organizer half-sheet. Students answer Ask. Component M organizer, alone: for each of Davis, Lee and Grant, one decision and its result; for each of Antietam, Gettysburg and Vicksburg, one immediate and one lasting effect. [Davis: the 1862 draft law; Lee: invasions of Maryland and Pennsylvania; Grant: the Vicksburg campaign. Each battle with a same-season and a longer effect.] alone from Sources A, B, C and F. Each leader needs a decision and its result; each battle an effect at once and one that lasted, with why it mattered. It is scored for decisions and effects, not for every cell filled. Walk the room, give no answers. Collect the organizers for day 89; they do not come back for revision.

10 min. Exit ticket

Uncover Sources D and E. Read their source lines, passages and glosses aloud, neutrally. Students answer Name where one decision of Davis, Lee or Grant played out, and say what the decision was. Compare one immediate effect of two of the three battles. Then explain how one 1862 land or railroad law extended federal power, and whose land it was. [A leader tied to a decision and a named place; two battle effects compared; the Homestead or Pacific Railway Act shown granting federal land taken from Native nations.] alone; places are named in words. Accept dictated answers, scribed without hints. Collect tickets.

Other rooms

Paper is the main path. Without a printer, copy the sources and the organizer's two tables onto the board; students draw the tables on paper.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: read Source G's description only; keep the model, the organizer and the exit. Longer: compare Lee's letter with what Antietam did to his plan.

Words and supports

Gloss conscription as required military service, propitious as favourable, and extinguishing Indian titles as ending Native nations' legal ownership. Name nations, not "Indians" in general: the Lakota and Cheyenne held land along the railroad's route. Read in chunks; describe places by the river and the states. An unfamiliar word is a language gap, not error D or error K: ask the question again in plain words before marking it.

Tomorrow

Tick the exit's four look-fors. Flag error D (offices confused, or one battle ending the war) and error K (empty land). Day 80's warm-up returns this exit and checks the s05-P79-1 answers aloud. If more than a third show error D, that warm-up sorts the offices and the battle dates; error K goes to a small group that reads "Indian titles" in Source E and names the nations. Day 80 documents the men and women who served, the United States Colored Troops among them. The organizers wait for day 89.
