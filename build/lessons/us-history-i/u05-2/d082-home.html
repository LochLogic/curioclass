Day 82: Test a memory against the record

At home

GoalExplain how the Lost Cause reshaped memory of the war, tested as a historical account against the record
Time37 minutes, one parent and one child (a teaching day)
Printthe student page and one exit ticket; this page and its answers are for you

Swap. No printer: copy the sources onto paper; keep the slip's part on its own covered sheet.

Skip. The warm-up; you are the partner.

  • 10 min. Test a claim. Read Sources A and B. Talk through Worked example 2 (side 1) before its steps. Say. "Why does the date matter?" Expect: Davis defends a lost cause later; Stephens says why the Confederacy was made, in 1861.
  • 12 min. Memory. Read Sources C and D. Talk about Pairs: memory 3 (side 2); the child writes alone. Say. "What does each leave out?" Expect: Lee leaves out slavery; Douglass refuses to.
  • 15 min. Exit ticket. Hand over the slip. The child answers the ticket question alone.

The questions

The child works on the printed page; the answers are for you.

WhereQuestionAnswer
Worked example 2 (side 1)Worked example: test Source A (Jefferson Davis, 1881) against Source B (Alexander Stephens, 1861). What does the later claim say, what does the wartime record say, and which is the better evidence for why the Confederacy was founded?Davis in 1881 claims slavery was only an incident; Stephens, the Confederate vice president, said in 1861 that the new government rested on slavery; the wartime statement by a founder is the better evidence, and the later claim was written after defeat to defend the cause.
  1. Read each source line: who, when, after what
  2. State the later claim in our words
  3. Find what the 1861 record says
  4. Ask when each was made and for what purpose
  5. Judge the claim by the record: it fails
  6. Say what the record cannot show: what every soldier believed
  7. Read a table like Source F: what is counted, and what the order of the dates shows
Samples (ours, untested):
  • Davis wrote 16 years after defeat to defend his cause; Stephens spoke as the government began. The wartime record shows the Confederacy put slavery at its center, so the later claim fails the test.
  • Davis and Stephens disagree, so each view is equally valid.
Pairs: memory 3 (side 2)In pairs: read Source C (Lee, 1865) and Source D (Douglass, 1871). What does each ask people to remember or forget about the war? Write one question the pair raises.Lee remembers defeat by numbers, not by the cause or by Black soldiers and people escaping slavery; Douglass insists on remembering what the war was fought over; a question such as who joined the Union army from the South.
Look-fors:
  1. what each asks people to remember
  2. what each leaves out
  3. a question that follows
Samples (ours, untested):
  • Lee asks people to remember courage and numbers, not slavery; Douglass asks them to remember that one side fought for slavery. Question: how many Union soldiers came from slave states?
  • Both are just opinions.
the ticket questionSay what the claim on the card asks people to remember or forget. Test it against Source E and write the question it raises. Then use Source F: say what it adds to your answer and what it still cannot tell you.The claim judged false or incomplete by the wartime record; a question that follows from the test; Source F used to investigate it, with a limit.
Look-fors:
  1. what the claim asks people to forget, named
  2. the claim tested against Source E, not balanced as a view
  3. a question that follows from the test
  4. Source F actually used
  5. a limit of Source F
Samples (ours, untested):
  • The Confederate constitution of 1861 forbade any law impairing property in enslaved people, and Article IV made every state let owners travel and stay with the people they enslaved, so the states' rights claim leaves out what the founders protected and how they bound the states. Question: did the states with the most enslaved people leave first? Source F shows the seven states that left before Fort Sumter had higher shares on average than the four that left after, and the slave states that stayed had the lowest shares; it cannot show what each voter believed.
  • Both sides have a point: slavery or states' rights.
