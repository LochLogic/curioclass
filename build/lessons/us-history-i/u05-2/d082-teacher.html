Before class

Print the source sheets and the exit-source slips; hold the slips. Mark day 80's organizers and exits. Day 81's s05-P81-1 stays in the essay folders with draft 1. Write feedback on draft 2 during days 82 and 83 for day 84.

The day

5 min. Warm-up

Return day 80's four-source organizer (s05-P80-2) and day 80's exit, as day 80 promised. Students answer Ask. Your day 80 organizer is back. Pick one limit you wrote. Is it about who made the source, when, or why? Rewrite it if it is about the topic instead. [The limit tied to the maker, the date or the purpose.] alone. Expect a limit tied to who made the source, when or why. If more than a third of day 80's exits showed error E, sort aloud what Source D on day 80's sheet shows (uniforms, rifles, a posed company) and cannot show (feelings, reasons for joining), then do the same for the cartoon. The practice and the organizer stay with students in their folders.

12 min. Worked example

The Lost Cause is an account written after defeat: it said the Confederacy fought for states' rights and honour, not slavery. Edward Pollard's 1866 book gave it its name; historians since, Black historians among the first, have tested it against the record and found it false. We test it; we never balance it as one view of two.

Before Source B, say that its claim is racist and hateful and that we read it as evidence of why the Confederacy was made. You read it aloud, once, plainly; no student reads it. Read Sources A and B with their source lines: Davis wrote in 1881, sixteen years after defeat; Stephens spoke in March 1861 as vice president, and the printed text is a newspaper report. Students attempt Ask. Worked example: test Source A (Jefferson Davis, 1881) against Source B (Alexander Stephens, 1861). What does the later claim say, what does the wartime record say, and which is the better evidence for why the Confederacy was founded? [Davis in 1881 claims slavery was only an incident; Stephens, the Confederate vice president, said in 1861 that the new government rested on slavery; the wartime statement by a founder is the better evidence, and the later claim was written after defeat to defend the cause.], then reveal the steps:

  1. Read each source line: who, when, after what
  2. State the later claim in our words
  3. Find what the 1861 record says
  4. Ask when each was made and for what purpose
  5. Judge the claim by the record: it fails
  6. Say what the record cannot show: what every soldier believed
  7. Read a table like Source F: what is counted, and what the order of the dates shows

Davis's claim fails against Stephens's statement; Davis's book is still good evidence of memory-making. Earlier is not always truer: timing, office and purpose decide together. Last step: before reading any table, read its title, what each column counts, and what the order of its rows shows.

15 min. Pairs: memory

Read Sources C and D aloud with their source lines. Pairs discuss Ask. In pairs: read Source C (Lee, 1865) and Source D (Douglass, 1871). What does each ask people to remember or forget about the war? Write one question the pair raises. [Lee remembers defeat by numbers, not by the cause or by Black soldiers and people escaping slavery; Douglass insists on remembering what the war was fought over; a question such as who joined the Union army from the South.], then each writes alone. Expect: Lee asks people to remember courage overwhelmed by numbers; Douglass asks them never to forget what each side fought for. For error H, opinions treated as equal, send pairs back to who wrote, when and why.

3 min. Debrief

Ask. Who is left out of a reunion story about brave armies on both sides? Expect: the enslaved, and the Black soldiers of day 80. Name slavery plainly.

15 min. Exit ticket

Hand out the exit-source slip. Read the claim card and Source E aloud, neutrally. Students answer Say what the claim on the card asks people to remember or forget. Test it against Source E and write the question it raises. Then use Source F: say what it adds to your answer and what it still cannot tell you. [The claim judged false or incomplete by the wartime record; a question that follows from the test; Source F used to investigate it, with a limit.] alone: what the claim asks people to forget, the test against Source E, their question, then Source F. Read Source F's title and columns without pointing to a pattern. A table cannot show any one person's motive. Accept dictated answers, scribed without hints. Collect tickets.

Other rooms

Paper is the main path. Without a printer, copy Sources A to D on the board; write the slip's three parts on a separate covered sheet.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: hear one pair in the debrief; keep the test, the question, Source F and the exit. Longer: set Lee's order beside Douglass's speech and say which better answers why the war was fought, and why.

Words and supports

Lost Cause: a later account defending the Confederacy and denying slavery's place. Memory: how the past is remembered. Read source lines aloud, use the glosses, and allow spoken answers first; a language gap is not error H.

Tomorrow

Tick the exit's five look-fors. Flag error A (states' rights replacing slavery as the cause) and error H (two equal views). If more than a third miss the test, a day 83 small group retests the claim card against Source E while the warm-up holds its own retrieval. This exit comes back again on day 87, which returns to questioning historians' accounts. Day 83 turns to the war's consequences. Draft 2 comes back with feedback by day 84.
