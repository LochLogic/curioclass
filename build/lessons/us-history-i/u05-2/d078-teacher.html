Before class

Print the listed pieces and fold Source E under so it stays covered. Have day 71's readiness R1 and R2 results ready, with the retrieval task from the grade 5 Civil War row. Keep day 77's essay 1 draft 1 for written feedback before day 81.

The day

5 min. Warm-up

Hand back day 71's readiness R1 and R2. Students answer alone: Ask. What did the Union say it was fighting for in 1861? Was it the same in 1863? Give one reason. [In 1861, restoring the Union; by 1863 freedom in areas in rebellion had become a war aim, pushed by people who escaped slavery, Black leaders and the needs of the war.]. The grade 5 retrieval task asked what students remember about emancipation; listen for error C, everyone freed at once, and error J, an aim that never changed. Hear two answers and contrast them: in 1861 the Union's stated aim was restoring the Union.

10 min. Teaching first

Read Sources A and D aloud with their source lines and glosses; students follow on paper. Source A: in April 1861 Lincoln ordered a blockade, warships closing Southern ports to trade and supplies, to restore the Union. Source D: in May 1861 three men escaped slavery to Fortress Monroe. Later accounts name them as Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory and James Townsend. Their flight, and the many who followed, forced Union officers to decide whether to send people back. Butler's letter is his view, not theirs. "Contraband" was the army's word; we say people who escaped slavery. Students mark A and D for their timeline.

10 min. Worked example

Read Source B aloud. Students attempt Ask. Worked example: Source B, Lincoln to Horace Greeley, August 1862. What does Lincoln say his aim is, what does his closing sentence add, and what did he not say in the letter? [His public aim, his official duty, is saving the Union, by freeing all, some or none; his closing sentence keeps his personal wish that all men everywhere could be free; he did not say he had already drafted an emancipation order.] before the model. Walk through the steps, pausing for students to mark the aim, the three choices and the closing sentence:

  1. Read the source line: who, when, to whom, published where
  2. Find the aim sentence
  3. Name the three choices he lists
  4. Read the closing sentence: official duty against personal wish
  5. Ask what the reader could not know: the draft order of July 1862
  6. Limit the claim: the letter shows his public stance in August 1862, not his whole view

The closing sentence matters: Lincoln separates his official duty from his personal wish that all men everywhere could be free. The letter was public; a month earlier he had read a draft order to his cabinet, so it shows his public position, not his whole plan. Students complete the frame on side 1.

12 min. Pairs: the timeline of aims

Read Source C aloud first, then its gloss. Ask. Place Sources A, B, C and D on the timeline from April 1861 to January 1863. Beside each, write what the Union was fighting for at that point and who was pushing for a change. [A blockade, April 1861: restore the Union by war on Confederate trade. D Fortress Monroe, May 1861: people escaping slavery force the question. B Greeley letter, August 1862: Union first, freeing used as a means. C Proclamation, January 1863: freedom in areas in rebellion and Black men into the army.]. One partner finds dates and evidence, the other aims and who pushed; switch halfway. Circulate: expect Sources A, D, B, C in order, with flight from slavery pressing policy.

3 min. Debrief

Ask. What came between Sources B and C? Expect: in September 1862, after Antietam, Lincoln announced the preliminary proclamation, a warning that freedom would come on January 1 in areas still in rebellion. Ask. Why did the Proclamation stop where it did? Expect: Lincoln issued it as a war measure, so it could reach only areas fighting the Union, and he needed the loyal slave states of Kentucky, Maryland, Missouri and Delaware to stay. Tennessee was never named; parts of Louisiana and Virginia were excepted. Freedom on paper still needed armies and people's own flight.

10 min. Exit ticket

Uncover Source E. Read its source line and passage aloud, neutrally; its first lines say Conkling's meeting was full of Union men who disliked the Proclamation. Students answer Use Source E (Lincoln to James Conkling, 1863), Source A and what you learned today to connect the blockade, the change in Union war aims and emancipation. Then name one place the Proclamation did not reach, and one obstacle Lincoln faced. [The blockade and emancipation were both war measures against the Confederacy; by 1863 Black soldiers and freedom were part of the Union's aims; the Proclamation covered only areas in rebellion and needed armies to enforce it, and opposition and uncertain battles constrained Lincoln.] alone on the ticket. Accept oral answers, scribed without hints. Collect tickets; timelines go in folders. Send the family note home.

Other rooms

Paper is the main path. Without a printer, copy the passages and source lines on the board, keeping Source E covered. No projector is needed.

Shorter or longer

Shorter: cut pair reports to one; keep all four items and the exit. Longer: partners add to the timeline what each source cannot show, and who made it.

Words and supports

Use the sheet's glosses for blockade, paramount, in rebellion and contraband; emancipation means freeing people from slavery. Read in short chunks and accept dictated reasoning. A student who explains the limit aloud without the word proclamation has met it. Frame: "This source shows , but it cannot show because (who made it, when, or for whom)."

Day 79's warm-up checks the timelines in pairs and returns this exit.
