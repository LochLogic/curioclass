Day 79 sources: leaders, battles and the home front

Read each source line first: who made it, when, and for whom.

Source A. Confederate States Congress, "An Act to further provide for the public defence", approved by President Jefferson Davis, April 16, 1862. Public domain. Statutes at Large of the Confederate States, First Congress, first session, chapter 31.

the President be, and he is hereby authorized to call out and place in the military service of the Confederate States, for three years, unless the war shall have been sooner ended, all white men who are residents of the Confederate States, between the ages of eighteen and thirty-five years at the time the call or calls may be made, who are not legally exempted from military service.

Gloss: call out, order into the army; legally exempted, excused by law.

Source B. Robert E. Lee to Jefferson Davis, September 3, 1862. Public domain. Official Records, series 1, vol. 19, part 2, p. 590. A private letter to his president.

The present seems to be the most propitious time since the commencement of the war ... to enter Maryland.

Gloss: propitious, favourable. Printed copies differ on one word where the dots stand.

Source C. Ulysses S. Grant, Personal Memoirs, 1885, chapters 33 and 34, on the Vicksburg campaign of April and May 1863. Public domain. Written twenty-two years after the events, while Grant was dying, to support his family.

I felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled since. ... I was now in the enemy's country, with a vast river and the stronghold of Vicksburg between me and my base of supplies. But I was on dry ground on the same side of the river with the enemy. ... I therefore determined to move independently of Banks, cut loose from my base, destroy the rebel force in rear of Vicksburg and invest or capture the city.

Gloss: base of supplies, the depot his food and ammunition came from; invest, surround.

Source D. The Homestead Act, May 20, 1862, section 1. Public domain (United States government work). 12 Stat. 392.

any person who is the head of a family, or who has arrived at the age of twenty-one years, and is a citizen of the United States, ... and who has never borne arms against the United States Government or given aid and comfort to its enemies, shall, from and after the first January, eighteen hundred and sixty-three, be entitled to enter one quarter section or a less quantity of unappropriated public lands

Gloss: enter, claim; a quarter section, 160 acres; unappropriated public lands, federal land not yet sold or granted. Much of this public land was the traditional or treaty land of Native nations, gained by the United States through treaties, often made under pressure, or by force.

Source E. The Pacific Railway Act, July 1, 1862, section 2. Public domain (United States government work). 12 Stat. 489.

The United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act and required for the said right of way and grants hereinafter made.

Gloss: extinguish the Indian titles, end the Native nations' ownership of the land, which United States law recognized, by treaty purchase or by force. The law promises the railroad this; it does not say how. Right of way, the strip the railroad runs on.

Source H. Treaty of Ruby Valley, between the United States and the Western Bands of the Shoshone Nation, October 1, 1863, article III. Public domain (United States government work). 18 Stat. 689. Made by Te-moak and other leaders of the Western Bands with two United States commissioners, fifteen months after the Pacific Railway Act.

it being understood that provision has been made by the government of the United States for the construction of a railway from the plains west to the Pacific ocean, it is stipulated by the said bands that the said railway or its branches may be located, constructed, and operated, and without molestation from them, through any portion of country claimed or occupied by them.

Gloss: stipulated, agreed as a condition; molestation, interference. Farther east, the line west from the 100th meridian ran up the Platte valley through land the United States had recognized in the 1851 Treaty of Fort Laramie as the territory of the Cheyenne and Arapaho (article 5). The Shoshone, Cheyenne and Arapaho nations govern themselves today.

Source F. Three battles: our facts card. Ours, from National Park Service park pages (public domain). Losses count soldiers killed, wounded, captured or missing on both sides.

Battle When Who led What happened at once Losses
Antietam, Maryland September 17, 1862 Lee; McClellan Lee's first invasion of the North turned back about 22,720 in one day
Gettysburg, Pennsylvania July 1 to 3, 1863 Lee; Meade Lee's second invasion turned back more than 51,000 in three days

Source G. Alexander Gardner, photograph, "President Lincoln on battle-field of Antietam", October 3, 1862. Public domain; Library of Congress, no known restrictions on publication.

In words: Lincoln, tall in a top hat, faces General McClellan outside a tent, with officers in a line around them. The Library of Congress record says Lincoln came to urge McClellan to attack.
