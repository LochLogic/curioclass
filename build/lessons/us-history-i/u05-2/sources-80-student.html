Day 80 sources: who served, and how we know

Images print in black and white; each is described in words under it. Keep Source F folded until the exit ticket.

Source A (worked example). Frederick Douglass, "Men of Color, To Arms!", March 1863. Public domain. A speech he gave in Rochester, New York, on March 2, then printed in his newspaper, Douglass' Monthly, and as a broadside, to recruit Black men for the 54th Massachusetts.

Action! Action! not criticism, is the plain duty of this hour. Words are now useful only as they stimulate to blows. ... There is no time to delay. ... Liberty won by white men would lose half its luster. "Who would be free themselves must strike the blow." ... In good earnest then, and after the best deliberation, I now for the first time during this war feel at liberty to call and counsel you to arms.

Gloss: broadside, a printed poster; luster, shine, worth; counsel, advise.

Source B. Kate Cumming, A Journal of Hospital Life in the Confederate Army of Tennessee, 1866, entry for April 12, 1862, Corinth, Mississippi. Public domain. A volunteer nurse's journal, published by her after the war.

I sat up all night, bathing the men's wounds, and giving them water. Every one attending to them seemed completely worn out. Some of the doctors told me that they had scarcely slept since the battle. ... The men are lying all over the house, on their blankets, just as they were brought from the battle-field. They are in the hall, on the gallery, and crowded into very small rooms. The foul air from this mass of human beings at first made me giddy and sick, but I soon got over it. We have to walk, and when we give the men any thing kneel, in blood and water; but we think nothing of it at all.

Gloss: gallery, porch; the battle, Shiloh, fought nearby that month.

Source C. Harriet Tubman, letter dictated at Beaufort, South Carolina, June 30, 1863. Public domain. Printed in the Boston Commonwealth, an antislavery weekly, in July 1863, and reprinted in Sarah Bradford, Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman, 1869. The paper says she dictated it: a helper wrote down her words for friends in the North.

Don't you think we colored people are entitled to some credit for that exploit, under the lead of the brave Colonel Montgomery? We weakened the rebels somewhat on the Combahee River, by taking and bringing away seven hundred and fifty-six head of their most valuable live stock, known up in your region as "contrabands," and this, too, without the loss of a single life on our part ... Of these seven hundred and fifty-six contrabands, nearly or quite all the able-bodied men have joined the colored regiments here.

Gloss: exploit, bold act; Union gunboats raided along the Combahee River in June 1863. The Commonwealth wrote that Tubman "piloted Col. Montgomery on his most successful expedition." Her "live stock" is bitter irony: enslavers counted people as property.

Source D. William Morris Smith, photograph, Company E, 4th United States Colored Infantry, at Fort Lincoln, District of Columbia, between 1863 and 1866. Public domain; Library of Congress, Civil War glass negatives.

In words: about thirty soldiers in uniform stand in two rows with rifles at their sides; their officers stand in front. It is a posed company photograph at a fort that guarded Washington.

Source E. Thomas Nast, "Franchise. And Not This Man?", Harper's Weekly, August 5, 1865. Public domain; Library of Congress.

In words: Columbia, the figure for the nation, rests her hand on the shoulder of a Black Union soldier who has lost a leg. The caption asks whether he too should have the vote.

Gloss: franchise, the right to vote.

Source F (exit ticket, fold until asked). Spotswood Rice, private, 67th United States Colored Infantry, letter to his daughters, Benton Barracks Hospital, St. Louis, Missouri, September 3, 1864. Public domain. National Archives, Record Group 393; transcribed by the Freedmen and Southern Society Project. Printed with his own spelling. The first lines are in his hand; another person wrote the rest. The letter reached the National Archives because a member of the family that enslaved one of the girls sent it on to a Union general.

My Children I take my pen in hand to rite you A few lines to let you know that I have not forgot you and that I want to see you as bad as ever now my Dear Children I want you to be contented with whatever may be your lots be assured that I will have you if it cost me my life ... If Diggs dont give you up this Government will and I feel confident that I will get you
