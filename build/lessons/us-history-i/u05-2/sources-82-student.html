Day 82 sources: testing a memory against the record

Source B holds a hateful claim. We read it because it tells us, in a Confederate leader's own words, why the Confederacy was made. Sources E and F and the claim card are on a separate exit-source slip, handed out only at the exit ticket.

Source A (worked example). Jefferson Davis, The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government, 1881, vol. 1, chapter 10. Public domain. The chapter's contents line begins "False Statements of the Grounds for Separation.--Slavery not the Cause, but an Incident." Written sixteen years after the Confederacy he led was defeated.

The truth remains intact and incontrovertible, that the existence of African servitude was in no wise the cause of the conflict, but only an incident.

Gloss: incontrovertible, beyond argument; servitude, slavery; in no wise, not at all; incident, a side matter.

Source B. Alexander H. Stephens, Confederate vice president, speech at Savannah, Georgia, March 21, 1861. Public domain. No official text exists; Henry Cleveland printed a newspaper report of it in Alexander H. Stephens, in Public and Private, 1866, and warned that the speech was "not reported with great accuracy." Dashes in that printing appear here as commas.

Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea; its foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests upon the great truth, that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.

Gloss: the opposite idea, the opposite of the idea, which Stephens said the founders held, that the races are equal.

Source C. Robert E. Lee, General Orders No. 9, April 10, 1865. Public domain. His farewell to his army, the day after its surrender; National Park Service, Appomattox Court House.

After four years of arduous service marked by unsurpassed courage and fortitude, the Army of Northern Virginia has been compelled to yield to overwhelming numbers and resources.

Gloss: arduous, hard; fortitude, courage in hardship.

Source D. Frederick Douglass, address at Arlington National Cemetery, May 30, 1871. Public domain. Frederick Douglass Papers Project. A Decoration Day speech at the graves of unknown Union soldiers. Dashes appear here as commas.

We are sometimes asked in the name of patriotism to forget the merits of this fearful struggle, and to remember with equal admiration those who struck at the nation's life, and those who struck to save it, those who fought for slavery and those who fought for liberty and justice. I am no minister of malice. I would not strike the fallen. I would not repel the repentant, but may my right hand forget its cunning, and my tongue cleave to the roof of my mouth, if I forget the difference between the parties to that terrible, protracted and bloody conflict.

Gloss: merits, rights and wrongs; may my right hand forget its cunning, words from Psalm 137: let me lose my skill if I forget.
