Day 78 sources: Lincoln's aims, 1861 to 1863

Read each source line first: who made it, when, and for whom. Write on your student page, not here.

Source A. Abraham Lincoln, proclamation of a blockade, April 19, 1861. Public domain (United States government work). A proclamation to the public and to foreign governments.

[I] have further deemed it advisable to set on foot a blockade of the ports within the States aforesaid, in pursuance of the laws of the United States and of the law of nations in such case provided. For this purpose a competent force will be posted so as to prevent entrance and exit of vessels from the ports aforesaid.

Gloss: blockade, warships stop ships entering or leaving a port; the States aforesaid, the seven states that had left the Union.

Source B. Abraham Lincoln to Horace Greeley, August 22, 1862. Public domain. A public letter, printed in the newspapers. Collected Works, vol. 5, pp. 388-389.

My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that. ... I have here stated my purpose according to my view of official duty; and I intend no modification of my oft-expressed personal wish that all men every where could be free.

Gloss: paramount, most important; official duty, his job as president, set apart from his own wish.

Source C. Abraham Lincoln, the Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863. Public domain (United States government work). National Archives. It quotes the preliminary proclamation of September 22, 1862.

all persons held as slaves within any State or designated part of a State, the people whereof shall then be in rebellion against the United States, shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free ...

And I further declare and make known, that such persons of suitable condition, will be received into the armed service of the United States to garrison forts, positions, stations, and other places, and to man vessels of all sorts in said service.

Gloss: in rebellion, fighting against the United States on that date. The full text never names the loyal slave states or Tennessee, so they were not covered. It also excepts thirteen parishes of Louisiana, West Virginia and seven Virginia counties, one of them Elizabeth City County, where Fortress Monroe stands.

Source D. General Benjamin Butler to General Winfield Scott, Fortress Monroe, Virginia, May 27, 1861. Public domain. National Archives, Record Group 107; transcribed by the Freedmen and Southern Society Project. A report to his commander, sent on to the Secretary of War.

The escapes from them are very numerous, and a squad has come in this morning to my pickets bringing their women and children. ... I have therefore determined to employ, as I can do very profitably, the able-bodied persons in the party, issuing proper food for the support of all ... As a means of offence therefore in the enemy's hands these negroes when able bodied are of the last importance.

Gloss: pickets, guards at the edge of a camp; of the last importance, very important. Three days earlier three men, Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory and James Townsend, had escaped to the fort. Butler wrote of people as property he could keep from the enemy; soon Union soldiers called such people "contrabands". We say "people who escaped slavery".

Source E (exit ticket). Abraham Lincoln to James C. Conkling, August 26, 1863. Public domain. A public letter for a mass meeting of Union men in Springfield, Illinois, September 3, 1863, read aloud there. Lincoln wrote to those among them who disliked the Emancipation Proclamation. The letter's one dash is printed here as a comma.

But negroes, like other people, act upon motives. Why should they do any thing for us, if we will do nothing for them? If they stake their lives for us, they must be prompted by the strongest motive, even the promise of freedom. And the promise being made, must be kept.
