Unit 11.1: Area and perimeter formulas (days 89 to 93)

The math, for the adult

Standard 4.MD.A.3: "Apply the area and perimeter formulas for rectangles in real world and mathematical problems." Students met both measures in grade 3 by counting: area as the unit squares that cover a shape, arranged in rows and columns (3.MD.C.7), and perimeter as the length of the edge (3.MD.D.8). This unit turns each count into a formula and then uses the formula backwards.

Area is rows times columns. A rectangle 6 feet by 8 feet holds 6 rows of 8 one-foot squares, so its area is 6 × 8 = 48 square feet. A = l × w says the same thing for every rectangle. The unit is a square (a square foot, a square meter), because what is counted is squares.

Perimeter is the four sides added. P = l + w + l + w. Because there are two of each side, the same sum is 2 × l + 2 × w, and by the distributive property 2 × l + 2 × w = 2 × (l + w). In words: length plus width is half the perimeter. Students should see this on a drawn rectangle (the edge cut into two equal halves, each one length and one width) before they see it in symbols. The unit is a length (feet, meters), because what is measured is a line.

A missing side is a missing number in an equation. If a floor covers 63 square feet and is 7 feet wide, the equation is 7 × ? = 63, so the missing side is a missing factor: 9 feet. This is the unknown-factor work of 3.OA.A.4 and the factor pairs of 4.OA.B.4. If a fence is 38 feet all the way around and the pen is 12 feet long, the equation is 12 + ? + 12 + ? = 38: the two long sides use 24 feet, the 14 feet left make two short sides, so each is 7 feet. Students write the equation for this rectangle, with its numbers and one unknown, and solve it. They do not recall a formula and fill it in. That is what "apply" means here (Progressions for the Common Core State Standards, Geometric Measurement).

Bigger and harder numbers. Day 91 uses sides of two and three digits (find l + w, then add the sum to itself) and sides that are fractions with like denominators (1 5/6 + 2 2/6 + 1 5/6 + 2 2/6). Day 92 asks students to decide which measure a situation needs, with no cue word. A cue word is any word that names the inside or the edge (covers, fills, around, edge, side, border of, whole floor); the situation (fence, tape, paint, trim, wire) is not a cue.

Limits. Rectangles only. Areas stay within 100, or a one-digit number times a multiple of 10. A side found from a perimeter comes from an even perimeter of 100 or less. Larger areas come back in stretches 12 to 14.

Why this model

Grid paper and tiles on day 89 make both counts visible: the squares inside, a finger along the edge. On day 90 the squares go away. Community Plot Planner gives each student a lot and five family notes ("we need 48 square feet; our plot is 6 feet wide"), so the student has to write the equation before the plot can be built; the squares fade as the student works. Where the tool is not built or there are no devices, the same notes are done on paper with grid paper and sketches. From day 91 a rectangle is a sketch with labelled sides, which is what students will meet on every later page.

The reteach (readiness gaps)

The readiness check is the last ten minutes of day 88. If more than a third of the class missed item 1 (area of a 6 by 7 rectangle) or item 2 (perimeter of a 9 by 4 rectangle), day 90's warm-up becomes this reteach, and the facts check moves to day 91.

  1. Each student draws a 5 by 3 rectangle on grid paper.
  2. Count the squares inside together, row by row: "5, 10, 15. Three rows of 5 is 15 squares." Write 3 × 5 = 15.
  3. Trace the edge with a finger, naming each side: "5, 3, 5, 3." Write 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16.
  4. Ask: "Which number tells how much paper the rectangle covers? Which tells how far an ant walks around it?"
  5. Students do a 4 by 6 rectangle alone, then compare with a partner.

For item 3 (8 × ? = 48) gaps: fact help goes into the warm-ups only, not into the lesson.

Words

Area, perimeter, length, width, square unit (square foot, square meter), formula, equation. Teach each on a labelled rectangle. "Covers" and "goes around" help at first; by day 92 problems use neither.

Readiness check: day 88, four items, marked before day 89. Exits: one each on days 89 to 92, written on the record sheet by error letter. End check: day 93, seven items, 30 minutes, unaided, no grid; items 5 and 6 are judged by the teacher with look-fors and answered in day 94's first five minutes. Area returns with larger sides in stretches 12 to 14; without that return the standard is only half taught.

20 min. Teaching first: one rectangle

12 minutes together: build one rectangle (Build a rectangle 5 tiles long and 3 tiles wide. Find its area and its perimeter. [5 × 3 = 15 square units; 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 units, or 2 × (5 + 3) = 16]). Each student builds along with tiles on grid paper and fills the frame on side 1 as you go, not ahead of you. In the box on side 1, they sketch the 5 by 3 and label its sides. The display shows the figure, then each step.

  1. Build it: a row of 5 tiles, then 2 more rows of 5 under it. Each tile is one square unit.
  2. Area is how many squares cover the inside. Count by fives: 5, 10, 15.
  3. That is 3 rows of 5. Write it with the sides: A = l × w, so 5 × 3 = 15 square units.
  4. Perimeter is the length all the way around the edge. Run a finger along it and count the unit sides: 16.
  5. Write it side by side: P = l + w + l + w, so 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 units.
  6. Stop halfway around: one length and one width, 5 + 3 = 8. The other half is the same 8.
  7. So P = 2 × (l + w): 2 × (5 + 3) = 2 × 8 = 16 units. The same answer.

Say as you point: area covers the inside, perimeter goes around the edge. Leave A = l × w, P = l + w + l + w and P = 2 × (l + w) on the board.

Example 1 (A garden bed covers 63 square feet. It is 7 feet wide. How long is it? [7 × ? = 63; 9 feet]). The missing side is a missing factor. Each square stands for one square foot.

  1. Sketch the bed: 7 feet wide, the length unknown, 63 square feet inside.
  2. Area is how many squares cover it. Each square stands for one square foot, and they come in rows of 7.
  3. How many rows of 7 make 63? Write 7 × ? = 63.

What it holds

Twelve problems in words, with no drawings, and no fixes: day 91 runs its own five fixes first. The twelve mix both formulas: an area from two sides, two areas with a side that is a multiple of 10, a missing side from an area, a missing side from a perimeter, a perimeter with two- and three-digit sides, and a perimeter with fraction sides (like denominators). The order is shuffled, so the words have to say which measure is asked for. The sheet says 18 minutes on its head.

The generator never uses a fact that the unit's lessons, exits or checks use: two sides with their area or their perimeter, in any order. So the sheet cannot hand students an answer they will meet on the end check. Each story thing comes in units and sizes it really has.

Making it

The sheet code printed in its foot (five letters) reprints exactly the same sheet; write it on the day 91 teacher page if you want it again next year.

Alone for 18 minutes, then 4 minutes in pairs checking against each other; where partners disagree, both redo the problem. Then put the key up. Most students finish 8 to 10; the pairs check what is done.

The day

5 min. Warm-up: two perimeters

Alone, on the back of the grid paper. A rectangle is 264 feet long and 129 feet wide. Find its perimeter. [786 feet] and A rectangle is 2 3/5 inches long and 1 4/5 inches wide. Find its perimeter. [8 4/5 inches; 6 14/5, 7 9/5 and 44/5 are also right]. Students add the length and the width, then add the sum to itself, as on day 91. Go over both at 5 minutes. A sum not doubled (393, 4 2/5 or 3 7/5) is error B: trace the four sides on the board.

If many made B on day 91's exit (383 or 5 5/10): Ben's wrong answer from day 91's display first, one minute. A (92 for the hall): open the dog pen with Lia's wrong answer: "Does the floor cover, or go around?"

20 min. The dog pen, then teaching first

10 minutes: the task, in pairs (A dog pen is a rectangle. It uses all 36 feet of fence. Which pen gives the dog the most room? [9 feet by 9 feet: 81 square feet. Every pen's length plus width is 18]). Read the task aloud once. Four volunteers hold the corners of the string loop as a long thin pen, then a square one: same string, which dog has more room? Pairs sketch two pens on the grid side, the long thin one and the square, and fill the rest of the table on side 1 by numbers alone: length + width must be 18. Watch for pairs who change the fence while changing the pen (a 10 by 10 pen): count its sides with them.

Ask. "Every pen used 36 feet of fence. What is the same about the length plus the width of each? Why?" Expect: "they all make 18"; then "the fence is two lengths and two widths, so one of each is half". Take the first, then press for the second.

Ask. "Two rectangles have the same perimeter. Must they have the same area?" Expect "no": point at 1 by 17 (17 square feet) and 9 by 9 (81).

Ask first, from the pairs' tables. Then close it, smallest pen first:

  1. Every pen uses all 36 feet: two lengths and two widths.
  2. So one length and one width use half the fence: 18 feet.
  3. Every pen has length + width = 18: 1 and 17, 2 and 16, and so on up to 9 and 9.
  4. Room for the dog is the inside: the area, length times width.
  5. 1 × 17 = 17, 5 × 13 = 65, 8 × 10 = 80, 9 × 9 = 81.
  6. The 9 by 9 pen gives the most room: 81 square feet.

10 minutes: two worked examples, whole class. Students fill the frames on side 1 as you go.

Example 1, times as long (A rug is 7 feet wide. Its length is 2 times its width. How much floor is under the rug? [Length 2 × 7 = 14 feet; area 14 × 7 = 98 square feet]).

  1. Sketch the rug: 7 feet wide, the length unknown.
  2. Draw the width as 1 bar of 7. The length is 2 bars of 7.
  3. "2 times its width" means 2 × 7, not 7 + 2. The rug is 14 feet long.
  4. The floor under the rug is the inside of the rectangle: the area.
  5. Area is length times width: 14 × 7 = 98.
  6. The rug is on 98 square feet of floor. Square feet, because it is area.
  7. Check: 14 is 2 sevens, and 98 is 14 rows of 7.

"2 times as long" is 2 × 7, not 7 + 2: the same as stretch 10's comparing. A child who writes 9 is adding.

Example 2, two steps (A chicken yard has 72 square feet of ground. It is 8 feet wide. How much fence does it need? [8 × ? = 72, so 9 feet long; fence 9 + 8 + 9 + 8 = 34 feet]).

  1. Sketch the yard: 8 feet wide, the length unknown, 72 square feet inside.
  2. Step 1: the 72 square feet is the inside, so 8 × ? = 72. 8 × 9 = 72: the yard is 9 feet long.
  3. Step 2: fence goes along the edge of the yard. That is the perimeter.
  4. 9 + 8 + 9 + 8 = 34, or 2 × (9 + 8) = 34.
  5. The yard needs 34 feet of fence. Feet, because it is a length.

In each step ask: "Is this number the inside or the edge?"

20 min. Six problems by hand

Alone, side 2. Read all six aloud once at the start. For each, students write "area" or "perimeter" first, sketch on the back of the grid paper (a plain rectangle with its sides labelled; do not count squares), then the answer with its unit. None of the six says "covers" or "around": the story tells which. A kitchen floor is 9 feet long and 6 feet wide. Each tile is 1 foot by 1 foot. How many tiles does the floor need? [9 × 6 = 54 tiles (area, 54 square feet)] Zoe makes a rectangle out of tape on the gym floor. It is 14 feet long and 9 feet wide. How much tape does she use? [14 + 9 + 14 + 9 = 46 feet (perimeter)] A bench seat is 2 feet wide. It is 6 times as long as it is wide. Mom paints the top of the seat. How much does she paint? [Length 6 × 2 = 12 feet; 12 × 2 = 24 square feet (area)] A dog yard is 6 feet wide. It has 42 square feet of ground. How much fence closes it in? [6 × ? = 42, so 7 feet long; fence 7 + 6 + 7 + 6 = 26 feet] A flag is 9 inches wide. It is 2 times as long as it is wide. Gold trim makes a border on the flag. How much trim is used? [Length 2 × 9 = 18 inches; 18 + 9 + 18 + 9 = 54 inches (perimeter)] Kai bends 24 inches of wire into a rectangle. It is 7 inches long. How wide is it? [7 + ? + 7 + ? = 24; 5 inches (the wire is the perimeter)]

After 15 minutes, pairs swap and check each answer against the story.

Check: Is your number the inside or the edge? Is the unit right? If not, put a question mark.

Watch for: a "times as long" length found by adding (8 for the bench, 11 for the flag); the wrong measure (30 tiles, 126 feet of tape, 162 inches of trim: error A); "54 feet" of tiles (error E); stopping at the side (7 for the dog yard). If many choose the wrong measure: each pair sketches problem 1 and shades what the story asks for, the inside or the edge. If many add for "times as long": draw 2 bars of 9 for the flag.

5 min. Written task

Alone, the box at the foot of side 2: Look at problem 2. Did it need area or perimeter? How did you know? [Perimeter, 46 feet: the tape makes the sides, not the floor inside]. Students may answer aloud to you instead (a scribe is allowed). Collect the student pages with the exit slips at the end; read after class.

Tick each look-for on the key alone; the key gives samples that meet, partly meet and do not (ours, untested). A child who writes "tape, lines, 46 feet" with no full sentence has look-fors 1 to 3: that is language, not math.

5 min. Pull it together

Whole class.

Ask. "Which problem was hardest to decide? What in the story told you?" Expect the dog yard or the flag. Show one decided from the story, then one from a word; connect them: "inside or edge?" before any number.

5 min. Exit: the slip

Alone, on paper: A patio is 4 yards wide. Its length is 3 times its width. (a) Ana paints the patio. How much does she paint? (b) She fences in the patio. How much fence does she need? Write the units. [48 square yards; 32 yards]. Read it aloud once. Write the results on the record sheet under E92.

Unit 11.1 family note: area and perimeter

English

This week your child is learning two ways to measure a rectangle.

Area covers. It is how many squares fit inside: a rug 6 feet by 8 feet covers 6 × 8 = 48 square feet.

Perimeter goes around. It is the length of the edge: the same rug has 6 + 8 + 6 + 8 = 28 feet of edge.

Your child will also work backwards. If a rug covers 48 square feet and is 6 feet wide, how long is it? (8 feet, because 6 × 8 = 48.)

Try this at home, five minutes. Ask your child for both measures of your kitchen table, a book or a doormat. Measure the sides together with anything you have, even hand spans. Ask: "Which number tells how much it covers? Which tells how far it is around?" The answer to the first is in square units; the second is not.

Spanish

Esta semana su hijo o hija aprende dos maneras de medir un rectángulo.

El área cubre. Es cuántos cuadrados caben adentro: una alfombra de 6 pies por 8 pies cubre 6 × 8 = 48 pies cuadrados.

El perímetro da la vuelta. Es el largo del borde: la misma alfombra tiene 6 + 8 + 6 + 8 = 28 pies de borde.

También trabajará al revés. Si una alfombra cubre 48 pies cuadrados y mide 6 pies de ancho, ¿cuánto mide de largo? (8 pies, porque 6 × 8 = 48.)

En casa, cinco minutos. Pídale las dos medidas de la mesa de la cocina, un libro o un tapete. Midan los lados juntos con lo que tengan, hasta con palmos. Pregunte: "¿Qué número dice cuánto cubre? ¿Cuál dice cuánto mide alrededor?" La respuesta a la primera va en unidades cuadradas; la segunda, no.
