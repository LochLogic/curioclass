## 1. Nests Need Space

[page 1]
A nest is a bird's home for its young. A bird builds a nest to hold its eggs. When the eggs hatch, the young birds stay in the nest. Their parents bring them food.
*Picture: a round nest of grass and twigs in a tree fork, with three pale eggs. Label: nest, eggs.*

[page 2]
Birds nest in many places. Some nests sit on branches. Some sit on ledges. Some birds use a cavity. A cavity is a hollow space, like a hole in a tree.
*Picture: a tree with a nest on a branch and a round hole higher up in the trunk. Labels: branch nest, cavity.*

[page 3]
Nests need space, because a nest is not safe if people come too close. Getting too close can scare the parents away. Young birds in the nest need their parents to come back.

So the best way to help a nest is to stay back and leave it alone. Look from far away. Do not touch it. Do not move it.
*Picture: two children and an adult stand far from a tree, looking up with their hands behind their backs. Label: stay back.*

[page 4]
There is another reason to leave nests alone. Most wild birds are protected by law. It is against the law to destroy a nest that has eggs or young birds in it.

If a nest is in a place that causes a problem, a child tells an adult. The adult asks a wildlife office what to do. Children never remove a nest.
*Picture: an adult on a phone, with a child beside her pointing at a small nest under a roof edge. Label: ask a wildlife office.*

[page 5]
People can also help birds that nest in cavities. Some birds, like bluebirds, use holes in trees. When there are few holes, people can build nest boxes for them.
*Picture: a small wooden box with a round hole, fixed to a fence post in an open field. A blue bird sits on top. Label: nest box.*

## 2. Windows Birds Can See

[page 1]
Glass is hard for birds to see. A person sees a window. A bird may see something else.

Glass can reflect the trees and the sky. When a bird looks at the glass, it sees the reflection. It thinks it is looking at open space. So the bird flies toward the window and hits the glass.
*Picture: a big window showing a reflection of green trees and blue sky. A small bird flies toward it. Labels: glass, reflection, bird.*

[page 2]
This happens a lot. More than one billion birds hit glass in the United States each year. Many of them are badly hurt.

Light can cause a problem too. At night, bright lights inside a building can pull birds toward the windows.
*Picture: a tall building at night with many lit windows and a few small birds flying near it. Label: lights on at night.*

[page 3]
There is something people can do. People can make windows easy to see. Marks on a window show a bird that the glass is there. The marks tell the bird, "This is not open sky."

When glass is visible, birds can fly around it. Making windows visible is a way to protect birds.
*Picture: the same window, now with small bright dots all over the glass. A bird flies past it in the sky. Labels: marks, visible.*

## 3. Marks Outside

[page 1]
Marks help birds see a window. But the marks need to be in the right place.

Put the marks on the outside of the glass. The outside is where the trees and sky are reflected. Marks on the outside break up the reflection, so the bird can see a barrier. A barrier is something that blocks the way.
*Picture: the outside of a window with colored dots on it, and a reflection of trees behind the dots. A hand-sized gap is crossed out. Labels: outside, marks, barrier.*

[page 2]
Marks need to be close together. A bird will fly through a gap if the gap looks big enough. Small gaps mean the window looks like a barrier. Big gaps look like open space.

There are many ways to make marks. People can use dots or lines. They can use tape or stickers made for windows. They can hang cords across the outside of the glass. They can put a screen outside the window. They can paint a pattern with paint that washes off.
*Picture: five small windows, each with a different kind of mark: dots, lines, tape, hanging cords and a screen. Labels under each: dots, lines, tape, cords, screen.*

[page 3]
People can also turn off lights they do not need at night. Then birds are not pulled toward the glass.

Our class will use paper windows to show how marks work. We will never put anything on real glass.
*Picture: children at a table with paper windows, small paper birds and sticker dots. Label: our paper windows.*

## 4. Feeders Need Adult Care

[page 1]
Some people put out a bird feeder. A feeder holds seed for birds to eat. Birds come to eat, and people watch.

But a feeder needs care. A feeder with old, wet seed can grow mold. Birds' droppings can drop into the seed too. Mold and droppings can carry germs that spread illness from bird to bird. Many birds eat at one feeder, so germs can spread quickly.
*Picture: a feeder with seed and four small birds on it. A little cloud with the word germs drifts over it. Labels: feeder, seed.*

[page 2]
A feeder needs to be cleaned. An adult cleans it at least every two weeks. An adult cleans it more often if the weather is wet or if many birds come.

The adult scrubs the feeder, soaks it in a cleaning mix, rinses it well and lets it dry. Only then does the feeder get fresh seed.

Cleaning a feeder is a job for an adult. Children do not clean feeders.
*Picture: an adult with rubber gloves scrubs a feeder in a large sink. Beside the sink, the feeder sits on a rack to dry. Label: adult care.*

[page 3]
A feeder can help birds, but only if someone takes care of it. No child needs to have a feeder at home. Reading about feeders helps us learn how adults protect birds.
*Picture: a child with a book open on her lap, looking out a window at a feeder far away. Label: read and watch.*

## 5. Birds Help Plants

[page 1]
Birds help plants. Birds help plants grow in new places.

Here is how. Many plants make fruit. Birds eat the fruit. The seeds are inside it.
*Picture: a holly branch with red berries and a small bird eating one. Labels: fruit, seed, bird.*

[page 2]
The seeds pass out of the bird in its droppings. A bird flies on, so the seeds land away from the plant they came from. When a seed lands in a good place, a new plant can start to grow.

Scientists who study trees say that holly plants can start from seeds in bird droppings in forests.
*Picture: a bird in flight with small seeds falling. On the ground, a tiny green holly sprout. Labels: seeds fall, new plant.*

[page 3]
Many birds have bright feathers. That is a fun fact, but it is not why birds help plants. Birds help plants because they carry seeds to new places.

Birds are helpful to plants. Plants are helpful to birds, too. Plants give birds fruit, and birds give plants a ride.
*Picture: a bird with red feathers on a branch. A big arrow labeled birds carry seeds points from one plant to another plant far away.*

## 6. Birds Help Animals

[page 1]
Birds help animals, too. Some animals need birds for food.

Crows are birds. Crows eat many foods. They eat fruit and grain. They eat insects such as grasshoppers and caterpillars.
*Picture: a black crow standing on a fence eating a berry, with a grasshopper on a leaf nearby. Labels: crow, insect.*

[page 2]
Other animals eat crows and their eggs. Raccoons, opossums and snakes eat crow eggs. Hawks and owls eat crows.

That means crows are food for other animals. Birds are part of a chain. They eat some animals, and other animals eat them.
*Picture: a drawing of a chain: a grasshopper, a crow, a hawk, with arrows pointing from each one to the next. Labels: grasshopper, crow, hawk.*

[page 3]
Animals need birds because birds give them food. Hunters, such as hawks and owls, need birds to eat. If a hunter has no food, it is hungry.

Birds help plants. Birds help animals. People who protect birds help the animals and plants that need them, too.
*Picture: a hawk on a branch, a raccoon on the ground, a small crow between them. Label: animals that need birds.*
