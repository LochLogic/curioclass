# Section 2.8 opener: a day-and-night poem, a plan and a shared poem

## What the section does

Days 63 to 67 read two sky poems and notice how a poem tells events in time order. The class builds the narrative-arc chart, analyses a model for ordered events, time words and an ending, plans and drafts a joint moon poem, gives kind and specific feedback, and revises the shared poem on paper and on a shared device. Facts about the moon stay true; the moon's feelings are imagined.

## How the days run

Each day is 120 minutes in two blocks of 60. The topic block holds the reading, the talk and the work; the foundations block holds oral sound work, letters and sounds, a rotation of small groups and a short record. Every exit is spoken to you: you read the card aloud, the child points, sorts, draws or speaks, and you hear the day's six children. A child outside the six is "not yet heard", never "missed". A child may pass at any time. On days 66 and 67 the class keeps the writing-process chart and the eight-box sentence sheet in view.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A poem can tell events in order.** First, next, then and at last say the order; an ending says how it comes to rest.
- **Imagined and true are different.** The moon waves in a poem; the moon shining in the dark sky is a fact.
- **Feedback helps when it is kind and specific.** A good note adds a detail the writer can use; a note that changes the topic does not.
- **Four kinds of sentence.** A statement, a question, a command and an exclamation, simple and joined with "and".

## The errors, in plain words

- **A:** events in the wrong order.
- **E:** a claim the page does not make.
- **F:** a list of facts where events should be told.
- **H:** a fragment where a sentence is needed.
- **P:** a change of topic where the writer should add a requested detail.
- **T:** watching on the device without entering anything.

## Marking

One scale holds all year: shown, shown with help, not yet seen. Mark on the record sheet and keep the sheet in your folder.
