# Section 3.1: Same amount, different parts (days 10 to 12)

## The math, for the adult

Standard 4.NF.A.1: "Explain why a fraction a/b is equivalent to a fraction (n × a)/(n × b) by using visual
fraction models, with attention to how the number and size of the parts differ even though the two fractions
themselves are the same size. Use this principle to recognize and generate equivalent fractions." This section is
the visual half: students explain with strips and number lines, and recognize and generate equal fractions by
cutting parts. The written rule, and finding a name with no drawing, are section 3.2's.

**One amount, many names.** Grade 3 met a few equal pairs, such as 1/2 = 2/4 (3.NF.A.3b). Now students see why
there are always more names. Shade 1/3 of a strip and cut every third into 2 equal pieces. The whole now has 6 pieces, so each is a sixth;
the shaded part has 2 of them. Nothing was added or taken away, so 1/3 = 2/6.

**Why both numbers change by the same factor.** When every part is cut into n, the whole has n times as many
parts, and so does the shaded piece. Each part is one nth the size it was. More parts, each smaller: the length
does not change. That is the explanation the standard asks for. Never call it "multiplying by 1": that needs a
fraction times a fraction, grade 5.

**On the number line a fraction is a point.** A fraction is a distance from 0, with the whole as the length from
0 to 1 (3.NF.A.2). Cutting each fifth into 2 puts new marks between the old ones, but the point at 2/5 does not
move: it gets a second name, 4/10. Fractions past 1 behave the same way, and day 11 does all of that work: 5/4 is
10/8.

**Cuts that cannot be folded.** Cutting each part into 3, 5 or 10 cannot be folded, so on day 12 students mark
these cuts on printed lines by hand, and decide whether two fractions are the same amount. Some are not: Jo's 3/4 =
5/6 (she added 2 to both) gives two different points on one line.

**Limits.** Denominators 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 12. No hundredths, no simplest form, no written rule.

## The strip and the line

- **Day 10.** Paper strips, folded: halves into fourths and eighths, thirds into sixths. Each fold is written as
  an equation with its sentence: "each part was cut into 2, so 1/3 = 2/6."
- **Day 11.** A strip laid on a printed line from 0: its end is the point. The object is tied to the drawing.
- **Day 12.** Strips only to check a claim; cuts into 3, 5 and 10 marked on printed lines. The exit has no strips.

The fraction strips sheet (strip-sheet.md): nine labelled strips, one whole to twelfths, all one length, on page
1; ten blank strips to fold on page 2. Print both pages one-sided, one set a student, for day 10; the cut strips
stay in an envelope all unit. The number lines sheet (number-lines.md), lines 1 to 12: on side 1, lines 1 to 3 blank from 0 to 1
and lines 4 to 6 from 0 to 2 in halves, fifths and fourths. Print it both sides, one a student, for day 11 only.

## Why this model

A folded strip shows "same amount" in the hand: the shaded length is plainly the same before and after. The number
line holds fractions past 1 and cuts that cannot be folded, and it is where fractions live from here on. Laying the
strip on the line carries the one to the other. No tool fits: Fraction Wall Duel (spec only) splits parts inside a
comparing game on a wall that stops at 1, and the planned number line splitter does not exist yet. The section
runs on print.

## The errors to expect, in plain words

| | What students do | What it shows | What to do when many do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Change one number only: 1/3 = 1/6 | They do not recount the shaded parts after the cut | Fold thirds into sixths again; count the shaded parts aloud |
| B | Add the same number to both: 3/4 = 5/6 | They take the pattern for adding | Day 13 opens with it; mark both on one line: adding moves the point |
| C | Count tick marks, not lengths, as when 0 is counted as "one" | A fraction is not yet a distance from 0 | Lay the strip on the line from 0 again |
| D | Think bigger numbers make a bigger fraction: 4/10 is more than 2/5 | They read the numerals as whole numbers | Two strips side by side: more parts, each smaller |
| K | Take the line from 0 to 2 as the whole: 5/4 named 5/8 | The whole is not yet 0 to 1 | Cover the line past 1, count the parts in one whole, then count on past 1 |

Wrong examples: A on day 10, C and K on day 11, B (Jo's claim) and D (Kai's claim, a talk) on day 12. Each day's
teacher page names the wrong answer that signals each error on its exit.

## The reteach (readiness items 1 to 4)

The readiness check (readiness.md) is given on day 9 and marked before day 10. If more than a third of the class missed an item, day 11's warm-up and first five
minutes are its reteach for the whole class. Otherwise run it with the students who missed it during day 11's
warm-up, and the five minutes go to day 10's exit.

1. **Items 1 and 4, fractions on a line, past 1.** Lay a one-fourth strip on a line from 0, again and again,
   counting "one fourth, two fourths ..." to "five fourths". Ask where four fourths landed (on 1). Then count whole
   steps to 3 and write 3 as 3/1.
2. **Item 2, the same amount in fourths.** Fold a strip in half, shade one half, fold again, and count the shaded
   fourths: 1/2 = 2/4.
3. **Item 3, which unit fraction is larger.** Lay the thirds strip beside the eighths strip. More parts, each
   smaller: one third is longer than one eighth. Day 19 asks this again with new numbers.

One more readiness question, half of a small pizza against half of a large one, is asked aloud on day 17.

## Supplies for the section

- Per student: the fraction strips sheet (day 10), scissors, an envelope; the number lines sheet and a ruler (day
  11 only); a pencil.
- Per class: the projector for the display pages; a board; three long paper strips (day 10). No document camera:
  fold a long strip at the front, then draw each fold on the board.
- A room with nothing: strips cut from scrap paper, all one length, and lines ruled by hand.

## Words

Equivalent, numerator, denominator, whole, cut, each on a labelled strip on the wall. Read fractions "three
fourths", not "three over four". Frame, to take or leave: "Each part was cut into __, so there are __ times as
many parts and each part is smaller. The length is the same, so __ = __."

## How the unit is checked

Readiness check, day 9 (readiness.md); exits on days 10 to 23, on paper; day 12's written task, judged with
look-fors on its key; the end-of-unit assessment on day 24, where items 3.1a and 3.1b are this section's. All of it
goes on one record sheet for the unit (record.md), by error letter. The family note (family.md) goes home on day 10.
