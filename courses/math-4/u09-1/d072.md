# Day 72: What is the small 1 worth?

## Teacher page

### Before class

Print the student page both sides, the column sheets, the exit tickets, the key and the family note. Put the place name cards over
the board, ones on the right. Put the display page on the projector. Have the marked readiness items 1 and 2
from day 71 beside you: list who missed item 1 (adding within 1,000) and who missed item 2 (writing a number in
digits). Seat the item 1 list where you can reach them in the pairs part.

### The day

#### 5 min. Warm-up: within 1,000, and times tables

Alone, side 1, then whole class: [[s09-P72-11]], [[s09-P72-12v2]], [[s09-P72-13]], [[s09-P72-14]]. Read the answers;
students fix in pen.

Check: the four answers are on the screen.

**Readiness items 1 and 2:** nothing moves today. Keep both lists: day 73 opens with their reteach.

#### 15 min. Teaching first: two ways, side by side

Whole class. Students fill the two frames on side 1 as you go, not ahead of you. Write expanded form on the left of the board, the columns on the right, under the place name
cards.

**Example 1** ([[s09-K72-1]]).
[[steps:s09-K72-1]]

Point from the 14 in the expanded form to the 4 and the small 1 in the columns. Say it plainly: the standard
algorithm is expanded form written short. Each column is one place, and ten of a place are written as one of
the next place, in the next column.

**Example 2** ([[s09-K72-2]]). The class says each step with you. At every small 1, ask the room "One what?"
before you show the next screen.
[[steps:s09-K72-2]]

#### 10 min. Pairs: both ways

Pairs, side 2 ([[s09-P72-1]], [[s09-P72-2]]). One partner writes expanded form, the other the columns. They
swap for the second sum. Then they compare sums.

Check: Do both ways give the same sum? Is each small 1 named?

Walk with one question: "Point to that small 1. One what?" Go first to the item 1 list. If a pair's two sums
differ, do [[s09-P72-1]] with them in expanded form, each carry said by its value. Watch for a sum that differs by exactly 10 or 100
(error A: a carry lost); the expanded form side shows the ten that went missing. Watch for a column holding
two digits, as 13 under the ones (error D): ask how many digits one place can hold. If many pairs get different
sums, stop and do [[s09-P72-1]] together, both ways.

#### 15 min. Columns alone: name every small 1

Alone, on the column sheet, side 2 ([[s09-P72-3]], [[s09-P72-4]], [[s09-P72-5]], [[s09-P72-6]], [[s09-P72-7]]).
The algorithm only now. Beside each sum on the student page, students write what each small 1 is worth: "1 ten,
1 thousand". The last three are five-digit sums: the ten thousands column works the same way. Items 10 and 11
are for fast finishers.

At 10 minutes, students check the sums they have done against the screen and fix in pen.

Check: Is your sum the same as the screen? Did you name every small 1?

Watch for "a 1" written as the worth (error C). Ask "One what?" and point to the column it sits over. If many
write "a 1", the class names the small 1s of [[s09-P72-3]] aloud together before going on.

#### 5 min. Fix it: Kai and Mia

Side 2 ([[s09-F72-1]], [[s09-F72-2]]). One minute alone on each, then the class: the display shows each wrong working beside the right one. Kai wrote the 4 but lost the ten: 14 ones is 1 ten and 4 ones. Mia wrote 14
and 12 whole: one place holds one digit, so 14 ones is written as 4, and the ten moves on.

#### 5 min. Pull it together

Whole class, at Example 1 on the board.

**Ask.** "Where are the 14 ones in the standard algorithm?" Expect, in this order: "the 4 under the ones"; "the
small 1 over the tens is the ten"; "14 ones is 1 ten and 4 ones, so it is split between two columns". Show the
first answer, then point to the small 1 until someone gives the second. Then ask the same of the 12 hundreds.

#### 5 min. Exit ticket

Alone, on paper ([[s09-X72v2]]). Read it aloud once if a student asks. Collect at the door; write results on the
record sheet under E72.

### Other rooms

- **No printer.** The warm-up and the sums on the board, one set at a time; columns on lined paper turned
  sideways.

### At home

**Swap.** No projector: point at side 1. No column sheet: lined paper turned sideways, so the lines make
columns.

**Skip.** The warm-up, items 6, 10 and 11, and the challenge.

- **12 min. Two ways.** Read [[s09-K72-1]] together: expanded form (3,000 + 700 + 40 + 6) on the left, columns on
  the right. The child fills the frame on side 1. Then [[s09-K72-2]].

    **Say.** Point at a small 1: "One what?" Expect: one ten (or hundred, or thousand), never "a 1".

- **8 min. Both ways.** [[s09-P72-1]]: you write expanded form, the child writes columns. Compare the sums.
- **8 min. Columns alone.** [[s09-P72-3]], [[s09-P72-4]], [[s09-P72-5]]. Beside each, the child writes what
  every small 1 is worth.
- **5 min. Fix it.** [[s09-F72-1]], then [[s09-F72-2]].

    **Say.** "Kai got 6,274. Where did the ten from 14 ones go?" Expect: he lost it; it goes over the tens,
    so 6,284.

- **5 min. Exit ticket.** The child answers [[s09-X72v2]] alone.

### Shorter or longer

Shorter: in the columns part, do [[s09-P72-3]] to [[s09-P72-5]] only. Never drop naming the small 1s, Mia's fix
or the pairs part. Longer: the challenge ([[s09-C72-1]]), on grid paper: three numbers make a column of 21, so
the small number is a 2. Ask what it is worth.

### Words and supports

Word Wall cards: sum, regroup (families may say "carry"; both are welcome), expanded form, standard algorithm, and the place names. A student who cannot write small digits may type columns or say each small 1
aloud. Frames on the display: "__ ones is __ ten and __ ones." "__ tens is 1 __ and __ tens."

### Tomorrow

On the ticket, 7,334 or 6,434 (error A: one carry lost) or 6,334 (A: both lost): day 73 shows the fix 3,746 +
2,538 = 6,274 again, each carry said by its value. "613134" (error D: a two-digit sum in one column): day 73
shows the fix 3,746 + 2,538 = 512714 again. "A 1" or "one ten" (error C: named as a digit, or by the column it
came from): students name each small 1 aloud all of day 73. Then day 73 teaches lining up numbers of different lengths, with an estimate first.
