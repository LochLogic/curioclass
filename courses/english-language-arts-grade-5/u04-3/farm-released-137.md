# The Last Irrigation

Upstream, the gate attendant let the water go. We heard a clatter somewhere beyond the lane, then a sound like dry peas rolling around in a tin. A narrow rush curled around the pale sandbag. Vale put one hand on the wheel and watched Dad at the first row. When he raised his thumb, she opened our gate.

From her stool on the opposite bank, Aunt Jo bent down and pointed. I could see her hand. What it pointed to was hidden from where I stood.

## Part 2. What the bend hid

“Come round here a minute,” Aunt Jo said.

I crossed on the plank and crouched beside her. From that side the ditch looked less like a straight trench. A shallow bend ran back toward us, and there, where the earth had crumbled, water was slipping out across a patch of bare ground. From my side, a ledge of dirt had hidden the spill. I could see the leaf again. It had fetched up against a pebble at the edge of the spreading puddle.

I wanted to say that somebody should have noticed it yesterday. I had walked past that bend myself carrying a crate, and that stopped me.

Dad and Aunt Jo reset the old sandbag across the opening. Vale brought two flat boards from the shed, and the adults braced the bag with them. I went to the first row to watch what changed. The flow there grew wide enough to wash over the toe of Dad's boot. In the ditch behind him, the leaf broke loose and swept around the bend.

“Now can we do the peppers?” I asked.

“We can do some of them.” Dad asked me to come back to the crate and look at the map again.

He had penciled a small box around the young trees. They had gone into the ground three springs ago. One had needed a new stake after a windstorm. We had been waiting for the first fruit for so long that I had almost stopped checking for it. If we carried them through the dry year, they would still be there when it was time to start over. We could plant peppers again from seed.

It was an excellent reason. I wished it had been a bad one so I could argue.

“How many rows could we do if we put the trees first?” I asked.

Vale studied the ditch. “Two short ones, maybe, if the flow holds.”

I picked the two short rows at the near end. They had fewer plants, and we could see when the water reached the last one. Dad looked at where I'd put my finger. Then he drew two little circles instead of crossing out the rest of the field. I liked that. I could look at circles without having to read the other marks.

At the end of the tree row, Vale called up that the water had reached the last trunk. Dad closed that opening, then sent water to the first row of peppers. I stayed at its far end. The stream took a long time to reach me. Close up I could see it splitting around every pebble, gathering again, making a dark edge in the dirt. The first time it touched the last plant I waved too soon. A little tongue of water ran forward and curled back. The second time, the dark edge spread all the way around the stem and stayed.

“Here!” I shouted.

We watched the second row the same way. Dad went back to close the opening. There was time left when the water reached the last plant, though less than it had taken to finish the row. I looked toward the long rows. I could see the peppers nearest the ditch. If I lifted a board, those plants would get a little. The ones farther down would go on waiting.

Across the plank, Aunt Jo had folded her stools. She was looking downstream. This time I could stand on her side and see what she saw. The narrow ditch twisted away around a stand of trees, and a little farther along, where the ground was open, I could see the top of the box where she kept her gate tools.

I asked Dad if we could send the extra water down now. He checked the time and called the attendant. Then he called Aunt Jo. She went on ahead, and Vale and I took the calendar back below the shed where we could watch the water pass our last opening.

In the clear August square I wrote TREES and TWO ROWS. The calendar had room for three more letters along the bottom. I wrote JO. Then I had to turn the pencil sideways to squeeze in an arrow. It pointed off the edge of the square.
