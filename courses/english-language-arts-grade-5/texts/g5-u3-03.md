# Opening a Closed League

## Reading notes for the teacher

Curio Class wrote this account in 2026 from the records of April 15, 1947. Its focus is the game and the changes visible on the field that day. For day 101, use the separate cold accounts and arguments in the unit plan.

## An out at first

Boston's Dick Culler grounded the first ball in play toward third base. Spider Jorgensen caught it and threw across the infield. Jackie Robinson waited at first and received the ball before Culler reached the bag. It was an ordinary way to get the first out of a baseball game. The person who completed it was new to this league. On April 15, 1947, when Brooklyn opened its season against the Boston Braves at Ebbets Field, Robinson became the first Black player in the modern National League.

Brooklyn's decision to put him in its lineup explains why that first out mattered. Clubs in the National and American leagues had long kept Black players off their teams. Robinson had already played professional baseball for the Kansas City Monarchs in the Negro Leagues and the Montreal Royals in the minor leagues. At Ebbets Field, Brooklyn gave him regular work at first base in the National League. A ground ball produced the first visible evidence of how he would work with the men around him.

## A run without a hit

Robinson batted second. Boston pitcher Johnny Sain got him out on a ground ball in his first trip to the plate. Robinson went hitless in the game. Read only the hits column, and a reader might think that he could have spent the afternoon on the bench without changing the score. The seventh inning explains why the column tells only part of the story.

Brooklyn second baseman Eddie Stanky started that inning with a walk. Robinson put down a bunt, tapping the ball along the ground to advance his teammate. The throw from the defense went astray and both men reached safely. The scorer gave Robinson credit for a sacrifice bunt. A sacrifice can move a teammate forward even when the batter expects to make an out. In this case it also left two runners on base.

Pete Reiser was next. He doubled, allowing Stanky and Robinson to score. Brooklyn went on to win 5 to 3. Robinson had scored without a hit of his own. He had other people to work with on both sides of the result. The walk gave him a runner to advance. The throw from the Boston fielder affected whether he could stay on base. Reiser's hit helped him finish the trip around it.

Before the inning began, Boston led 3 to 2. After Reiser's hit, Brooklyn led 4 to 3. One more Brooklyn runner came home that inning. A recap that gives only the final score can tell us the margin of victory. Beginning with the walk shows why a game with a two-run margin could turn on the path of a soft ground ball and the time it took the defense to get it to first.

## What the game record can tell us

The scorebook that sportswriter Tom Meany kept that afternoon tracks Robinson through his turns at bat and around the bases. Its marks help settle the chronology, or order of events. Meany was there, so his scorebook is a firsthand record. A secondhand account like this one uses records from others to describe what happened. The box score, an official summary of the game, can corroborate the scorebook by confirming who scored and how the game ended. Each source has a limited view of what happened after the game.

One conclusion fits the evidence from April 15 alone. Brooklyn had put its change in hiring into practice in a regular game. Robinson made the first out with a teammate and contributed to a rally with two others. The record lets a reader see how a historic decision became part of the work of a team. It also leaves an important question open: would this change reach beyond one team and one day?
