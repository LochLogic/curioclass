# The Last Irrigation

## Part 1. The blank square

On the morning of our last irrigation turn, I carried the kitchen calendar outside. June had a brown thumbprint on it. July had two dates circled in blue and crossed out in red. Only the August square was clean. I wanted to see water in the ditch before I put anything in that square.

My sister Vale was standing on the plank above the gate. Below her, the ditch turned around a pale lump that I took for a stone. It was our old sandbag, bleached almost white. I used to have to lean out over the plank to see it through the water.

“Looks like we get an hour,” Vale said.

An hour had sounded generous on the phone the evening before. It sounded different with the whole field in front of me. The pepper plants closest to the shed were bent at the tips. The little trees above them still looked sturdy from a distance, but up close their leaves felt as rough as paper.

I drew a line around the field with my finger. “If we hurry, we can get some to everything.”

“Tell me how it'll get to the far end.”

I pointed at the ditch. Vale waited. She knew the answer I was giving her was a place, and she had asked for a way.

Back at the shed, Dad had the farm map spread over an empty crate. When the water came, he explained, it would run along the head ditch past each row. At each opening, we could send it down a row or leave it in the ditch to go farther. On a good day we opened several rows together. This time he wanted to know how far a single small stream would travel before we opened another.

“Hang tight till we see what we've got,” he said. He drew a finger down the map, where thin black lines stood for rows of peppers. I pictured him passing over each plant in turn, the way a person walks past seats that are already taken.

The peppers were mine in a way the young trees had never been. In February I had written the names on the seed trays. One whole tray said HOT in block letters because I had confused the packets and did not know which plants were which. At the Saturday stand, I used to let customers choose the glossiest peppers. This summer I had started turning the good sides of the smallest ones toward the front.

“Have we got a chance if the water doesn't come?” I asked.

Dad rolled up the map. “We've got some choices either way.”

That was the kind of answer that made me want to ask the question again, louder. I wanted a chance I could point to.

Aunt Jo came over from the next farm carrying two folding stools. Since June, we had waved across the ditch more often than we had walked across it. I had started thinking of the ditch as a zipper somebody had pulled shut between our places. Now she set one stool on each side of the plank and sat down opposite me.

“You all waiting for word too?” Aunt Jo called to Vale.

“Ask Ada. She's got the calendar.” Vale pointed at me.

“We've got the hour before yours,” I said. I heard the edge in my voice after it was too late to take it back. I thought she had come to make sure we would leave on time.

“Good. Can you show me where it backs up?” She nodded at the head ditch. “I can see the bend from over here. I can't see your end from my field.”

Upstream, the gate attendant let the water go. We heard a clatter somewhere beyond the lane, then a sound like dry peas rolling around in a tin. A narrow rush curled around the pale sandbag. Vale put one hand on the wheel and watched Dad at the first row. When he raised his thumb, she opened our gate.

Water slid into the head ditch. A leaf that had been lying on its side stood up, tipped over and sailed a little way. I followed it at a walk. Then the leaf stopped.

I knew how those peppers looked from the market side of the stand. Nobody there could see the dry roots through the crate. Watching that leaf, I imagined putting a green cloth over a row of empty boxes. My fear was a weight I kept shifting from one hand to the other. I tucked the calendar under my arm, left it there for two steps, and nearly dropped it.

Dad called from the first opening. “Let's see what it does here.”

I reached for the board at the second row. It was already loose. We could open another and give it a head start. I had lifted it an inch when Vale called my name. The stream at Dad's boots went thin as a shoelace.

I put the board back.

From her stool on the opposite bank, Aunt Jo bent down and pointed. I could see her hand. What it pointed to was hidden from where I stood.

## Part 2. What the bend hid

“Come round here a minute,” Aunt Jo said.

I crossed on the plank and crouched beside her. From that side the ditch looked less like a straight trench. A shallow bend ran back toward us, and there, where the earth had crumbled, water was slipping out across a patch of bare ground. From my side, a ledge of dirt had hidden the spill. I could see the leaf again. It had fetched up against a pebble at the edge of the spreading puddle.

I wanted to say that somebody should have noticed it yesterday. I had walked past that bend myself carrying a crate, and that stopped me.

Dad and Aunt Jo reset the old sandbag across the opening. Vale brought two flat boards from the shed, and the adults braced the bag with them. I went to the first row to watch what changed. The flow there grew wide enough to wash over the toe of Dad's boot. In the ditch behind him, the leaf broke loose and swept around the bend.

“Now can we do the peppers?” I asked.

“We can do some of them.” Dad asked me to come back to the crate and look at the map again.

He had penciled a small box around the young trees. They had gone into the ground three springs ago. One had needed a new stake after a windstorm. We had been waiting for the first fruit for so long that I had almost stopped checking for it. If we carried them through the dry year, they would still be there when it was time to start over. We could plant peppers again from seed.

It was an excellent reason. I wished it had been a bad one so I could argue.

“How many rows could we do if we put the trees first?” I asked.

Vale studied the ditch. “Two short ones, maybe, if the flow holds.”

I picked the two short rows at the near end. They had fewer plants, and we could see when the water reached the last one. Dad looked at where I'd put my finger. Then he drew two little circles instead of crossing out the rest of the field. I liked that. I could look at circles without having to read the other marks.

At the end of the tree row, Vale called up that the water had reached the last trunk. Dad closed that opening, then sent water to the first row of peppers. I stayed at its far end. The stream took a long time to reach me. Close up I could see it splitting around every pebble, gathering again, making a dark edge in the dirt. The first time it touched the last plant I waved too soon. A little tongue of water ran forward and curled back. The second time, the dark edge spread all the way around the stem and stayed.

“Here!” I shouted.

We watched the second row the same way. Dad went back to close the opening. There was time left when the water reached the last plant, though less than it had taken to finish the row. I looked toward the long rows. I could see the peppers nearest the ditch. If I lifted a board, those plants would get a little. The ones farther down would go on waiting.

Across the plank, Aunt Jo had folded her stools. She was looking downstream. This time I could stand on her side and see what she saw. The narrow ditch twisted away around a stand of trees, and a little farther along, where the ground was open, I could see the top of the box where she kept her gate tools.

I asked Dad if we could send the extra water down now. He checked the time and called the attendant. Then he called Aunt Jo. She went on ahead, and Vale and I took the calendar back below the shed where we could watch the water pass our last opening.

In the clear August square I wrote TREES and TWO ROWS. The calendar had room for three more letters along the bottom. I wrote JO. Then I had to turn the pencil sideways to squeeze in an arrow. It pointed off the edge of the square.

The dry year lasted into fall. We took smaller boxes to the stand, and we put them all on one shelf. One Saturday, Aunt Jo set her empty crate beside ours while she helped us fold the canvas roof. I brought out the bag of peppers I had saved for her and put it in the crate.

The next spring Dad drew another map. The young trees were on it, in the old place. The peppers took up two short blocks closer to the ditch. Before we took the map out to the shed, I asked him to add the bend, clear around to the other side. There was just enough room.
