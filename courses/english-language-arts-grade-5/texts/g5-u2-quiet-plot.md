# Recording a Quiet Plot

*Invented for practice. The class, plot and drawings are fictional.*

## Two views from one roof

The sign inside the shelter read “El Yunque, Puerto Rico.” From the bench, I could see two small patches of ground beyond the railing. The guide, Sra. Ríos, had brought us there to watch both patches from one place. Our class wanted to find out why the leaves in the two patches looked different. On our first visit, the leaves on the right looked brighter. Mine was the job of sketching what grew under them.

I drew the right patch first. A broad leaf on that side stood in a bright shape like a folded napkin. The patch on the left looked gray by comparison. Only after I started drawing did I notice how many leaves I could see there. Their edges overlapped, with some of the smaller ones half hidden. I left room on my page to count them later.

“Now look higher,” Sra. Ríos said.

Above the right patch, two treetops opened onto a thin strip of sky. On the left, their branches overlapped from where we sat. We waited while a cloud moved past. For a little while, the bright shape faded on the right. When the cloud went on, the bright shape came back to the same broad leaf. The left patch stayed in shade both times.

I wanted to label my sketch “The Bright Patch Grows Better.” Sra. Ríos asked which part of that sentence I could see. I could point to the bright patch and show where the light came through. I couldn't point to any leaf that had grown while I was looking.

Before we left, I held both sketches on my knees and tried to point out the patches to Iris. She could follow my finger when I was beside her. Our drawings needed a way to do the pointing after we left.

## The next marks

We had planned two more short visits. We would sit under the same roof and sketch the same leaves each time. From the bench, the right patch lined up with a railing post. Sra. Ríos drew that post on the corner of each record sheet. For the left patch, she drew two branches that crossed above it. When I held up the sheet, I could find both places. The records would show which patch we meant even if the light looked different later.

On our second visit, both patches looked dark when we sat down. I looked at the bright shape on my first drawing. Now I could cross out “bright” and call it “dark.” My partner Iris pointed to the post where it belonged on the drawing. We left the name as “right patch” and put the lighting in a column of its own. A few minutes later, light passed through the strip of sky and fell on the same broad leaf. Both conditions belonged in the column. The post helped us keep the place steady while the light changed.

This time Iris noticed a folded bit of green beside the broad leaf. I compared my first sketch to the plant. Either the folded leaf was new or I'd missed it the first time. We marked it on both of our new sheets. Sra. Ríos had us put a separate mark beside our question. The mark kept the question with the drawing where it belonged.

Iris wanted a closer look, so our guide lent us a small pair of binoculars. I could see the fold from the bench. Iris drew its shape beside the date and handed the binoculars back.

## What we could show

At our last visit, the bit of green had opened enough to show its pointed tip. I drew the short vein down its center. By then we could put two dates beside the change. On the left, we could follow another leaf that had become easier to see. The plant in front of it had shifted enough to leave its edge in view. Seeing more of that leaf gave us little reason to say it had grown.

Back in class, Iris and I spread all three drawings in a row. A person reading from left to right could follow what we saw on each date. Then Iris set the right and left drawings from the second visit beside each other. That arrangement helped us compare spots viewed at the same time. We shaded the places where leaves above the patches blocked the light. We left a clear space for the path of the light through the opening.

We gave the drawings a title, “Three Visits from One Bench.” Below it we put two statements we could explain. The opening above the right patch let light reach the broad leaf when the sky was clear. Watching from the same place helped us see the folded leaf change between the last two visits. Farther down, we wrote a question. Would that plant change in the same way in a darker patch? To answer it, we would need more than the drawings we already had.

On the next school day another pair used our record sheets to read the drawings. They found our first two sight marks without having to ask us about them. One reader put a finger beside the two dates on the right. She could tell which leaf had opened. Then she moved her finger to our question and asked what we would have to keep the same on a new visit. I went to the drawings to look for a place to start.
