# An Argument Against Discrimination

## Reading notes for the teacher

Read “The hearing and its two points” on days 97 and 98. Hold “Why the examples matter” for day 99. The wording is Curio Class’s. On day 101 use the separate cold assessment passage and spoken argument in the unit plan.

## The hearing and its two points

In 1949, Jackie Robinson spoke at a congressional hearing during the Cold War, a period of rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union when some U.S. leaders suspected Communist influence behind protests. His testimony challenged attempts to dismiss protests against racial discrimination as merely communist influence. The underlying injustice, he argued, mattered regardless of who objected to it.

Our explanation draws two connected points from that argument. First, public understanding requires taking Black Americans' opposition to discrimination seriously. Their own experience gives them reasons to resist unfair treatment, regardless of who else speaks against it. Second, defending democracy requires opposing discrimination beyond baseball. Robinson identified unequal treatment in military service, transport and employment. These examples show that equal opportunity concerns several parts of daily life. The first point explains why protests deserve attention. The second connects democratic principles to specific injustices.

## Why the examples matter

Leaving discrimination unchallenged allows it to shape a person's choices in more than one place. Take transportation. A person might have a ticket for a train and still be directed to a separate car because of race. Robinson's example matters because buying the same kind of service does not give people equal treatment when they try to use it. His employment example makes a related point. If an employer refuses to consider people because of their race, religion, or birthplace, those limits affect who can earn a living there. These examples make a general claim about equal opportunity specific.

Robinson also drew on something he knew as a ballplayer. At the hearing, he reported that there were only seven Black players among about four hundred major league players. This was a count he gave in July 1949, partway through the season. It pointed out a limit that his own presence could easily hide. Listeners could admire his chance to play and still have a reason to ask whether the same chance had reached many others.
