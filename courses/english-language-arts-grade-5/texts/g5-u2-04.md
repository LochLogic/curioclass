# From the Crown to the Ground

Stand in a tropical rainforest and look up. You might see one tree crown rising above the others. The scattered crowns of exceptionally tall trees form the emergent layer. They reach into stronger light and wind than much of the forest below.

Lower your eyes a little and look for the main spread of tree crowns. This is the canopy. Where neighboring crowns spread over one another, their leaves intercept much of the light. When a branch falls or a tree loses some of its leaves, direct light can pass through the new opening. A map drawn from above would show a cover with thinner places and openings.

Below those crowns, shorter trees and other plants grow in the understory. Light here can vary across a distance as short as the space between two trunks. One plant may grow beneath a dense crown, while a nearby plant gets more light through a gap. As living branches spread into a gap, the patch of bright light underneath can shrink.

The forest floor is the level of roots, soil and fallen material. A leaf that grew in the canopy can land there. Fungi and bacteria break down dead leaves and other material. Some nutrients that were part of that leaf can enter the soil and become available to living plants.

The layer names tell where things are. To understand what the layers do, follow something moving across them. Direct sunlight passes through an opening and changes the conditions below. A leaf falls and carries matter from a crown to the ground. Both changes begin above and affect something lower in the forest.
