# Four Views of the River, 2022

These four accounts describe the Colorado River drought as it was discussed in 2022. Each is a summary of a named source.

## Colorado River Indian Tribes

*Tribal scoping letter, December 29, 2022, pages 1 and 2. Chairwoman Amelia Flores, Senate Committee on Indian Affairs, March 23, 2022, pages 20 and 21.*

The Colorado River Indian Tribes described the river as central to the lives and culture of their community. Their letter asked federal planners to respect the nation's distinct water rights, which had been established by court decree. It also sought enough flow to sustain people, plants and animals along the river. Chairwoman Amelia Flores told senators that the nation had already left water in Lake Mead by choosing to keep some productive farmland out of use. She sought greater authority for the nation to decide how to use or share its water. The December letter used the nation's past conservation to show how voluntary agreements could help and asked for wider participation.

## A farmer and rancher

*Patrick O'Toole, farmer and rancher and president of the Family Farm Alliance, Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources, June 14, 2022, pages 27 and 28.*

O'Toole emphasized how hard it was for farming and ranching families to plan their future when their supply was uncertain. From his perspective, preserving farms also protected food production and the rural communities that depended on it. He pointed to a reservoir that had helped his own valley in earlier years. He thought storage would matter even more during the Colorado River drought. That experience shaped his argument for improving storage and maintaining the equipment and structures that deliver water to farms.

## A city water manager

*John J. Entsminger, general manager of the Southern Nevada Water Authority, Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources, June 14, 2022, pages 15 and 16.*

Entsminger emphasized what cities could do to stretch their supply. He reported that southern Nevada was using less water than it had around the turn of the century, even though its population had grown. He pointed to watering schedules, rules against wasting water and programs that helped people replace grass with plants watered by drip irrigation. Those examples supported his point that changing outdoor use can make a difference. He also described how his area recovered indoor water for return to Lake Mead. However, he said cities could address only part of the river's shortage.

## A federal researcher

*Charlie Stern, specialist in natural resources policy at the Congressional Research Service, Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources, June 14, 2022, pages 53 and 54.*

Stern placed the Colorado River drought within a dry period across the West. Records showed that two years ending in spring 2022 had been among the driest recorded across several western states. He explained that state, local and tribal governments led many drought responses and could seek federal help. He described different kinds of responses, including watching for drought through better monitoring, planning before a shortage and finding ways to increase supply or reduce demand. His account gives lawmakers a way to compare responses across communities.

## Words for comparing the accounts

An **allocation** is a share set aside for a particular user or purpose. A **tributary** is a stream or river that flows into a larger one. **Conservation** means protecting a resource or using it carefully. An **agreement** sets out what the people or governments involved have agreed to do. A tribal **nation** has its own government. Its account speaks for that nation in the named documents.
