# Looking Back at 1947

## Reading notes for the teacher

Curio Class wrote this account in 2026 using records extending beyond the 1947 debut. Its focus is the pace and reach of integration across teams. For day 101, use the separate cold accounts and arguments in the unit plan.

## The same day from farther away

Start a timeline on April 15, 1947. Jackie Robinson took first base for Brooklyn at Ebbets Field. On the first play he caught Spider Jorgensen's throw to retire a Boston batter. Later he put down a bunt and reached base on a throwing error. Brooklyn beat the Boston Braves 5 to 3. The order of the plays is one way to tell what happened. From years later, a different detail stands out. Brooklyn made a place for a Black player in a regular National League game after its league had long excluded Black players.

The change was real and had a boundary. A roster lists players attached to a team. Robinson's place on Brooklyn's roster gave him a chance to make the plays recorded that day. His name could appear again when the club posted the next lineup. For a player waiting for a call from another city, the relevant decision still rested with the people in charge of that team.

## Follow the names across teams

In July 1947, Larry Doby joined Cleveland in the American League. Like Robinson, he came with experience in the Negro Leagues. Doby was a Newark Eagles player before he took the new job. Robinson's April game put a Black player on a National League team. Doby's July debut put one on an American League team. He had to build his own relationships with new coaches and teammates as Cleveland decided how to use him.

Doby began with one turn at bat. On July 5 in Chicago, Cleveland sent him in to bat for a pitcher in the seventh inning. He struck out. A timeline keeps the importance of that result in proportion. In the game, one batter made an out. In the history of the American League, the team had given him a turn it had long refused Black athletes.

The changes reached other teams at different times. In 1950, Sam Jethroe played for the Boston Braves, the very club Robinson faced on April 15. Jethroe had been one of the players who tried out alongside Robinson in Boston in 1945 without getting a job. The club that held that tryout was a different Boston team, the Red Sox. That team finally played Elijah “Pumpsie” Green in 1959, becoming the last club in the two leagues to do so. The city stayed the same across several entries in the timeline. The names of the clubs and the dates tell a story of separate decisions.

## What the longer view reveals

Hiring changed at a different pace from the progress of a runner around a ball field. Team officials made choices in different cities. Players like Doby, Jethroe, and Green took chances to compete, in circumstances that were their own. A teammate's memory might give one person's perspective on that opening game. A timeline provides a contrast by tracing different decisions over several seasons. Robinson's first game shows that an established barrier could be challenged in a regular season. Later dates show that the challenge could reach other clubs, and that it took years to do so.

Another part of the story comes into view when we follow where the players came from. There was already a professional league where a fan could watch Doby play. As more stars took jobs in the National and American leagues, Negro Leagues clubs lost players and public attention. Some players found new opportunities while teams and businesses built by Black communities faced new pressures. By keeping the timeline going past Robinson's opening game, a reader can see both the reach and the limits of what had begun at first base.
