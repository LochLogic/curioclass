# Segregation and Opportunity

## Reading notes for the teacher

Preview racial exclusion, then read alongside packet 1 of the biography. The 1891 Tennessee train law is an example of how the system was established before the 1940s.

## Separation backed by power

In the United States in the 1940s, a rule could determine where a person could go to school, sit on a train, or look for a home. Under segregation, laws and practices forced people of different races to use separate places or services. These rules kept Black people out of opportunities available to White people. Many of the laws that enforced racial separation were known as Jim Crow laws. The rules varied by place. A traveler could face a different set of restrictions after crossing a city or state boundary.

Segregation differed from choosing to spend time with people you know. The people being separated did not control the decision. In some places, state laws ordered separate schools for Black and White children. When officials assigned children by race, a family could not simply choose the other school. The buildings, books, and teachers a child could reach depended on which school the rules allowed them to attend. Asking who wrote or enforced a rule helps show how a barrier worked.

## A law and a practice

Segregation also affected travel. A Tennessee law required railroads to separate Black and White passengers. The railroad could provide separate cars or divide a car with a partition. Buying passage on the same train could still mean being directed to different seats. The law gave companies a duty to separate people, and it took people at stations and aboard trains to carry out that duty. The separation affected an ordinary task that many travelers would otherwise take for granted.

People could also block opportunities through agreements or habits. In the 1940s, a housing agreement in Arlington County, Virginia, barred the buyers from selling their home to anyone outside a group the agreement classified as White. In this case the restriction was written down, but it was a condition on property rather than a seating rule. The individual family seeking a home could face a barrier put in place long before it asked to buy. A practice could be enforced by employers, property owners, or other people with the power to say who would have access.

## The baseball color line

For years, clubs in the American and National leagues excluded Black ballplayers. Baseball's color line was enforced as a hiring practice. The people who ran those clubs chose whose abilities they would consider. Black players could show great skill and still have no route to a regular job with one of those teams. At the same time, Black owners and players developed professional baseball in the Negro Leagues, the historical name of several leagues that offered opportunities to players excluded elsewhere. Black sportswriters recorded their games and argued for equal access.

Integration means opening places, teams, or institutions that racial separation had kept apart. In baseball, Brooklyn's decision to hire Jackie Robinson let one skilled player do work a National League club had long denied to Black players. That change was something a spectator could see when Robinson took the field. A team in another city still had to make its own decision. Other kinds of segregation, in schools, travel, housing, and jobs, also had people and policies that would have to change.

The word opportunity helps connect these examples. A chance can depend on talent, preparation, and effort. It also depends on whether the people with authority will consider an applicant, admit a student, or sell a ticket that grants equal access. An athlete can be ready for a job while a barrier still keeps it out of reach. Following both sides of the decision helps explain how people who challenged segregation made new opportunities possible.
