# Three Athletes Who Led Change

## Reading notes for the teacher

Read Abbott on day 111 and Clemente and Rudolph on day 112. Preview disability, racism, a serious childhood illness and a fatal plane crash. Keep attention on the athletes’ choices and the conditions that shaped them. Discuss Abbott’s equipment method through the text.

## Day 111 · Jim Abbott: Choosing a Method

### The glove on the mound

When Jim Abbott pitched, his left hand threw the ball. It also caught the next one if a batter sent it back toward him. The baseball glove therefore had to change places in the short time between pitch and play. Before the pitch, Abbott held the glove against his right arm. After releasing the ball, he moved the glove onto his left hand. That left him ready to catch. If he had to throw again, he could steady the glove against his right arm and free his left hand to grip the ball. The method turned several fast decisions into one practiced motion.

Abbott was born without a right hand. He grew up in Flint, Michigan, and played several sports, including baseball and football. He could see how other pitchers prepared to field a ball, but he and his father had to find a method that suited how he threw and caught. They practiced with a rubber ball and a brick wall. The wall sent the ball back, giving him repeated chances to get his glove on in time. When he felt ready for less time between the throw and the catch, he could move closer. There was a way to make progress without waiting for the next game.

### Getting the chance to pitch

In high school Abbott pitched and played quarterback. A quarterback receives a football from a teammate and may have to throw it while several other players move around him. A pitcher begins with time to choose a delivery, then has very little time to handle a ball hit back in his direction. Those two jobs asked him to move quickly in different ways. Playing both gave him opportunities to compete on teams and become known for what he could do in a game.

At the University of Michigan, Abbott became a successful college pitcher. At the 1987 Pan American Games, he carried the flag for the U.S. team and won two games as a pitcher. Carrying a flag was one way to represent the group. Pitching well was a different responsibility the other players counted on him to carry out. He pitched for the United States at the 1988 Olympics in Seoul, South Korea, and helped the team win the baseball tournament. For his next job a club selected him in the first round of the professional draft. A draft is an organized process through which teams claim the chance to sign players. Selection gave Abbott an opportunity to work at a new level. He began his professional career with a major league team, making his debut in 1989.

### One game and a ten-season career

On September 4, 1993, Abbott pitched a no-hitter for the New York Yankees against Cleveland. A no-hitter means the opposing team finished the game without a hit. A batter may still reach base in other ways, such as by drawing a walk. Fielders still have to catch balls and make throws. A successful pitcher relies on those other players as well as on the pitches he controls. The scorebook can record a game without a hit only after every required out has been made.

Abbott had come close in an earlier game that year. A hitter reached on a ball that dropped into the outfield late in that attempt. Remembering a near miss gives the later achievement more meaning. It shows how many things need to go right in a single game. Abbott has talked about the role of luck, as well as preparation, in putting together the whole performance. During his ten seasons in the major leagues he had difficult stretches as well as games that people would remember. He stopped pitching in the majors in 1999.

### Passing along the experience

After his career, Abbott spoke to groups about his experiences. In 2016 he returned to the University of Michigan to address graduating student athletes. In an interview before the event, he talked about the opportunities the university had opened to him. He had spent years learning a method that worked for his body. He could also point to people and places that had given him a chance to use the method: a father who practiced with him, a college that gave him a place to pitch, and teams that trusted him with the ball.

When he was interviewed at a baseball museum, he described his experience of growing up with one hand and his choice to talk about struggle along with success. A talk gave him a way to include the earlier near miss and the setbacks that a short account of the no-hitter might leave out. It also gave him control over which parts of his experience with disability he wanted to share. The audience could hear about the method in the pitcher’s own terms and see a problem a player and his family had worked out together.

Abbott's public work used evidence that was different from an award or a game score. He could explain why a coach's decision to give someone a chance matters and why a player might find a way to perform a familiar job that looks different from the usual method. A scout who judged only the shape of his hand might never stay to see whether he could get an out. Abbott's career offers a reason to look closely at the work an athlete actually does. His later talks let him bring that lesson to people who would never see him pitch.

## Day 112 · Roberto Clemente: Service Beyond the Field

### From Carolina to Pittsburgh

Roberto Clemente Walker was born in 1934 in Carolina, Puerto Rico. As a young player there, he learned from Roberto Marín, who coached him in softball. Years later, after collecting a milestone hit in the major leagues, Clemente publicly thanked the people of Pittsburgh and remembered Marín. An achievement before a stadium crowd gave him a chance to name someone who had helped him when he played much closer to home.

Clemente played professional baseball for Santurce in Puerto Rico before becoming a major league outfielder in Pittsburgh. His main position was right field, where players cover the area beyond first base. A runner deciding whether to advance toward third has to judge the distance to the ball and the fielder's ability to throw it back to the infield. A strong throw can change the runner's decision before it is ever made. Clemente was known for his throwing and won awards for his work as a fielder as well as building a long record as a hitter.

### An audience in more than one place

During his major league career Clemente wore a Pittsburgh uniform, faced teams from cities across the United States, and remained closely connected to Puerto Rico. Some reporters tried to turn Roberto into an English nickname. He objected to being represented in ways that disregarded his Puerto Rican identity or made fun of the way he spoke English. By asking reporters to use the name he used himself, he drew attention to how their choices affected the people they covered.

Clemente won the National League's Most Valuable Player award in 1966. In 1971 he and Pittsburgh won the World Series, and he was named its Most Valuable Player. The first award recognized a season's work across many cities. The second recognized his play in a series against a single opponent with a championship at stake. In 1972 he collected his 3,000th regular-season hit by doubling against the New York Mets. At the game, a Spanish-language broadcaster described the play for a station in Puerto Rico. A record of the hit can say how far the batter reached. The broadcast report shows where people were listening. A single play could be followed in more than one language by people who knew different parts of Clemente's life. That reach would matter again when he asked people to pay attention to something happening outside baseball.

### Aid for Nicaragua

In December 1972, an earthquake struck Managua, the capital of Nicaragua. A damaged city can need large quantities of basic supplies quickly. People who live far from the damaged area may have goods to share but need someone to organize the gathering and delivery. In Puerto Rico, Clemente helped organize a relief effort. His public standing gave him a way to draw attention to the need. The work that came after drawing attention involved gathering supplies and making plans to get them where people could use them.

On December 31, Clemente boarded a plane carrying aid from Puerto Rico toward Nicaragua. It crashed shortly after takeoff. Clemente and the others aboard died. The crash brought an end to the journey he intended to make. His action before that point still tells us something about the purpose he chose. He gave time to organizing help and took part in the attempt to deliver it. A season award had recognized his value to a team. The relief effort brought him into a group whose success would have depended on getting resources to people in a different country.

The choice matters alongside his career because it shows what he could do with an audience built through professional play. A batter at a famous milestone can call attention to the coach who helped him as a child. An organizer can use recognition to get people interested in where help is needed. Neither action is the same as delivering the aid itself. A full account of leadership can recognize the specific work people undertook and the work they intended to do after the first steps were taken.

### A place for young players

Vera Zabala, Clemente's wife, carried forward another plan after his death. She established a sports center in Puerto Rico based on an idea he had wanted to put into practice. The center was meant to give young people a place to develop their athletic abilities, including children whose families had limited resources. A field close enough to use and a place to practice can change how much time a young player has to learn a sport. A facility also depends on people to build and use it, much as a baseball team depends on more than the person who hits.

The sports center continues the part of Clemente's story that began with a coach in Puerto Rico. Someone trained him when little of his later public record existed. Years later, one of his own aims was to put resources within reach of more young people. Vera made that plan into a place where people could go. She also attended the ceremony when Clemente entered the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1973. The ceremony and the center gave his work two kinds of public recognition. One preserved the memory of an athlete. The other gave children a place to begin doing work of their own. Clemente's life offers evidence of two kinds of service: organizing relief for an urgent need and making a plan that another person could carry forward over time.

## Day 112 · Wilma Rudolph: A Public Choice

### A runner asks who can attend

In 1960, residents of Clarksville, Tennessee, were preparing to welcome Wilma Rudolph home from the Olympics. The city planned a parade and a banquet. Rudolph set a condition for taking part. Black and White residents had to be able to attend together. In a city with a history of racial segregation, the requirement changed what the event would be. Organizers could hold a separate celebration under the old practice, or they could welcome the runner on the terms she had set. They chose a celebration open to Black and White residents together.

The decision was possible because of what had happened far away that summer, in Rome, Italy. Three races had brought Rudolph worldwide attention. The welcome gave her a chance to decide how that attention would be used at home. Her medals put her name on the invitation. The part of the invitation that interested her was the part that decided who would get to share in the occasion.

### Growing up near Clarksville

Rudolph was born in Tennessee in 1940 and grew up near Clarksville in a large family. As a child she had polio and used a leg brace. Family members helped with her care. She later played basketball at school. The movement between those pieces of her childhood is not measured by one decision or one race. She had people to help her, time to learn what she could do, and school sports in which she could work with others. Basketball gave her experience responding quickly when the play moved from one side of the court to the other.

Ed Temple, the track coach at Tennessee State University, noticed Rudolph when she played basketball. He brought her into his training program. Temple led a women's track team known as the Tigerbelles. Training with his athletes helped Rudolph learn the demands of a new sort of race. A sprinter works on the start, on reaching full speed promptly, and on keeping control through the finish. A relay runner adds another timed task: passing or receiving the baton while moving. Coaches and teammates can help make each of those small actions more certain through repeated practice.

Rudolph became part of an Olympic relay team in 1956, when she was sixteen. The team won a bronze medal in Melbourne, Australia. That result came years before the celebration in Clarksville. It gave her experience with an international competition and with the shared work of a relay. Afterward she continued training and racing with Temple and the other runners. The Olympic result told people what four runners did on one day. The days of work that followed gave them many chances to develop before the next Olympics.

### Three races in Rome

At the 1960 Olympics Rudolph won gold in the 100-meter dash, the 200-meter dash, and the 4-by-100-meter relay. The first two races recorded how fast she covered a distance on her own. The last recorded how four members of the United States team moved one baton from start to finish. The relay took coordination: a runner receiving the baton had to be moving fast without running beyond the area where the pass was allowed. The result gave Rudolph her third gold medal and depended on the other runners as surely as on her last stretch to the line.

The fact that she won individual and team events helps explain why people paid such close attention to her return. She could have attended a celebration in whatever form the organizers first offered. By making her participation depend on who could join the occasion, she gave those organizers a concrete decision. The people planning a parade and a banquet had to carry out a specific change if they wanted her there.

A street parade and a banquet offer different kinds of participation. People can gather beside a parade route and watch from different points along it. A banquet brings people into the same room and gives them a place to sit together. In arranging both events as integrated gatherings, the organizers addressed access to a public street celebration and to a meal indoors. Residents could share the welcome in more than one setting.

### What a celebration could change

The integrated events were an important public occurrence in Clarksville. People who had been separated could celebrate together. A successful event did not by itself change the rules of every school, shop, or restaurant. Those places had their own decisions and histories. Rudolph's choice had a clear reach: it changed access to an event the city wanted to hold. The recorded outcome, a parade and banquet that welcomed Black and White people together, gives evidence of what that particular choice accomplished.

After her racing career Rudolph finished her degree at Tennessee State and worked in education. She coached, including at DePauw University, where she directed the women's track program. She also started an organization to support young athletes. A coach has repeated chances to make participation possible through training and guidance. The city celebration had offered Rudolph one well-timed chance to change who could take part. Her later work brought the problem of opportunity into the slower routine of helping athletes develop. Rudolph died in 1994. The results from Rome tell us how three races ended. The decision in Clarksville and her work with young runners help explain how she used the opportunities those results gave her.
