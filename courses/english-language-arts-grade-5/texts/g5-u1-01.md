## Reading notes

Maren and her family are fictional. The novel opens in Wisconsin and follows their move to the fictional Minnesota town of Bracken. Start each reading with the next whole chapter. At 120 words a minute, each packet requires about 14 to 17 minutes of first reading. Give time for pauses and shorter rereadings.

The story carries an inference across a season. Readers track the height marks, yellow bowl, cart, folding table and written records as their uses change. Three linked threads move from the family's departure to the work-hours record and the community hall. Concrete actions and dialogue carry most of the meaning. The narrator stays close to Maren, who sometimes sees only one part of a situation.

Preview a parent's death before the story, the loss of rented housing, the move, missing pay, and a short episode of heat illness in chapter 11. The adult crew does the paid farm work. Children help in home and community gardens. Offer a different reading or a quiet pass, and keep questions and performances about the characters.

Chapters 1 and 2 establish what the family can carry and how Mom checks the work offer. Chapters 3 and 4 connect the work on either side of the clipboard gap. Chapter 5 gives a problem of individual ownership. Chapter 6 moves to shared access. Chapters 7 and 8 set up both meetings. In chapters 9 to 12, compare Maren and Tal at the closed door, reread the zipper metaphor, compare Jo and Kira in the corn, and follow Jo's saying from the cardboard steps to the actual building. Chapters 13 to 17 bring back the signs, hands, clocks, chairs and things left for the next person. Chapter 17 gives two contextual senses of *light*. Use the end of each packet only after the first passage used for modeling.

The novel ends at chapter 17. For day 22, pull only the separate cold passage and its language lines. Keep them out of packets, examples and advance reading.

## Chapter 1. Radishes

The morning of the last radishes, Maren put her ear against the kitchen wall. She could hear a thin rattle from the pipes. It came in threes, with a pause after the third.

"What's it saying?" Leon asked.

"Somebody left the outside tap on."

He had expected a message. She could tell by how slowly he took his own ear away. Dad used to knock three times when he came in with both hands full. Leon had started knocking that way, too. The rhythm had made sense when there was somebody on the other side.

"In the garden?" he asked.

"Unless you've got another outside tap."

They went out in their socks. A thread of water ran from the tap into a dent in the ground. It was spreading toward the radishes. Maren turned the handle and watched the thread break. The dirt drank the rest until just one silver dot remained.

"Can we pull them now?" Leon asked.

"Get the bowl."

Her brother brought their big yellow bowl, the one with a nick in the rim. All spring he had wanted to pull a radish. You couldn't tell from the leaves which roots would come out big. The first one he pulled was thin as a pencil. He held it up anyway, pinched between his finger and thumb.

"This one can be mine."

Maren made a hole beside his second radish so he could see the pink shoulder above the dirt. He took the leaves with both hands. The root came up with a tuft of mud, and he fell backward against her knees.

"That one can be both of ours," she said.

He rubbed it clean on his pajama top.

Mom was sitting on the kitchen steps with an envelope on her lap. She had put on her work shirt but hadn't tucked it in. When she saw the new mark on Leon's top, she smiled and held out her hand for his radish. She used the hose to rinse it. Halfway through taking a bite, she shut her eyes.

"Hot one?" Maren asked.

"Just the way he liked them."

Dad had put salt in the bottom of the yellow bowl and rolled the wet radishes in it. Leon remembered only the time one rolled all the way off the table. He told that part every time. The radish had rolled under a chair, hit Dad's shoe and come back into sight. In Leon's telling it was always trying to get away.

"Can we bring the bowl?" he asked.

Mom set down the radish. Maren waited for her to ask where. Instead Mom turned the envelope over, then laid it between them.

"We can bring the bowl," she said. "And we can take the cart if we get the wheels off."

Maren had known the house was for sale. On Saturday she had sat through a showing at Noor's with her math folder on her knees. Her shoes were still under the table when she got home. It had felt like proof. Strangers had opened their closet, but her shoes were still where she left them.

"Did somebody buy it?" she asked.

"Yes. Our rental ends this month. Mr. Bell wrote down the day we need to turn in the keys. I've called every place on the list that I can pay for."

"And?"

Mom pressed the envelope flat with one hand. "The one by the tracks said no. Two were already taken. The place with the purple door would take most of my pay, and we still have to eat."

Leon used a garden twig to draw between the steps. He drew a purple door, even though the wood gave him only one color.

"There's a place in Minnesota," Mom continued. "My friend Jo is working at a vegetable farm there. She put me in touch with the manager. She says there's steady work through the fall. There might be a job for me."

"Is there a house?"

"There's an apartment in town. I talked to the woman who manages it. We've got it for a month, and there'll be another family using the upstairs. After that we'll have to see."

A month sounded like something people gave you when they couldn't give you a real answer. Maren tried to picture an apartment from the word alone. All she got was a door, and that door was purple.

"We live in Wisconsin," Leon said. He said it the way he announced the answer to a problem someone else had gotten wrong.

"We do," Mom said. "For the next two weeks."

Leon had drawn a knob on the door. He drew another knob beside it, then three more. All those knobs, Maren thought, and none of them opens anything.

Leon brought the dish towel from inside. Inside it was a flat wrench he had found where Dad kept his tools. Maren remembered Mom calling the tools their inheritance. They were things Dad had left for them to use. She unwrapped the wrench and set it beside the bowl for when they had to fit the cart in the car.

She walked around the house to get the red cart. One of its wheels caught against the corner of the porch every time she turned it. Dad had said he would fix that wheel in the spring. In April she had tried to fix it herself. She couldn't turn the nut then, so she had painted a crooked white arrow on the handle to warn anyone who borrowed it. The arrow pointed toward the wheel that stuck.

Last fall Dad had taken the wheels off the cart so it would fit inside the shed. He had laid them side by side on a flour sack and gone inside for dinner without washing his hands. Mom had handed him soap instead of a plate. Maren had looked at the four wheels and wondered if you could still call it a cart when it couldn't go anywhere.

Dad died in February, after weeks in which the adults had said things might get better. The next morning Mom had asked what Maren wanted for breakfast. Maren couldn't stand that there could be a next morning. She had said toast, because she couldn't bear to say anything important.

Now she gripped the handle and rocked the cart backward until the wheel came free. On the far side of the fence Noor was hanging a wet towel over the railing. Noor wrapped it around her head, made a crown of the loose end and announced that anyone who could whistle through a radish was invited to her palace.

"What about through your teeth?" Leon called.

"Only on Wednesdays."

It was Tuesday. Maren watched him count the days on his fingers.

"Come over," she told Noor. "I have something to tell you."

Noor took the towel off her head. She climbed the three fence slats she always climbed, dropped down on the wrong side of the flower bed and lifted her shoes one at a time out of the mud.

"Going somewhere?" she said, looking at the cart.

Maren hadn't heard herself say it before. "In two weeks we're moving to Minnesota."

"All of you?"

There were three of them now. Noor knew that as well as anyone. Sometimes she had to start a sentence over when there used to be a fourth person in it.

"All three," Maren said.

Noor sat on the edge of the cart. It tipped and pushed its front board into Maren's shins. They sat there, one holding the handle and the other holding the back edge. The cart would have tipped the other way if either of them shifted.

"Can I do anything?" Noor asked.

Maren wanted to ask her to make somebody give back the house. Instead she said, "Do you want radishes?"

"You hate giving me your radishes."

"You can have one."

"Well, don't go wild."

Maren let out a breath that was nearly a laugh. It was the first sound she'd made all morning that surprised her.

They took the cart to the fence and lifted it over the lip at the gate. In the garden there were six more radishes big enough to pull. Noor picked one with two leaves missing. Leon put the others in the yellow bowl. He made a place in the center for the first little root.

On the back of the envelope, Mom had drawn five boxes. She had put a check in one and written `place to sleep` beside it. The others said `work`, `school`, `ride` and `later`. Maren touched the check with one muddy finger.

"Shouldn't work have a check if Jo said there's a job?"

"Check it when I have a start day and know what I'll get paid."

"What goes in the one for later?"

"I wanted a box for things I don't know yet."

Maren offered to make labels for everything that would go. Mom fetched a roll of paper tape. Leon drew tiny pictures on it until his fingers grew too sticky to hold the pencil. He stuck a drawing of a bowl on the yellow rim and a drawing of a cart on the crooked wheel. Then he drew four stick people around a table and handed it to Maren.

She had meant to tell him that things weren't people. She had meant to say that labels were for furniture and dishes. Instead she peeled the tape and stuck his picture inside the cart. She pressed each white edge back down where it wanted to lift.

That afternoon they ate half the radishes and gave the other half to Noor's family. Leon put his thin one in a glass of water, leaves and all. He said it was for tomorrow. Maren helped take down the height marks from the kitchen doorframe. The marks had been made in pencil, so she held paper against the wood and rubbed with the side of a crayon. It caught dents from the wood but missed nearly every line she wanted. She had to go back with a pencil and copy the dates by hand.

At the top was a line as high as her forehead with her name and last September's date. Below it Dad had drawn a second short line. Beside that one he'd written `standing on toes`. She marked both. He had done it to tease her, and she'd resented the second mark for months.

"What will you do with the paper?" Mom asked.

"Find another frame," Maren said.

She folded the sheet to fit in her shorts pocket. The fold came right between her two marks. On her way out she put a finger over the crack where the kitchen door met the frame. She could still stand in this house. For now that was a fact she could test by standing still.

## Chapter 2. Lettuce

By the time they cut the last heads of lettuce, the yellow bowl had been packed twice and unpacked twice. No one could agree which box it ought to go in. It fitted inside the big stew pot, but no one wanted the pot on top of anything breakable. Maren left it on the counter to make a salad.

Noor came over to help. She put her pile of lettuce on Maren's side of the counter, then stole the last clean knife.

"Do you use every dish just before you move?" she asked.

"We're using three."

"Four. I'm going to eat mine out of a mug."

"You have a perfectly decent hand."

Noor used it to snatch the smallest lettuce leaf. "So do you."

Lettuce left in the garden was easy. What fitted into the trunk was hard. The harder problem sat in the middle of the living room: the table where they ate dinner. It had a long scar that Dad had filled with glue and sawdust. The patch was smooth, but it was darker than the rest of the wood. When you sat in the chair Dad used, it looked like a pencil line someone had tried to erase.

Mom had called four people about a truck. One could help the week after they had to be out. One wanted more money than Mom could put aside. Two never called back. The table was too wide for the car, even with its legs off. Maren knew this because she had measured twice, using a piece of string when she couldn't find a tape measure. She had put the string on the table, then pinched it at the width of the car. A long tail was left dangling from her fist.

"It's just a table," Leon said. He was stretched across two chairs, trying to see the patch from underneath.

"Keep your feet off it, then."

He took his feet off and set both palms flat on the wood. "I mean somebody else might use it."

Maren looked in the boxes for the tub of glue. She found a flat screwdriver instead. If the extra leaves came apart at the seams, perhaps she could stack them. She crouched down to try the first screw. It took so much pressure that the slot slipped and the handle struck her elbow.

"You'll take chunks out of it," Mom said from the doorway.

"Then we can patch them."

"Can you tell me what you'd keep if you could keep only one piece?"

Maren slipped the screwdriver in again. "I'd keep the whole thing."

Mom knelt on the other side. From the knees down she looked ready to slide under the table and help. "I want it, too," she said. She ran her thumb along a leg until it bumped into the little groove where Dad had hung a bag of rubber bands. "I also want your mattresses to fit."

The lettuce waited on the counter until its cut edges started to dry. Noor kept asking whether to set forks out. Maren said yes, then no, then that she could do it herself. Noor opened the drawer gently and stayed where she was.

"I can save seats at our table," she finally said. "Come eat over there if you want."

"We're still eating here," Maren said. She heard how flat it sounded.

"Okay. I'll be next door."

After Noor left, Mom checked the message from the new place. The manager at the farm had offered to call that evening and explain the job. Jo could drive Mom to the first shift until they knew how the schedules lined up. There still wasn't a truck.

Leon went back under the table and lay on his back. He said he had put a sign there the year he was five. Maren found it on the inside of a crosspiece. The letters said `MUSEM`, with a drawing of a key.

"Admission five buttons," Leon said. "You lost your membership."

"How?"

"Everybody did when I needed the buttons for my coat."

Behind the sign was a rectangle of cardboard, small enough to cup in one hand. It had Dad's handwriting on it. There were three boxes, like the boxes on Mom's envelope. The first said `level`. The next said `leave room`. The last said `ask L`.

"That's you," Maren told Leon.

"Why would he ask me?"

"How high to put the museum sign."

They had worked on the table on the same rainy Saturday. Dad had filled the scar and Leon had waited to open his museum. Maren didn't remember the sign until she saw it. She did remember making a fuss because she wanted the darker patch gone. She had set out four placemats together so they covered it. Dad moved one of the mats back to its own corner before he ate.

Maren slipped the cardboard into her pocket beside the pencil marks she'd copied. Then she carried a head of lettuce over to Noor's and knocked. Noor answered with her coat half on. She had been about to bring over forks anyway.

"Would your family use our table?" Maren asked.

"We have a table."

"In the back room? There's nothing in there except the sewing machine and the bike pump."

They stood looking at each other a moment. Then Noor called her dad. He came out rubbing a streak of black grease off his wrist. He and Mom talked. He said there was enough space for the table if they shifted the ironing board and stacked the boxes of winter clothes. He could pay them a little for it. He made the offer to Mom, then asked Maren what she wanted to happen to the sign under it.

"That comes with us," she said.

He waited. Maren had told Noor they'd keep exactly one piece if they had to. Now she found herself asking for something else. "Can you send me a picture if you eat at it?"

"First meal," he said. "And any time Noor spills soup."

"That could use up all your phone space," Noor said.

They made a plan to carry the table over in the morning. They did it while the grass was still wet. Maren took one corner and Noor took the other on the same end. The grown-ups carried the end with the patch. Leon ran ahead to open the fence gate, then held the gate wide with his back. There was more room between the posts than Maren had remembered. Nobody caught an elbow.

The table still looked like theirs in Noor's back room. They could see it from the door next to a stack of cloth and a pair of red rain boots. The light there came through a window facing the yard instead of the street. It made the dark patch shine. Maren set her palm on the top. She waited for a feeling that would tell her whether she'd made a good decision. From behind her she heard Noor shove the bicycle pump into a corner, then ask whether it was all right to put a lamp on the table.

"As long as it's your lamp," Maren said.

By lunch Noor had sent a photo of the lamp plugged in, with a red sock laid flat beside it for mending. Nobody had to pretend to be eating to make a place for the table.

In the afternoon the farm manager, Devin, called. Mom put him on speaker at first so she could make notes while her hands were clean. He told her the work would start on Monday if she wanted it. There would be harvests and work in the packing shed, and he named an hourly rate. Mom wrote that down. He explained that there was a different form for the hours once a crew moved from the field to the shed. Mom asked whether the hourly rate changed. He said it didn't.

Maren heard something in the pause and looked up. Mom had almost said thank you while he was still talking. She stopped and waited for him to finish. Then she asked him to send the offer in writing. He did, before they finished the last of the lettuce.

"One box," Leon said, looking for the envelope. "We can check one now."

"Find it when I finish this call," Mom told him.

He found it on the windowsill. The paper was so soft at the folds that it had started to split. He set it on top of the biggest box in the room and held down both ends while Mom drew the check.

Maren and Noor ate the last two servings of salad on the front step that night. There was a narrow pool of dressing in the bottom of the bowl, and Noor took the last corner of bread to wipe it up. A pair of headlights went along the road. Each time they passed, Noor said, the wall of the living room lit up for a moment before it went dark again. Maren leaned over to see whether she was right. The headlights slid in just where the table had been.

"Maybe you can tell me when you find a new table," Noor said.

"What if we don't?"

"Tell me what you eat on."

That was something Maren could do. She made Noor promise to tell her what the sock looked like after it was mended. Then they opened a game on Mom's phone and played until the screen was too bright to see their hands. Noor won twice. The third time she stopped a move short of winning and let Maren finish. Maren would have been angry if she hadn't been able to tell. She made Noor play one more round seriously.

Later, Leon asked how much longer they had to live in the house. Maren looked into the kitchen. There was no table, but the square of light from the window still landed on the floor. If she stood in it, she could feel the heat through her socks.

"Three dinners," she told him.

"What if we eat four?"

"Then eat four."

They sat inside the square and planned tomorrow's two dinners. Before they went upstairs, Maren used a fresh strip of tape to fasten the museum sign to the inside of the yellow bowl. It would travel there unless someone needed the bowl again.

## Chapter 3. Kale

Maren could see the red cart handle in the rear window for most of the drive. At first it looked like a finger pointing back the way they had come. After an hour she leaned her head toward the other window and could make it point forward.

She told Leon about it. He changed seats at the next stop, with Mom's permission, so he could make it point forward, too. They didn't talk about the house after that until evening. They played a game in which everything outside the car was something you might eat. A silo was a tube of crackers. A car carrying a canoe was a spoon carrying a pea that had rolled off. A truck tire was a very bad doughnut.

They ate peanut butter sandwiches at a picnic table beside a parking lot. Maren used the yellow bowl to keep the bread bag from blowing away. It was the first thing they had eaten on since Noor asked her to keep track. She took a picture to send. Noor sent back one of the red sock with its new blue heel. Someone was wearing it.

The apartment was in a town called Bracken. On the last turn a sign for Laurel Farm pointed away from town, across a wide empty stretch with ditches on either side. Jo had warned them by message to keep going to the second traffic light. The second light was small, with a bell hooked to the pole beneath it. A white dog waited at the corner while its person pressed the button. It walked when the bell rang. Leon was so impressed that they had to stop twice to let another person use the crossing.

Ona May, who managed the apartment, met them in the driveway. She wore a tape measure around her neck and carried two sets of keys. She and Mom walked through each lower room together. She showed Mom which handle turned off the water under the sink, then handed her a copy of the rental agreement. The stairs to the upper apartment started at the other end of the shared entry.

"Where can I put my cart?" Maren asked.

"There's a painted square in the entry for each apartment. Can you keep it inside yours? People need the middle clear."

The square was too small for the cart fully built. Maren brought the handle inside and left the wheels in the trunk. She was trying to stand the wood up on end when the upstairs neighbor came in with a sack of groceries under her arm.

"I can go around," the woman said, stopping on the mat.

Maren tipped the handle toward herself. It came down on her toe. The woman set her sack down and waited for Maren to pull her foot free.

"I'm Pilar. My son Tal will be home after dinner. He's always carrying something wider than he is. We'll learn where the corners are."

Maren stood the handle upright. Pilar wedged one side with a folded piece of cardboard. They both let go, a finger at a time. This time it stood.

In the kitchen there were two chairs and a counter. They ate standing up. Leon wanted to record every surface, including Mom's hand, from which he took an apple slice. Maren sent Noor one picture of the counter with all three shadows in it. She didn't send the next one, which had only hers.

On Monday Jo collected Mom before the sun reached the front window. An adult named Rey had let the family visit the community room at the town's meeting hall, where she worked. She'd arranged for Leon and Maren to come back in the mornings while Mom started work. She had a wall of donated games, a low sink to fill water bottles and a rule that no one moved the big boxes alone. Leon greeted her by asking to see the boxes. Maren chose a place with a view of the road and set down her bag.

Tal was there making a flag for a cardboard city. The other children kept moving his city limits. When a girl wanted to put a bridge from the zoo to the bakery, he asked Maren to hold down the piece of paper while he patched it. She held it in the corner instead of the middle so he could draw under her hand. By lunchtime he had stopped asking. He simply turned the board so the corner came toward her.

After work Jo and Mom came to get them. A smudge as pale as chalk crossed the front of Mom's blue shirt. She looked tired and had a bunch of kale wrapped in a damp cloth. Jo had worked there since spring and had offered to bring them out after the crew left, so Mom could show them the shed from the pickup area. They drove back past the farm sign and parked where a row of flat stones marked the edge of the lot.

"Is that all you picked?" Leon asked about the kale.

"It's all I brought home. We carried crate after crate down the rows. Jo showed me which leaves fit in the bunches, and which plants still had small leaves to grow. This one came off when I set a bunch down too hard."

"Does it still work?" he said.

Mom grinned. "Take a leaf and find out."

Leon took a bite, chewed for a long time and handed the rest to Maren. It tasted greener than lettuce. She folded her leaf over twice until the thick vein snapped.

Mom showed them a long board with the names of the field crews, under the shed eaves. It slid into two brackets by the door. The board had room for a start and a finish, but not for a time in the middle. At eleven, Mom explained, Devin had called for her crew to come to the shed. They set the last leaves down, then spent forty minutes clearing crates and loading the wash tables. Inside, the sheet on the clipboard said to start writing hours at noon. Devin had been talking with a driver, so Mom had written the actual time in the margin. When she went back for her water jug, she saw that the margin had been crossed out.

"Maybe it goes on the field sheet," Jo said. "I remember signing at the first door."

They looked at the board. The finish time for every name was eleven. Mom ran a finger down the column until she reached her own. The bottom of the wooden frame was gritty.

"Then it goes on neither," she said. "Who counts that time?"

Jo took a picture of the two columns and said she'd ask Devin in the morning. She had worked fewer days in the shed than on the harvest crew. When she had gone from one to the other, she had trusted the people with clipboards to tell her what to write.

Maren looked through the screened window at the wash tables. A few drops of water caught the light on the floor. She could imagine Mom walking from the rows with a crate in each hand and no free thumb to point to a clock.

"Maybe he's going to add it later?" Maren said.

"Maybe." Mom turned her head to see if Devin's truck was there. It wasn't. "I'm going to ask and make sure."

As they waited for the car to cool enough to sit in, a person in a striped shirt came up from the far side of the shed. Mom introduced him as Amos. He'd been driving the little wagon that carried the trays to the packing table. He was there to put the chain back across the lane after the truck left. He clipped it to one post and stood there with the end in his hand.

"You get put down for the afternoon?" he asked Mom.

"I'm trying to find that out."

He had stayed out in the rows all day. He said he'd seen her bring his cart back with the empty trays before his crew stopped. "That was while I had the chain down," he said. "You can ask me if they need to know when."

Maren could hear a little tap at the post as the chain swung. She thought he might be waiting for someone to help. Then he showed Leon that he could let the end go through the loop, leaving the last link over the top. The rest of it made a curve in the middle of the entrance. A driver could see at a glance that the road was shut.

"That's a funny little job," Leon said.

"It is, until somebody drives through when a truck's backing up," Amos told him. "Then I want everybody to see it."

Maren turned to look at him from the car. He was writing something on a clip at the post. He lifted it so she could see the time and the word `closed`. She wondered how many people would have to stop and look at a sign before there would be enough room to write a job on the board. Before they drove off, Mom got the time written in her own notes, too.

At home Mom spread the paper with the job offer over the counter. She had put it in a clear sleeve along with the directions to the apartment and the names of the neighbors who had offered to help. There was nothing about a different rate at the shed. In fact, she had underlined `the same hourly rate for picking and packing` before she'd even said yes.

Maren wanted to take a picture of the whole page and send it straight to Devin. Mom covered the bottom with her hand. It had their new address on it. Maren put her phone down.

"Could we find out what everybody else signed?" she asked instead.

"We could ask them whether they want to look at theirs." Mom tapped the counter twice. "First, I can ask him about mine."

Leon had made a city of salt grains beside the stove. He pretended his fingers were trucks and swept them into a heap. Mom asked him to get a cloth, then cut the kale leaves into pieces. She showed Maren how to lift out the thick stem. When Maren tore a leaf in half, a drop of water jumped into her eye. She shut it and went on tearing by feel.

Jo sent a message after dinner. Devin said the shed hours were on a different clipboard, and he'd check whether their crew had been added. On the back of an envelope Mom wrote down what she'd been doing, and when, while she could still remember. She started with the field and ended with the end of her shift. In the middle she wrote `clear crates, load wash tables`. It looked like an ordinary list of things she'd done. Maren thought about the board at the door, which could make forty minutes vanish with one straight line.

When they went to bed, the cart handle was still standing in the hallway, wedged with Pilar's piece of cardboard. Maren let her finger trace the crooked white arrow on the grip. It pointed toward the bare axle. Without the wheel, the arrow could not tell anybody where the trouble was. She took a scrap of tape and marked the square on the floor where the wood belonged. In the morning she would get the wheels out of the trunk before anyone needed to move the car.

## Chapter 4. Peas

Somebody had left a row of pea plants beside the community room. The vines leaned against strings hung from a bent metal frame. You could see through them if you stood close, but from the end of the yard the frame was a green wall.

Rey said the peas were ready to pick. A volunteer had planted them before the weather warmed. Anyone in the room who wanted to harvest could pick a bowl and eat from it. Maren thought Leon would choose the biggest bowl there. He chose a small one for Rey, because she had a phone call to finish and might not get outside before the others picked all the good ones.

Tal found a pod at the very bottom of the frame. He turned it sideways and held it against the sunlight. Each pea made a dark bump. Maren showed him how the seam would open if he pressed his thumbnail between the two sides. She knew because Dad used to get her to shell peas while he made dinner. He always left one on the top of the pile for her to open with her teeth.

"Is your mom doing this all day?" Tal asked.

"She's working at Laurel Farm. She was on kale, then on washing and packing. I don't know what today's crop is."

He handed her an open pod. "You should bring her some of these."

It was the first time anybody here had asked about what Mom did, and Maren was glad Tal had asked while she had a handful of loose peas. She tipped two into her mouth so she would have time to think. Yesterday Leon had told a child they had come because their dad died. The child said she knew about dying because of her fish, and they went on building a tower. Maren had sat silently beside them, ready for trouble. None came. Now Tal had asked a different question, and she could give him a different piece of their story.

"My dad used to fix desks at my school," she said. "When somebody put the screws on backward, he used to tell me they'd done half the job already. He'd hand me a screwdriver."

"What was the other half?"

"Turning them around, I guess."

They peeled the last pod without spilling a single pea. In the room, Tal arranged the shells to make a row of boats on the edge of the paper city. Rey moved the paper away from the bowl of water he brought to float them in.

That evening at supper Mom said she'd spoken to Devin again. He had made a note on the second clipboard. She would look for the missing time on her first pay statement, which was due Friday with the pay for her first days on the crew. Until then she was keeping a record of her own hours. She showed Maren where she'd begun, on the back of the same envelope.

"What if he fixed it?" Maren said.

"Then I don't need to take it any further. I still want my own record."

Maren had pictured something with colored lines and neat boxes. Mom's record was less grand than that. She had written the day, the two tasks and the time in a hand that slanted up when it reached the edge of the paper. The last number nearly fell off. Below it Mom had made a separate line for the next day.

"Do you keep one here when you're not at work?" Maren asked.

"I do now."

They found a small notebook in the school-supply box and moved the entry into it. Maren tried to draw the table for Mom, then realized Mom hadn't asked her to. She kept her hands on her knees while Mom lined up the columns by eye. There was room for what she needed.

Over the next few days Maren and Tal added neighborhoods to the cardboard city. Wherever they joined two pieces of paper, he left room for her to draw a door. The city had a newspaper office, a bakery, a patch of river and a school with a window very much like the one Maren had left. She put a cone beside it, where the custodian's cart would go. A child whose older sister was a bus driver added two strips of paper so buses could drive all the way around the school instead of having to back out.

On Thursday a woman named Jodie came to visit the room. She had helped build one of the planter boxes in the garden. She asked the children to show her something they'd drawn in the city. Leon chose the bakery. He couldn't reach over the school to touch it, so he tapped his finger in the air over the roof. Jodie crouched until she could see it from the side.

"I can see where the bread goes," she said. "How does a person get a bag of it home?"

Maren showed her the road, which was so narrow between the bakery and the zoo that Tal had decided to make it one way. Jodie asked where the deliveries went. There was an alley on the far side by the river. It fitted between two pieces of paper and looked shorter than it was. Maren made a small truck out of an eraser and showed Jodie where it could turn.

"What if the delivery goes to the zoo?" Leon asked.

"That's the same road," she told him.

"But he'll need a different place to stop."

When the visitor left, Leon brought another eraser. He drew a lion on one side. The trucks now looked so different that Maren wanted to make two doors for them. But it was enough to add a small sign above the second door. She turned her head and tried to read the sign from the other end of the table. A line of rooftops hid it completely. Tal lifted one end of the sheet so they could see it better.

"A person wouldn't pick the whole city up to read a sign," she told him.

He took the sign off its post and raised it up where she could see it from both ends. Then they had to find it a new place to stand.

On Friday afternoon, while Leon counted his way down the front steps, Mom came in with her pay statement folded into quarters. She put her grocery bag down without coming all the way into the kitchen.

"Is something broken?" Leon asked.

"Would you put the cold things away first?" she said. She sat down at the counter.

The statement listed hours and pay but didn't show which tasks had filled the hours. Mom worked through the numbers with the notebook beside her. She came up forty minutes short. She tried once more. Maren had seen her check a sum that way before, moving backward to see whether she landed on the same total. The number stayed the same.

"Forty minutes isn't very much," Leon said. He was trying to fit a carton of milk on the shelf under a jar that was too tall for it.

"You've waited forty minutes for a bus," Mom said. "Did that feel short?"

"No. We made up a whole song."

"I worked all the way through that much time. That's part of how I pay for what we need."

Maren took the carton from Leon and moved the jar to the door. She wanted to ask again if they could call Devin. Mom was already writing a message. It said which day, which jobs, when they started and why the forty minutes should be on the next pay statement. She read it once more before sending it. What Maren noticed was how little it matched her own mood. There was no exclamation point. There was no sentence about how many times they'd already asked to get it fixed.

At supper they added the peas Tal had picked to a pan of rice. There were just enough to give Leon half a dozen in his first spoonful. He said the rest of his rice needed its own peas. Mom scooped some from her plate onto his.

"How are you going to do that if nobody gives you your minutes back?" he asked.

Mom looked up. "Do what?"

"Keep giving me your peas."

"I've got enough peas for supper." She tilted the pan so he could see what was left. "I'm going to keep asking about the pay because there ought to be enough tomorrow, too."

That was when Jo called. It turned out her statement was short that week as well. Her missing time was on a different afternoon, when they had sorted returns before clocking in at the shed. She'd thought Mom wanted to complain about the field crew, so she hadn't said anything beyond the first note. Now she wanted to compare statements and dates together.

Maren pushed her plate away and reached for a pencil. "Could I write it all down while you talk?"

Mom said Jo might want to keep her pay private. They could talk about when the crew changed jobs without laying out what everyone earned. Maren took an empty page and drew a narrow time line, with no one's name on it. Mom took it over the next day and used the spare half to mark where Jo's shift had changed.

By the end of the weekend there were two photographs of empty clipboards, a few times each woman had copied from her own notes and a message from Devin agreeing to look at them. The message came while Leon was on his knees in the entry, trying to put the cart back together. He had set one wheel on the axle without its washer. The wheel went on, but it wobbled. Maren pointed out the extra metal ring on the mat. He slipped it on and held the axle steady while she tightened the nut.

The crooked wheel was still crooked. The cart would roll if she tugged the handle to the right when she turned it. That was enough for trips to the garden at the meeting hall. Rey had said she might borrow it afterward for the boxes in the front room, if she had permission.

In the kitchen Mom was on a second call with Jo. She had one finger pressed against the notebook to hold it open. Maren overheard her say that the crew should be able to see the time when they crossed to the shed. The crew could check one record together if the board had a place to write what happened between the field and the shed.

"Would you go with me when I ask?" Mom said.

Maren couldn't hear the answer, but she could see the muscle in Mom's cheek relax. The page she had been holding down rose as soon as she took her finger off it.

Before bed Maren went to the entry to check the cart. She rolled it three feet straight out from its painted square and three feet back. Then she tried it around a corner. It caught exactly where she had known it would. She tugged right. The wheel gave a little turn, and the cart came after her without a sound.

## Chapter 5. Beets

All through the first Saturday with a working cart, Maren kept finding more reasons to take it out. The first trip was to the meeting hall to fetch beets. The next was home to look for a bag that could go around the beets inside the cart. By the third trip, Tal was waiting outside with a little patch of dirt on the back of his neck where he had missed with the cloth.

"You can leave it," he said, as she moved the cart out of his way. "It's the only thing around here that isn't asking me to stand still."

They hauled a bag of beets back to the apartment. The largest root had grown in ground that was packed too hard for them to pull it. Rey had loosened it with a fork. Tal declared it two beets that had decided to grow into one. Leon found a vaguely heart-shaped beet and said it had been trying to make a Valentine. Maren wanted the one as round as a fist.

Pilar was moving a small dresser through the shared entry. She had taken the drawers out, but the dresser was still broad enough to close off half the passage. Maren left the cart beside the door on the paved strip so Pilar could turn the dresser. She put the bag of beets under the counter, then went upstairs to help Tal hold one drawer steady while he twisted a loose knob back on. The knob was painted blue. When it finally caught, the blue streak on his finger looked like a little bird.

From upstairs she heard the crunch of tires in the driveway. Ona May had said someone would pick up two cracked chairs from the marked area by the entry. Maren watched through the window as a man in a brown hat lifted the first chair. He hooked the good arm over the second so he could lift both together. She went back to the knob. By the time she came downstairs, the chairs and the red cart were gone.

She stood where it should have been. Two crescents of clean pavement showed where the cart's back wheels had stood. She put her toes in them, one at a time. The front of the cart couldn't have been more than a foot from the chalk rectangle. Whoever had loaded it had been able to see the crooked white arrow.

"Did you tell him the cart was trash?" she asked Pilar.

Pilar had got the dresser against the wall exactly where she wanted it. She looked surprised. "I didn't see him take it."

"Didn't you hear the truck?"

"I heard it leave."

Maren wanted to go straight to Ona May's office. She was on the second step when Mom caught up with her. "What are you going to ask her?"

"Where is it?"

"Good first question."

Ona May worked at a narrow desk in a room over the laundry. Two fans turned in opposite corners. When Maren came in, she could see the small print on the calendar date behind the woman's head. There was no lesson in whether she knew the date. She needed to know how far a truck could get since she stopped looking out a window.

Ona May found the pickup sheet, with two chairs in the box beneath the address. The man had added `red garden cart` to his list. He had probably taken it to the town's reuse shed. It might still be on the truck, but the shed closed at noon and it was already eighteen minutes past.

"We'll let him know," Ona May said.

"Does he get to keep it until he feels like giving it back?"

"No. I'll call today."

"Did it have to be in the square all the time?"

"For people to get through, yes. That's separate from what's marked for pickup." She tapped the list, above the man's handwriting. "We told him what to take. I should've put a tag on the chairs, too."

Mom took out her phone to check how to reach the shed. Maren discovered she had curled her right hand around the edge of the desk. A loose corner of the map under its glass cover was pressing into her little finger.

"If somebody else has it, I can show them where the picture is," she said. "My brother made it."

Ona May wrote down the details. Red slats. White arrow. Paper picture with four people. She stayed on the phone long enough to leave all three details in a message. Then she pulled out another pad and wrote Maren's name at the top.

"I'll call you when I hear. You can come back here if I forget. I'm here till five."

"I never put my name on the cart," Maren said when they went down the stairs.

"You were at home with it for years," Mom said. "Writing your name on everything you touch would be a lot of work."

Maren remembered Leon's tape pictures on the bowl and the cart. She had wanted him to stop using so much tape. Now she was glad he had drawn all four people and not just one. There might be dozens of red carts. She doubted there was another cart with a picture Dad had never seen.

At home Leon wanted to make a missing poster. He drew the cart from underneath, where he had been looking when they put the washer in. It had six wheels because he wanted the person who found it to be able to see them all from both sides. Maren made him look out the window at a car. He changed six to four.

They folded the drawing into a blank envelope and took it to Ona May in case the man at the shed needed it. She was gone, but she had left a note taped to the top of the desk. She had heard back. The cart was still in the truck. It could come back on the next run or be claimed Monday at the shed. She had given the man Maren's name and the exact marks to look for. There was also a card on the desk, with a space for the pickup driver's signature and one for the owner's. At the bottom she had written, in small neat letters, `I can drive`.

"Next run could be a week," Leon said.

"The shed is right by the school. I could ride with Pilar on Monday."

"Do you want to?" Mom asked.

Maren read the note again. She did want to ride with Pilar. But she wanted to get the cart back with Leon even more. She asked Mom if they could wait until after work and go together. Mom called the shed to make sure it would be open. Then she left Ona May a note of her own, to explain where the card would be.

When Mom asked if the driver could leave the cart where he'd found it next time, Maren said she would use the apartment square, with Pilar's dresser out of the way. They went down to measure how much room there really was. The paint along one edge of the square had worn away. Maren thought the back wheels could stay there, although the handle might stick out far enough to catch a shirt on its way by. She remembered how quick she'd been to blame Pilar for the cart disappearing. She could still see Pilar's empty hands in the doorway.

Pilar came down as they finished. She wanted to know if they'd heard anything. Maren told her what the note said and showed her the new space for the cart.

"It was my decision to leave it outside while we put the knob on," Maren said. "You didn't tell him to take it."

"No." Pilar said it quietly, without smiling. "I would've expected you or Leon to be around if anybody was going to ask. I'll move the dresser a little farther in. See whether you can leave room for both sets of feet where the floor turns."

There were four of them, plus Tal, taking turns walking from the door to their stairs or apartment. Every person found a slightly different use for the middle. Mom carried the grocery bag across in one arm. Leon stood with a foot on each side as wide as he could, then turned and stretched out one arm. Pilar was the only one who automatically checked whether the door would close behind her. In the end they put a short mark on the floor where they'd be able to turn past the handle. Maren knew the mark would still be there if anyone forgot why they had made it.

In the kitchen that night, Maren scrubbed beets while Leon tried to hold a dish towel around his waist like an apron. The dirt came off the small ones quickly. There was a patch in the curve of the heart-shaped one that resisted. Maren kept at it until a pink stain spread to her thumb. She was about to cut away the last rough bit when Leon asked her why.

"It doesn't look finished."

"That's where the top of a heart goes."

She held it up to the window. She could see the shape he meant. She put it back among the other beets with the rough place facing up.

On Monday the man at the shed turned the cart upside down to help look for the arrow. Leon said it would be on the handle, and very hard to see from underneath. A woman who was looking at the old chairs said she could see the arrow plainly if she stood where she was. The driver set the cart on its wheels. He rubbed the arrow with his thumb, then looked down inside to see the picture. It had folded at one edge, but all four people were visible.

"Didn't see that at the apartment," he told Maren. "I saw the chairs and thought the three things all went together. I should've checked the sheet again."

He showed her where to sign. He had already written `returned` on the line above it. She took the pen in the hand that still had a pink stain by the nail.

Outside, the left wheel stuck as soon as she turned. The driver said he might have a spare wheel in the back. Maren tugged the handle right and the wheel came around. She wanted to keep that wheel until she knew what a repair would do.

"Ask me another time," she said.

Mom lifted the cart into the trunk with her. Leon walked around to check that the red handle could be seen through the back window. At the apartment they stopped at Ona May's desk. Maren handed over both copies of the signed card and waited for Ona May to give one of them back. On it she wrote her name again, larger. She folded it so that the line about the cart coming back was outside, where she could read it without unfolding anything.

That evening there were three fresh tags on the entry wall. For the things being picked up, Ona May had written. A picture on each one showed a small chair with a tag hanging from it. Leon drew a cart with four wheels on an old luggage tag and ran it through a hole on the handle. The first time he tugged it, the string slid off. Maren helped him find a hole that went all the way through the board. This time he tied the string to the cart itself.

## Chapter 6. Broccoli

On the day Rey brought broccoli out to the meeting hall steps, Leon decided it was the right size to be a forest behind the cardboard city. He set six pieces on a plate with the cut ends down. Three fell over. Tal made the rest stay up by surrounding them with bread crusts. A child named Bea laid a pretzel between the trees and announced that it was a fallen log across a trail.

"This city needs a cellar," Tal said. "So we can put it somewhere when we have to clear the tables."

For two weeks they had carried the city to a shelf in the meeting hall office each afternoon. The shelves belonged to Rey's work. The meeting room had cupboards, but most were full of old coffee cups, spare games and folded tablecloths. In the third cupboard there was a long bare shelf, with more room than all the paper neighborhoods took up together. Rey pulled the door almost shut and let them look at it. She said she could ask at the office whether they could keep the city there. Leon wanted a door on the cellar in the model to match it. Bea was making a small blue latch when the office clerk came in with a clipboard.

To use the shelf, an adult needed to fill out a form on behalf of the group. The form required an address. That sounded easy until Tal pointed at the line underneath. `Storage for year-round Bracken households only`, he read.

Tal and Pilar lived upstairs from Maren all year. Bea's family did, too, in a narrow house near the school. Maren had lived in Bracken for nearly three weeks, but the agreement Mom signed for the apartment had a date in the blank for when it ended. They still hadn't filled in the box for later on the back of their envelope.

"Could our address still go on it?" Maren asked.

The clerk, Ed, looked over the top of the clipboard. "I don't think so. We started that rule after a group left supplies behind. No one on the hall committee could reach them when it was time to empty the shelves."

"What happened to the supplies?" Leon asked.

"We put them in a box for a while, then donated them. Somebody from the group came back the next year, expecting them right where they'd been."

"We're here every day," Maren said.

Ed rested the clipboard on the back of a chair. "I can give you a place in the room until you go home. For a reserved shelf I have to use what's on the form."

He offered to bring a tray. It would be easier to move the city all at once if they built it on one. He said it kindly. Maren could see he was making a real offer. She could also see how small the tray he was measuring with his hands would be.

Tal said they could put Pilar's name on the form. He and Maren had put almost every part of the city together. As long as one of them could reserve a place, he said, both of them could get their pieces out. Bea nodded and picked up her blue latch.

"What if we made two cities?" Maren said. "Could mine go on the shelf by itself?"

Nobody answered her at first. At the end of the table, Leon ate two of his broccoli trees. Tal sat on his hands when he wanted to say something and needed to wait. He sat that way now.

"I wouldn't want two cities," he said finally.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

They finished eating outside. Maren had a good view of the white truck parked by the hall door. The clerk had carried the tray into the room after all. It was the size she had guessed. When Leon went back inside, he lifted the zoo off the board so there would be room for some of the houses. He got the houses onto the tray. The zoo sat alone on the table with its paper bridge hanging over the edge.

Rey came to the steps and sat down at the other end from Maren. She had packed a bowl of broccoli for Pilar. She shook the bowl so that the smallest pieces moved to the bottom. In a minute she said she had heard the question about the shelf.

"What would you want the form to ask?" she said.

"Where we live," Maren said. "That's fine. If we move, maybe where to call us."

"What about an end date?"

"We could sign up for a week and then do another one."

Rey picked up a dry piece of leaf and rolled it between her fingers until it broke. "Write it the way you'd want to read it."

"Will you put it on the form?"

"I can't on my own. I can take your version to the committee. They meet every month, and next time they're taking a few minutes to hear from people who use the room."

Maren brought a pencil out from the office. At the top of the page she wrote `address`. She looked at it again, frowning. Tal sat on the step below her and asked what she was doing.

"If you lost your apartment," she said, "what would you put here?"

"How would I know where I'd live?"

"Exactly."

He opened his mouth again and shut it. Then he pointed to the part she hadn't written yet. "Maybe they want to know how to find you, even if they don't know where you sleep."

She crossed out the first line. She wrote `how we can reach you`. Underneath she wrote `when you'll come back for it`. She had put `who owns it` on the next line when Bea came down with the blue latch stuck to the middle of her palm. She asked if she could put her name on the new page, since the gray brick school in the model was hers. Leon asked to put his name on, too. He'd made the bakery. Maren moved that line down to the space where there was room to write more than one name.

A man leaving the office held the door for them. He was carrying a pair of boots tied together at the laces. He said he had kept a set of large wooden letters on that shelf for a few winters. His group played with them after supper on Tuesdays. Their sign-up form would still work with a date, he thought. He'd rather set a date than let the boots sit in the hallway another month with no one sure whose they were.

Inside, Ed listened to Rey describe the idea. His eyes went straight to the line about when they'd come back.

"How would we tell somebody their time was up?"

"You could ask if they wanted to renew when you got in touch," Maren said. She'd heard Mom use that word when she asked Ona May about the apartment. "They could say if they still needed it."

Ed pointed to the zoo on the table. "You'd want that to last longer than a week, right?"

"Yes. But a week would get us to next Saturday."

When he asked if he could keep the page, she offered to make him a copy. He brought her a piece of paper with writing on only one side. She didn't have a ruler, so she used the edge of the clipboard to draw three straight lines. Then she copied the names from the old page onto the new one, keeping each letter in the order its owner had written.

Before they could leave, one of the Tuesday players opened the tall cupboard to find a napkin. He closed the door when he saw the folded cloths. Maren thought he was about to try the next one. Instead he leaned in and read the little label on each handle. The first one said `office` in tiny print. A napkin didn't seem to belong to the office. He decided to ask.

There were two shelves that no one had asked about. One was too high for Leon to reach. The second was exactly high enough for him to look right into it. It held golf pencils, a folded flag and a neat pack of paper towels with its end open. Ed had things there that needed to stay out of the way while the room was used for meals. Maren told him about the paper towels. He found a roll that would fit at the low sink, where somebody could see what it was for and reach it with wet hands.

"Maybe there's already space we don't use very well," he said.

She looked at the empty spot the towels left. It wasn't any wider than before. Ed made a mark about seven inches long where they might move the pencils if they needed that shelf later. Maren asked whether he could measure it while a person was there to open the door. When the cupboard was closed, the hinges took up space he couldn't see. The little mark he'd made stuck out across one of them.

He let her hold the door. They measured again from the inside edge. Tal cut the new measurement out of a scrap of paper so they could put it down right inside the cupboard and still remember what it meant when the door was shut.

That afternoon Pilar came to get Tal. She offered to sign for the room shelf when he told her about the tray. Maren waited to see whether he would say yes immediately. Instead he asked if Pilar could help them carry the whole city upstairs for a few days. Bea set the zoo back in place before she went home. Leon carried the car he had made separately in the pocket of his shorts.

They laid the city on Tal's bedroom floor on open grocery bags. Tal put three wooden blocks on the corners to hold the paper flat. Maren watched a fourth corner lift in the draft. She put her elbow down until he could find something else heavy enough to use.

"Can I tell them yours is here when they ask where it went?" he said.

"Tell them where the whole thing went."

He slid a red block up against her arm. She laid her copy of the form on the dresser. The blue knob was holding firm. She remembered that she and Tal had turned it from opposite sides, each one trying to tell by feel what the other was doing. It had taken longer than she expected. The next morning, she decided, she would bring a straightedge and make another copy with more room at the bottom. The committee couldn't answer what she hadn't left them space to say.

## Chapter 7. Green Beans

Jo made up a rule for snapping beans: the last person to find a string had to answer a question. She gave Maren a small heap and herself a big one to make the rule fair. Before they were halfway done she'd asked Maren which class she'd never want to give up. Maren said art, and Jo guessed math.

"Why math?" Maren asked.

"You found a way to show forty minutes without putting anybody's pay on paper."

"I like making things so you can see them."

"That's a decent reason to like both."

They were sitting at the kitchen counter. Mom had brought a third chair from the entry. With three chairs nobody could open the bottom drawer. Leon made them stand twice to get bowls, then remembered where the bowls were and brought the last one from an open shelf. It was the yellow one. He had taken the sign out and taped it under the counter, which was the closest thing they had to a table top.

Jo held up a string that was nearly as long as her little finger. Maren had to think of a question. She wanted to know whether they'd fixed the pay. Jo and Mom had just driven in from a meeting with Devin. Maren had seen Mom take the notebook out of the car with both hands, still folded open to the page she'd shown him.

"Did he listen?"

Jo put the string on the table between them. "Yes. At first he said they always rounded to the nearest hour when a crew changed. I asked how that was supposed to work when people started one job at eleven and got a space to write at noon."

"What did he say?"

"He tried using the sheets he had. When he got to eleven, Nia moved the two sheets together and showed him the gap. He ran his finger along the spaces the way I run my finger down a row to check for beans. There was nowhere for him to stop in the middle."

Mom put down her knife. "He said he'd get the missing time added to the next check. For both of us. He wants to put a place for a transfer time on the shed sheet."

"What about the others?" Maren asked.

"He says he doesn't know who else it happened to. He's going to ask for the old sheets and get back to us."

Jo had put a handful of beans on her side before she answered. After she was done talking, she picked up one with a bend in the middle and laid it apart from the rest. She kept choosing the bent ones instead of the longest ones. At the end she showed Maren the little pile she'd made. "These are the ones I'd have missed in the field today," she said. "I know to go back over the row now, but I used to think the first pass was enough."

Maren wanted to ask whether she could see the new shed sheet once it was up. Before she spoke, Mom brought the beans over to the sink.

"Did you ever have a job on a farm before this?" Maren asked her.

"One summer when I was younger. I liked the work. I remember being afraid to take a drink in case I made the line stop."

"What happened if you stopped?"

"Mostly, people went around me. I wish somebody had told me they could."

Mom washed the dry ends off the beans and showed Leon where the water was dripping through the holes in the colander. Jo told him it was like a little shower for anything that could sit on top of it. They stood there until the dripping came to an end. Leon leaned over so he could count the last drops.

On her way out Jo told Mom that some of the crew wanted to talk after the next pay statements came. They lived outside the town and had rides as far as the meeting hall. If there was a place they could sit down together, they could work out what information they wanted to ask the farm for.

"Ask Rey," Maren said.

"The room where you go?" Mom said.

"It's open to anybody who needs it, unless somebody already has it. You'd need Ed for that." She thought of the white tray Ed had brought out. "He'll tell you what he can do."

Rey said the room was free Thursday after supper. Ed said it was, too, when Mom called the following morning. The way he said free meant there was no other group on the schedule. After Mom asked about staying later than seven, he had to check whether there was someone to close. Out in the garden, Tal and Maren were helping Rey pick green beans to take home. Tal was hunting for the bent ones now that Maren had shown him where they could hide behind the leaves.

When Rey came back from the phone, she said Ed had asked whether the crew would want the spare table in the room. It was at the back wall under a cloth. It was supposed to fold, but the bar that held up one end kept dropping when a person tried to move it.

"I'll have a look," Maren said.

The folding table was lighter than theirs had been. The top was white and seamless, with a dark line near the center that could have come off with soap. She found the bar under the top and tried to lift it into place. If she pushed at the wrong end, the near leg leaned out and pulled it away. As soon as she had to steady the leg, the bar fell again.

She asked Tal to hold the other end. He tried to lift, but she had him move his hand lower, where the bar dropped. When it was level he slid the ring that locked the brace into place. Rey tested it and brought two boxes over to put on it. The table held both boxes and Leon's tin of colored pencils.

"We can carry it wherever they need it," Tal said. He was looking over at the hall door. "Does your mom want us there?"

"She didn't ask."

They found brown paper and wrote out the date and time. Rey let them tape it up, then asked if they had left room for somebody to write another event below it. Maren moved it up so there were two inches clear. Ed put a question mark beside closing time while he checked with the custodian.

The sign made the meeting look settled. Maren wanted to look at the schedule a second time to be sure. She waited for Ed to open the binder. He looked for Thursday and found two rows already written in. The evening row had a pencil mark he could erase if they'd started with the right number. The other row said the hall committee would be there earlier in the day.

"That's the one Rey said we could go to," she said.

"Three o'clock. It's hard to move that one. We have to finish before people come in for the evening."

Maren thought of the sheet with the proposal for the shelf. It was still on Tal's dresser. She had made another copy with more space at the bottom. She had left the top line as it was. She didn't know how many of the people who could use a shelf could come in the middle of the afternoon and say it. Mom would be lucky to be back by five.

"Can Mom send what she wants to say for three if she can't get here?"

"If somebody gives it to me beforehand, I'll make sure they have it." Ed closed the binder. "I can put your names on the agenda, but I don't get to write the agenda myself. Rey knows who to ask."

Tal bumped her shoulder with his, then went to check whether anyone had left the cardboard city windows open. He made a habit of closing the paper windows every time he went out of a room. Maren thought he might be ready to try something else if she could tell him what else there was to try. She made a note of the committee hours for Mom and taped it below the message about the work meeting.

At home she told Mom about the table. "You can put it near the window or by the wall. We know how to get it open."

"We can manage a table," Mom said.

Maren put both hands up. "I bet you can. I just thought you might want to know which ring."

Mom set the beans beside the sink, smiled and asked her to show her later. They found a long piece of masking tape and used it to measure a rectangle on the kitchen floor the same size as the folding table. They stood beside it, side by side. There was space for four people to sit and a fifth to stay back by the wall. Mom tried standing in the corner with her notebook and found a spot where she wouldn't block the door.

"If I sit second from the left like that," she said, "could you still open the drawer?"

"Sure."

Mom pulled the first tape up. The ends stuck together and made a loop. She slipped it over Leon's wrist. He asked right away if he was allowed to go to the meeting, since he was already wearing a bracelet for it. She said they could talk about that later. For now what she needed from him was to pick a serving bowl.

This time he picked the yellow one. Before she sat to eat, Maren put four equal piles of beans on a plate for Leon to compare. Two were straight and two were bent. He ate a crooked one first and closed his eyes. He said he was trying to tell which side the good taste came out of. She didn't tell him the quick answer. She waited for him to have a question he wanted her to hear.

## Chapter 8. Cucumbers

Pilar had just cut two cucumbers from her aunt's vines. She split the first one lengthwise while Tal sharpened a pencil at the counter. Pale seeds lay against the knife. He wanted to use another half for a boat and see if it could float with those seeds still in it. Pilar gave him a shallow dish of water and said that if he ran out of room at this counter he might begin to understand the trouble with their bedroom floor.

The cardboard city sprawled across the rug. A little row of chairs beside the painted river had caught on a loose thread. Maren sat down to repair it. The new sheet for the committee lay on the dresser beside her. Tal had written their names at the bottom and left space for anyone else who wanted to sign. His pencil was ready. He wanted to leave right then so they'd reach the hall office in time to put it on someone's desk.

"We have to find Mom first," Maren said. "She wanted to put a note in about the time they meet."

"She can send it later."

"Look at the time."

Tal looked at the clock. It was nearly two. If they took the hill down past the school, he said, they could get to the office and be home before Mom was back. He lifted the paper. Maren put a brush on the back of his hand to hold him still.

"Somebody needs to check the bottom of this before we go," she said. "What if the number for the office is wrong?"

"You copied it out of the binder."

"Maybe it's a different number after three."

"Well, if we go now we won't need it."

The cucumber floated until Tal put his pencil across it. Then it lay quietly on the bottom of the dish. He took his pencil out and drew a wet line on the proposal by mistake. It dried before they left. He was still embarrassed by it when Rey waved them over at the foot of the school hill.

Rey had the names of the people who wanted to be heard, in order. The people at the hall had decided to put off the shelf request until the following meeting. They were getting bids on a roof repair and needed more time to talk about the cost. There was still a few minutes for letters. Rey could read the proposal after the bids if they wanted to leave it with her. She could also ask for another time when the committee would be able to hear more people.

"Is there a time after five?" Maren asked.

"They can talk about it. What changed?"

Maren said that if people needed rides after work, people who left before five had a better chance of coming to talk. Rey asked what that had to do with the cupboard. Maren said she didn't want just one adult who lived here all the time telling the committee which shelves the children needed. Rey had put her papers in a folder without closing it. At this, she drew their proposal back out.

"Can you stay for the last part today?" she asked.

"I can ask. But we went through the city to put our names on it. We haven't asked the other people whose things don't fit in that cupboard."

"That's something I hadn't thought of."

Rey asked Maren to write that down separately. Tal held the folder steady against the fence while she wrote. It was a bumpy fence. When she rubbed over one of the words to make it easier to read, the pencil tore a little hole in the paper. The word itself was still there.

They brought the folder inside and waited while people took seats. The roof repair drew quite a crowd. A man brought a small piece of plaster from the back wall and held it up like something he'd just dug out of his yard. When he turned it to show a water stain, a corner broke off in his hand. A woman asked how long the patch on the roof would hold. It was an old question to everyone else. To Maren the crack was wider than she'd noticed when she was in the room every day. She looked up while the committee spoke and tried to see where it began.

At the end, Ed read two letters. Rey read their new form as a suggestion for the next meeting. She read the note about who should be heard, including the people who couldn't get there at three. A person in a green shirt said some evening times would work and some would be harder because of the hall schedule. The committee agreed to start the next one at six if someone could stay later to close. Ed said he'd check. Maren noticed he used a pen to write down the suggestion. He still used pencil on the calendar.

Afterward Tal said he'd thought Rey would read out only the three lines of the form. He'd been surprised when she read their note first. "It sounded like she was talking about how they run the whole room."

"Maybe she was."

"That's more than a shelf."

Maren knew that. She had never asked anybody in charge to do more than give her back something she had lost. The paper in her hand had gone further. This time she couldn't point to one thing with a label that said it belonged to her. She had to ask which part of the room ought to be available to people she didn't know yet.

After the meeting a boy tried to push a bicycle through the back gate. The middle part was narrow, and his handlebar made a little scrape. The gate had been open all day but he needed to turn his front wheel to get through. Rey asked him to wait until she could hold the gate all the way back. He set his bicycle down. He had somebody's paper in an inside pocket and wanted to find out whether it made any difference if Ed got it the day after a meeting.

The paper was from his grandmother. She had come for an evening program two years earlier and had got one date wrong. Once she was looking at the new copy of the calendar she'd be able to tell them which day worked. Rey said the main thing they needed to know now was what time she liked to come. She could tell them whenever the boy had a chance to ask her.

"She works till six on Tuesdays," he said. He turned the paper over and showed Rey that his grandmother had printed two times herself. The easier one for her was a Saturday afternoon. Nobody had mentioned Saturday in the discussion about the next meeting.

Maren found herself drawing a dot on the side of her planner. She wanted to put Sunday beside it. Then she asked him what sort of event his grandmother wanted. He said she liked to come when the games were out. She sorted the small pieces into jars if she came early. There were things a person might ask her about if she could get there before everybody started. Maren wanted to leave room on the next page for Saturday and Sunday, in case she'd been thinking of one kind of event and the rest of the town had been thinking of others.

When Mom came to the hall that evening, she had a plastic folder and a damp cloth wrapped around another cucumber. She'd found the vegetable in the crate of extras by the shed. The crew members filed in one at a time. One brought a notebook with a rubber band around it. Two brought nothing. Jo held the door until the last person had come through, then put the extra chair beside him.

The crew moved the folding table closer to the back wall so nobody could see their papers from the entry. Maren showed Mom the ring once, then held the door for them. Afterwards she took Leon outside to eat the cucumber in thick slices. The skin was tough enough that he pressed it against his teeth to make little half moons. In the last part of the meeting, somebody laughed loudly. A bike came along the road right then and rang a bell. For a moment it sounded as if the person riding was laughing, too.

The crew came out with a short list. Before the next pay period they wanted a new line to show when a crew changed jobs and a clear place to ask for a correction. They also wanted Devin to let the crew check the old sheets. A woman named Kira said she had never kept her own hours, but she had saved messages about urgent loads and when trucks left. With two people looking at the same day, they could sometimes work out when the crew had switched.

"Was there room for everyone?" Maren asked Mom after the others had gone.

"A little more than I figured. We moved the chair from the corner to the side." Mom rubbed her shoulder. "I thought I'd say the whole thing at once. Turns out people knew pieces I didn't know."

Before they left, Ed lowered the window on the office door to talk to Rey. At six each evening, he explained, the custodian locked the office and went home. Thursday had worked because he himself had volunteered to wait. He could do it again for the next committee meeting, but he couldn't commit to doing it every week. Rey had offered to take a turn. The room still had to have someone responsible for it if they opened it after hours.

Tal put the last cucumber slice he was carrying on Leon's napkin and went inside to ask about the next day. He and Maren had planned to bring the model back down for a few hours. Ed said the room would be available unless there was work on the ceiling. He looked up. At the corner over the cupboard, a faint line could be seen even without the lights on. He didn't know if it had got longer. He said he would check in the morning.

At home Leon wanted to use the cart to bring the city over. Maren told him they'd need to check before picking anything up. She had seen the committee look at something that had broken at a corner and decide it needed their time. She understood why. She still found it hard to watch a different problem fit into a space she'd saved for hers.

They laid their copy of the proposal beside the blue drawer knob upstairs. Maren added a line in the space at the bottom: `Find out who could open and close the hall in the evening.` Tal looked at it over her shoulder. He added: `And how we tell everyone when it's open.` Maren started to ask how many people he meant. She stopped when she saw that he was making the letters small so there would be room for another line.

## Chapter 9. Summer Squash

The summer squash Rey had cut that morning and left for Maren on the garden bench was the color of an egg yolk. It was also long enough to stand on one end and tip all the way into the grass. Leon wedged it between the handles of the red cart so it could ride home without doing that. He was about to ask Rey whether she wanted the rest for the hall when he saw the paper taped to the front door.

The big word on it was `CLOSED`. A pane of glass made the top of the paper look bright enough to read from the garden. At the bottom, past a strip of wrinkled tape, it said the garden and the outdoor benches could still be used. The hall and the washrooms inside would stay closed until the ceiling had been made safe. For questions about an event, people could call the office or check for a date on the door.

"What if our event is right now?" Maren said.

Tal pushed the handle. It didn't move. He pulled his hands back and read the smaller print a second time.

"We can put the city on a bench," he said. "I can draw a new piece here."

Maren stared at him. "We came to bring back what we already made."

"We can't bring it inside."

"I can see that."

She had two corners of the map rolled into the cart beside the squash. The whole city would go on the folding table if they could get to it. Lately she had wanted to open that table herself and set the cardboard down without asking Tal how much room he had in his bedroom for it. She knew exactly where she would have put the first row of houses. She could still see the table in her mind, which made the locked door worse.

Tal fished a folded piece of paper out of his pocket and ran his thumbnail along the edge to smooth it. He was already making a new map. She recognized the shape he drew for the stairs, with four lines and a little bend at the bottom. When he put an arrow in front of the door it was too large, and he rubbed it out.

"Are you just going to act like it's fine?" she said.

"No. I'm going to see where it is fine to sit."

She took a crayon out of the cart. Above the white strip that said to check back for events, she tried to write a message about how long they'd waited for the cupboard. The wax slid across the glass. Before she could decide whether to try the paper instead, Rey came around the corner with a stack of clean towels. She asked Maren to put the crayon down while they talked.

"We had a plan," Maren said. "It keeps getting moved. Who's supposed to tell us?"

"I should've called early this morning. The plaster broke away last night. The committee can't leave people in there while the repair crew is working overhead."

"Do they know how long?"

"They'll look at the ceiling this afternoon."

Rey set the towels on the nearest bench. Tal asked if he could see what other spaces the town used. His mom had mentioned a room beside the school gym where his class had gone when it rained. Rey said there was a long walk from that room to the public washrooms and a flight of stairs at the short entrance.

"Maybe we should just ask the school," Tal said.

Maren paced to the end of the walk. She could have walked home from there. She had a cart and somebody who would notice if she took too long. The people coming later to talk about work took two different roads to get here. Some might come straight from a ride, with no place else in town to wait. She thought of Mom looking through the hall window and seeing the white folding table with nothing on it.

"Could we put that on the sign before someone comes after work?" she asked.

Rey had a message out to Jo already. There was a chance the crew could meet on a covered porch at the school if it stayed dry. She asked Maren if she'd go around to the garden entrance and check whether there was a notice there, too. There wasn't. From that end of the building, you couldn't see the front door at all. Maren called to Rey. When Rey brought over a second copy, Maren asked for a blank sheet and wrote `Check here for a new place to meet` in thick letters. She set the words low enough that Leon could read them without stretching his neck. Then she helped Rey tape the sheet below the notice.

Tal was sitting on the low wall with his map on his knees. He had sketched the bench, the school and the porch, as if they were blocks he could pick up. When Maren told him about the crew coming from work, he drew two dotted lines toward the front of the hall.

"Where do the rides actually stop?" he asked.

"By the pile of gravel. Your mom stops at the door, but not everybody can."

"Is there a sidewalk from the gravel?"

She went to look. The side of the path by the gravel was packed dirt, with a dip that held water after rain. She knew from pushing the cart across it that one bad wheel could make the whole thing turn. There was a paved strip to the main door from the street. To reach it from the normal pickup place, someone would have to go out by the road and come back.

They tried following the strip with the cart and the two rolled corners of the city. The ditch was closer to the street than Maren had guessed. When they went single file, Tal ahead of the cart and Maren behind it, Leon had to leave one hand free to hold the squash. Along the narrowest stretch his shoulder brushed a bush. He stopped to see if it would brush him the same way coming back. Maren asked him to stay where he was until they were past the road.

They drew the paved way on Tal's map and brought the map to Rey. His arrows made clear which part of the road had no path. Maren had written beside it that a new place needed a safe place to get dropped off. The more she looked at the sentence, the more she wondered whether they should call it a safe place or just say what made it safe: enough room to get out, and a path all the way to the door.

Rey read the new words and asked whether she could copy them for the office. Tal said she could take the map if he put the benches in first. He added their squash as an oval by the garden. Leon said it looked like a puddle. It was supposed to be big enough to see, Tal told him.

At lunchtime Pilar came to take them home. She had brought three washed forks and a bag of noodles. She had meant to mix lunch inside the room. Instead they ate at the bench, with the cart pulled up close. Maren cut the squash with a kitchen knife Pilar had carried in its cover. The slices made a little squeak when she lifted them with wet fingers. Pilar stirred them into the warm noodles and gave Leon the spoon to check whether the part he'd rescued from the grass was too big.

Tal unrolled half a street on his lap. Maren had spent all morning wanting both corners of the city out of the cart. Now one corner came loose and cracked right at the fold. It had got bent under the weight of the squash when they went around the ditch.

"That's the corner of the bakery," Tal said.

"I know what it is."

He looked up. She could see that he had an answer ready. He waited a moment, and instead of saying it, he got out the roll of paper tape. "Do you want to do it or do you want me to?"

She wanted to tell him where to hold the paper. She wanted to show him the break before he saw it by himself. She wanted another corner, one that had never been bent.

"Hold this end," she said.

He held it while she set the edge. The tape would have to show across the brickwork. She used the blunt end of the pencil to draw the bricks over it one by one. When she finished, he turned it toward the light. The new bricks were darker than the old. He thought it made the bakery look as if a window had been filled in when a different family owned it. He was going to make a sign for the family that used it now.

Back at home the garden squash was missing a middle piece, but it still looked longer than it ought to be. Maren wrapped it to save for supper. She told Mom about wanting to write on the door and about what she'd put near the garden instead. Mom asked who she wanted the note to reach. Maren said she was glad the work crew had heard from Rey, but they weren't the only people who would read it.

"They can read the small part when they're standing here," she said. "They can't read it when they're deciding where to get out."

Mom fetched the notebook with her first record of hours. On the next blank page she wrote down a question for the hall. Where could somebody with no ride go after five to find out whether a room had changed? She handed the book back to Maren. Maren wrote `ask Tal` underneath. The question was his as much as hers. He had looked away from the door when she still wanted it to open.

## Chapter 10. Eggplant

The eggplants Pilar had picked that morning had glossy skins and green caps that felt rough if you ran a finger against them. She had borrowed a long-handled reacher from the school room so she could pull her gloves down from a high shelf. Leon asked whether it would take the cap right off a plant. She let him try it on an eggplant she had already cut. He picked up the whole vegetable and lowered it into the basket like a pilot bringing a plane down through clouds.

Maren was there to borrow a ruler. On the map Tal had drawn a person could walk to the porch, but the extra arrows needed words. She wanted to get the words straight enough that somebody could read them beside a line of people. The committee might be able to use the school porch for a meeting while the hall ceiling was open. For today, Rey had permission to use the nearby classroom. She needed to know what directions to give families before they set out.

They brought the eggplants with them, in case anyone wanted to take one. The long route from the school parking lot led around a fence and through the main doors. The short route went through a side door from the porch. From the sidewalk the doors looked the same, with a round knob and a long rectangle of glass. Maren went up the stairs to the side door. She could see Tal inside, walking down the hall from the other direction. His steps fell out of time with hers, then came together as he got close.

"How long did it take?" she asked.

"I stopped to help two people find the room. I forgot to count."

"What did they say?"

"One thought the main door was the porch door. She heard the porch was covered, and all she could see at the main door was the sky."

They moved the sign for the room so it was inside the main door, facing the direction people walked. After that they needed to carry two benches away from the place where the line would turn. A man with a folding walker waited while Rey and another adult carried one bench down the hall. Maren had never seen a walker close with the push of a button. She stared at the little hinge until he let it click shut and open again. Then he asked her what she wanted to know.

"Does that go up the porch steps?" she asked.

"I can get it up there if my son carries it. I get myself in through this door."

"Did the map show you the right way?"

"The map's in the hall." He lifted one end of his walker and put it down just inside the entrance. "I was figuring out whether I could get in before I'd seen the map."

That afternoon, outside the classroom, a queue bent back on itself. A woman inside was asking people whether they had come about the changed room or to pick up their children. She tried to give each answer slowly enough to be heard. Every time a person stepped up to speak, the rest of the queue tightened behind them. The rest of the building was quiet. Maren could hear the crackle of a bag being squeezed near the back of the line.

The queue was a zipper. At the front, each person fitted into the place left open by the next. Farther back, it drew people in from both directions until there was no gap between them. Maren watched a father try to back out to let a child past. He couldn't move without making the people behind him take steps, too.

"Can we open another way through?" she asked.

"Which one?" Tal said. He stood on a chair to see around the adult in front of him. They found the coat alcove at the end of the hall. It was closed at both ends by the same rail. You could move one end away from the wall to make an open corner. Rey and the teacher moved it together. After that, a person could step to one side and wait for a child without standing in the line of questions.

Maren drew a new map of the walk to the room. This time she started on the sidewalk where the walker had been. She used one thick line for the route with no steps. She gave the porch a thin line and wrote `stairs` beside it. Tal watched her letter the word. "Which one are we telling people to use?"

"They can choose."

"Some people can't use one of them."

Maren nodded. That was why she was writing it down. She handed him the ruler because he could hold it in place while she drew all the way to the road. He didn't need to ask where the line ended.

The woman who was helping with the line asked how they'd learned which entrance worked. Maren wanted to tell her that the short route looked better until you'd tried it with a walker. Instead she asked the man if he'd like to tell her. He told her about the two routes and the time he'd once got his sleeve caught in a rail. He was laughing by the time he showed them the spot on his sleeve. He wanted both routes on the map. He asked whether they could write down which entrance had the door that opened by a button. Maren gave him her pencil and let him find a place for it.

The classroom had desks in rows, but most people there wanted to talk in small groups. Maren and Tal carried the empty eggplant basket off to a shelf while Rey showed a group where to meet. Some people were waiting for a parent from the farm. Others were in town for a game or to get a jacket they'd left. People spoke to one another in whispers because this room had always been a classroom to them. Rey told them it was all right to move a chair around, and the sound of scraping legs went from one end of the room to the other.

Maren picked up the basket to take it out. The man with the walker asked what it had held. When she said eggplants, he told her he had lost a whole pan of them once by answering the door while his brother moved the pan from the oven to the stove. He had come back to find his brother and his cousin sitting at the stove with forks. Now when he took a pan out, he counted the forks before he set it down. "That way I know whether I've got time to go and answer the door."

Tal laughed first. Maren offered him the last small eggplant, which had rolled out of its bag and under a desk. He asked which two people they'd promised the others to. She told him and held the basket while he put it in the bottom. He wanted to see whether his brother would try cooking just one.

Afterward Maren and Tal sat on the porch. You could hear the shoes of people taking the stairs and the faraway sound of cars pulling out of parking spaces. Tal said he liked the little room better once they'd moved the desks. Maren liked the low windows at the old hall. At the school you could see the tops of passing heads but you couldn't tell where people were going from the way they moved below the windows. She would have missed seeing the driver who'd taken the cart.

"I bet people miss stuff here, too," Tal said.

The porch now had two signs. In the morning there had been only one. Maren went down the steps to see if a person walking from the road could read either one. From the far side of the tree the word `stairs` was too small. She asked Tal to get the file of used paper. He ripped a sheet down the fold and handed her the bigger half.

"I was going to take a picture of it," she said.

"After you make the letters bigger?"

She laughed and picked up the marker. She made the first letter almost as tall as the sheet. He held it at arm's length while she walked down the sidewalk to check. She could see it from where the gravel began. Then she asked him to stand on the lower step so she could see it from where the strip of pavement turned.

That evening Jo came by to show Mom a pay statement with a separate line for added time. She folded the paper down under the line so the rest of the numbers were covered. Mom took her own out of the folder. Hers had the line, too. Maren was happy a thing could change that someone had said would change. She went to look for a pencil, hoping she could put a check on the back of the first envelope.

Mom said the change was for the two of them. The crew still needed to hear what Devin had found on the older records. A worker named Kira had asked whether they could get that answer in writing, because she couldn't stay after her next shift. Jo nodded. She had two new names for people who might have worked during gaps. She was going to ask them if they wanted to be included in the note.

Maren let go of the drawer handle. She had felt for a minute as if the zipper could open from the bottom or the top if enough people moved their hands to the right place. She wasn't sure which end she had hold of now. Mom saw her standing there and asked her what they had found out about the new room. Maren brought the map to the counter. She showed Mom where the steps were, and how there were two ways through for people who could use either. The yellow bowl was sitting on the map. Mom lifted it away so that together they could look all the way to the road.

## Chapter 11. Sweet Corn

Leon came down the stairs one morning with a sheet of paper big enough to hide his face. It had a crooked ear of corn on it. The kernels were squares with the corners rounded. He'd colored them yellow except for seven in the middle. Those he left blank for somebody to eat.

The first sweet corn had come off the farm the day before. Mom brought home three ears, wrapped tight in their leaves. Maren could smell green stalks when she opened the refrigerator. Jo had warned them it would be hot in the rows when the crew went back to finish, so the farm was starting the shift earlier. The work crew had a cooler near the packing shed. Devin and Kira had carried another one down to the end of the corn rows so people could reach it without walking back.

"You should put a drink on the picture," Maren told Leon, who was waiting to be told whether it was finished.

"Wow, a picture of a glass," he said. "Do you want to stand in my museum all day and look at it?"

He drew in the glass anyway. Maren helped him hang the picture on the wall. The corner dipped. He stuck a third piece of tape right in the middle where it could do no good. Maren liked the picture better tilted, so she didn't tell him.

It was nearly noon when Jo brought Mom home. She came in first with Mom's bag. Usually Mom liked to set her own bag down where she could find it again. This time she walked straight past it and rested both hands on the counter. She said she was all right, in a voice that made Maren turn around.

"We stopped in the shade when she felt sick," Jo said. "Devin covered their row until we got a look at the rest of it. I drove us back early."

Mom pulled out a chair. "My head still hurts. I'll call the clinic now."

"Are you going back?" Maren asked.

"Tomorrow, if I'm well enough."

The `if` seemed to take up all the air in the kitchen. Maren went to the refrigerator and held it open until Jo asked for a glass. Maren thought about the kind of help she could do fast. She got two glasses and the pitcher of water, then put one glass and a plate on the counter for Leon even though he hadn't asked. Jo put the used cloth from her bag in the sink.

When Dad first got sick, the grown-ups had called each other in the kitchen. Sometimes they used ordinary words, like `home`, to mean a thing that would happen only if nearly everything got better. Maren had tried to do every errand so fast that no one could see she was listening. The better she got at folding towels into thirds, the more she could learn without asking. Now she had a water pitcher and a glass in her hand. She knew Mom could see her face. She wanted something to fold.

"Could you call me next time?" she said to Jo.

"When I'm driving?"

"After you stop. So I know."

Jo nodded. "I can tell you we're on the way."

She stayed until Mom asked her to pass the bag. Mom wanted to message the crew before people went back to the last rows. She asked them to keep the cooler where they'd put it. If it emptied, she asked, could the person who went to the shed to fill it say so before leaving? There was a brief reply from Devin. He'd asked another person to take the rest of the row and would tell the crew what they could leave until morning.

After a few minutes a message came from Kira. Next time she wanted a signal to use when she needed someone to cover her part, because in the corn she had to look hard to see people on the other side of two tall rows. She had kept picking for a long time that morning after she first felt too hot. She couldn't see the person ahead of her and thought she would miss the next truck if she walked to the end. By the time Kira called to the next row, everybody else was ready to go back to the shed anyway. She'd had to ask twice before somebody heard.

Jo had already taken her own name off the list for the afternoon. She told Kira she'd come by with a fresh jug of water if the person in charge of the row wanted one. Kira asked whether she could ride back with her if she stopped early tomorrow. They agreed to talk after dinner. Jo asked how many other people would be nearby if somebody called from the far rows. Mom said they could figure that out when they started. There was room on the board for a name at each end.

Maren knew the difference between the two women from the way they had asked. Jo had pulled up beside the row and been willing to drive anyone who wanted to leave. Kira had kept on until work slowed, then worked out how a quieter person might be heard. Maren could imagine a signal you could see above the tallest ears. She wanted to draw it on Leon's page. She didn't do that before asking Mom what she needed.

"For you to bring me the other chair so I can put my feet up," Mom said. "And for Leon to tell me what he was going to put under the corn in the picture."

Leon had been standing in the doorway. He came up to show her that he'd written a title in pencil. His paper was called `Seven Missing Pieces`, because he thought a museum picture ought to have a mystery in it. Mom read the title aloud and asked how the pieces had gone missing. Leon started to tell her about a little mouse that waited under the paper, where the tape went. Maren could still hear him twenty minutes later, when she went to get the bags of rice and salt out of the cupboard.

Maren and Leon went to the apartment steps for a while. Leon wanted to use the bit of shade just beyond the rail to read. The sun moved far enough while he looked for a book that half the bottom step was already bright. They took the book up one step. A person walking home past their driveway had a dog with short white legs. It stepped onto the grass when it left the sidewalk. The person waited and held the dog's lead with an open hand.

Leon looked at the house and said he thought Mom had been talking about Dad when she said tomorrow. Maren closed the book where her finger was.

"I thought that for a minute, too," she said.

He looked at the upstairs curtain floating in the open window. "But tomorrow is just a day, isn't it?"

"Right now it is."

They sat long enough that when the book door opened in the story, Leon couldn't remember why the person was going through it. Maren offered to go back a page. He wanted to tell his own version first. He said a mouse pushed the door because it could see what was on the other side. She said there was a different animal on the page. He said his mouse might still be right about opening a door. She slid her finger along the picture until he got quiet. Then they started again one sentence before the noise. This time he could hear the part before the door opened, so he knew what it was doing there.

Afterward Leon asked her to keep reading while he went inside to see if Mom was awake. Maren kept her thumb under the page. She sat on the step alone but still close enough to hear him ask which chair in the kitchen had the folded towel on it.

In the late afternoon the crew got a message from Devin. They could try a signal the next time they worked a crop that hid people from one another. He'd keep an extra set of jugs ready near the shed. On the time sheets he had filled in corrections where the crew's messages matched the arrival of a truck. For other days, he said, people would need to tell him what they remembered. Mom said she'd want the crew to see a list of the dates he still needed, so people could say where they'd been without putting their pay in a group message. She put the phone down and went in to rest as the nurse at the clinic had suggested.

When Pilar came to check on them, she brought fresh ears of corn from her aunt. Maren showed her how to start a tear in the outer leaves, then let Leon show her the better way when he had figured it out. The leaves fell away with a little snap at the stem. They set the cobs on a clean towel. One cob still had a thread curling beside every other kernel. Leon picked them off, one thread at a time, and put them in a little row on the corner for the mouse.

After supper, Mom came out to sit with them at the counter. She looked less pale than when she'd first come home. She ate half a cob while Maren told her about the last patch of houses the children had put up at the school. The newer children had put a large pond right next to one corner. "It's a pond because they can only draw islands around the outside," Maren said. "If they drew them in the middle, no one could reach."

"What does Tal think?"

"He wanted a station there."

"Can you fit both?"

Maren looked at the way the cobs had been laid out, one bit hanging over the towel where Leon had cleaned the threads. "I think we can find room."

Later she carried the corn leaves out and came back to check on Mom. Mom asked her to bring the phone so she could set an alarm early enough to call the farm before the shift. Maren brought it. She watched Mom's thumb move the alarm from six to five, then back to six so she could get more rest and still call.

Maren turned to go to her own room. She'd meant to carry the bowl into the kitchen, but she was holding it like something she needed to hold even though the two kernels in it weren't going to spill. She set it on the windowsill under Leon's tilted picture. Standing very still, she waited to hear whether Mom had gone back to sleep. She heard her turn a page. Then she heard a second page, slowly, as if there were time for it.

## Chapter 12. Peppers

Pilar's aunt picked the first ripe peppers and brought them in a paper sack. She had rolled the top down. Most were green. One had a stripe like somebody had started coloring it red and put it away. Leon wanted to cut it with the red part on one side so he could make a face. Tal wanted to weigh it to see whether it was as heavy as the green one the same size. Pilar put both on the counter and said they could each do the thing they wanted if they left her something to put in supper.

Maren was measuring a stack of cardboard for the school room. For their next project they were trying to make a scale model of the meeting hall. They already had the city and could use it to show where people tried to walk. Tal thought it would be easier to show where steps went if the model had steps of its own. Maren was trying to get each one the same height. She had a piece of the original floor plan Rey had copied. It only showed where the hall met the street. The back edge ran off the side of the page.

"How long do you want these stairs?" Tal asked.

"The same as the other ones."

"The other ones go to somewhere. These go to a line."

He had peeled up a little corner of cardboard to show her. She pushed it flat. It rose every time she let go.

The room could take a while longer to open. The committee had to wait until the roof was dry to finish inside. In the meantime they had set the next meeting for six at the school. All the families who used the old room were invited to come look at options for when the hall opened again. A bigger room or more shelves might sound better to someone who could picture only the cardboard city. A better way to tell people when the door was open might make more difference to someone who had waited by a road.

Maren cut the first stair before she measured the place where it went. It was too long. She took more off the end. Now it was long enough to cover both sides of the spot but the shape of the corner was wrong. She reached for another strip.

Jo had stopped by after work and was eating half a green pepper like an apple. She watched Maren line up the strip. "My dad used to say measure twice, cut once," she said. "I'd do it once on the paper and once where it was going."

Maren put the strip down against the model. The new line on it was exactly where the old one had been. She set the cardboard to one side and got Tal to help her set the map next to the door so she could explain the problem to him. He put his own piece down and looked at the two strips she'd cut.

"The steps go here," he said. He showed her the short fall in the angle between the road and the wall. "See the line they cut off the picture?"

"Where does it go once it gets cut off?"

They were both trying to use the model to make up what was missing from the picture. Maren took one of the shorter strips and put it in her pocket. They asked Pilar if they could walk down to the hall after dinner and see the place itself. She went with them and carried the bag of peppers to Rey, who was there to put a date on one of the signs.

The stairs at the back were narrower across the top than at the bottom. The railing flared at one end. Beside them a paved path led straight to a different door. It didn't look like a little step on the map because it was barely higher than the yard at the top. Maren asked if that door went to the room with the long shelf. Rey said yes. It used to have a key that was kept in an office drawer, because the handle didn't latch easily after snow.

"Could we tell people to use this way from the lot?" Maren said.

"They could use it if it were open." Rey stepped back and looked at the door the way Maren had looked down the path. "We can ask somebody to look at the latch. It'd be easier for people coming from the garden, too."

Tal put one hand against the way in that didn't have steps. He asked Maren what she wanted the new side of the model to look like. There was hardly any size to it if you drew it from the side. From above it looked like as much of a way in as the other one.

They went around twice, once the way people got dropped off by the road and once from the path in back. On the way out a person coming to tend the garden opened the back gate with her shoulder. She had taken the same route she always had, with a bag of tools on one arm. Maren asked how she would expect to find the door. The woman said she would look for the flag with the hall name, which was around the far side of the building. Maren asked where she would put it if she could move it. The woman looked around a long time before she picked a spot beside the gate. She wanted to be able to open the gate without brushing it with her shoulder.

Afterward Maren brought peppers home for Mom, who spread them out on a cloth and asked which of them were for supper. Maren chose the one with the stripe. It was missing a little triangle below the stem, where Leon had cut out a mouth. He had folded the triangle into a boat for the cardboard pond.

Mom said she wouldn't be able to go to the school with them the following week. One of the crew had invited her to see the new time sheet after a late truck. Jo had offered to go in her place, but Mom wanted to ask the people who worked the last shift what they were still missing. She'd take Maren to the school before dinner so they could rehearse putting their maps up. Pilar could take the children home after the meeting.

"Will they read a note if you can't go?" Maren said.

"Ed said they would. I was going to write about what we needed at the work meeting. Do you think it should go with the model?"

Maren had meant to fix only the front of the building. Now the strip she had brought in her pocket told her what she'd measured without having looked. She drew a separate model piece for the path by the garden. She set a block beside it where the cast-off cardboard stair had gone. Mom looked at it in silence a little longer than Maren expected. Then she said she'd like a path to the door from the place where the drivers dropped people off. "I didn't get anybody to tell me about the garden route," she said. "Can both ways be on the same page?"

They made a paper map of the school too, so the people who looked at one didn't confuse it with the other. Maren put large titles on both. She read the titles out loud and pointed to each map without looking first, the way Leon sometimes did with words he thought he knew. Halfway through pointing she caught herself and moved her finger to the right page. She switched the order of the maps. The school map would be posted first, so everyone would know where they were.

In the afternoon Tal came back with a photograph of the back entrance. The flag was off to the right in the photograph. The handrail, which both of them had drawn on their maps, was mostly hidden behind the gate. He asked whether the new map should show the side of the building with the flag or the side a person would first see if they came from the gravel. If they drew all the ways at once, people might walk around more than they needed to.

Maren took the photograph to the kitchen and set it against a jar. From the seat farthest away she could see a patch of brick and the big leaves of one plant. She could just tell there was a door. She had never relied on one photograph to find a place. She normally read some lines of an address and could read them again when they got there. She wrote `use the path beyond the garden gate` on a fresh sheet.

Tal wrote `go to the light at the back door` below her words. Pilar read both lines that evening. She asked whether the light would be on when the hall was closed. It might be easier to say what the door looked like where the path ended. Tal went back to the photo and picked the narrow window next to the handle. He ran a finger beside it.

"Would you be able to see that if you came at six?"

"We'll find out at six," Maren said.

Their dinner had been early. They asked Mom to walk over afterward, while there was time to see where the light landed. She waited at the gate with the folded pepper sack in her hand while they walked along the path three abreast. In the end Maren gave her the words without the picture. Mom found the door on her first try.

There was one more check she wanted to make. She took her drawing out to the entry. The shared entry had two squares, with the clear way between them. Leon's tag hung from the red cart. She looked at it from where someone actually stood to read it. A corner of the wall cut the last wheel from view. She changed the hole the tag hung through so the picture faced forward. "Try it now," she told Leon when he came out.

He ran from outside to the entry with a pepper seed on his cheek. He put his face right up to the tag until their two heads almost touched.

"Back by the door," Maren said.

He stood back and told her what he could see. A red cart. Four wheels. A loop where the artist had drawn Dad's hand. He could see it before he could touch it. Maren was pretty sure Jo's dad would call that a second measurement.

## Chapter 13. Tomatoes

Tal picked the ripest tomatoes from the school garden for the meeting. He brought them in a box with paper between the layers. Somebody at the school had put out plates and glasses for water, and he thought people might want something small to eat while they waited. When he set the box down, a teacher asked him to move it so the second half of the table would be empty. Maren got one of the tomatoes out and put it on the far edge of the box to hold down the paper.

"Careful," Tal said. "They roll if you don't keep the stem down."

She turned the tomato over. It was a tiny one, round as a marble except where the skin wrinkled at the stem. The wrinkled part was just enough to stop it.

The old form for reserving a shelf was clipped to a board. Two notices from the hall were also on the board. One promised an update the next week. The other asked which hours would work for people when the hall opened again. Some people had never seen that one because it had gone up at the hall after they stopped coming there. Ed left copies next to the glasses and said people could fill them out after they heard what the schedules might be.

Maren had wanted to bring the cardboard city to show how much shelf space it took up. It didn't fit on the table with the model of the hall, so Tal had taken only the bakery, which was the part with the crack taped over. On the table it looked like a little building that had been knocked off a shelf. She thought she could put it next to the scale model to show how much space one wing needed. Tal asked her if she would wait until Ed had explained the agenda before she moved anything.

Ed read the date and time. Two committee members had brought a pile of messages from people who couldn't be there, even at six. The first was about the repair. The second was about the shelf request. Next to each was a bit of space for the committee to write its answer. When Ed finished reading the second message, a person at the front asked whether the old year-round address rule could stay in effect up to the day the hall opened. It seemed easier to reopen with a form people knew.

Maren was holding her own sheet. She could see the pencil strokes from all the names, including the one Leon still wrote with the last letter backward. She meant to hand it to Tal so he could read the first line. Instead she pulled it close to her chest.

"There are people here now who couldn't reserve a shelf with that form," she said.

"I understand," the person said. "We're wondering whether we know a way to do it that won't leave the shelf full of lost things."

"We put down that you could call us." Maren had spoken hardly above a whisper. She tried again. "People can live here when you put their stuff away and when they come to get it. Just use the dates they want."

A woman two chairs away asked whether a group had to have a parent in town all year to use the room at all. Ed said that a group could meet in the room on an open day without reserving anything. The woman said she wanted to know about groups that carried supplies. She helped lead a game night for people who shared a ride home. They brought game boards in three bags. When they got to the room after work, all three bags had to come out of whoever's car they'd put them in. They wanted a place to leave one bag, so that if a ride fell through the others could still start a game.

Tal pulled the bakery over to where she could see it. He wanted to know whether she could picture the size of the bag next to it. She put her fingers down a foot apart and said the game bag would be wider. He gave her his extra pencil and held a sheet of paper down for her to draw.

The person with the old form looked at the drawing. "If we do this by dates, how do we decide when a group wants a shelf for the rest of the year?"

"You can ask them to choose a date on the form," a man said. "Ask when they need it and when they can clear it. That's what we did for the stage at the school."

"Should everybody get the same amount of time?"

The person who asked that had sat so quietly that Maren had made up a story in which she hadn't wanted to be here. Now she set a little paper clock on the table. She said her group used the hall once a month. A weekly signup meant they might not be able to leave their things between meetings, even when no one needed the space.

Maren was about to say they could all start with a week, the way she and Tal had put down. Then she remembered the man with the boots. He'd asked for a date he could keep. It might have been better to ask him when he met. She drew a single line through `one week` on the draft she held. She wrote `check the calendar with the group` and showed it to Rey.

At the next chair a boy dipped his finger into a cup of water and tapped the corner of the paper with it. His dad found him a napkin. Maren waited while he mopped up the drops. Then she asked the committee if they would try the new form with all the groups for the first month. If it left a problem, the office could write down when it happened instead of guessing what kind of people would cause it.

Rey put out Mom's note at that point. Mom had sent a picture of the gravel pickup area in the morning and a short description of how the work group used the hall. She said she wanted to hear whether someone who came on a ride knew where to go if the hall was closed. Maren held the scale model up while Tal pointed to the drop-off and to the route they tried when the cart went sideways. He showed the paved path on the garden side. The committee wanted to make sure there was light on both routes if they opened the hall at night.

A person named Mel asked whether they could choose which door to repair before the opening day. Rey explained about the latch. A second person wrote down the name of the custodian who could take a look. They agreed people needed to know how to get in before they proposed later hours. Then Ed asked whether he could copy the drawing of the school route for a person who had followed the wrong sign this evening. Tal took the pencil back and added an arrow by the door with the button.

The meeting went a little past seven. Nobody wanted a tomato by the time they got to the shelf vote except the small boy, who took two. The committee agreed to try the new form when the hall opened. In the meantime they would post a copy at the school and accept requests there. Groups could use the same contact and date fields regardless of how long they lived in Bracken. The committee decided to look at actual requests before saying how many shelves each group could use or how long it could hold one.

Maren put her own copy in the folder. Some of the things she'd thought ought to be decided were still questions. She felt oddly light, like when she'd taken a heavy winter coat off at the wrong time of year and found she could use her arms. She waited until the woman with the games finished copying the school map and asked how many people they got at game night.

"It depends," the woman said. "Do you play any games that fit in a coat pocket?"

"I know one with buttons."

"Bring it the next time we're here. Better bring enough buttons to share."

Outside, Tal offered to carry the model or the tomatoes. Maren took the tomato box. It was lighter now. There was still one at the edge, holding the folded paper in place. She touched it with one finger and set it rolling to the other side. Tal waited until she'd put the lid on before lifting the model. He knew the front of it hung a little lower and needed his second hand.

Pilar had parked close to the path, where the lights from the school reached just as far as the side of her car. She was talking to a teacher about a grow box the children could use at school. Maren put her own paper next to the door light so she could read the names again. Two people had added theirs in the room, below the spot where she had left extra space. One of the names ran into the margin. They had written it sitting sideways to use the table, which she could tell by which way the letters leaned.

"Does it count if we're only trying it?" Tal asked. He buckled his belt with the model between his knees and the seat.

"It counts for the first month."

"What about after that?"

"Then we'll know if it works."

He tried to get a better look at her copy. She folded it so only the blank space they had left at the bottom showed. Below the last name she wrote the first day on the trial calendar. She didn't draw a box around it. She wanted there to be a little extra room if it turned out somebody had to write something else.

## Chapter 14. Carrots

The first morning school opened, Mom let Maren take a carrot with its green top still on. One of the crew had brought a bag of them to the shed and said if the rest went home in the same bag it would be too much for her arms. Jo had taken some for soup. Mom had picked this one because the leaves curled around her finger when she reached for it.

Leon told Maren the carrot looked like a bird. If she laid it in the grass outside the front door, he could see where it had left two little footprints. She told him there were no orange birds that big in Bracken. He put his coat on and said he would count how many he saw on the way to school.

On the first day the coat hooks were labeled, and the labels told Maren exactly where her name belonged. She was old enough to find her own room from its number. She used the visitors' map anyway, because the map she and Tal had made for the hall meeting showed only one corridor. It had begun with the question of which door to use. Today she needed to know where to go beyond that door.

At the corner outside her classroom, Tal stepped out of a different line to tell her which way the lunchroom was. A teacher asked him to get back in place. He took two steps sideways and called over his shoulder that the carrot top would leave green bits in her bag. The same person had once laughed when he called across a room at the hall. Here their first real conversation lasted four words and one shouted guess about dirt.

At lunch Maren set her carrot on a napkin beside the rest of her food. A girl across from her asked if she lived on a farm. She could see how the girl had got the idea. Someone who had only seen the label with her address knew she lived on Spruce Street. Someone who had only seen the carrot might have a completely different story ready.

"My mom works on one," she said. "I live over by the hall."

"That's closed," the girl said.

"For now."

The girl's name was Imani. She had spent summer afternoons in a different room at the school, where some children could leave projects in a cupboard as long as they brought a bright tag. She told Maren about the girl who had tied her tag to a knitting bag with so much string she eventually had to cut it off. The leftover loops were now part of a tall knitted hat. Imani could bend an ordinary bendy straw into a figure eight without spilling her milk. Maren tried once and put a drop of milk right on the green carrot top.

Later their teacher, Mr. Santos, asked Maren to tell the class one thing about her summer if she felt ready. He had given everybody time to write one thing down first. Around her there were papers with titles like `My Cousin` and `Swim Race`. Maren had written `Find a Door`. She wanted to tell the class about the maps. For a second she thought she'd also tell them about Dad's old job at her school, to explain why she knew where the cart in their cardboard city would go.

She couldn't get that sentence out the way she had written it. She put a finger on the crossed-out part. Then she told them about drawing a sign big enough to see from a road. When someone asked what was on the sign, she showed him the way she had drawn the word `stairs`, with each letter built of short straight bars. Imani made a tall S in the air and laughed when it came out backward.

That afternoon Maren and Leon took a bag of clean towels to the farm after the last crew came in. Mom had sent a message saying she could ride back with Pilar and the children, and asked if they could stop by. Maren knew where the wheels had to stay in the pickup area. She had never been so close to the board after a day of school. Her arms ached from carrying three books around because she didn't know which ones to leave in the classroom. The two names at the end of the carrot line on the board had smears where people had leaned on them.

Kira was helping Devin pin the first copy of the revised time sheet to the empty corner. The new box was right between field and shed. Jo was reading the boxes from the outside toward the middle. On the line for her own crew she read aloud the time they had stopped pulling carrots and when they had started sorting. As she arrived at the next name she stopped. It belonged to another crew. She called a question into the shed and waited for that crew to answer it.

Mom came out of the shed carrying a wet towel. She took a look at the board and called to Jo that the new line in the middle said that she had started packing at ten. "I had already rinsed a set of crates by then," she said. "Does that still count as packing?"

Jo said she'd seen the crates pushed up against the drain before the driver had checked them in. Kira reached for the pen and asked how much earlier. The three women talked over each other a little. No one told them to stand in a line and talk in order. Devin asked whether they could all see the same clock from both places. In the end they figured out the driver kept a receipt with the order of the crates and the time the shed took them. Devin would have to leave the extra minutes blank until he looked at the receipt.

Maren had a question about what part of the old board had been changed. When she stepped up, Mom made a little space and let her see the new line. But when Maren tried to answer Kira's question about when the crew had stopped picking, Mom touched her arm. "Ask Jo. She was there."

Maren could picture Mom in their kitchen, giving her a single potato chip when she asked whether she could finish the bag. The touch meant there was room for what she'd asked, plus a limit she needed to see. She moved closer to the edge of the group and asked Jo to show her where she had been when the time changed.

They stood on the same spot by the door where Jo had taken a picture of the old board. From there a person could see both clocks through openings. One was outside with a red second hand. The other was inside behind a basket. On the end of the packing bench there was a carrot with two roots that went in opposite directions. Devin had set it aside for someone he knew who taught drawing. Maren asked to take a picture. He drew the basket out of her way before he said yes.

"Could you see both clocks when you were standing there working?" Maren asked Jo.

"Both after we moved the basket."

Devin rubbed a finger over a spot where the new sheet curled away from the board. "The basket goes back after pickups. I can hang the clock higher in the morning."

"Put it where you can see it from the drain," said Kira.

A driver had a set of short boards leaning against the side of the shed. He wanted to get them out of the lane before Pilar moved her car. The space between the truck and the wall looked wide enough from where Maren stood. The driver explained he'd tried it once with nothing in his hands, then found he couldn't turn when he came back with the boards. He needed another person near the corner to tell him which way to lift them. Kira volunteered. Maren rested the bag of towels on a post and watched them talk across the corner.

Next to the post, there was a place painted yellow where people waited for their rides. A person could see the truck through a narrow opening from there. In the powdered dirt outside the line Maren noticed wheels of three different widths and the pattern left by Leon's sneakers. She called to him to come back in and showed him where to stand. He wanted to explain his own route first. She let him trace it on the dusty edge of the pavement, then asked what he could see from inside the painted line.

The driver could still see them. He waved to tell them he was done. Kira set the boards one at a time against the wall. They tilted away from the corner. In school the same number of turns had got Maren from the coat hooks all the way to the lunchroom. Here she had made as many turns to learn why one place was a better place to wait. When she helped Leon dust off his hands, she asked him if he knew how to show Imani what he'd learned without drawing the whole farm. He drew one line across her palm and used his thumb to make a little person standing behind it.

In the car on the way home, Maren told Mom about taking four words to speak to Tal. "We weren't supposed to call across the line. At the farm you all just talked when you remembered something."

"Did you hear me by the sink?" Mom asked.

"I heard you before that. Everybody did."

"I had waited all day to find that out."

At the table that night there still wasn't a table. They ate on the counter with three seats. Two were the same height, and one was low enough that Leon had to sit on a folded towel. Maren told Mom about Imani and the tag on the knitting bag. She drew the two places on the back of a piece of paper for school. The school corridor stretched around a corner she couldn't see past. The farm was drawn with a large open space to stand beside the board. She drew an extra chair on the edge of the picture. It looked like somewhere you might use while you tried to hear. Then she rubbed out the chair and drew a window, because that part of the farm had no chairs at all.

In the morning she had thought the printed labels at school meant somebody knew where she ought to be. In the evening she knew their way worked best when someone already understood what happened behind each classroom door. She took the map out of the bag and penciled in the milk cooler, the washrooms and the bend she had missed on her first walk. Over the lunchroom she drew a little carrot with the top still on. Under the drawing of the farm, she put a clock high above the basket, where someone could look up from their work and read it without asking.

## Chapter 15. Cabbage

Rey cut a cabbage from the garden for the first open afternoon at the hall. It had grown in the garden during the weeks when no one could use the room. She and Leon had checked it each Saturday, first to see if it would have a head, then to see if they could fit their hands around it. On opening day her two hands couldn't meet around the middle. She carried it to the folding table to show the people coming in.

Maren put a big ring of folded newspaper under it so the cabbage wouldn't roll. The newspaper looked like a little white fence. She went to the cupboard and found the new reservation form clipped beside it, with a pencil attached to a cord. The loop of cord was long enough to reach both sides of the cupboard. The open shelf was divided into thirds, with a letter hanging over each part. The game group had the middle section twice a month. In the first section, three people had put construction paper in a pile and left a note about which side was for clean scraps.

Maren and Tal had the third section for three weeks. They'd been asked to get the city out in time for someone else to use the shelf at the end of the month. Maren wanted to go get it from Tal's room straight away. Tal said they ought to ask Bea which side to put toward the hinge. There was a tall part above the zoo with a flag planted in it, which neither of them wanted to bend.

Leon pressed one finger against the cabbage leaf that hung over the paper fence. "You could make a sign nobody can take it. Like the things we put outside for the truck."

"You could ask people before you take it out of here," Maren said.

"It's a cabbage."

"It's Rey's cabbage until she says what to do with it."

Leon ran a finger along the edge of the leaf. After a minute he asked if she thought Rey might let them eat it. Maren thought that was probably what she'd been waiting for him to ask.

An hour later the table smelled of vinegar and chopped cabbage. Rey and Pilar had cut most of the vegetable into strips. The shreds looked nearly transparent beside the bowl where the rest had been set to soak. A woman who had come in from the farm offered to carry one bowl to the next table. She turned sideways to get past a chair and had to turn back before she reached the edge.

A notice asked for someone to stand by the garden door for a little while. The new latch had been repaired, and they needed to find out whether the door opened easily from the path. A volunteer named Mel had put a stone beside the door to keep it open temporarily. Maren watched the door move slowly toward it when the wind fell. It stopped with a dry knock. The old door at her house had made that same sound when Noor's dog lay with one paw across the mat.

"Do you want me to hold it for you?" she called to a woman approaching with a bag of games.

"No, Maren, I need to see how it works when nobody's there."

Maren stepped back. The woman put down her bag and pushed the stone out with her shoe. Then she closed the door from the inside, walked around the hall and approached it a second time. She found the handle easily. There was a strip of shiny metal across the bottom that caught the toe of one shoe. She opened the door a second time when Maren asked if she would show Rey exactly where it had caught. The second time the woman put her foot down differently. Rey asked if she could look later with the custodian who had repaired the latch.

Out in the garden, Leon had put his hand across the lower part of another sign. He could cover every letter with his palm. Last time the hall was closed, he'd read a sign beside the garden because Maren and Rey had put it down low. Now he was trying to figure out who would have to crouch to read it. He held up the hand with the marker stain on it and stepped back to where they had put the bench on the map.

"It's where I can read it," he called.

"Would you go to the front and see if you can find the other one?" Maren asked.

"Can I finish eating first?"

"Yes, you can finish."

He rolled up a shred of cabbage and tucked it into his cheek. Maren stayed by the garden door long enough to hear a person read the hours, look at a watch and ask whether the time beside Monday was morning or evening. Maren knew the answer. She added `p.m.` next to the time so the next person could see. The paper under her marker was uneven, and the loop in p looked like half a butterfly. She colored it to make the time clearer.

Tal had brought the bakery in a shoe box. He wanted to be able to take it out, lay it on the chair by the cupboard and leave the shoe box on the shelf without shifting places. His arms wouldn't reach both places at once. Lane, the child in the game group who'd only seen the picture at the school, came up beside him. He thought the shoe box was for the pieces of the game. He started to slide it toward the middle shelf.

"Yes, we have a box," Tal said, "but this one's for us."

Lane looked inside. The little red strip Maren had laid on the roof was falling forward. He put the box down before it fell out. "I see it now. Where is it supposed to go?"

Tal pointed to the hinge. Lane held the door while he looked for Bea, who knew which side the old flag would swing to. They stood there long enough that Rey asked whether they were trying to decide what to keep. Lane said he thought they were trying to decide what went first. They had two things to put away and one hand free.

"We can use the floor if somebody can pick it up again," Tal said.

"Yes, but you won't be able to open the door, will you?" Lane answered.

Maren could hear them across the room. She had been about to shout where she thought the shoe box went. She let the two of them find a solution first. They put the shoe box on the chair, then exchanged places so the game bag went in on the left before Tal reached across. From where Maren sat, the first words she'd heard sounded like an argument. Now they were both trying to see whether the chair could stay there without being in everybody's way.

Ona May arrived carrying a toolbox from the apartment. She had promised to help the room committee put a second shelf up if the early trial showed they needed one. She pulled the folding table away from the wall so another work group could bring it over to the garden door. When the leg slipped, Maren showed her the ring. Ona May tried it again and got it locked on the first push.

"Did you go to see the cart yet?" she asked.

Maren knew it was in the entry at home. She shook her head, wondering whether someone had moved it again.

"I left a note with the handle. There's a little room out back that can be locked. You can park it there if your mom is going to keep renting. I thought you might want to know before signing anything."

Maren went straight to find Mom. She was making a plate for Jo, putting the bread next to a cabbage strip that was wider than the paper. Mom said she had asked Ona May about keeping the apartment through the winter. They'd agreed on a new rental term, starting the next month. It was still small, but they could get to school and work from there. The farm offered some winter sorting shifts, and Mom was applying for more hours nearby. She'd been waiting to tell them until she'd checked the rent and the end date.

"Did you ask about a table?" Maren said.

"I asked how wide the alcove is. You can ask about a table when you know where you want one."

The woman who'd turned sideways with the bowl came up to them. She asked whether Mom had heard from Devin about the last pay records. Mom said the dates they'd asked for were in the shed now. A few statements still needed corrections. Those people could use the folding table today to write down which shifts they remembered. Maren looked at the crowd around the food. At the school the committee had cleared half of the top for the model. Today people in front of the table were helping themselves to cabbage shreds over the same space someone would need to put a notebook down.

"Can we move it by the window, like you did the other time?" she asked.

"That can work, can't it?" the woman said. She looked over there. They'd have to shift a chair but the way in would still be clear.

They took the bowls off. Leon pulled the folded newspaper out from under the cabbage, which had been cut too low to roll anymore. The younger part of the group counted to three so two people could take the table by its ends. This time Maren let the person carrying nearest the door decide how far to turn. They made it to the window. Then they put the chairs where the people at the table asked for them.

Maren checked the ring under the leg first. She could feel it knock against the stop as it slid fully down. From there she could see people's shoes coming and going on both sides of the table. The first time she and Tal had set it up, she'd drawn a rectangle with tape in their kitchen and guessed where people would fit. She had put the extra chair in the corner. Today a person pulled it up to the curve of the window, took out her notebook and gave the other side of the table her whole attention.

## Chapter 16. Winter Squash

Rey and the garden volunteers had picked the winter squash the day before. Now they leaned against one another on the windowsill at the hall. The stems made a row of handles that pointed different ways. On the far end Rey had put a card that said where they came from and when anyone could take one. Leon picked up a small one, looked under it and asked Maren whether it was finished yet. She turned it over with him. The underside was paler where it had lain on the ground.

They were waiting to see what the committee wanted to do after the shelf trial. It had gone well enough that there was almost always a project on each shelf, with room for two groups to share part of the bottom one. The white board where Ed wrote the dates had arrows pointing to every shelf. One group had come back twice to ask for more time. The games group had only used its whole space once, when a bus driver left a separate bag to pick up after supper. Bea wanted to show the committee that there was room for the bus driver's bag and her folded parts of the city, if both could use a shallow tray.

"I thought we hated trays," Tal said.

"We hated that tray," she said. "This one's deep enough to hold what I want."

It had tidy corners. She had made it by folding an old box and fastening the ends with blue string. She showed him how to stand the city up by taking one piece off its paper hinges. Maren wasn't sure she wanted the long river to come apart. She set the squash down on the sill and went to look at what Bea had unfolded.

The cardboard city occupied almost the whole table in front of the cupboard. When Maren had first met Tal, she had wanted a town she could see all the way across. Now the city had so many sides that she no longer knew which direction was the front. At the near end, where Tal had put the shutters, Imani had placed a schoolyard with a knitted hat hung over the fence, drawn small enough to see only if you leaned close. The game tables were on the side facing the window. One had a plate with six buttons glued to it.

The corner she'd repaired with tape showed a little at the bakery. Bea started to pull on that part of the cardboard. Maren put both hands on the table.

"Stop. You'll tear it."

"I can undo it at the other fold. See?"

She laid the river flat and showed Maren the seam. It still made a river if you unfolded it all the way. It came apart where Tal's first bridge joined the original paper. Maren could have lifted one bank and watched the rest droop toward it. She pushed a finger under the bridge. Then she and Bea lifted two corners and held them apart so Tal could see how the pieces would fit in the tray.

On the other side of the room, Jo was waiting for a call from the farm. She said the last people who'd asked for corrections had heard back. One had needed to compare her statement with her work notes, and two had found a way to check their hours against a trip the other crew remembered. New shifts were showing up on the log the way they did on the checks. Jo was pleased the time change box was still right in the middle. She wanted that box left there when a new manager came in for the winter orders.

"Can you tell if it will stay?" Maren asked.

"The people who use it can tell you if it goes away." Jo looked across the room at Mom, who was setting out folded chairs. "They can say why both crews need it."

Maren went back over to the cupboard. She held a narrow strip of paper with one line she'd written out the night before. It said that the shelves could stay the way they were now. That was as far as she had thought. She'd read the sentence aloud three times at home, first for Mom, then for Leon and then because Leon liked the way she paused before `stay`. She slipped it into her pocket and got out a new one.

"You've got that look," Tal said.

"What look?"

"Like there's a piece you've been trying to see past."

Maren handed him the note. "Do we want everyone to keep using the same amount of space?"

"Somebody has to decide how much can fit. We have more to put away than most people."

"We had almost the whole table today."

"Other people did, too." He pointed to the line of small purple squares from which a game had been made and then pushed the city a little farther from the edge so it wouldn't cover them. "Ask Bea how much space she wants for the tray if somebody else gets to use part of it."

Maren asked. Bea put the tray on the table sideways and showed her how she could turn a piece to leave room for the game bag. Maren had planned to ask for space for the city as one piece. She wrote it down differently now. Instead of promising that the rule was good because it helped the city, she wrote what had happened since the form changed. The cardboard city could use one part of the bottom shelf, and the game group had found a place to keep a bag between the days it met. The little trail of folded paper across the bottom took the room of just one game board.

That was still only what she had seen. She brought her note to Ed and asked if the committee had heard from the people who used the room earlier in the day. He showed her three notes that had come in with the reservation forms. One asked for clearly marked times to pick things up. One said the group liked being able to leave a phone number instead of an address. A third said there ought to be help reaching the top shelf when the office was shut. Ed had written the number for the caretaker beside that one, so they could test whether he was usually there at the right time.

Maren read the notes a second time. Then she and Tal put a chair at the corner by the cupboard, where someone waiting for the caretaker could sit without closing the door. It wasn't a complete answer. Seeing the empty chair helped her form the question she wanted to ask.

Somebody had put a winter hours card on the end of the folding table. Maren checked the days printed beside the little squares. One was a Thursday, which she'd expected. The other was Tuesday, the evening Jo said she still got home late because her crew sorted for Wednesday. Maren would have picked Monday, since everyone in her apartment got home early enough to visit for a little while.

A person from the coat drive stopped at the end of the table and asked if she could use it in two weeks. She got to the hall only when a friend was able to walk with her. They had already made a plan for Tuesday. Maren asked how long the woman needed the table. The woman said she needed to lay all sizes of coats out. People sometimes tried two or three before they chose one. They'd have to move the table farther away from the wall than they had for the model.

Maren got a friend of Bea's to help her find four strips of paper and show her how to mark the space around the folding table. They measured one short end and one long end first. After they had got to the corner, a person coming out with a bucket made a detour around their paper. Maren went after her and asked where she had hoped to walk. She had only a short answer: she wanted to take her bucket out the garden door without spilling on the floor. Maren stepped back with the end of the paper and let a little more room show.

When she looked at the hours again, she could picture enough people to make more than one answer reasonable. On the back of the card she penciled a question about a third evening people might take turns using. She kept it below the first two days. Those had already let people make plans.

The committee kept the new form and the trial hours on two days a week, as long as an adult assigned to the room could stay. There might be enough money for a second shelf, but they wanted to watch where groups moved things first. Rey asked everyone to write down any time they needed help putting something away. Maren had got up to explain what Bea showed her. She used three sentences, not the two she wrote on the paper or the five she came up with while she was waiting. Bea spoke next. She said she wanted children to be able to reach what they were going to carry. The man who had asked for the door with a button raised his hand to agree.

When the room emptied, the squash had all been claimed except the small pale one. Leon asked if he could take it up front for the woman who'd been trying to see over the top of the cupboard. He and Bea carried it across the room, one hand each beneath it. Maren waited while they delivered it. She put her notebook in the cart. The two halves of the paper city went on top in Bea's folded tray, taking up less room than Maren had thought. There was room beside them for the yellow bowl, so she set it there and wheeled the cart out into the paved strip. Before she stepped down, she looked to see who might be coming through the same door.

## Chapter 17. Turnips

Maren thought the turnips would be the last thing left in the garden. Rey said somebody always found a row she hadn't seen. They went down the middle path with a digging fork and looked at the places where leaves stuck through the straw. Leon asked if the leaves in his pocket counted. He had brought them because he didn't want to start the day with nothing to put in the yellow bowl.

"Let's see what you find if you put those back first," Maren said.

He slipped the leaves under the straw. After that he chose a plant near the end of the row. Rey loosened the soil. Leon put both hands on the leaves. The first turnip came up with so little effort that he sat down hard, holding it upright in both hands. Dirt dropped down the front of his coat in dark crumbs. He waited long enough to see who had seen him. Then he tried to fall exactly the same way when he pulled the second one.

The turnips were white at the bottom and purple at the top. Maren rubbed a small purple bump with her sleeve. When she put it in the yellow bowl, it struck the little nick beside the rim and made a sound like a spoon. At one time she had thought this bowl was the only thing the rest of the kitchen would fit around. Now it had made plenty of trips by itself. She set it on her hip and started down the paved path, leaving room for Leon to carry the last root where nobody would bump his hands.

The red cart had three of its old wheels and a new one at the front. A person who sorted things at the reuse shed had offered Maren a wheel a second time. This time she had asked to see whether it was the same size. She brought the old wheel home too, in case it ever turned out to be good for another cart. The white arrow still pointed toward the wheel that used to stick. She could turn the cart to the left or the right without tugging it now. Leon wanted a second arrow to show the wheel was fixed. Maren gave him the paint and let him decide where to put it.

At the far end of the hall the cupboard was open. Bea was waiting to show somebody how the paper city folded, and the first thing Maren saw was her thumb resting right on top of the bridge. There were two other children there, both new to the room. One of them was tall enough to reach the middle shelf without a step stool. He was holding a sheet of folded blue paper.

"Do you want to see where the river goes?" Bea asked him.

"Over there?" He pointed to the outside wall.

"That's one way you could make it go."

He unfolded his paper just enough to show them a row of trees beside an empty band. He wanted to see whether there was a place for them. Maren told him he could put the paper beside the city first so they could check. The blue part was wider than all the cardboard houses. It went all the way from one edge of the table to the other. Maren held down the nearest corner while he flattened the farther one. There would have been no way to put it there had Bea left the houses in one piece.

They ate a slice of raw turnip on a bench and brought the rest home. Noor had sent Maren a photo before breakfast. A person with red socks was sitting at the table in the back room with something turned upside down in front of them. It took Maren a while to see it was a stool. The top seat was against the table. A dark spot on the table made her realize when the picture was taken. There was time to send an answer before Noor had to leave for her own school. Maren took a photo of the yellow bowl on the bench, with one slice each for her and Leon.

At home Mom had put the new rental agreement in the folder. She'd made a copy of the keys for a hook low enough that Leon could reach it. He wanted to hang them on a string instead, to make a noise when anyone came in. Mom said they could put a wind chime at the window, where anybody could hear it when the window was open. He asked whether it mattered if he made it from old spoons, and went to fetch a pencil to draw how many.

Before she came inside, Maren saw a new note taped by the apartment stairs. Ona May had written it with an arrow that showed where to get a tag for donated things. Beneath the arrow Pilar had written the two dates the truck would come. The note was large enough that somebody using the upper steps would see the dates before reaching the bottom. The chalk rectangle no longer had a place beside the entry. It was on the other side of the driveway, with a strip of paint between it and the way people had to walk.

The man who had taken the cart parked there now if he came to pick up chairs. Once, when he saw Leon standing near the paint, he had let him see how a tag hooked onto the top of a chair. They couldn't make a tag stay on the smooth part under the seat. He found a way to run the cord through an open space in the wood. Leon had kept the first tag they used in the kitchen, where he filed useful pictures around the corner from his museum.

Maren checked the dates and thought about a stool that didn't fit the new table. She could put it out tomorrow and use the hallway to get it there. Before she fetched it she asked Leon if he wanted to keep it for another place. He tried to picture himself sitting on it where his feet could touch something when he kept one over the other. They walked up the stairs to look at a corner by the window. Leon said he would use the stool there if he could wash the top. Maren handed him a cloth before she went in to look at the table again.

The family had found a table at the reuse shed. It was narrow and made of plain wood, with a line near one edge where the old finish had worn off. Ona May had checked the length of the alcove with Maren. They left a bit of room to walk between the table and the counter. When they put the chairs all on one side, there was room for someone to open the drawer. They turned the table the other direction when they had guests.

Maren slid the old pencil marks out of the clear sleeve with the papers from the move. The paper still showed how far below her first height Dad had drawn the second mark. She had wanted to find another frame. Here the frame in the kitchen had three holes left from a hook, above the spot where Maren's head would be. She could put the paper as high as she wanted. Instead she held a corner at the old height as best as she could remember, then asked Mom to help her put some tape across the top. She marked the new line beside her name and let Leon put his mark on the other side of the page.

Next they took Dad's cardboard out of the folder. The three boxes were still clear. In the kitchen Mom unfolded the old envelope, now covered with names, times and notes. There was a check by `school` and a newer one by `ride`. In the box for later, some ink had run because it had got wet on the way to the farm. Mom said she might want to keep it blank. She'd write down the next things on their own sheet of paper once she knew what they were.

The grown-ups still talked about winter hours. The red cart could fit in the little locked space out back. The hall could keep two evenings open, as long as the people who used it helped tell others which days the lights would be on. Some things had a place now. Other things needed another pencil line. Maren set the yellow bowl on the table and took it off again to see where she wanted it. Across from her Leon tried a low sound he'd learned to make by tapping a spoon against the table's leg. There was also a low shelf by the front door where people could leave extra garden vegetables. She put three turnips in the bowl and set it there.

After supper she called Noor. For a few minutes they talked about the weather in two places that didn't feel equally far apart anymore. Noor held the phone where Maren could see one corner of the old table. She'd been working on a stool. She turned it upright and sat on it for the length of the call. Maren showed her the place on the wall where they'd put their height marks. Noor stood and pointed to the places on her side where Maren ought to measure if she came to visit. A little scratch on the dark patch was new. It made the patch look like what it was, a place people could still use.

Maren set the phone down when Noor went to eat. She went to see whether anybody had taken a turnip. One was gone. She turned the bowl so the notch faced outside, which was the best way to pick it up without catching a finger in the cracked place. The door was light when she swung it toward the frame. On the high shelf a light came on where Mom had set a lamp. She looked out across the pavement and saw Leon running back from the garden with one shoe unlaced.

He could have called to ask her to wait, but he didn't. When he got to the step he knocked three times on the frame, softly, as if he were wondering what kind of sound it would make. Maren knocked once from her side. Leon stopped long enough to knock once back before taking a turnip out of his pocket and laying it in the bowl.

"Somebody took one," he said.

"I saw."

"There's another one there now."

Maren moved the yellow rim a little farther from the shelf's edge. Then she held the door while he came in.

# Day 22 only. The Blue Card

*Invented for practice.*

Maren found a blue card on a low shelf at the meeting hall. A drop from a raincoat had landed on it. A pale curl rose like a shaving from a sharpened pencil. It had been glued over a date, but she couldn't read the number beneath it.

"That's last week's," Lane said. "Green's for this week. I put that one upstairs."

Both cards invited people to a game. A player had promised to bring the pieces from the school. Maren had brought down an extra chair where anyone could reach it.

"Throw the wet one out," Lane said.

"We need the address on the back."

"We can copy it."

"Let's find out what else we need to copy."

Upstairs, tables stood so close together that a person with a box would have to walk sideways. Downstairs, a bench had space to set down a box and open it. Both places had a sign for the game. Only the one upstairs had a time you could read. The damaged card was a closed fist: something was inside, but Maren couldn't make it give up what she needed.

She asked Lane who had written the date. "I did, but Ms. Hale gave it to me. Her ride changed."

Lane brought down the green card. Maren crossed out the old time before copying the address. They left a plain note upstairs pointing to the bench downstairs. On the garden path, Lane told her which words he could read. She moved the card closer to the door, then moved a chair out of the way. Lane tried the route again with both hands full.
