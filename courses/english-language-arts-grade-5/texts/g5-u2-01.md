# A Year Above the Forest Floor

*This account follows a fictional Puerto Rican scientist and team. Their field notes are invented for practice. The published study findings are identified in the account.*

## Packet 1. January: A bite in a leaf

A piece of leaf was missing. In the pale beam of her lamp, Lucía Rivera could see a small insect working along the fresh edge. She held her notebook open with one hand. Before she could finish sketching the bite, a frog sprang from the stem above. When its feet touched the leaf, the insect was gone. The frog rested with its throat against the gleaming surface.

Lucía and her colleague Mateo were watching from a path in El Yunque, a tropical rainforest in northeastern Puerto Rico. They had gone out at dusk to see what changed as daylight faded. Above them, the canopy, the spread of crowns formed by the upper branches of trees, was already hard to make out. The leaf they were watching was on a much shorter plant. It had seemed like an ordinary leaf until something wanted to eat it.

The plant used energy from sunlight to make food in its leaves. The insect obtained food by eating the leaf, and the frog obtained food when it swallowed the insect. At each step, food carried matter and energy from one living thing to another. That chain was part of a food web, with many feeding paths. Another insect might visit the same plant for shelter, while a different animal might eat the fruit of the tree above it. One plant could supply more than one animal, and each animal could use more than one part of the forest.

Mateo lifted his lamp just enough to check the frog. He could see its outline, but the frog slipped under a leaf before he could identify the species, or kind of living thing. Lucía wrote, “Frog took insect from leaf. Species uncertain.” She then drew arrows from the leaf to the insect and from the insect to the frog. Those arrows captured a relationship she had evidence for, even with two names missing.

El Yunque's biodiversity, its variety of living things, could fill books. Lucía's notebook would tell the chronology of one year in a few patches of this ecosystem, where living things interact with each other and their physical surroundings. On the first page, two arrows were enough to get her started.

## Packet 2. February: Making a place findable

The following month, Lucía and Mateo chose two small study plots beside the same research path. A plot is an area marked for study. At one plot, tree crowns met above most of the measured area. At the other, a gap interrupted the cover. The team picked spots close enough together to reach on the same visit. Both plots had plants below the trees and a layer of fallen leaves on the ground.

First they chose a fixed point for looking up in each plot. Mateo drew the positions on a map and checked that Lucía could find each one from his drawing. Next they took an upward photograph at each point. The edges of the photographs included the same branches they would use to line up their view again. Afterward they wrote the date, time and plot code beside each image. With those details, a photograph of branches also became a record someone could match to a place and a visit.

Their other job was to make their visits comparable. A visit at noon and a visit just before dusk would tell them different things about light. They arranged to measure during the same part of the day. That still left some differences they could not control. Clouds could cover the sun during one visit. A brief shower could wet the leaves. They left room on the record sheet to describe those conditions. Their aim was to see what repeated across visits and to notice what changed from one day to the next.

Just before leaving, Lucía tried the route with only the map and a copy of the record sheet. She stopped at the wrong spot the first time because two trunks looked alike. Mateo drew a bend in the path and added the angle from which to look. On her second try, she lined the same branches up in the frame. They saved the corrected map beside the photo records. By the time they left, another team member could return to the same points and understand how the first records had been made.

## Packet 3. March: The space below a crown

In March, Lucía led a student group to the observation point with the gap. She asked them to look as far up as they could, then lower their eyes until they were looking between the trunks. Finally they looked at their feet. What appeared first from the forest floor changed with every tilt of the head.

In many tropical rainforests, the tallest individual trees rise into an emergent layer. The canopy, just below those tallest crowns, is the overlapping roof of branches and leaves. Where the cover is thick, upper leaves intercept much of the direct sunlight that would otherwise reach plants below. A gap in that cover lets a patch of light through. As branches grow sideways across a gap, they can shade plants that were previously in brighter light.

Below the canopy is the understory. Smaller trees and other plants grow there among the trunks of taller ones. Near the edge of Lucía's gap, the group saw sun patches on leaves at shoulder height. At the covered plot most of the shoulder-high leaves were shaded. The contrast gave Lucía a way to explain microclimate, the conditions in a small area. The word part *micro* means small. Even on the same afternoon, the amount of light can differ from one patch to the next because of what grows above each patch.

The forest floor includes soil, exposed roots and fallen material. A living leaf can spend months high in a crown, then become part of the litter below. Fungi and bacteria break down fallen leaves and other once-living matter. As decomposition proceeds, some of the nutrients in that material become available for plants to take up again. On her sketch, Lucía drew a fallen leaf at the end of a broken line from the canopy. She drew the next arrow near a root. The picture would have been easier to draw as four separate shelves. Falling leaves and growing branches made those shelves hard to keep separate.

On the way out, one student looked back at the gap. “If the roof fills in, the sunny spot moves?”

“It might shrink or disappear,” Lucía said. “First let's find out how much the branches grow.”

## Packet 4. April and May: Two lines of evidence

At the next visits, the team began taking regular light readings below the canopy. On each visit they measured at both fixed points. Over several visits, the point below the gap tended to be brighter. An afternoon cloud reduced the light at both, and the researchers noted when it passed. A pair of readings gathered close together could help them compare the two plots even when the weather changed during the visit.

The researchers also tracked leaves on understory plants in the view of their photographs. New growth near the gap was easy to pick out because several leaves unfolded where the old photographs showed bare stems. On the covered side, new growth was harder to follow in the shade, so they checked their images against what they could see in person. The light records described the surroundings. The plant records described how living things in those surroundings changed. Putting the records side by side suggested a possible link between more light and growth, while leaving open other differences between the plots.

During a rainy visit, two corners of Lucía's record sheet stuck to each other. The plot code remained readable, so she could match the record to the photograph when she got indoors. Another note had only the words “leaf opened” and a time. Both plots had been visited around then. Mateo drew a question mark by that note and filed it with the day's records. They could still read the sentence, but they could no longer tell where it belonged.

In the public display she was preparing, Lucía placed a simplified forest diagram beside her account. The diagram came from a separate source. She wanted its arrow from the canopy toward the understory to show what the leaf cover did to light. Another arrow tracked the path of a falling leaf. The team kept the two arrows distinct. One represented a change in conditions. The other showed where a piece of matter moved. Lucía credited the diagram separately so a reader could trace the general description of layers and tell it apart from observations in her team's notes.

## Packet 5. June: What a survey can say

Before the first June visit, Mateo set the recording schedule. He chose listening stops on the same route and made sure the team could return to them. Next he prepared a sheet with a separate line for each stop. Just before dusk, the observers arrived at the first point and waited for quiet. Only then did they record which calls they heard and which calls they could confidently identify. After the final stop, they checked the sound records against their written notes.

At the first point on the next visit, rain ticking on leaves made it difficult to separate individual calls. At another point, Lucía heard two bursts of the same frog call with a gap between them. She could not see whether one frog had called twice or two frogs had called once each. No matter how carefully she listened, the sound alone would still leave that question unanswered. Mateo asked her to keep both bursts in the sound record and mark the number of frogs unknown.

In a federal forest report, Lucía found an example of that kind of caution. The report described surveys of four kinds of coquí frog. It indicated possible changes for some kinds, but the researchers wanted more intensive surveys over a longer time before drawing firmer conclusions. Coquí frogs do not all belong to the same species. Even a confident record about one kind leaves questions about the others.

A habitat offers the conditions and resources a living thing needs. Survey records can help people recognize which habitats animals use and which places deserve more attention. A single listening session might tell Lucía that a call was heard near an opening. Repeated visits might help show whether the call was regularly heard there. If the team wanted to ask what made that spot useful to frogs, they would need to compare it with other places, taking into account rain, plant cover and the time of day. Lucía put a new column on the next sheet for conditions that might affect what the team could hear.

## Packet 6. July and August: From a bite to a web

That summer Lucía reopened the January notebook. The small bite on the leaf suggested a bigger question. If frogs hunted around the plants, what might happen to the plants themselves? Scientists working in the Luquillo Experimental Forest had studied this kind of relationship. In their experiment with common coquí frogs, plots with frogs had fewer invertebrates above the ground and less feeding damage to leaves. The results did not show the same reduction for invertebrates in the leaf litter.

Those results described more than the order in which two creatures ate. An insect that feeds on a plant can damage its leaves. A frog that feeds on those insects can reduce how much leaf tissue the insects eat. In this way, the frog's feeding can affect the plant, even though the frog gets its food from the insects. The plant, a producer, uses light to make its own food. The insect and frog are consumers, getting food from other organisms. Lucía traced the links on her January page and added a small circle around the plant.

A plant can support consumers in other ways. Forest records describe Puerto Rican parrots feeding on palm fruit along the Río de la Mina. In a different part of the web, fungi and bacteria act as decomposers, breaking down dead material. Leaves come down from above, and nutrients from decaying material can return to the soil. The canopy supplies some of the matter on the floor, and the floor helps supply resources to living plants.

The published experiment helped Lucía see a possible effect of frogs on plants, but her own single observation could not tell her whether frogs reduced leaf damage at her plots. That would take a study designed to test the connection. She rewrote the caption for her drawing. One sentence gave the event she had seen. A second described the larger relationship supported by the experiment. Anyone who wanted to use the caption to explain the forest could now tell which evidence went with which claim.

## Packet 7. September and October: Reading the old records

In September, the team brought out an older set of records. They had started the year with photographs of a gap and a covered plot. What would happen across a forest if many crowns opened at once? Lucía wanted to compare the team's photographs with a published account of a much larger change.

Hurricane Maria damaged Puerto Rico's forests in 2017. In the Luquillo Experimental Forest, scientists studying the canopy found that its height dropped across much of their study area, with more damage in some places than others. Openings in the crowns let more light reach plants below. The change in canopy cover affected both light and humidity, the amount of water vapor in the air. The hurricane also sent fallen leaves and other material to the ground in different amounts at different sites.

Lucía studied a summary of the research. The scientists could review conditions before and after the storm because they already had records of the forest. They saw some surviving crowns spread sideways and some vegetation grow upward. Those changes helped close gaps. As crowns spread, the conditions below them could change again. The first measurements after the storm mattered, and further measurements helped show which changes lasted.

On a later visit to the team's own gap, Lucía found that a branch had reached partway across the opening. Its leaves appeared in a place that had been sky in the February image. The small change gave her a way to picture a process described by the hurricane study. The study involved many trees and a severe storm, and her plot followed an ordinary opening at one spot. She set the records next to each other to compare the process, keeping their separate histories on the page.

## Packet 8. November and December: Leaving a useful record

In November, Lucía and Mateo printed the photographs in order. In the first image from the gap, a wide patch of pale sky filled the middle. By the last visit of the year, new leaves reached into part of that patch. The opening was still there. Several of the leaves below it now spent more of the observation period in shade. The team could connect the change in light at their viewing point to a specific branch they could follow in the photographs.

At their final meeting in December, Lucía shared three pieces of evidence: the sequence of branch photographs, the light records, and a listening sheet with questions still written on it. Then she described two jobs for the coming year. Someone could return to the plots to see whether brighter and darker patches continued to shift. Someone could also plan a separate study to ask whether certain frogs used particular conditions. Recording the first change had given the team a better question about the second.

People can protect forest habitat by keeping places that supply food and shelter. Records can help them see what those places supply and whether conditions change. A study of tree crowns can reveal changes in shade, while surveys of living things can reveal which species people are finding and where more information is needed. Neither set of records can do every job, but together they can tell more about what might need protection.

Lucía returned to the first notebook page. An insect had fed on a leaf, and a frog had fed on the insect. The pair of arrows was still there. Now she could set beside it a record of shade, a sketch of where leaves fall, and a list of questions about how people can help the animals keep their habitat. She put a clean sheet behind the December record. Whoever took the next measurements would have a place to begin.
