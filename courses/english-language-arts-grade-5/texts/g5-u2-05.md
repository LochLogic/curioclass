# Waiting Beneath the Leaves

*Fiction set in El Yunque. The observation walk and its route are invented.*

## Part 1. The space after the bend

On the morning our class went to El Yunque, I brought a square of paper with a drawing of my street on one side. On the other side were four empty boxes. There had been six boxes at first. I had erased two to give the forest more room.

Our class was putting together a sound map of places we knew. Other people had brought in the squeak of the bakery door and the rattle of a domino game. I wanted to bring in a sound with a real surprise in it. Something the class couldn't guess, even with the map in front of them.

Mami set my water bottle on top of the paper.

“Keep that where you can reach it,” she said.

“The paper or the water?”

“The water, Isabelita.”

She called me that when she wanted to make me laugh and listen at the same time. At school I was Isa. On the recording we played at the end of the project, I had decided I'd be Isabel. Isabel sounded like somebody who knew what she was doing.

Our guide, Carmen, met us near the start of the walk. My classmate Dani said her name before she could introduce herself. Carmen had helped his sister with a project the year before, and he wanted us all to know it.

“Then you can help me explain the plan,” Carmen said.

Dani held up the route sheet so we could see the three places we would stop. One circle was beside a covered bench. Carmen said we would stay on the marked path throughout the walk. If we lost sight of the group and could not find it by calling, we would stay where we were safe and use the whistle she showed us. I tucked mine into an outside pocket. That was the last I thought about it for a while.

I put all my attention into the first pair of sounds. There was a steady rumble from water somewhere downhill. Above it, quick ticks skipped from leaf to leaf. I had to hold still to hear the small sounds through the big one. When the guide said we were moving on, I copied the two into my top box as dots over a long wavy line.

At the next stop, just beyond a bend, there was a low wooden roof over a bench. Our teacher was at the front of the group, beside Carmen. I could see both of them through the opening from my seat.

“Do you hear that?” Dani said.

I heard a sharp knock from somewhere above us. It was plain as somebody tapping a spoon against a glass. I lifted my chin and saw a spread of leaves so thick that searching it with my eyes felt like trying to look through a woven basket. Here and there, a bright spot showed through. None of them moved enough to be a bird.

“Again,” I said, even though nobody could make it happen.

The knock came once more. I could have put *bird?* in the box. I had already put a question mark beside the first sketch, though, and I was tired of having a map of guesses. I put *bird in trees*. Then I drew a line up from the bench to the top of the page.

Rain pattered on the roof. Carmen called that the rain was passing and the group was moving on. I pushed my paper into its sleeve and got up. As I stepped into line, Dani pointed back at the bench.

“Your bottle.”

I was about to tell him I had everything. I even patted the bag, though the sleeve inside it felt nothing like a bottle. I had been so busy trying to tell what made the knock that I'd forgotten putting the bottle on the seat. Now that Dani had pointed, I could see the lid under the bench. A thin line of water crept toward it along the floor.

It was lying under the bench, where it must have rolled when I took out the sleeve. I stepped back to get it. A strap on my bag snagged on the corner of the seat. By the time I pulled it free, the last bright shirt had disappeared around the bend.

I called Dani's name. The forest gave me the sound of the rain, softer now, and water rolling somewhere below the slope. I went as far as the opening beside the bench, where I could see a little way up the marked path. At my height, the path seemed to fold into itself. The green curtain at the bend fell all the way to the ground.

They'll come back around that corner in a second, I thought. Then we would laugh about my bottle and I would ask Carmen about the bird. I could already hear Dani saying, “You got the whole forest, huh?” That was how I imagined it. Out on the actual path, nobody said a word.

I had one foot pointed toward the bend. From where I stood, I would need only a few steps before I could see around it. Then I would need a few more to see around the next one. I remembered how neatly Carmen's pencil line had turned on the map. From the bench, I couldn't tell how many folds that line had.

I sat back down. The empty end of the bench looked bigger than it had when Dani was beside me. I called again and heard my voice disappear into the steady water sound. I reached for my whistle.

Then came that sharp knock. This time, without anyone near me, it sounded as if someone else might be sitting under the roof. I knew exactly where I'd have to look to find out. I took a breath and turned my head.

## Part 2. The empty square

A drop hung from a leaf bent over the back of the bench. The whole leaf sagged with the weight of it. I watched the drop grow round enough to hold a tiny bit of pale sky. When it fell, it struck a dry hollow stem caught across the bench's end. The stem gave a small wooden knock.

I heard it clearly. I wanted to hear it twice.

A second drop began to swell. It stretched and slipped down the tip. Knock.

I took my paper out of its sleeve and stared at *bird in trees*. I had drawn the arrow all the way up, even though the sound I could finally explain had come from hardly higher than my shoulder. I drew a line through the words. A drop in the corner of the map made a gray island of the pencil dust.

There could still be birds above me. There could even be a bird that made a noise like that. I could say where two of the knocks had come from because I had watched them happen. I put those two in the box. Somewhere inside my chest, where I had been feeling the need to do something quickly, I could feel my breath again.

I took out the whistle and blew. The first note was thin. I stopped, listened and tried again. My fingers felt stiff around the little tube. After that, I kept it in my hand, where it was easier to find. I called out “I'm at the bench!” because somebody who could hear my voice might know the place. The water below the path kept up its steady rush.

My grandfather has a saying for when I'm about to do something in a hurry. “Haste makes waste,” he says. He said it once when I tried to carry four bowls to the sink at the same time. It had made sense right away, since we were standing in a puddle of soup. On the bench I had nothing broken in front of me. I had to imagine it. If I rushed around the bend while the others came back from the other side, they could reach the bench and find it empty.

I stayed. Rain hissed, softened and stopped. For a while, the drips kept falling so fast that I could hardly tell one from another. The big water sound stayed underneath, too steady to count. Later it seemed a little farther away, though I knew my ears might simply have grown used to it. A breeze brushed the side of my face. I ate half the sandwich in my bag and saved the other half for when the tight feeling in my throat passed.

I thought about my teacher back on the path. I could imagine her calling my name and then stopping to count. I could also imagine that she hadn't counted yet. Each picture seemed true until I made the next one. As long as I sat looking at the bend, I kept making up the part I couldn't see. I dropped my eyes to the paper, where I had a place for what was actually in front of me.

I worked on the map in short bursts. In one square, I wrote what I had heard before the rain. In another, I drew the leaf, the stem and the shape of the roof. A leaf above the bench moved in the wind, but the dry stem lay still. I thought of the knock coming from the two together. The drawing made it easy to see a connection I had missed while my eyes were searching the highest branches.

When I heard my name, the first syllable sounded like a high note from the whistle. Then it came again with my teacher's voice attached.

“Isa, can you hear me?”

“Here! I'm here!”

I jumped to my feet before anyone came into view. My teacher appeared at the bend with Carmen and a forest employee. Carmen slowed down as she came near.

“We're right here,” she said.

I had been ready to explain about my bottle and how I'd almost walked around the bend. Instead, I held up the whistle. My teacher's shoulders dropped.

“I heard that,” she said.

Carmen checked that I was well enough to walk. Then she and the employee took me back with my teacher to meet the class. It was early afternoon. The walk back went fast, though we had to pause at two narrow places to let other people pass. I remember only the employee pointing at a slick board with a boot and my teacher beside me the whole way. When I saw Dani again, he was holding a crushed sandwich bag. He unfolded it slowly with both hands.

“I saved your place,” he said.

“On the bus?”

“At the table. At both.”

The next day, when we set our maps out at school, I put my street drawing face down. In the upper right square was a mark where the rain had dried. The words *bird in trees* were still faintly visible under their line.

“Want another sheet?” Dani asked.

“I think I want this one.”

The teacher had asked us to prepare a short caption for the recording. I tried saying, “It sounded like somebody up there tapping to get my attention.” That was how I'd tell Dani, with my finger on the first box. Then I wrote my caption on a clean slip of paper.

“Two drops struck a hollow stem at the bench. Each drop produced a sharp tap.”

I could say what I had seen and what I had heard. The caption didn't say who had been there with me. It didn't say what my feet had wanted to do. There was room on the map for those things anyway. I kept the bench as a dark square, my place on the path as a dot, and the part beyond the bend as a plain white space.

Dani started to reach for the paper, then waited for me to turn it toward him.

“Do the sound,” he said.

I put two fingers on the desk. Tap. Tap. The people nearest us stopped talking for a moment. I put my finger down beside the bench to show them how close the sound had been.
