# Below the Bend

## Part 1. The upper crossing

From the hay shed, Eli could see a strip of silver below the bend. It was all he could see of the river from that distance. The rest lay behind a low bank and the cottonwoods. The silver looked wide enough to him. All summer, when his father said the river had shrunk again, Eli had pictured that bright strip and wondered how his father could be so sure.

His older cousin Mara came into the shed holding a wrench and a piece of hose. “The trough's almost dry. Want to come with?”

He had one foot on the hay bale he was supposed to roll. He let it down.

The family's cattle drank from a trough in the pasture. A pump drew water from the river through a pipe that ended at the upper crossing, where there used to be a deep pool. Eli could have drawn the pipe's route with his eyes closed. He had followed it so many times while carrying tools that he knew where the ground would change under his boots before he looked down.

At the trough, Mara unscrewed the hose. She held its open end over the shallow water and waited. Nothing came out. Six cattle stood on the other side of the fence in the shade. Every once in a while one shuffled forward and another took its place.

“How long before they need more?” Eli asked.

Mara bent so she could see the line inside the trough where the water had been that morning. “Your dad's calling about a delivery. Let's go have a look at the pipe.”

Eli knew what his father would have to do if they could not keep water in the pasture. He had heard him at the supper table, asking who might have room for part of the herd. Sending cattle away for a while sounded simple until Eli imagined the pasture after the truck left. He kept seeing the gate swing back into place behind an empty lane.

They followed the pipe. Where the bank narrowed, Mara took the higher path and told Eli to stay on it. He could see the end of the pipe without stepping any farther. It lay on its side among round stones, high enough above the water to cast a small shadow. He was used to looking for it by the ruffle the river made over the wire screen. He had never noticed the scratches on the screen before.

“Could we just make the pipe longer?”

Mara set the wrench down. “You can see water below us. What can't you see?”

“The bottom.”

“Or how deep the water is tomorrow.”

Eli looked back at the stones. The river had left a gray belt across them that ran from just below the pipe, down to the shrinking pool. Above the belt, they were the color of ashes. He counted three different marks on the big stone he used to sit on. He could remember when the water touched his boot from that seat. Now the dry bank stretched past it for several long steps.

At the shed, his father had put the phone down on a feed sack and opened a ruled notebook. He looked up when the door scraped. Eli told him where the pipe ended. Mara put the hose on the bench and lined it up with the edge.

“Think we can bring a load in by evening,” his father said. “We're fixing to use the holding pen by the lane. Truck can get right up to it.”

Eli thought of the lower pasture. There was a level patch there beside the wide part of the bend, and grass still stood in the shade. “Couldn't we use the lower place till this is over?”

“Show me on the map.”

Eli put his finger on the broadest shape of blue. His father put a pencil beside his finger and asked him to mark where the old road went. Eli remembered it coming down the slope, ending, then starting again on the far side.

“The truck has to get there with a full tank,” his father said, and went out to clear the lane.

Eli was still looking at the map. The old road ran straight into the blue. If the river was as shallow as it looked from the crossing, there might be a way. He took the map out to the porch. The strip of silver was there, bright as the blade of the wrench, so wide it seemed to settle every question.

Then a gust came uphill. The silver broke into separate pieces, with dull patches between them. By the time the wind died, Eli had folded the map and gone to find Mara.

## Part 2. The lower bank

Mara brought the map when they went down to the bend. She stopped at the top of the old road, which ended at a shallow drop where the river had eaten away the edge. Eli could see a pair of tracks on the far bank. The two parts of the road were still lined up, as if a truck might roll straight across if it didn't have to touch the ground.

The wide strip below them was half water. The rest was smooth pale mud. Eli could see the marks of a bird's feet stop at the edge of the darker mud, then start again several feet away. Between the marks was a shine he could have mistaken for the river from the shed. Farther off, the water itself was moving in a series of quiet wrinkles.

“That's what I was pointing at,” he said.

Mara held the map down against her leg. Eli noticed she was watching the road edge. A thread of dirt fell from it and disappeared. He put his heels back on the packed ground.

At home there was a pen by the lane where the neighbor's truck could reach the trough. Eli had never thought of it as pasture. The grass there had already been worn away where they fed the cattle in winter. Still, the trough would hold water, and the gate was wide enough for a trailer. For the first time, he pictured the gate opening from the road side.

He turned the map over. On the blank back Mara drew the lane, then the pen. Eli added a box for the trough and a line for the fence. There was another pen on the same side of the lane, past the hay shed. It had some shade, but its gate was blocked by the bale he'd left that morning.

“Could set the portable trough by that gate,” he said. “The truck could fill both from the road.”

“Ask your dad where it can turn around,” Mara said.

When they got back, his father was walking the lane with the neighbor who was bringing the tank. Eli took the map to them. He stumbled once over the flattened hose he'd carried out to the trough, then stopped right where he was. The pen on his drawing was closer to the gate than the real pen was. He laid the map on the fence post and drew a longer line for the lane, then walked out the distance with his father.

“At the wide place, there's room for you to turn at the hay shed,” his father said to the driver. He looked at the map again, then asked Eli to clear the gate with him.

That evening they shut the upper pasture. From the lane Eli watched the cattle walk between the two fences. Half the herd went into one pen, half into the other. Two stood by the portable trough when the first water splashed in. They held their heads high for a moment, then lowered them together. The low, steady sound of their drinking was almost lost under the sound of the truck.

The truck came back on a schedule the neighbor and his father worked out. His parents arranged to move part of the herd to a ranch with water farther inland. There would be room and feed for the ones that stayed. Eli kept the copy of the map with the delivery times on it, clipped to the shed wall. When his father gave a time over the phone, Eli wrote it down where the driver could see it from the lane.

On the morning the trailer came, he stood to one side of the gate with Mara. The cattle that were leaving filed up the ramp. A red calf paused with its feet on the wood. Eli had watched it learn to walk in the upper pasture, and he had been hoping all morning for one last look at it. It took two more steps and went in.

After the trailer pulled away, Mara said, “Want to come check the other trough?”

He went with her. The cattle that stayed were lying together in the shade by the bale they'd rolled out of the way. Their ears turned at the sound of the gate. Up by the shed, the silver strip still showed below the bend. Eli could see where the strip widened now, and where the sharp little crease in the light was. He put his hand on the trough and felt the faint movement of the water as a cow stood and came over to drink.
