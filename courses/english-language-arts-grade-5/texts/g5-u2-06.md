# The Survey Route

*Fiction set in El Yunque. The survey, its route and its records are invented.*

## Part 1. The first mark

Gabriel's grandmother had given him a pocket notebook with a stiff red cover. At breakfast she asked what the first page was for.

“The parrots,” he told her.

“I meant that little space at the top.”

He had already drawn a line across it. Amina, who was leading the morning survey in El Yunque, had told him to put the date there. His grandmother offered him a pencil with a fresh eraser. Then she put the blunt pencil he'd been using into his shirt pocket.

“There,” she said. “Now one can get away from you.”

The notebook was a spare. Amina carried the survey sheets, and two adults on her team carried binoculars. Gabriel could take his own notes if he could write and keep up. His job on the walk was to repeat the names of their listening stops so his aunt Maribel could check them off. His aunt did surveys often. He had heard her talk about sitting through mornings when she saw nothing, then getting one clear look at a bird as she put away her things.

At the start of the path in El Yunque, Gabriel opened his book to the date. He imagined the first mark under it. He had seen pictures of Puerto Rican parrots, with green feathers and a patch of red above the beak. He could picture one leaving the bough it stood on. Out here, where the green crowns overlapped, he had trouble seeing even the branches.

Amina waited until everyone had reached the first stop. “No need to fill the silence,” she said. “We came here for what's already in it.”

The path was still damp from a shower. Somewhere above Gabriel, a drop fell. He could follow it by ear as it landed on a leaf, then on a second leaf with a duller sound. The air smelled like wood left out in the rain. His aunt sat down on the wide bench and set her bag beside her. Gabriel perched on the end. The wet edge of the seat made a cold stripe through his pants.

“First stop,” he whispered.

Maribel drew a check on her sheet. Gabriel drew one too. It was smaller than the parrot he hoped to draw. Beside the check, he wrote *drip, drip*, moving the second word a little to the right, where he thought the next leaf was. There was only a strip of gray between two crowns, so narrow that it seemed as if a bird would have to turn sideways to fly through it.

Maribel handed him the binoculars for a moment. He pointed them toward the gray strip, but the trees seemed to move in the opposite direction when he raised his hands. All he could see was a single leaf, slick and green. When he lowered the binoculars, the strip of sky was still there. He handed them back and chose two leaves where he could rest his eyes, one on either side of the opening.

Just before they left the stop, there was a hard, sharp sound. Gabriel looked up. Maribel had lifted her face too. He waited for her pencil to move. Her eyes passed slowly along the tops of the trees. Her pencil stayed on her knee.

“Did you get it?” he asked.

“I heard it,” she said.

“So, parrot?”

“Try putting down what reached your ears.”

He wrote *sharp call*. By the time everyone had stood up, he'd added *from the left*. Then he looked at what he had written. Left would mean something different to any reader facing the other way. He changed it to *from the side with the tall palm*. He liked the idea that he could go back to the bench a year later and know which way he had turned.

The second stretch of the walk passed quickly. They went around two bends, crossed a short plank walkway and stopped where the path grew wider. Amina went ahead to check a signed stretch of their route. She came back a few minutes later. A fallen branch blocked part of the planned path, and she was taking them to a different prepared stop. Gabriel looked at the date on his page. The next line of his notebook was waiting. He remembered that his aunt's list had three stops and that she had often come back with empty lines.

“Could we watch from here?” he asked.

“Only if we can keep the rest of the path clear,” Amina said. She pointed out the narrow part behind them. Another group was approaching. Everybody drew in to let them pass. Even with all four people standing close, Gabriel's elbow brushed the wet leaves beside the path.

“What happens to number two?” he asked after the people had gone by.

“Write it as skipped,” Maribel said. “If you call the new place number two, nobody will know which place you mean.”

Amina led them on the marked alternate route. Gabriel put a line beside stop two and wrote *branch across path*. At school, leaving a space empty usually meant he had missed a question. Here the line told the next person exactly why there was no entry. He filled the next few minutes watching his aunt's heels, each step bringing up a wet shine where the tread pressed the ground.

At the alternate stop, a small deck widened the path enough for the team to spread out. There was a bench at either end. Vines and leaves hid one part of the canopy from the lower bench. The higher bench gave a clear view of a pale opening between crowns. Amina and one observer took the lower end, while Gabriel stayed with Maribel at the higher end. They could see Amina's notebook if she held it up. They couldn't see the side of the sky she had chosen to watch.

Gabriel wanted to stand where the others were, in case they saw something first. Maribel touched the empty space on the bench with two fingers. He set his notebook there and sat. For a while only the old drip reached him. Then the sharp sound came again.

## Part 2. The space between two crowns

Gabriel kept his eyes on the little space he could see. The sound seemed to be behind his right ear. He had heard the first one on the left at the other stop. There might be something moving through the forest. There might be different things making noises that seemed alike.

Below him, someone drew in a breath. The dry scrape of a pencil came from the direction of the lower bench. He thought of turning. If Amina had put a mark on her sheet, he wanted to see it. His aunt leaned forward, so slowly that he only noticed when the wet cuff of her shirt came into view.

“Above the palm,” she said.

Gabriel looked at the empty space just past the ragged edge of a leaf. At first, all he saw was an extra sliver of sky. The sliver widened as the leaf dipped. A bird crossed the opening. Its wings went down together, then lifted far enough for him to see a blue flash near the tips. For an instant it was at the near edge of one crown, with the light clear behind it. He saw the short shape of the head, the green body and the way the blue vanished when the wings swung back.

The opening went pale again. The crowns made a narrow window, and the breeze nearly drew its green shade. Then another bird crossed the same patch. He felt his pencil touch his leg through the pocket before he thought to reach for it. By the time he had the pencil in his hand, both birds were behind leaves.

“Two!” he said. It came out louder than he meant.

“Two passed here,” Maribel said quietly. She kept watching the opening. Gabriel could hear the word she had added. *Here* was a short word for a small patch of sky.

From the lower bench, Amina lifted one hand to show she had seen something. The other observer was still searching the branches with the binoculars. Gabriel felt the pull of all that space just past the deck. He could have seen more sky a little farther up the path. Amina had asked them to stay at their stops until she signaled the end. He kept his eyes between the crowns. A little later the leaves dipped again. Only light passed through this time.

When they came together, the observer from the lower bench showed a pencil mark on his sheet. He had seen part of one bird come out from a crown and watched it until it slipped behind leaves. He was able to show the others roughly where it had come out. Amina had seen two silhouettes farther along that side but hadn't seen colors. Maribel had seen two clear enough to identify as Puerto Rican parrots. The times and directions on their notes put the sightings close together. They might have watched the same two birds from different places. The team agreed they could report two confirmed Puerto Rican parrots during that pass. They kept the lower bench records in the file, where the partial views could be compared with the clearer ones.

On the survey sheet, Amina made a little sketch while each observer pointed. The two sets of arrows approached the same crown from different sides. At first Gabriel thought the arrows marked the birds' paths. Amina showed him the small squares at their bases. She had begun each arrow at a bench. Her drawing showed where people had looked, and no line claimed to know where the birds went behind the cover.

Gabriel looked over his page. He had drawn a short line from a call to the point where he had seen the first bird.

“Did you see its beak open?” Amina asked.

He put the pencil tip on the line. The sound had reached him before he'd seen any wings. It might have come from one of the birds. He thought again of the broad leaf lifting and lowering. Everything behind it had been hidden from his bench.

“Nobody saw what made the first sound,” he said. “Or the second one, I guess.”

Amina showed him a place to record sounds separately. Gabriel copied the sound into that space and rubbed out the line. He still had both things on the page. It took him a moment to see that he was giving up only the string he had drawn between them.

With the survey finished, they made their way back. He noticed the wet edge of the first bench in passing, and then the dark stripe had dried from his pants. By the time they reached the start, he and his aunt were arguing about whose shoes were muddier. Amina gave him a few minutes at a table to copy his notes into a form the team could read. He told his aunt, “I got the blue part, right there between the trees!” He pointed to the edge of his sketch. On the form he wrote, “Two birds crossed a canopy opening. Blue wing feathers were visible during the crossing.” He left room for Maribel to add the features she had seen. He signed his own entry.

At home, his grandmother set two glasses on the table and moved one aside so the notebook could lie flat. She pointed at a folded corner.

“Is that where you saw them?”

“Here,” he said, showing her the sketch.

She followed the pencil lines up to the opening. He had drawn leaves around its edges, with a white space wide enough for two birds to pass. He started to make the faint marks at the other end of the page darker. His grandmother turned the book toward the light.

“What are those?”

“Two sounds that might be the same kind. They stay over here.”

There was a bare space between the marks and the drawing. He let her look at it for a while. Then he turned back to the first page and showed her the date he'd put above it all. When he closed the book, the pencil with the fresh eraser was inside.
