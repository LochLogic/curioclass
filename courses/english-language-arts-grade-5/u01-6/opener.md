# A character’s monologue

## What to notice

Drama is writing for performance with cast, setting, dialogue and directions. Dialogue is the words spoken by characters. The play moves from shared dialogue to one character’s speech. Use the released scene portions and leave the reserved moments covered.

Orientation gives the listener a speaker, a place and an immediate situation. Pacing gives more time to a significant choice. A sensory detail lets a listener imagine what someone might notice. Time links keep actions in order. An ending grows from something the speaker has done, seen or decided.

On the two-lane timeline, place the completed action beside its reference time. Locate the helping verb and participle. Changing the time marker can change the order. On another example, compare what a listener hears when a writer expands or reduces one sentence.

## Support and feedback

For an unnamed situation, locate who, where and what has happened. For a flat list, mark the moment that deserves time. For an unprepared ending, trace a consequence to an earlier choice. Give one cue and keep the draft and independent change together.

Use private local audio or a live word-reading log. Make every turn reachable. Keep personal experiences of bereavement, housing and work private. If the second audience cannot attend, prepare two audience-specific pieces and use live questions.

## Lay out

Covered play, two-lane time strip, sealed reading cards, dictation lines, three-part plan, audience notes and private word-reading log.
