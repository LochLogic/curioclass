# Section 8.1 opener: is it light or dark at 5:30 in the evening, and will spring be like fall or like winter?

## What the section does

Five meetings across the year: day 9 in the fall, days 62 to 65 in spring. On day 9 the class sets one dated evening report beside a day strip, asks whether the daylight will stay this long, colors a first guess for winter and spring, and starts the class daylight chart. Between day 9 and day 62 the teacher adds a square each month in the two-minute weather chart row, outside lesson time (the monthly look). Day 62 sorts the year's squares into fall, winter and spring and revises the guess. Day 63 slides full-day strips until the sunrise pictures meet and compares winter with fall and with spring. Day 64 is the observed check on strips from an unfamiliar place. Day 65 returns picture comments, repairs by letter and tries three fresh strips. Nothing is scored but day 64, and day 64 is marked on one scale: shown, shown with help, not yet seen.

## How the days run

Each day is 30 to 35 minutes. Children read very little: the teacher reads every prompt, and every answer is a touch, a coloring, a slide, a drawing or a few words the teacher writes. A child may always pass. Every exit case is invented for practice and said aloud to be so. A rotating six is heard on each exit and the record sheet has a heard column. Pip the puppet is wrong on purpose, one line at a time, and no child is ever the one who made the mistake.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A day strip is one bar for one whole day.** Every bar is the same length. The light part runs from the sunrise picture to the sunset picture; the rest is dark. Strips are drawn by an adult from the federal sunrise and sunset table in standard time, so they are calculated records, not our own sightings, and they do not show cloud. Children compare the lengths of light parts. They never read or count hours.
- **A report is one moment; a strip is the whole day.** At half past five the evening is light in the fall and spring and dark in midwinter. That answers the compelling question for our place, but a single report cannot tell how long the day was light.
- **The pattern in our records:** winter's light part is shorter than the sampled fall and spring days. This is a finding for our place and these dates, not a rule for every place. A November strip is shorter than an October strip and still fall. Why the light parts shrink stays an open question on the board; the cause is not taught.
- **Clocks:** an adult handles any clock change. Changing a clock adds no daylight.
- **Limits:** only more or less daylight. No counting of hours, no fractions, no talk of spinning, tilting or circling. Never look at the sun; the prepared record replaces any sun watching. No child is asked about their evenings beyond light or dark, and no family is asked for a report.

## Where reports come from

An evening report is "light" or "dark" at about 5:30, said by the class or by any adult who chooses to give one. Families are not asked. A month with none leaves its report square blank, and the strip carries the record. An adult may add a photograph from a classroom window, same clock time, aimed at an empty ground scene with the sun outside the view; it is one moment, like a report. Omit it if it is not safe.

## The errors, in plain words

- **Error A:** every season has the same daylight. Repair: lay Pip's equal bars under the real strips and point to the shorter light part.
- **Error B:** winter has more daylight. Repair: put the winter label on the strip whose light part is shorter.
- **Error C:** one evening report is the whole day. Repair: choose the full-day strip to answer how long the day was light.
- **Error D:** the dark part is the daylight. Repair: trace the light part from the sunrise picture to the sunset picture with a finger.

## Safety and set-up

Seated paper work. Per group of five: five papers, crayons and one large strip set. The class keeps one daylight chart, and day 9 adds five large light and dark tokens a group. Adults print and cut every set the day before, check that every bar has the same total length, and keep clock values off every child sheet (they sit on the strips-key page only). Keep the day 64 strips, the evening-only report and the held-back exit displays in sealed envelopes until their day. Tokens stay out of mouths; keep aisles clear; replace torn cards. No chemicals, special gear or eye protection. Never look at the sun. Reserve two clean-up minutes each day, three on day 64.

If the school is in Denver or Boston, use the other city for the held-back and day 64 sets. With no printer, draw each strip with a ruler on a bar of one fixed length. With no table access, use Denver's October 15, January 15 and April 15 strips and say they are historical stand-in data, not the class's own.
