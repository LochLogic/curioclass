# Section 5.1 opener: what lets us see?

## What the section does

Five days, 38 to 42. The class meets a shoebox with a peephole, a flap and a toy inside. Children look through the peephole with the flap shut, then with the flap open. The toy stays put, and what changes is whether we can see it. The compelling question is asked aloud and no cause is given: why can't we see the toy inside the box until we open the flap? Day 38 starts a seeing drawing (the thing, where we look, an arrow for light, seen or not seen) and the class chart under seeing, sources and barriers. Day 39 compares the box, a gently dimmed room and cave pictures, and meets things that make their own light. Day 40 revises the drawing and writes one supported sentence. Day 41 tests the account on a lamp beside an unlit toy. Day 42 applies it to new objects and asks what happens when light is blocked. Section 5.2 begins on day 43 with the barrier question parked on the board. Nothing is scored in this section. The record sheet notes what each child shows, with one scale: shown, shown with help, not yet seen.

## How the days run

Each day is 30 to 35 minutes. Children read very little: every question is spoken, and every answer is a point, a drawing or a few words. Groups of five take turns at one box with a flashlight while the others draw their own sheet. A child who dislikes a dim room may hold the flashlight or sit by the door and still answers from the cards. The teacher keeps the chart and the question board and hears a rotating six at each exit. Every exit card is invented for practice, and the teacher says so aloud. A child may always pass.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **No light, no seeing.** An ordinary object is seen when light shines on it. The toy never disappears; our seeing changes. Children say "light shines on it" and "makes its own light". The adult words illuminated and self-luminous stay on this page, and so does the explanation that light from the object then reaches the eye: nothing about the eye is taught.
- **A source makes its own light.** A switched-on lamp, flashlight or night light is seen with nothing else lit. The wall beside the lamp is seen because the lamp shines on it, which separates seeing the lamp from seeing the wall.
- **A dim room is not no light.** Eyes slowly adjust, so a box that leaks a little light can bring the toy into view after a wait. That is where error A comes from. The box must be fully dark, lined and taped, and the repair is a closed bag with a sticker inside, not an argument.
- **Blocking.** Day 42 predicts that blocking outside light hides an ordinary object but not a source. No shadow-length work and no eye model.
- **Limits.** No light entering the eye, no reflection mechanism, no speed of light, no moon. The sun appears only in pictures and is never looked at.

## The errors, in plain words

- **A:** "We waited, so it showed up." Repair with a closed bag, then open. Met on day 38, repaired on day 39, again on days 41 and 43.
- **B:** "The lantern needs a lamp to be seen." Repair with a book light off and on inside a closed suitcase, then a bike light in a drawer. Met on day 39, repaired on days 40 and 42.

## Safety and set-up

Never look at the sun or shine a light into anyone's eyes. Flashlights are cool, on AA or AAA cells in taped compartments, never button cells, held below faces and aimed down into the box, never at the peephole. The day 39 and 41 lamp is a cool battery LED with no cord, switched by the teacher alone. Dimming is gentle and never dark, seats by the door are open to anyone, and the exit is lit. Leak-test the box the day before; if it leaks, use the adult-made dated picture sequence and say it is a recording. A shoebox with a hole, a cloth and a household flashlight do everything. The cave photographs are previewed by the adult and credited to the National Park Service.
