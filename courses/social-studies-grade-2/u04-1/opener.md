# Section 4.1 opener: continents, countries and who governs

## What the section does

Four days, 48 to 51, of 35 to 36 minutes. The unit asks: why do countries need each other? Day 48 tells physical geography from political geography: one continent, Africa, is read on a physical map and a political map, each by its own key, and it holds more than 50 countries. Day 49 teaches what makes a country (people, land with borders, a government and laws) and the six things a country card shows. Day 50 teaches what a government is for and that leaders are chosen in more than one way. Day 51 compares two countries, Japan and Saudi Arabia, in four rows from cards written from named public pages.

## How the days run

Each day opens with a repair or a retrieval, then a short teaching part, a part for the children to work with, the day's rotating six heard at the teacher's table, a question-board turn and a one-picture exit. The teacher reads every card, caption and prompt aloud. Children read labels of one to three words.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A continent is a very large land area. A country is a governed place with people and borders.** One continent holds many countries. Africa is a continent, not a country.
- **A physical map shows land and water. A political map shows countries and their borders.** Each map has its own key. Color alone tells nothing; read the key.
- **A flag does not make a country.** A club can have a flag, members and rules and still have no land with borders and no government for everyone. A flag and a capital are usual, not enough.
- **A government is for something.** It keeps people safe and provides things everyone uses, such as schools and roads. A purpose is said with "so people can".
- **Leaders are chosen in more than one way.** Voting is one way. In some countries the job passes in a family. Both are met as facts, with no judgment of either. Japan today: elected representatives choose the prime minister, who leads the government; the emperor has a role set by the constitution and does not govern. Saudi Arabia: the leader's job passes within the ruling family and is not decided by a popular vote. Neither country stands for a faith or a people.
- **A card shows examples.** What a card shows some people do is not what everyone does. A missing maker or date is written "the card does not say".

## The errors, in plain words

Pip says each wrong idea once and the right idea is said beside it: Africa is a country (A, day 48); the map with more colors shows the borders (B, day 48); a flag makes a place a country (C, day 49); every leader is chosen by voting (D, day 50); Japan's emperor runs the government (M, day 50); the government has a school, with no purpose (N, day 50); everyone in Japan does one thing (E, day 51); a missing date can be guessed (P, day 51); a printed opinion is a fact (Q, day 51); I think it because I like it (K, day 51).

## Supplies for the section

The Africa Map Pair A, the label of one classroom object, the Tanzania Crop, the leader cards, the Kelda two-map pair, the Characteristic Cards and six-box chart, the Tarn eight cards, the purpose cards, the how-chosen chart, the purpose frame strip, the Tarn school and road-crew cards, Country Cards A, the four-row chart, the date and photograph cards, the repair cards, the exit cards, the record sheet and the question board. The teacher says aloud that Kelda, Tarn, Orrin and every club or country with a made-up name are invented and print "invented for practice". Real places (Africa, Egypt, Tanzania, Nigeria, Japan, Saudi Arabia) appear only on Curio Class maps and cards written in our own words from the pages named in the unit's source list. No child is asked about their home, family, money, religion or where they are from. A question about belief gets: "people in every country follow many beliefs, and a card does not say what one person believes".
