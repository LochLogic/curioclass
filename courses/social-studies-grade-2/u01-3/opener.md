# Section 1.3 opener: why flat world maps differ

## What the section does

Four days, 9 to 12, of about 35 minutes. Day 9 shows that a round skin cannot lie flat without being stretched, folded or opened, so every flat map of the world changes something. The teacher tears a printed paper shell along a printed line; pairs press a paper cup flat. Day 10 teaches the Mercator map: it keeps the shapes of very small places and draws lands far from the equator too big, so Greenland looks about as big as Africa though Africa is much bigger. Day 11 teaches the Peters map, which keeps sizes and stretches shapes, and asks who made each card. Children choose a map for a stated job and say a claim, evidence and a because. Day 12 labels North America from a word bank and repairs two errors.

## How the days run

Day 9: a repair, the hook and the shell, pairs with cups, the question board, the exit. Day 10: a warm-up repair, the Mercator card beside the globe, pairs with cut-outs, the exit. Day 11: a retrieval warm-up, the Peters card, claim and because, partners with a different purpose, the question board, the exit. Day 12: two repairs, a model outline, pairs labeling, the exit. Every exit has a mark and a spoken part from the rotating six.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A projection is a way to draw the round Earth on flat paper.** Any flat map misrepresents something: directions, distances, areas or shapes.
- **The Mercator map keeps small shapes and keeps a straight line at one compass direction,** which suits sailing a course. It stretches lands far from the equator. A line of constant direction is not always the shortest route.
- **The Peters map keeps sizes** (it is an equal-area map) **and stretches most shapes.**
- **No flat map is wrong, and none is the answer to every job.** Choose the map for the job.
- **The model changes, the Earth does not.** Opening a paper shell does not put gaps in Earth.
- **No one scale bar holds across a flat world map.** Children say much bigger and much smaller, not numbers. Latitude is not taught.
- **The equator** is the line round the middle of the globe, halfway between the poles; it is named, not tested.
- Native nations live across North America today. The day 12 map shows countries and waters only and never says the land was empty.

## The errors, in plain words

- **Q:** opening the shell makes gaps in Earth.
- **G:** a flat map keeps everything the globe does.
- **K:** Mercator is useless.
- **E and J** return on day 11 when children read the cards' credit lines.
- **A and H** appear on day 12: a symbol read as the wrong feature, a continent written as a country.

Pip voices every wrong idea.

## Supplies for the section

A globe or a printed globe net with a soft paper sphere, the World Projection Cards, a printed paper shell with a tear line, a paper cup for each pair, paper cut-outs of Greenland and Africa, a claim frame, the North America sheets, and the exit cards. The teacher alone handles a peel in a demonstration; children handle paper only.

## A room with nothing

A ball of paper and a flat sheet; a drawn globe beside two hand-drawn maps; a paper cup for each pair to flatten.
