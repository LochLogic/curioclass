# Section 1.1 opener: reading a map's parts

## What the section does

Five days, 1 to 5, of about 35 minutes. Day 1 opens the unit's big question, Which map shows the world best, and best for what? The class sees a globe and two flat world maps, puts a counter in a cup, and writes its own smaller questions. A one-page start-of-year check follows, not scored. Days 2 to 4 teach one map part a day: the title and the key, the compass rose, and the scale. Day 5 applies all four parts to maps that show how people live with land and water. Every map in this section is made up for practice, the teacher says so aloud, and the card prints "invented for practice".

## How the days run

Each day has a short repair at the start for the last day's most common miss, a worked example with an invented map, pairs work, and a two-part exit: every child marks a printed card by circling, pointing or placing a token, then the day's rotating six say why, one at a time. The record sheet names who was heard.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **Each map part has one job.** The title says what the map shows. The key says what each symbol stands for, on this map only. The compass rose shows directions. The scale ties a length on the map to a distance on the ground.
- **A symbol is not the place.** A square on a map can stand for a gate, a tree or a well. The key says which.
- **North is where the rose points,** not the top of the page. A compass needle points in the general direction of north; a room with steel in it can spoil the needle, so the class may name one wall north and say so.
- **Scale is counted in whole units.** Equal paper strips are laid end to end and each one stands for the number of blocks or steps the label says. No ruler, no ratio.
- **Land and water are used and changed.** A good answer says what people did, not only what is there: they dug a ditch, they built a raised bank.
- **The best map depends on the job.** A pretty map is not the best map for every task.

## The errors, in plain words

- **N: choosing by looks.** Pip says the prettiest map is best. Beside it: the best map has what we need for the job.
- **A: one part doing another's job.** Pip says the title tells what the star means. The key does.
- **B: north is the top of the page.** North is where the rose points.
- **C: counting marks and ignoring the label.** Three lengths of five paces is fifteen paces.
- **D: naming a feature and no action.** "There is a creek" does not say how people use the land.

A wrong idea is always voiced by Pip, never by a named child. No child reports on home or family.

## Supplies for the section

A globe or a printed globe net, two World Projection Cards, counters and two cups, the question board and chart paper, the start-of-year page, the invented map cards for each day, paper roses on fasteners and four wall signs, six equal paper strips for each pair, the four-part card, and the exit cards. The record sheet has a row for each child and names the rotating six.

## A room with nothing

Sketch each map on scrap paper. Fold strips from one sheet. A turned sheet of paper stands for the rose, and the class names one wall north. Two scrap-paper circles hold the counters. A projector is not needed; if there is one, show a map on the screen and let children mark on paper, never on the screen.
