## Growing Food Together, day 143

A food pantry helps distribute food to people who need it. While volunteering at a pantry, high school student Landon D’Agate noticed a need for nutritious food. He decided to organize a garden whose produce could be donated. The federal 2024 project report describes his work in Bay Shore, New York.

Landon’s idea needed other people’s help. He obtained grants and recruited community volunteers. Together, the volunteers built raised garden beds and planted vegetables. The report names tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers and carrots among the foods grown. It describes produce donated to a local food pantry.

These details support a specific explanation of the garden’s work. Volunteers grew vegetables, and the produce supplied food for donation. The garden was also used for outdoor learning about gardening. It served more than one purpose: growing food and providing a place where people could learn together.

A report of action is useful evidence, but its reach has limits. Donations show that food was supplied. They do not establish how long each donation lasted for a household. The report does not interview every recipient or show that every person in town received food.

That distinction helps a reader investigate impact, the effect of an action. Asking about impact does not mean dismissing the work. It means matching an explanation to what the source actually reports. A useful next question might ask how the garden organized its volunteers or how the food reached the pantry. A claim that the garden ended all hunger would require evidence this report does not provide.

### Reserved ending, exit 143

A student’s garden supplied vegetables for a food pantry. The federal 2024 report describes donations, not whether every household received enough food.

### Reserved source line, exit 144 only

## Organizing a Garden, day 144

Before volunteers could plant vegetables in the Bay Shore garden, they needed places to grow them. The federal 2024 project report describes several raised beds built by community volunteers. The beds came before the planting. This sequence gives a reader information about organization, another aspect of the same garden described in Growing Food Together.

Landon D’Agate recruited help and obtained grant support. Volunteers built the beds, filled them with soil and plants, and seeded foods such as tomatoes and carrots. The project combined planning, materials and people’s work. Its food donations depended on more than the first idea of making a garden.

The report also describes the garden as a place for outdoor education. Community members could work together while learning about gardening. Growing food and sharing knowledge were related purposes, but they are different categories for research notes. A note about building beds belongs under preparation. A note about donated produce belongs under food provision. A note about outdoor learning belongs under education.

Reading the two articles together helps connect those categories. The first emphasizes food supplied for donation. This one emphasizes how a growing place was organized. Both use the same federal report. Agreement between them is not a second investigation confirming the facts independently.

A careful report can explain the sequence without inventing details. It can say volunteers built beds before planting. It cannot name a building date the source does not give. It can credit Landon and the volunteers without claiming that one person performed every task. Shared work deserves an account that keeps the helpers visible.

### Reserved ending, exit 144

## A Composting Place, reserved for first reading on day 146

In Barrington, Rhode Island, four students organized environmental work in their town. Their group established a public place where residents could leave material for composting. The federal 2024 report describes that collection site. The report supports a claim that a local service was organized. It does not establish that every resident used it.

## Reporting Local Work, reserved for first reading on day 146

The same students communicated with residents through a newsletter. They also invited people to environmental events and volunteer cleanups. These details show several ways the group encouraged participation. They do not establish how each reader responded to a newsletter. A report can describe an invitation without proving that everyone accepted it.
