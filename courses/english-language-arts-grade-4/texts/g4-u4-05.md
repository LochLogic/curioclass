## Organizing for the Ballot

Black women worked for voting rights. They organized clubs, asked lawmakers for change, and helped neighbors prepare to participate. Their work joined the struggle against racism with the struggle for women’s suffrage, the right to vote.

Ida B. Wells-Barnett helped establish Chicago’s Alpha Suffrage Club in 1913. The club worked to make Black women a stronger force in local political decisions. Its organizing included reaching voters and helping with registration. Wells-Barnett and other members were choosing methods and pursuing goals of their own.

Yet some white suffragists tried to separate Black women from the movement’s public work. Before the 1913 suffrage parade in Washington, organizers told the Illinois group that Black women should march at the end. Wells-Barnett refused that separation. She joined the Illinois delegation during the parade. Other Black women also marched, including students led by Mary Church Terrell.

Terrell’s work extended beyond that afternoon. In 1896, she became the first president of the National Association of Colored Women. Black clubwomen petitioned Congress for a constitutional amendment supporting women’s suffrage. They also sought federal protection for Black men’s voting rights. Their argument connected voting with the wider struggle for racial justice.

Racism within the suffrage movement was not a harmless disagreement. Excluding Black women limited whose voices and interests the movement represented. Naming that exclusion matters. So does naming Black women’s leadership, planning and continued action. An account that shows only a closed door leaves out the people organizing beyond it.

## Barriers After Ratification

In 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment prohibited denying or restricting a citizen’s vote because of sex. That was a major legal change. It did not say that every other barrier to voting had disappeared.

Discriminatory state voting laws continued to prevent many women from voting. Poll taxes and literacy tests could be used to keep Black women from the polls. Such rules were tools of exclusion, not evidence that the people excluded lacked the ability to take part.

The distinction is between a stated legal protection and access in practice. Access means being able to use an opportunity. A historical account must ask both what a law said and what happened to people trying to act under it.

This also changes how we read a participant’s statement. A person may explain a vote by calling suffrage a right. That gives evidence about the explanation the person offered. It does not record every later attempt to register, every local rule, or every woman’s experience.
