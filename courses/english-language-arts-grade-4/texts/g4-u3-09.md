### Momotaro and His Companions

Japanese tradition, from Japan. Momotaro is a Japanese folktale told in different versions. This one follows Yei Theodora Ozaki's older English telling. It shortens the fighting and omits graphic injury. Its ogres are imaginary beings, never a name for a real people.

1. An old couple worked hard near their home. One day the woman went to wash clothes in the river while the man cut grass in the hills. A great peach floated downstream. She brought it home, intending to share it, but a child appeared from inside the fruit before they could eat it. The couple welcomed him and called him Momotaro, the son of a peach. They cared for him as he grew, and he became strong and courageous. This marvelous beginning belongs to a Japanese tale, not to a record of an actual child.

2. When Momotaro was older, he asked to make a journey. On an island lived ogres who had carried people away and taken their belongings. Momotaro wanted to stop them and bring the captives home. The old couple worried about parting, but they let him go. They made rice cakes for the journey. Taking leave was difficult even for a confident traveler. Momotaro promised to return and set out with food from the home he was leaving. His strength did not remove his affection for the people who had raised him.

3. Along the way he met a dog, a monkey and a pheasant. The meetings did not all begin peacefully. Questions, threats and demands came before agreement. Momotaro shared pieces of his cakes and accepted the animals as companions. Each could move and act differently: the dog ran on the ground, the monkey climbed and the pheasant flew. A journey that had begun with one traveler became a journey of four. Food helped bring the group together, but sharing food did not immediately make every companion friendly toward the others.

4. The dog and monkey quarreled as they walked. Later, the dog and pheasant also fought. Momotaro stopped them and insisted that they travel in harmony. If they kept attacking one another, how could they meet the difficulty ahead? The animals agreed to stop quarreling. Their progress now depended on more than gathering strong companions. Those companions needed to work together. Having different abilities could help the group only if disagreement did not prevent them from using those abilities. They continued toward the coast with a shared purpose.

5. At the sea, the travelers faced something unfamiliar. The animals had crossed hills and valleys, but the open water frightened them. They asked to remain with Momotaro rather than be left behind. He found a boat, and they sailed together. At first the movement of the water was strange; gradually they became accustomed to it. They watched for the island, talked and passed the time together. The sea crossing separated the gathering of companions from the danger that awaited them at the ogres' stronghold.

6. At the island, the pheasant flew ahead toward the fortress while Momotaro approached with the dog and monkey. High walls and strong gates blocked the way. Two captives showed Momotaro a small back entrance. Their knowledge helped the companions enter where a direct approach had been difficult. Together they overcame the ogres. Momotaro freed the prisoners and gathered the belongings taken from their homes. The purpose stated before the journey could now be fulfillled: the people would no longer remain prisoners on the island.

7. Momotaro and his companions returned. The rescued people reached their families, and the old couple welcomed Momotaro home. The journey ended where it had begun, but he returned with experiences and companions he had not possessed at the start. This adaptation shortens the fighting in Ozaki's telling and omits graphic details. The ogres are fantastic creatures within this story, never a name for an actual people. The ending asks readers to remember both the helpers who made the journey possible and the people whose release gave it a purpose.
