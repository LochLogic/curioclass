### A. Pufferfish: Changing the size of the problem

#### One name, many fish

Pufferfish are a group of fish rather than one species. Many live in salt water, while some live in fresh water. A statement about one pufferfish should not automatically become a statement about all of them. Research begins by noticing the scope of a source. An article about a family of animals can explain a shared defense while leaving some species details unanswered.

The defense that gives pufferfish their familiar name is inflation. To inflate is to fill and become larger. A pufferfish can rapidly pump water into its stomach. As its body expands, it looks larger to a threatening fish or bird. In water, this change depends on taking in water, not on filling with a mysterious gas made inside the fish.

#### Following the water

The stomach has a different role in this defense from the role it plays in an ordinary meal. Water entering it can increase the fish's body size. First water is pumped in. Then the body expands. The changed shape presents a larger object to a predator. Cause and effect connect the steps: incoming water causes expansion, and expansion changes what the predator faces.

Inflation can make a pufferfish look harder to take as prey. That does not mean every predator leaves or every encounter ends safely. A careful explanation uses "can" because the defense offers a possibility. It avoids "always" because no observation in this article supports that promise. A fish has a defense without having complete control over everything around it.

#### A useful distinction

Some pufferfish also carry powerful toxins. The yellowfin pufferfish is one example associated with a toxin that can be dangerous if consumed. That fact should not become "every pufferfish has the same poison." Inflation and toxicity are different features. One changes shape; the other can make eating the animal harmful. A source needs to support each claim separately.

Pufferfish and porcupinefish are related, but they belong to different families. Both can inflate. Some porcupinefish have much longer spines that stand out when their bodies expand. A picture of a long-spined porcupinefish does not prove every pufferfish has those spines. Classify means to place things in groups by shared features. Classification helps a researcher avoid moving a detail from one group to another.

#### Looking for relevant evidence

Suppose an illustrated observation scene shows a small spiny fish becoming rounder as another fish approaches. This scene is invented for practice. The observer writes, "The outline changed." That is different from writing, "The predator became frightened." The first records an appearance; the second assigns a feeling that the drawing cannot establish. Relevant evidence answers the question being investigated without adding an unsupported thought.

For a notebook question about how inflation works, the path of water is relevant. For a question about habitat, the distinction between salt water and fresh water is relevant. Both details concern pufferfish, but they answer different questions. Useful research notes preserve the location of each detail so the writer can return to its context before using it.

The animal book explains how several defenses differ. This article concentrates on inflation and warns against transferring traits between families. Read together, the texts support a more precise explanation than either title alone. A body can enlarge by taking in water, and that enlargement can contribute to defense. The evidence explains a mechanism without turning every pufferfish into the same imaginary fish.

### B. Nine-banded armadillo: Protection that bends

#### A particular armored animal

The nine-banded armadillo is a mammal with a bony covering over much of its body. It is mostly active at night. The word "armadillo" can refer to several different animals, so the full name matters. A fact about a three-banded armadillo cannot simply be pasted into an explanation of this species. A research question becomes clearer when it names the particular animal.

Its armor contains bony plates called scutes. Jointed bands cross its middle. Those details describe a covering with connected parts, not a single solid lump. The hard plates help protect the body, while the banded arrangement permits bending. Protection and movement work together. The animal can travel through its habitat without leaving its covering behind.

#### How the covering helps

Think of a side view of an armadillo walking. The curved upper surface places a hard layer between much of the body and the outside. The bands allow that covered body to move. A mechanism is the way parts or actions produce an effect. Here, the explanation must connect hardness with protection and jointing with movement. Merely listing the word "scutes" would not explain either relationship.

The nine-banded armadillo cannot curl completely into a ball. That limit matters when comparing it with some other armadillos. If a source uses a picture of a tightly closed ball, check which animal it describes. A mistaken species can produce a convincing-looking explanation that is wrong. The detail may be true somewhere else and still be wrong for this subject.

#### Moving through a habitat

Smell helps this armadillo locate food. Its diet includes insects and other small creatures. A long, sticky tongue helps gather food. These are useful details for investigating how the animal lives, although they do not turn its tongue into armor. An animal uses different parts for different tasks. Research categories keep those tasks from becoming a muddled list of interesting features.

When startled, a nine-banded armadillo can jump upward. That response can surprise a potential predator. It is a behavior rather than a plate or a band. A sudden jump and a hard covering are therefore different kinds of evidence about survival. Neither detail means the animal is safe in every situation. A response that helps in one encounter may not solve every problem.

Imagine a nighttime observation, invented for practice. An armadillo moves past a fallen branch, pauses and sniffs the ground. A researcher remains at a distance and draws the bands across its back. Nothing in the observation tests the strength of the covering. The sketch records appearance, while the scientific sources explain the covering's protective role. Separating those jobs makes the evidence easier to use.

#### Building an explanation

The animal book emphasizes the relationship between hard plates and flexible bands. The federal excerpt adds an explicit limit: this armadillo cannot close into a complete ball. These details belong together in an informative paragraph. One explains how the covering helps; the other prevents a reader from imagining protection that the animal does not have.

An essay can group facts by aspect: covering, movement and other features of daily life. Another investigation might group them differently. A heading should tell a reader what the next group explains. In the conclusion, return to the connection among parts, actions and protection. The nine-banded armadillo's defense is a moving body with a hard covering, not a magical sealed container.

### C. Striped skunk: A warning before spray

#### A noticeable coat

A striped skunk's coat contrasts black with white. Its stripes can vary from one individual to another. The conspicuous pattern serves as warning coloration. Conspicuous means easy to notice. That is different from camouflage, which can make an animal difficult to detect against a background. A warning may be useful precisely because another animal notices it.

Striped skunks live in several kinds of habitat, including open and wooded areas. They are mostly active at night and often rest in dens during the day. Hollow logs, brush piles and abandoned animal dens can provide shelter. A habitat is the setting where an animal lives. A den is a particular shelter within that setting. Those words answer related but different research questions.

#### From glands to a directed defense

The skunk's strongest-smelling defense begins in two glands near the base of its tail. A gland is a body structure that produces and releases a substance. These glands supply a strong-smelling fluid. The skunk can aim and spray it toward a threat. The fluid can irritate an attacker's eyes as well as producing a powerful odor.

This mechanism joins structure and behavior. The glands supply the fluid; aiming and releasing it are actions. A researcher who writes only "The skunk smells" leaves out much of the explanation. A more useful account identifies where the defensive fluid comes from, how it is directed and why an approaching animal might draw back. Each part connects a detail with its role.

#### Warnings in sequence

A skunk may stamp its feet, arch its back and lift its tail before spraying. These movements can warn an approaching animal. If the animal withdraws, the encounter may end without spray. If the threat continues, the skunk may release fluid. This is a possible sequence, not a rule requiring every encounter to follow exactly the same steps.

In an observation scene invented for practice, a wary fox pauses beside a path. The skunk stamps. The fox recoils, drawing back sharply, and the skunk moves away. The scene illustrates how a warning could interrupt an approach. It does not claim that every fox responds this way. Nor does it show a measured trial of which warning works best.

The skunk's supply of spray is limited and takes time to replace. Warning an animal away can therefore be useful without using fluid. The exact amount available varies, so a simple explanation does not need a fixed number of sprays. The supported relationship is enough: a limited supply gives meaning to a defense that can begin with visible warnings.

#### Explaining a limit

The spray discourages many predators, but some animals still prey on skunks. A defense need not stop every predator to matter. Avoid changing "can discourage" into "nothing can approach." That stronger statement would go beyond the evidence. Accurate scientific writing leaves space for variation among animals and among encounters.

The federal excerpt gives a concise description of the spray. This article adds the glands, warning posture and limited supply. Integrating the sources means connecting those details, not copying one paragraph after another. The white stripes can make the animal recognizable; the movements can warn; the glands provide a further defense. The details describe several parts of one protective system.

In a conclusion, connect appearance, body structures and behavior to the central question. The striped skunk has more than an unpleasant smell. It has a directed chemical defense and recognizable warnings that can discourage an approach before the fluid is used. Those connections explain why a visible animal may still be a difficult choice for a predator.

### D. North American porcupine: Quills and a route upward

#### A coat with sharp points

The North American porcupine has quills along its back, sides and tail. Quills are modified hairs with sharp, barbed tips. Modified means changed in form. The quills are body structures, not objects the porcupine collects from the forest. They provide a defense that depends on contact with another animal. The porcupine does not launch them through the air.

When threatened, a porcupine can turn its back toward the threat and raise its quills. Turning presents the protected surface. Raising the quills makes the points more exposed. The structure and behavior work together: sharp hairs supply the protection, while a change in body position puts them where a predator may encounter them. Listing the two details separately would leave their connection unexplained.

#### Why loose attachment matters

Quills are loosely attached to the porcupine's skin. They can come away if another animal touches them. That attachment is an important part of the mechanism. A predator that makes contact can encounter sharp points, and quills can detach. The explanation does not require a porcupine to throw anything. Contact, rather than flight through open space, links the bodies.

Loose attachment and sharp points do different jobs. The point concerns what meets the other animal. The attachment concerns how a quill can come away. A diagram can separate those relationships so a reader notices both. Describing a quill as merely "hard" would leave out the details that make this particular defense understandable.

#### A second kind of protection

Porcupines are slow on the ground, but they are strong climbers. Their claws help them climb trees. A tree can provide refuge, a place of safety from a threat below. The route upward is different from the quill defense. One depends on the animal's coat and contact; the other depends on movement to a different place.

Porcupines eat plant material, including leaves, roots and bark. They are mainly active at night. Trees can therefore be important in more than one part of a porcupine's life. A source about food and a source about defense may both mention trees while answering different questions. Notes need to preserve the reason a detail belongs in a particular category.

#### Reading an observation carefully

Imagine a porcupine near a tree in an invented observation scene. A larger animal approaches. The porcupine turns its back, and the approaching animal pauses. Later, the porcupine climbs. An observer could record the turn, pause and climb. The observer could not tell from that sequence alone exactly what the other animal was thinking. An inference should remain connected to the details actually available.

The animal book briefly compares quills with an armadillo's armor. This article and the federal page give the porcupine more room: coat, positioning, loose attachment and climbing. A useful summary preserves the central relationship among those details. It does not need every plant in the diet, but it should not erase the distinction between a sharp coat and a route to refuge.

An explanation of survival can therefore have more than one section. One might explain quill contact; another might explain climbing. A conclusion can bring them together without claiming perfect safety. The porcupine has protective body structures and useful behaviors. Their combined possibilities help explain how a slow animal can still be difficult for a predator to catch.

### E. Common octopus: Staying hidden, then moving away

#### A soft body near rocks

The common octopus has eight arms and a soft body. Its arms carry suckers that help it grasp. It can occupy a den in a rocky crevice, a narrow opening between rocks. Unlike the nine-banded armadillo, it does not depend on a broad covering of bony armor. Its body and its actions support other kinds of protection.

The name common octopus identifies a particular kind of octopus. Other octopuses can have different traits. This article focuses on the common octopus's camouflage, ink and movement. A source about a different species may help with a comparison, but it cannot automatically supply every fact about this animal. Keeping the name beside a note helps preserve that boundary.

#### How skin changes appearance

An octopus can change its skin pattern against its surroundings. Tiny skin structures called chromatophores contain pigment, a colored substance. Muscles control expansion and contraction of pigment sacs. Expansion makes more color visible. Many such changes across the skin alter the animal's appearance. This is a mechanism involving body structures, rather than a layer of material borrowed from the background.

Skin texture can also change. Projections called papillae can make the surface appear rougher or smoother. Color pattern and texture together can resemble a rocky setting. Camouflage means an appearance that makes an animal harder to detect or recognize against its surroundings. It can help a soft animal remain unnoticed without making it truly invisible.

Background matters. An appearance that blends with one surface may stand out against another. A diagram of an octopus alone can show parts, but it cannot show the whole camouflage relationship. To explain concealment, include something about the surrounding surface and the viewer. The animal remains present even when its outline is difficult to locate.

#### When concealment is not enough

A common octopus can release ink when threatened. Ink can confuse a predator while the octopus moves away. The animal also has a siphon, a tube-like opening used to direct expelled water. Water enters the mantle cavity, a space within its muscular body covering. Contraction then forces water through the siphon, helping propel the octopus.

The phrase "jet propulsion" names this movement by a directed stream. Eject means to force something out; the octopus ejects water through its siphon. Ink and movement contribute differently. Ink can interfere with a predator's view or attention. Jetting changes the octopus's position. Neither response means the predator is guaranteed to stop or the octopus can escape through any opening whatever its size.

#### From evidence to a story

An invented scene might show an octopus remaining against a rock as a fish passes. A second scene might show ink followed by movement toward a crevice. These are plausible possibilities supported by the defense mechanisms, not records of events that researchers observed here. A story can invent a particular encounter while keeping those biological details accurate.

The animal book explains camouflage beside several other defenses. This article adds a closer account of the siphon and mantle cavity. Reading both helps connect a defense with the structures that make it possible. In a notebook, appearance and escape could become separate categories. In an essay, headings could explain each mechanism. In either form, the conclusion should connect the animal's soft body with several ways of avoiding a threat.
