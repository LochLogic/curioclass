## Installment 1: The shelf above the coats

The blue tin was supposed to contain buttons. That was what Grandmother June said when she asked Maya to bring it downstairs. Maya wanted round brass buttons for a cardboard boat. The boat already had a cabin and a smokestack, but its windows were holes, and the whole thing looked surprised.

"Top shelf, above the winter coats," Grandmother said. "Ask Leo to reach."

Maya did not ask. Her brother was making a paper bridge across the kitchen chairs, and asking meant hearing a speech about triangles. She carried the little step stool to the closet instead. Grandmother followed and held it while Maya climbed. Behind a hatbox stood a dull blue rectangle, with a pale circle where its paint had worn away.

The lid gave a small metallic sigh. There were no buttons. Folded papers filled the tin, and a brown envelope lay across them like a sleeping hand. Maya could read one word on it: Letters.

"Wrong tin," she said.

Grandmother put her hand beneath the box. "Perhaps the right mistake. Bring the stool back first."

They set the tin on the clear end of the dining table. Sunlight made a bright square on its lid. Leo arrived carrying half a bridge and immediately asked whether the box contained money. When Grandmother said no, he stayed anyway.

Maya unfolded the top sheet. The writing leaned steeply to the right. Some letters were loops, others little hills, and the line at the bottom seemed to run off the edge. At the top was a date from 1920.

"Whose letters?" Maya asked.

"I believe some belonged to Ruth Bennett, my mother's older sister. Your great-great-aunt Ruth. They came with other family papers. I haven't sorted them."

Ruth was a name Maya knew from a photograph. In it, a woman stood beside a chair without sitting down. Maya had always thought she looked as though she had somewhere else to go.

"Was she famous?"

"She was family," Grandmother said. "That is enough reason to read."

One line was clearer than the rest: We must have the notices ready before the meeting.

Notices sounded boring. A meeting sounded worse. Maya turned the sheet and found another line about women and the vote. Now she leaned closer.

At school, a vote usually meant choosing between two books or deciding which game to play. This letter seemed to mean something larger. She pictured Ruth standing beside that chair, holding an enormous notice, telling a whole room what to do.

"Maybe she helped change the law," Maya said.

"Maybe," Grandmother answered. "Let's find out what the paper actually says."

Leo lowered his bridge onto a chair. "We could make a display for family Saturday."

Family Saturday was a loose arrangement. Relatives brought food to Grandmother's house, children filled the hallway, and someone usually told a story twice. This month's gathering was only a few weeks away. A display would be something new.

Maya saw it already: the blue tin in the middle, Ruth's photograph standing behind it, her own carefully drawn signs all around. She would finally have something to show besides a boat that would never float.

"I'll write the account," she said.

"What kind?" Leo asked.

"The account of what happened."

Grandmother brought a notebook. "Then you need somewhere for what you know and somewhere for what you wonder."

Maya wrote Ruth and the Vote across the first page. Under it she copied the line about notices. She wanted to publish something beautiful, even if publishing meant hanging a sheet in Grandmother's hallway. Leo suggested a picture of the meeting. Maya drew a rectangle for a room, then stopped. She did not know what the room looked like.

"We can discuss the picture later," she said.

The first page of her notebook seemed small beside the stack of letters. Each sheet might contain another piece of information. If she could integrate those pieces, fit them together, perhaps the whole story would appear.

But beneath her excitement was a slight worry. Grandmother had said some letters belonged to Ruth. Some was a word with a hole in it.

Outside, a delivery truck rattled past. The ordinary afternoon continued while Maya held a sheet from another time. She listened to Grandmother read the crooked line once more. For a moment the notices seemed less boring. Someone had needed them ready.

Maya read the date 1920 on a letter, then wrote today's date in her notebook. "It mentions a meeting, but not who could attend," she said.

## Installment 2: A careful picture

On her next visit, Maya found the tin waiting on the dining table. Beside it lay a photograph, a clean towel, and a note from Grandmother: We will work upstairs today. The dining table was needed for rolling dough, and paper did not belong beside flour.

The attic room was not the shadowy place Maya remembered. Grandmother had cleared an old sewing table beneath the window. Two chairs faced it. A wooden shelf held labeled boxes, and a small lamp made a yellow pool in the daylight. There was room to look without holding everything on a lap.

Leo went straight to the window. "You can see the tops of people's heads."

Maya went straight to the photograph. A young Black woman stood beside a wooden chair. Her dark skirt fell in straight folds. She rested one hand on the chair's back and held a closed book in the other. Her eyes faced the camera; her chin was slightly raised.

On the reverse, someone had written Ruth, then a question mark in darker ink.

"Even the photograph is asking," Maya said.

Grandmother placed it on the towel. "That question mark is mine. I think this is Ruth. I would like another reason besides thinking."

Maya had brought a drawing for the display. In her picture, Ruth stood on a platform under a banner. Rows of smiling people faced her. It had taken Maya nearly an hour to draw all their ears.

"What do you think?" she asked.

Grandmother looked carefully. "Tell me which parts came from the photograph."

Maya pointed to the straight skirt and the hand on the chair. Then she realized that her drawing did not contain a chair. She had changed it into a lectern, the tall stand a speaker might use.

"I thought it would show her work better."

"It shows what you imagined about her work," Grandmother said. "That can be a picture, too. But it needs the right label."

Maya wanted to fold the drawing and hide it. All those ears seemed foolish now. Yet Grandmother had not said it was bad. She had asked what supported it.

Leo pulled up a chair. "I can draw the room in the photograph. No ears needed."

"I can do it," Maya said, too quickly.

She drew again. This time she kept the chair, the closed book, and Ruth's raised chin. The blank background was hard to leave alone. Maya wanted to add a window, a plant, something that would make the picture feel complete. Instead she wrote Background not shown in photograph beneath it.

Grandmother smiled. "That is a careful picture."

"A cautious picture?" Leo asked.

"Related words," Grandmother said. "Careful means paying close attention here. Cautious suggests watching for a possible risk. Maya is doing both, perhaps."

Maya liked careful better. Cautious sounded as though she was afraid of a drawing. But when she reached for a folded envelope, a split along its edge opened farther. She froze.

Grandmother slid a clean sheet beneath it. "Leave that fold for now."

Maya looked at the tiny tear. One quick movement, and the old paper had changed. She could redraw an ear. She could not put these fibers back.

Together they read a few lines from a letter headed July 1920.

Dear Elsie,

You asked why the notices say suffrage. It means the right to vote. We want the words understood before the meeting begins. An amendment would change the Constitution. For the states to ratify it means they formally agree to it. We must explain those words, not merely print them large.

The next line ran into the fold. Grandmother did not force it open.

Maya copied the clear sentences. Now the movement for the vote had words she could follow. It also had ordinary work: explaining, printing, carrying notices. Her imagined platform suddenly felt much farther away.

"Does exclusion mean leaving someone out?" she asked, pointing to a word near the torn edge.

"Yes. We need the rest of the sentence before we explain this use."

The lamp hummed faintly. Below them, someone closed a cupboard. Maya considered her first picture again. The waving banner had seemed full of information, but most of that information had come from her pencil.

She set the two drawings side by side. One would go in the display. The other would stay in her notebook, with its own honest label: First idea.

Maya put down the torn envelope. "I will copy the words before unfolding it again." She fetched a clean sheet.

## Installment 3: The voice in the room

Grandmother could read the slanting handwriting faster than Maya could. That afternoon she read a letter aloud while Maya followed its lines with a finger held above the page. Leo sat on the floor drawing the attic window. His window had exactly eight panes, though the real one had six.

"I like mine better," he said when Maya noticed.

"Then call it a window you imagined."

"I will."

Maya felt oddly pleased. She had borrowed Grandmother's question and made it her own. Perhaps that was how families worked: people left words lying around until someone else picked them up.

Grandmother began again.

Dear Elsie,

The notices are ready! We counted them twice because I had written one address on two different envelopes. Annie caught it before the bundle went out. I told her she had saved me a journey. She said she would rather save me two.

Maya laughed. The letter sounded more like a person now. Ruth, if it was Ruth, could make mistakes. She could also joke about them.

Grandmother read the next sentence quietly.

I thought the hardest part would be getting our words onto paper. Now I wonder who will get to hear them.

"Why did you change your voice?" Maya asked.

"How did it change?"

"You got quieter. And you stopped longer before now."

"The exclamation point earlier gave me one clue. The question this writer raises gives me another. But a reader still makes choices. Another reader could sound thoughtful without sounding sad."

Maya tried the sentence herself. At first she rushed it, as though the writer was still laughing about the envelopes. Then she slowed before Now. The sentence changed without any of its words changing.

Leo put down his pencil. "I can hear the turn."

Maya wished the display could talk. A visitor might pass a written label without noticing it, but a voice could catch the pause she had nearly missed. She asked Grandmother whether they could make a short recording for family Saturday.

"You can read a copy," Grandmother said. "Keep your first attempt until you have tried another."

They used the household recorder on the desk. Maya sat upright and announced every word so carefully that the letter sounded like a list of station stops. Leo laughed at the final Dear Elsie, which she had accidentally read twice.

Maya stopped the recording. "That was awful."

"It was understandable," Grandmother said. "Now decide what else you want a listener to understand."

They listened again. The words were clear. The joke was missing, and the wondering sentence sounded exactly like the sentence about envelopes. Maya marked her copy: a small upward curve at the joke, a slash where she wanted to pause. She did not mark the original.

On the second recording, she imagined the writer looking across a table at Elsie. The words softened. The pause became a moment when someone might have looked out a window.

"That part about the window is your idea," Leo reminded her.

"I know. I'll keep it out of the label."

A later line in the letter said that a reply had not yet arrived. Nothing explained why. Maya could imagine a lost envelope, an argument, a busy person who had forgotten. None of those possibilities belonged in the account yet.

Grandmother went downstairs to check the oven. Leo followed the smell. Left alone, Maya played the second recording once more. She heard her own breath and, beneath it, another person's questions. The distance between them had not vanished. The writer had known things Maya did not know, and Maya knew things the writer could not have guessed.

She wrote two headings on a fresh page: Written details and Reading choices. Under the first she listed the exclamation point and the change from counted to wonder. Under the second she listed her brighter voice and the longer pause. Both columns mattered, but they did different jobs.

From downstairs Leo called, "There's bread!"

Maya reached to put the letter away. A small corner caught against the rim of the tin. She could free it quickly, or she could move the whole sheet first. The pause from her recording seemed to remain in the room after the sound had stopped.

"Not yet," Maya whispered. She held the brittle page flat.

## Installment 4: Only an initial

Rain kept the family indoors on Saturday. Drops tapped the attic window, and a loose branch made a soft scraping sound against the wall. Grandmother had asked Maya to choose one object for the hallway display before lunch. It seemed a simple request until Maya tried to choose.

The photograph had a question mark. The first drawing had a platform no photograph showed. The letter about notices had a clear date but an uncertain signature. Every object seemed to bring along a question that needed its own object.

Leo suggested displaying the tin closed.

"Then we can say it holds secrets," he said.

"It holds papers."

"Papers can have secrets."

Maya was keen to begin. She brought a large sheet upstairs and drew a chart with generous boxes. One box would hold the writer's name, another the date, another what the writer had done. She sharpened two pencils so that nothing could slow her down.

The rain stopped. A pale stripe of sun crossed the table, making the faded ink look darker. This was the good moment, Maya decided. She could finally write something definite.

### Checkpoint scene, first read on day 123

Maya stood beside the attic table, eager to open the blue tin. Inside lay a folded letter and an envelope with a faded stamp. She copied the last line onto her chart: an initial, R., followed by a looping mark that might have been part of the signature. Below it she wrote, Ruth wanted everyone to vote.

"Does that letter say Ruth?" her brother asked.

Maya looked again. Only an initial appeared where she had expected a name. The letter spoke about a meeting but did not say who had been allowed inside. Her excitement had carried her farther than the words.

"Ruth?" she said. She added the question mark to her chart. "I want to find out, but I shouldn't put words in her mouth."

Checking every letter suddenly seemed a Herculean task. Maya remembered the hero facing one difficult labor after another. Still, she drew a second column beside her first. She headed it Questions and moved her claim into it. The tin held a beginning, not all the answers.

### After the chart

Leo watched her move the claim. Maya expected him to say that he had been right. Instead he asked whether Questions needed a wider column. She nodded, and he held the ruler while she drew a new line.

"I thought eager meant impatient," he said.

Maya considered the sharpened pencils. "I wanted to start. But I wasn't angry about waiting. Not until you asked me that question."

"So then you were impatient?"

"A little."

They both laughed. Grandmother came upstairs carrying a tray with three cups. She set it on the shelf well away from the papers and listened to their explanation.

"Is the question mark permanent?" she asked.

Maya shook her head. "Until we find something else."

"Then it is doing useful work."

Downstairs, the telephone rang. Grandmother went to answer it, leaving the children with the chart. Maya looked at the empty box where her bold claim had been. She did not like its emptiness. A visitor would want a story, not a row of holes.

But she remembered the mythical hero Heracles from the tale they had read in class. One labor did not finish all his work. Perhaps a hard task could begin with one small part. She could identify the date without identifying the writer. She could explain the notices without inventing the whole meeting.

She wrote A letter dated 1920 mentions preparations for a meeting. The sentence did not shine like Ruth wanted everyone to vote. It also did not pretend.

Leo read it. "Could we put the question underneath?"

Maya wrote Who signed this letter? Under that she added Who was able to attend?

When Grandmother returned, the chart had two entries and a wide Questions column. Maya was no longer reluctant to show it to her. She wanted to explain the correction as well as the discovery.

Outside, the branch scratched once against the wall. Maya picked up her first drawing and placed it behind the chart. She had not thrown it away. There was a reason to keep an early idea, even when it needed changing.

The cardboard boat waited in her bag downstairs. Its empty windows could wait another afternoon. For now she had found a different kind of opening: a question she knew how to ask.

## Installment 5: Dear Elsie

The next envelope contained two sheets. One was a letter. The other was a list of names with small squares beside them. Maya recognized the handwriting on the list: the same steep loops, the same long tail on the letter y. She wanted to place it beside the letter they had already read.

Grandmother brought a third sheet from another box. It was a much later note, written in clear blue ink. At the bottom stood the full name Ruth Bennett.

"A signature we can read," she said. "It came with a birthday card."

Maya laid her copies side by side. Ruth's capital R looked like a person stepping forward. The old initial had a similar forward step, though its ink was faint.

"They look alike," Maya said.

"They do," Grandmother agreed.

"So can I write Ruth now?"

"You can explain the evidence. Matching handwriting helps. It may not settle every sheet in the box."

That answer felt slower than Maya wanted. Still, it was better than having nothing. She wrote Probably Ruth Bennett on a loose label and added a sentence about the matching handwriting. Leo suggested drawing a tiny stepping person beside the R, but Maya saved that idea for the back of her notebook.

Then they read the new letter.

July 1920

Dear Elsie,

I have taken your advice and made a list. I would rather remember a neighbor's name than write it down, but memory does not carry a bundle very well. On Tuesday I brought notices to three houses and forgot the fourth. Annie asked where I had been, and I answered everywhere. She put her finger on the blank square beside Mrs. Bell's name.

We walked there together the following afternoon. Mrs. Bell had been waiting to hear the time of our meeting. She said she could not leave her work until evening. I had not thought about that when I helped choose the hour.

Annie has asked the women to tell us which evening they can come. We shall ask first and choose afterward. It takes longer to ask. It might take much longer to choose a time when nobody can attend.

Maya stopped. Her own display was going to be in the hallway because she liked the hallway's light. Had she asked Grandmother whether people could stop there without blocking the kitchen? She put a small question beside Hallway in her notebook.

"Keep reading," Leo said. "What happened with Mrs. Bell?"

The letter did not tell them. It continued with news about a missing spool of thread, Elsie's cough, and a cake that had collapsed in the middle. Maya wanted to skip the cake. Grandmother read it anyway.

"This wasn't written for your display," she said. "It was written to Elsie."

Maya pictured a person waiting for news of a cough and a cake. Ruth had been working for the vote, but she had not been working every minute. Perhaps Elsie would have been disappointed by a letter containing only important events.

The next paragraph made Maya sit straighter.

You wrote that we should join the other committee. Annie and I went to ask about it. The woman at the door said their gathering was for white women. We are Black women, Elsie, and the word women on a notice ought to mean us too. Annie answered that our own meeting would go ahead. This evening we worked on our notice again. We chose the place, counted the chairs, and left room to hear questions.

I will not make this letter only an account of that doorway. You asked what we are doing. We are learning which neighbors want to speak. We are trying to hear those whom we have missed. I have asked Mrs. Bell to bring a question of her own.

Your loving sister,
Ruth

For a while nobody spoke. Below the table, Leo turned a pencil between his fingers. Maya looked at the line about the doorway. She had drawn rows of smiling people in her first picture. She had never asked whether the rows included Ruth.

"Was that allowed?" she asked.

"The letter describes an unfair choice in this story," Grandmother said. "Racism also excluded Black women from parts of the real suffrage movement. We'll read history alongside these family papers. One family's account cannot describe every place."

Maya read Annie's answer again. The meeting would go ahead. She had first imagined Ruth as a lone speaker under a banner. Now she saw neighbors choosing a time, counting chairs, and asking who had been missed.

Leo wanted the display to include the cake.

"Why?" Maya asked.

"Because she's funny. And because it really mattered to Elsie."

Maya almost said that the cake was not important. Instead she copied its sentence onto another sheet. The display could not hold everything, but her notebook could keep something she had not yet learned how to use.

They carried their copies downstairs. At the kitchen table, Grandmother asked them to explain the letter without reading it word for word. Maya began with the names, then wandered into the cake, then lost the meeting's time. Leo asked where Mrs. Bell belonged.

Maya started again. This time she kept the important events together. Ruth and Annie had missed a neighbor, asked about a better time, and continued organizing after another committee excluded them. The cake was still there, but it did not need to carry the whole account.

When she returned to the attic, the names on the list looked different. They were no longer decorations around a story about one person. Someone had needed to reach each one.

Maya, who had hurried earlier, slowed down. At the desk where her notes lay, she checked each name before copying it.

## Installment 6: Two kinds of paper

Leo brought a printed history page to the attic. It came from a federal archive, a place that keeps records and helps people study them. Grandmother had helped him find it. At the top, he had written its title and address so they could return to it.

"This one isn't from the tin," he announced.

Maya made space for it. Until now she had thought of the tin as a whole world. The new page made that world seem smaller, but it also gave it a place on a larger timeline.

The archive explained that Congress had passed the proposed Nineteenth Amendment in June 1919. Tennessee ratified it on August 18, 1920. Later that month, the ratification was certified. The legal text said a citizen's vote could not be denied or restricted because of sex.

Maya read the sentence twice. She underlined because of sex on her copy. Those words were more exact than her first idea, which had been that the amendment simply let everyone vote.

"Then the July letter was before Tennessee ratified," she said.

Leo drew a long line on a clean sheet. They placed July before August without inventing a day for Ruth's letter. It looked strange floating between two marks, but floating was better than fastening it to the wrong point.

The archive also explained that discriminatory state voting laws kept many women from voting after ratification. A change in the Constitution had not removed every barrier. Grandmother asked Maya to leave room for that information beside the timeline.

"I thought we were making the timeline simpler," Maya said.

"Clearer," Leo said. "Maybe not shorter."

They returned to the letters. A sheet near the bottom of the tin had separated along an old fold. One narrow piece held only the words I will bring. The rest of the sentence remained on the larger piece. Maya set them side by side, but their edges curled apart.

### Reserved moment E1-a

Maya reached toward the wastebasket, then stopped with the torn note in her hand. She laid a clean sheet beneath both pieces and another above them. On a separate slip she wrote Torn note, pieces kept together. She put the packet flat inside an empty folder and set it beside the tin.

### The afternoon continues

The next letter was less damaged. Its first lines were clear.

August 1920

Dear Elsie,

We heard news from Tennessee today. Annie came with the report, and we sat without speaking for a little while. Then Mrs. Bell asked what we would do next. We had thought so often about this change that the word next surprised me.

We shall meet again. There are neighbors whose questions have not been answered. There is still work before us. I am glad, Elsie, and I am not finished.

Maya read the last sentence aloud. She had expected the letter after the news to sound like an ending. This one sounded like someone taking a breath.

Leo tapped the printed archive page. "The letter is one person's account. This page is a later explanation."

"And both have dates," Maya said. "But the date isn't the only difference."

She wrote Letter beside Ruth's words and Archive account beside the history page. Under both she left a space for what each could establish. The letter could tell her what its writer described doing and feeling. The archive could help explain the legal change. Neither could supply the missing part of the other just because Maya wanted it.

Downstairs, Grandmother called that supper was ready. Maya covered the tin, then looked once more at the line I am glad, Elsie, and I am not finished. She carried that sentence with her down the stairs.

## Installment 7: A sentence with edges

At supper, Maya told her father that Ruth had helped win the vote. She said it with the confidence of someone who had spent hours reading difficult handwriting. Her father listened, then asked what helped meant.

"She sent notices and held meetings."

"Which meetings?"

Maya looked toward Grandmother. "The ones in the letters."

"That sounds worth showing," her father said. "I just want to understand how much the letters tell us."

His question irritated her because it was fair. Maya wanted her work to sound large. Notices seemed small beside a constitutional amendment. Yet she could not connect Ruth to a lawmaker's decision merely by drawing a line between them.

After supper she returned to the attic with Leo. They read the archive page again, this time looking for what belonged on the public timeline and what belonged in the family account. Maya made the two lines run beside each other instead of joining them with an arrow.

The public line named the amendment and Tennessee's ratification. The family line named Ruth and Annie preparing notices. It also named Mrs. Bell, whose work hours had changed their plans. Keeping the lines apart did not make either disappear.

Leo found another short family note.

Dear Elsie,

You ask whether the meeting was a success. I know we heard Mrs. Bell's question. I know Annie explained the notice after I hurried over it. I know two neighbors stayed afterward to talk. I cannot tell you what every person carried home.

Maya smiled at the repeated I know. Ruth sounded certain about some things and careful about others. It was possible to be both.

"A sentence can have edges," she told Leo.

He held his pencil against the paper. "Most do."

"I mean an edge around what it says."

She tried a label: Ruth changed the country. It ran past every edge she could see. She crossed it out on her draft, then tried Ruth's letters describe preparing notices and listening to neighbors. That sentence had less thunder in it, but she could place a source beside each part.

Leo wanted to add a note about the late supper. Maya told him that their supper was outside the display's subject. He pointed to the cake in Ruth's letter. She had to admit that choosing details was harder when the details belonged to you.

They spent the next half hour moving copied sentences onto separate cards. One pile held actions the letters described. A second held questions. A third held interesting things that might not fit. Leo named that pile Cake.

Grandmother came upstairs and asked whether they were making progress. Maya showed her the two timelines and the crossed-out sentence.

"I thought a correction would make the display look unfinished," Maya said.

"Perhaps the visitor should see how you reached the sentence you kept."

Maya considered that. She did not want every messy draft pinned to the wall. But one early label might explain why the final wording mattered. It would also mean admitting that she had been wrong.

On the shelf, Ruth's photograph stood beside its question-mark label. Maya had begun to like the slightly raised chin. It no longer looked like proof that Ruth had been a speaker. It looked like a person who had agreed to have a photograph taken, while the rest of her life remained outside the frame.

Before leaving, Maya added one more sentence to the draft: These family letters show some of the work around the vote, not the whole movement. She left space underneath for a visitor's question. For the first time, the space looked planned rather than empty.

## Installment 8: The missing square

Cousin Tessa arrived with a roll of purple paper and an idea. She was staying with Grandmother for the weekend, and she had heard there would be a display. By the time Maya reached the house, Tessa had made a heading with letters almost as tall as a dinner plate.

OUR FAMILY CHANGED HISTORY.

"You can see it from the kitchen," Tessa said proudly.

Maya set down her bag. It was exactly the sort of heading she would have made a week earlier. She liked the purple paper. She liked being part of a family that could fill such a large sentence. She did not like having to say what came next.

"We need to check what we put under it."

"It's only a heading."

"People read headings too."

Tessa rolled the paper halfway closed. "Then you do it."

Grandmother was downstairs talking with Maya's father. Leo had gone to find scissors. The room seemed suddenly too quiet. Maya wished someone would interrupt and make the argument belong to everyone.

Instead she asked Tessa to look at the letters. She showed her the notices and Mrs. Bell's name. Tessa read without speaking, then pointed to Annie.

"Who is she?"

Maya opened her notebook. She knew Annie helped with notices, caught a repeated address, and asked neighbors about a meeting time. She did not know Annie's last name.

"Maybe we can find it today," Tessa said.

That was a useful question, and Maya had nearly lost it by wanting Tessa to admire the display first.

They found a letter written in a smaller, rounder hand. At the top was a date in August 1920. It began My dear Ruth. Maya felt a quick thrill. Here was a letter addressed to Ruth, with her full name on the envelope.

My dear Ruth,

You need not apologize for the short letter. I was glad to read it. You and Annie seem to have enough to do without writing an account of every hour for me.

I have thought about Mrs. Bell. Did you ask whether she would help choose the next meeting time? Being asked to attend is one thing. Being asked to help plan is another.

Your sister,
Elsie

Tessa looked up. "So Elsie has ideas too."

Maya had imagined Elsie lying quietly with a cough while news arrived. Now she pictured her reading the letters, thinking, and sending questions back. The tin held a conversation. Half of it had been harder to hear because Maya had listened mostly for Ruth.

She made a chart with a box for each person. Under Ruth she wrote Helps prepare notices. Under Annie she wrote Checks addresses and asks neighbors. Under Mrs. Bell she wrote Explains which hours she can attend. Under Elsie she wrote Asks who helps choose.

"What about her last name?" Tessa asked.

The square beside Annie stayed empty.

Leo returned with scissors and a sheet of pale green paper. He had also found a long envelope inside a box marked Family notes. It contained a family tree Grandmother had drawn years earlier.

Maya wanted to search it immediately. Then she noticed that two different people were called Anne. One had a penciled question mark beside her birth year. Neither line said Annie helped with a meeting.

"Anne and Annie could be the same person," Tessa said.

"Could be," Maya answered.

Tessa looked at her sharply, but Maya was not correcting her. She was trying the words herself. Could be left a path open without saying they had reached its end.

They went downstairs to ask Grandmother. She studied the family tree and shook her head.

"I can't identify this Annie from that sheet," she said. "I used several people's memories when I drew it. Some names need checking."

Maya had thought a family tree would settle the matter because it looked so organized. The lines were straight, the names neatly spaced. Now she saw that a tidy page could still hold uncertainty.

Tessa unrolled the purple heading again. "What about Our Family Papers and the Vote?"

Maya said the words slowly. They were less grand, but they fitted the box. Tessa cut away HISTORY and began making PAPERS. She saved the cut letters in a pile rather than dropping them on the floor.

"We could use these for something else," she said.

Maya helped hold the paper flat. She wanted to thank Tessa for changing it, but she did not want to sound like a teacher handing back an assignment. Instead she asked if Tessa would make a smaller heading for Elsie's questions.

"Of course," Tessa said. "Those are the interesting part."

Back upstairs, the chart still had a blank beside Annie. Maya no longer thought the blank ruined it. It showed them where to look next.

Maya, whose chart had an empty space, asked her brother to check another source instead of filling the gap with a guess.

## Installment 9: The label on the shelf

Tessa's purple letters covered the dining table. Leo's green arrows covered the floor. Maya had agreed that the display needed both, but now everyone seemed to be making something except the person who had found the tin.

She carried her notebook upstairs, determined to finish three labels before anyone asked another question. The attic smelled faintly of the apples Grandmother had stored in a basket near the stairs. Maya opened the notebook to the chart of names and began copying.

Ruth Bennett. Elsie Bennett. Annie.

The blank after Annie bothered her again. It was like a missing tooth in a photograph. She tried writing Annie, Ruth's friend, but friend was another word she had supplied. The letters showed that they worked together. They did not describe how they first met or what they called their relationship.

She replaced friend with fellow organizer. Then she checked the word organizer in the dictionary downstairs. The definition fitted someone who helped arrange a meeting. The longer word had earned its place by saying something more exact.

When she returned, Leo was examining the empty envelope from the shelf. "This says recipe notes," he said.

"That is where the family tree was."

"Then the recipe notes went somewhere else."

Maya shrugged. She did not need recipes. She needed a name.

Leo put the envelope on the table. "Grandmother asked if we'd seen the little card with her mother's bread recipe."

Now Maya remembered a small card that had been under the letters. She had moved it aside on the first afternoon because it was not about voting. Where had she put it? Beneath the photograph? Inside the hatbox? She could see her hand making the movement, but the place beyond her hand was blank.

They searched the table without moving the old papers. Maya checked her bag, then the floor beneath the chairs. Nothing. Downstairs, Grandmother said it was all right to keep looking later. That made Maya feel worse. She had wanted to be the person who put the family story in order, and she had misplaced a piece of it.

"Haste makes waste," Leo said.

"Please don't."

"I mean I hurried with my bridge and wasted a whole sheet. I wasn't talking about you."

Maya sat down. Her cheeks felt hot. She had heard a saying as an accusation because she was already accusing herself.

"Sorry," she said. "I rushed past the card because it wasn't part of my project."

Leo opened his notebook and showed her a crooked bridge. "This was my first one. It collapsed before I finished the last side."

They spent a few minutes explaining the two mistakes. His had come from fastening parts before checking their position. Hers had come from moving an object without remembering its place. Both had happened while they wanted to finish quickly. The saying did not mean that every fast movement was bad. It meant something useful here, in the company of these particular mistakes.

Maya made a place card for the recipe, even though they had not found it yet. She wrote Recipe card moved on first afternoon, location unknown. Then she added it to Questions. Leo said a question could be about yesterday as well as 1920.

A new letter gave them a welcome change of subject.

Dear Ruth,

You asked why I write so many questions. I could send only praise, but you would learn nothing from it except that I am your sister. You already know that.

When I ask about the meeting, I am trying to see the room you see. You say the neighbors listened. What did they ask? You say Annie helped. What did she do? Tell me the small parts. They are the parts I can picture.

Your loving Elsie

Maya laughed at the sentence about praise. She imagined asking Tessa for nothing except compliments. The display would be larger and probably less accurate. She also imagined Elsie waiting for an answer that included something she could actually picture.

She took the letter downstairs and read it to Tessa. Instead of praising the reading, Tessa asked whether the display could show a question and its answer together. Maya said yes. It was the first yes she had given to a new idea without first feeling it take something away from hers.

Later, Grandmother found the recipe card inside the towel folded beneath the photograph. Maya must have slipped it there when they moved to the attic. They put it back in its envelope, and Maya corrected her place card instead of throwing it out.

Before carrying the envelope away, she checked the writing on its front. It was not the same envelope Leo had found with the family tree. Their brown paper had fooled her memory. She wanted to put each one where it belonged this time.

"Better safe than sorry," Maya said. She returned to the shelf where she had found the envelope and checked its label.

## Installment 10: The date on the other side

The following afternoon, Grandmother brought out an empty calendar sheet. It had no year and no month, only a grid. She used its back for a larger timeline, leaving the printed squares hidden against the table.

Maya liked the smooth paper. Her notebook timeline had become crowded with arrows. On this one she could write the public dates along the top and the family dates below. Tessa offered to make the small cards. Leo offered to check that the cards were in order.

For a little while they worked without speaking. Scissors whispered through paper. The lamp made a faint clicking sound when Grandmother adjusted its shade. Maya felt the pleasure of a plan that everyone understood.

Then Tessa held up a folded sheet. "This one has two dates."

At the top it said August 1920. At the bottom of the last page, in smaller writing, it said November 1921. Maya had read only the first side before. She turned the sheet over and over, looking for a simple explanation.

"Maybe Ruth took a very long time to finish it," Leo said.

"Or maybe someone reused the paper," Grandmother suggested.

Neither answer was on the page. They needed to read.

The August letter was from Ruth to Elsie. It described a visit from Annie and a bundle of notices. On the reverse, the later note began with a different greeting.

Dear Ruth,

I found this old sheet in my desk and am using the blank side. Your letter made me smile again. I had forgotten the story about the crooked notices. Perhaps that is why it is useful to keep letters, even the ones about an ordinary afternoon.

The rest concerned a planned family visit. It was signed Elsie. The two dates belonged to two pieces of writing on one sheet. Maya had been about to put the whole sheet under August 1920, as though paper could carry only one moment.

She made two cards and drew a small linking mark between them. The cards would remain together in the display's folder, but their dates would be explained separately. Leo said the paper had traveled twice. Maya wrote that phrase in her notebook because it helped her picture what the two sides meant.

Tessa wanted to draw a tiny envelope moving between the dates. Maya agreed, as long as it did not suggest that they knew the exact day of either journey. Tessa rolled her eyes, then drew the envelope without a day.

"You can just say that once," she said.

Maya looked up. "Say what?"

"That we don't know everything. You don't have to say it after every pencil mark."

For a moment Maya wanted to defend every correction she had made. Then she looked at the chart. Almost every card ended in a warning. Some needed one. Others simply said a date and a name, followed by a caution that added nothing.

She crossed out three repeated cautions on the draft. Careful writing could still let a reader move forward. It did not have to stop the reader at every step.

Grandmother asked for a short summary of the new discovery. Maya began, "It's a very confusing letter."

"That's how it felt to you," Leo said. "What happened on the paper?"

Maya tried again. Ruth wrote in August 1920. Elsie later used the other side for a November 1921 note. The sheet carried two dated messages.

"Much clearer," Tessa said.

They gave the summary a small heading: One Sheet, Two Messages. Maya liked it better than Confusing Letter. The first heading told a visitor what to expect. The second told a visitor what Maya had felt before she understood.

Inside the tin, another note lay between two dated letters. It began Thank you for bringing the chairs and ended with a torn edge. There was no date at either end. Maya had placed it near the August meeting because chairs seemed to belong with meetings. Now she noticed that the note also mentioned a birthday.

A birthday could need chairs too.

She studied the two letters on either side of it. One was clearly dated. The other had a clear date on its reverse, different from the one she had copied. She checked them against the calendar before moving anything. The little thank-you note could not borrow a date merely by lying nearby.

Tessa held the empty Questions folder open. Maya did not need to explain what she was doing. She placed the note inside, leaving the dated letters on the table.

Maya found a second date, compared it with the first, and moved the undated note to Questions.

## Installment 11: A name behind the stain

Annie's last name appeared at last, or seemed to. A narrow label had come loose from an envelope and settled beneath the tin's lining. Grandmother lifted the lining without tearing it, and there it lay: Annie, followed by a longer word beneath a brown stain.

Maya could see the first letter. It might be A. The next marks might be n and d, or they might be one large m. She stared until the letters began to look like anything she wanted them to look like.

"Annie Anderson," Tessa said.

"Annie Andrews," Leo said.

"Annie Almost," Maya said, and surprised herself by laughing.

Grandmother put the label beside a clear sheet. "Let's make a copy of what we can see, including the spaces we cannot read."

Maya copied Annie, then an A, then a row of small squares. It looked less like a name than a puzzle somebody had forgotten to finish. She wanted to rub the stain away, but Grandmother said they would leave the original as it was. They could change how they looked at it without changing it.

They moved the lamp. They tried daylight. Leo looked from one side while Tessa looked from the other. Each angle made a different part seem clearer. No angle produced a complete word.

"The letters are fish under muddy water," Tessa said. "Every time I think I've caught one, it moves."

Maya liked the picture in that sentence. The ink was not moving, but their guesses were. She wrote Tessa's sentence on the back of the copy, then asked permission to use it in the display.

"Only if you put my name on it," Tessa said.

They put the label aside and opened a longer letter. This one began with a question Elsie had asked about Ruth's meeting.

Dear Elsie,

You ask what I said when the meeting began. I had written a fine opening. It was so fine that Annie asked me to explain it. Then Mrs. Bell asked the same thing. By the time I had made it clear, I could see that I ought to begin with the clear words instead.

Annie says I sometimes use a long word like a coat too large for me. It drags on the floor and catches on everything. I told her that her own letters sometimes arrive without enough words to keep them warm. We laughed, and then we worked on the notice together.

We want a neighbor who has not heard our earlier conversation to understand it. A notice cannot depend on knowing what we meant last Tuesday.

Maya imagined a word wearing an enormous coat. She drew a small coat beside the passage in her notebook. Then she looked at her own label, Fellow organizer concerned with community participation. She could understand it. Would a little cousin?

She tried Annie helped arrange meetings and ask neighbors' questions. The sentence had more words but less fog. It described an action instead of hiding it inside a phrase.

"Longer isn't always harder," she told Leo.

"And shorter isn't always clearer," he said, pointing to his first note, She helped.

They changed both labels. Maya began to understand why Ruth had wanted to explain suffrage rather than merely print it large. A word could be exact and still need company: an example, a definition, a sentence that gave it room to work.

At lunch, Tessa asked if they could finish the purple title. Maya said they needed to settle one thing first. Did family papers include the history page? The page was not a family paper, but it belonged beside them.

Grandmother suggested a smaller heading for the history. Tessa made two signs that stood next to each other. The arrangement solved the problem more neatly than Maya's attempt to squeeze everything into one sentence.

By afternoon they had a working layout. They still had no last name for Annie. Maya put her copied squares beneath Annie's first name and looked at the brown-stained label again. Earlier she would have called it a failure. Now it was something harder: a possible answer they could not yet read.

She would show the readable part, leave the rest blank, and explain the gap if someone asked. She did not have to choose between pretending to know everything and showing nothing at all.

"This label is a fog," Maya said. She could not read its last word, so she left the name blank.

## Installment 12: Two readers

Grandmother had to leave for an appointment. Before she went, she asked the children to keep working from copies and leave the originals in their folders. She would be back before supper. Maya promised, then immediately wondered how much they could settle without her.

Tessa had gone home to fetch a sweater. Leo wanted to test a taller paper bridge. Maya looked at the pile of copied letters and felt the old urge to do everything herself. It would be faster than explaining which page belonged where.

She began sorting. Ten minutes later she had two piles that looked almost identical and could no longer remember why she had separated them. One was supposed to contain dates. The other contained places. A letter could contain both. She had made categories that fought over the same paper.

Leo's bridge collapsed with a soft thump. He looked at her piles. "Maybe you need cards instead of piles. One letter could have two cards."

Maya wanted to point out that his bridge was not standing. Instead she handed him a blank card. "Will you try one?"

He sat beside her and chose a letter from Elsie. Maya chose one from Ruth. They agreed to note only the date, the greeting, the signature, and an action the letter described. Each person would then explain the card while the other checked it.

Leo wrote quietly. Maya could hear his pencil stopping and starting. She had never liked waiting for someone else to finish. Now she used the time to read her own chosen paragraph again.

Dear Elsie,

We changed the notice after Mrs. Bell read it. She asked where a person could bring a question before the meeting. I had left that out because I knew where I would be. Of course she could not read what I knew. Annie suggested one plain line beneath the time. We added it, and the notice was better.

Maya read the paragraph to Leo. He grinned. "That sounds like our labels."

She wrote Notice revised after a reader's question. Then she crossed out revised and wrote changed beside it, testing which word helped explain the action. Both could work. Revised meant changing written work to improve it; the paragraph supplied the improvement.

When Leo finished his card, Maya checked it against the copy. He had written that Elsie planned to come on Friday. The letter said she hoped to come if her neighbor could accompany her. Maya pointed to hoped and if.

Leo groaned. "I made it happen already."

Maya remembered the heading she had wanted to keep. "I do that too."

He changed planned to hoped. The smaller correction made the whole card more faithful. Maya had not needed to tell him that his card was careless. She had needed to show him the two words.

Tessa returned and joined them. She read the cards before looking at the letters. Her questions were different because she had not heard their conversation. Who was Mrs. Bell? What was the notice for? Why did hoped matter?

Maya answered the first two, then asked Leo to answer the third. He explained the condition in the letter. Tessa said that she would have missed it without his changed card.

When Grandmother returned, the cards covered only one side of the table. Maya expected her to ask why they had not done more. Instead she asked how the new arrangement worked.

Maya gave a formal explanation, standing beside the table. "We made separate cards for dates and actions. One letter may support more than one card. We checked each other's wording against the copies."

Leo raised a hand as though he were a visitor. "And how would you tell me that at lunch?"

"We split it up and caught each other's mistakes," Maya said.

Grandmother nodded. Both explanations preserved the same work. One gave a visitor more background. The other could depend on a brother who had been there.

Maya did not feel that her lunch words were worse. They belonged to a different conversation. She wrote the formal explanation on the back of the display plan so they could remember how to introduce it to guests.

Later, while Tessa helped Grandmother downstairs, Maya chose two remaining pages for a fresh check. One had a date crowded into its upper corner. The other had a date written along the fold. Maya read the main paragraphs and nearly overlooked both corners.

She stopped. There were people in the house who could help without taking the project away. Asking them was a choice she could make, not a sign that the project had become too large for her.

Maya asked two relatives to read separate pages, then compared their notes. One reader caught a date the other missed.

## Installment 13: Which meeting?

The invitation was folded into a square so small that Maya first thought it was another label. It had a decorative border, a printed time, and a blank where someone had written a name. At the top stood the word Meeting in large black letters.

Maya placed it beside Ruth's July letter. Here, finally, was something a visitor could see without reading a whole page of handwriting. It seemed to belong exactly where the display needed a bright, simple object.

"What kind of meeting?" Leo asked.

Maya pointed to the letter. "The one Ruth wrote about."

"Where does the invitation say that?"

She read it again. It named a room and gave a time, but it did not use the word suffrage. Below the time was a small line: Members will choose music for the coming season.

Tessa leaned over Maya's shoulder. "Maybe they sang at the meeting."

"Maybe," Maya said.

This time she heard how much work she was asking maybe to do. A singing group could hold a meeting. A family could hold a meeting. The word itself could not carry the invitation across the gap between them.

Grandmother brought a folder of later family papers. In it was a second invitation with the same border and the same room name. This one clearly named a singing circle. Its date was several years later than the suffrage letters.

The match did not prove everything about the first invitation. It did give them a reason to reconsider. Maya moved both invitations to a separate space. She wrote Possible singing-circle meeting on her draft and added the reason underneath.

Tessa sighed. "Now the nice border doesn't fit."

Maya understood. She had wanted that border in the display too. She liked the way it would look beside the plain letters. Yet an attractive object could be the wrong object for a particular story.

They decided to leave a smaller copy in a side folder called Other Family Activities. It could be interesting without becoming evidence for something it did not say. The main display would have to manage without its decoration.

A letter from Ruth helped fill the space with something less pretty and more useful.

Dear Elsie,

We borrowed chairs again, but not enough. Mrs. Bell said we should ask how many people plan to come instead of counting only the people who came last time. I had assumed the same number would be enough. Annie said that was a comfortable guess for the person who already had a chair.

We changed the arrangement before anyone arrived. I moved the little table to the side and stood near the back. From there I could hear a question I had not heard when I sat in front.

Maya could picture the room more clearly from those few sentences than from her original drawing. The table moved. Ruth stood. A question came from the back. These were actions a reader could follow without inventing rows of smiling people.

She tried writing a summary. Ruth, who helped arrange the meeting, changed the room after Mrs. Bell questioned the number of chairs. It was a long sentence, but its parts belonged together. Who pointed back to Ruth, and after explained the order.

Leo tried another: The room where the neighbors met had too few chairs at first. Tessa suggested combining the two, then decided that the result would be so crowded it needed its own extra chair.

Maya laughed and kept them separate.

They practiced saying the sentences to Grandmother without staring at the paper. On the first attempt, Maya put the emphasis on too few, making the whole account sound like a failure. On the second, she gave more weight to changed. The shortage remained in the account, but so did the response.

"I don't want every mistake to swallow what happened afterward," Maya said.

Grandmother asked her to write that thought in her notebook. Maya did, then drew a line to her own first label. She had made an unsupported claim. She had also changed it. A fair account needed both actions.

Near the end of the afternoon, Tessa found a narrow card with a room name and a meeting time. Unlike the decorated invitation, this card included the word suffrage. Maya felt the old excitement rush back. Now the border no longer mattered.

She wanted to put it straight onto the timeline. Instead she compared it with a dated invitation elsewhere in the folder. The room name matched, but the hour differed. One might describe a changed plan. They read the letter nearest it and found a sentence about moving the meeting later so more neighbors could attend.

Maya wrote the sequence on scrap paper before touching the display. The earlier invitation named one time. The letter described a change. The small card gave the later time. Each piece had a different job.

She did not need to make the account sound perfect. The change was the part that explained how the organizers listened.

Maya found a label. The label named a meeting. She checked it against a dated invitation before adding it to the chart.

## Installment 14: The word in the middle

A few days before family Saturday, Maya tried to explain the display to her father. She began with the tin, then moved to the July letter, then stopped to describe the two-sided sheet. By the time she reached the changed meeting time, her father was studying the labels instead of listening.

"I lost the path," he admitted.

Maya heard criticism before she heard the useful part. She had checked every label. She had made the timeline. She had read handwriting that her father had not even tried to read.

"It's all there," she said.

"Yes. I need help knowing where to begin."

That was a different problem. Maya had been trying to make every fact accurate. She had not decided how a visitor would move among them. The attic table had grown by discoveries; the hallway display needed an order somebody else could follow.

They carried copies downstairs and laid them on the floor along the hallway wall. Tessa put the title first. Leo put the timeline next. Maya put the first letter beneath it, then moved it back. She wanted visitors to meet Ruth before meeting a row of dates.

Her father suggested trying both arrangements. He stepped away while they changed the order, then returned as though he had just arrived. Maya asked him to say where he wanted to look next. His answers surprised her. He wanted the photograph near the first letter and the archive page near the public timeline.

"They help me know which kind of account I'm reading," he said.

Maya wrote a short spoken introduction. It had four parts: finding the tin, meeting the letter writers, comparing family papers with history, and seeing the questions still open. She crossed out a joke that depended on knowing about Leo's collapsed bridge. She kept a sentence about the misplaced recipe because it explained how they had learned to track objects.

Leo listened from the stairs. "You could say the change in your work is the main thing."

"What change?"

"At first you wanted a big story. Now you want the right-sized story."

Maya wrote right-sized in the margin. It was Leo's phrase, and she would credit him if it went onto a label. For now it helped her hear the theme she had been circling. The topic was family letters. The message growing through the events had something to do with changing a claim when evidence required it.

Tessa wanted to use the word theme on the display plan. Maya wanted summary. Grandmother asked what each word was meant to do. Tessa said theme would name an idea running through the work. Maya said summary would give the important events briefly. They kept both headings and put different notes beneath them.

Precision, Maya decided, was not always finding a fancier word. Sometimes it was keeping two familiar words from doing each other's jobs.

That evening she read another copied letter. In it Ruth described rewriting the notice after Annie and Mrs. Bell asked questions. Maya summarized it aloud, leaving out the spool of thread and the cake. Leo said her summary was much easier to follow than the first one she had tried in the kitchen.

"It has a path," he said.

Maya underlined a sentence that seemed to show Ruth refusing help. It read, as she first understood it, I can do this work alone. That startled her. She had just explained how Ruth listened to other people. Had she chosen only the passages that fitted her idea?

She read the sentence again. The writing crowded together near a fold. Perhaps it said I cannot. Perhaps the last part of the word had faded. She did not like either possibility: one threatened her explanation, and the other might be a convenient rescue she had imagined.

Instead of settling it at once, she wrote both readings on a scrap. Under each she described how its meaning would change the paragraph. If it said can, she would need to explain a difference in Ruth's response. If it said cannot, it would fit the surrounding request for help. The surrounding words mattered, but they could not create a missing letter by themselves.

Leo came upstairs carrying the recording device. He had been practicing the introduction and wanted Maya to listen. She promised she would. First she held out the copy and asked him to read the whole line without seeing her two versions.

He leaned close, then traced the word in the air above the page. He pointed to a narrow mark Maya had passed over. On the clearer copy, it was visible between the crowded letters. The word was cannot.

Maya did not celebrate being right. She had not been right on her first reading. She changed her note, then set her own explanation aside so she could listen to Leo's recording. When he stumbled on ratification, she waited while he tried the word again.

Afterward she returned to a shorter copied passage near the bottom of the stack. She could probably have finished it herself. That was no longer the only question she asked about a task.

Maya asked for a second reading. Her brother spotted a word she had skipped. She corrected her note and thanked him.

## Installment 15: The old heading

Maya had put her first title inside the display folder weeks earlier, before she understood the difference between a change in law and the ways people could be prevented from using it. Now the sheet fell out while she searched for a blank card.

EVERYONE COULD VOTE.

The letters were blue, carefully shaded, and surrounded by small stars. Maya remembered making them at her own kitchen table. She had imagined a finished ending before she had read the whole story.

She could have hidden the sheet. Tessa would never know it existed. Leo might have forgotten. Instead Maya set it beside the new title. The difference was worth thinking about, even if she did not want to hang her first mistake on a wall.

"We could show how a heading changes," Leo said.

Maya shook her head, then hesitated. She did not want the display to become an account of Maya's mistakes. Yet a visitor might understand the purpose of their questions more quickly if the questions had something to push against.

Grandmother brought the folder of invitations. Maya had asked to look again at the meeting card with the later time. She wanted to be certain that the display distinguished the invitation from any record of who actually came.

### Checkpoint scene, first read on day 137

The letter's paper was as thin as an onion skin. Maya steadied it on the table where she had spread her notes. She had given the display a cheerful title: Everyone Could Vote. Now she reread the invitation to a meeting. An invitation was not a seat at the table. The invitation said nothing about who could register afterward.

"The amendment opened the door," her brother said.

"But this letter cannot tell us who got through it," Maya replied. She crossed out Everyone and wrote A Change and More Questions. Beside the title she added, What barriers remained?

Their grandmother glanced at the growing pile of sources. "Look before you leap," she said. "Read the next account before you finish that display. And let us help. Many hands make light work."

Maya divided the reading among them, then checked every suggested label against a source. It was still a Herculean task, but she no longer had to finish it alone.

### Room for both

The old heading became a small card beneath the main title. Maya wrote Early idea above it and Later question below the new wording. The stars remained around the crossed-out words. She did not need to make her early self look foolish to show that her idea had changed.

Tessa asked whether they should put a red mark through the entire sheet. Maya said no. The mistake had been a claim, not the paper itself. They could show exactly what changed.

They returned to the history account. It explained that ratification had been important while discriminatory voting laws still kept many women from voting. Maya placed that information beside the question about remaining barriers. The family invitation could raise a question. It could not answer a national question merely because it was old.

Leo found a blank card and wrote Progress on one side, Unfinished work on the other. He wanted visitors to turn it over. Tessa said that made the ideas seem like opposites, as though seeing one meant hiding the other. She suggested putting both on the same side.

Maya listened while they discussed it. She had often treated a suggestion as something to accept or defeat. This time she asked what each arrangement helped a visitor see. Leo wanted movement and surprise. Tessa wanted the two ideas visible together. They made a card with a fold that opened to show both phrases at once.

The solution delighted Leo so much that he made three more folded cards before anyone could stop him. Tessa used one for Elsie's question and Ruth's reply. Maya used another for her first claim and its correction. They put the last one aside for a question that had not arrived yet.

Grandmother watched them from the doorway. "Do you still need me?" she asked.

Maya almost said no, then remembered the stack of copied pages. "Would you read this label as though you hadn't helped us?"

Grandmother sat down. She read slowly and asked why one card said Ruth while another said probably Ruth. Maya explained that the first was attached to a letter with a full signature and the second to the sheet with only an initial. They added a short note so a visitor would not have to guess.

It was a small change. Nobody clapped. Yet Maya felt the same quiet satisfaction she had felt when the two timelines first fitted on one page.

Before leaving, she looked at the crossed-out heading again. It had once seemed like the end of the story. Now it was one part of a longer account, kept in proportion by the pages around it.

She put the folded question card into the display folder. There would be room for a visitor to wonder without being told that wondering spoiled the celebration.

## Installment 16: The person outside the frame

Maya's father offered to carry the display boards to Grandmother's house. He found her standing beside the cardboard boat she had begun before discovering the tin. Its unfinished windows still looked surprised.

"Are you taking this too?" he asked.

Maya shook her head. "It doesn't belong with the letters."

He lifted the boat and looked through its empty cabin. "It belonged to the afternoon when you found them."

That was true. She had gone looking for buttons and found a question instead. Maya put the boat into a separate bag. It could sit downstairs while the display stayed in the hallway. Not every connection needed to become a label.

At Grandmother's house, the boards leaned against the wall. Tessa had made a small paper pocket for extra copies, and Leo had built a stand that actually stayed upright. Maya admired it without mentioning the earlier bridges. Leo looked pleased, then explained the triangles anyway.

Grandmother brought out one last envelope. It held a later letter in Ruth's clear handwriting, the same handwriting as the birthday card they had used for comparison. This letter mentioned Elsie by name and recalled preparing notices with Annie. It did not identify Annie's surname, but it connected several things they already knew.

Maya copied its date and full signature. Then she wrote a short note explaining how it related to the earlier letters. The first uncertain initial remained uncertain on its own. The new letter made the connection stronger without changing the ink on the old one.

The letter also contained a paragraph about the photograph.

I remember that picture, Elsie. I wanted the chair moved because its leg caught my skirt. The photographer wanted it left where it was. I was thinking more about getting home than about looking important. You always say I look ready to give an instruction in that picture. Perhaps I was only ready to leave.

Maya stared at the photograph on the table. The raised chin, the straight skirt, the hand on the chair were still there. She had used them to imagine a serious speaker. Elsie had imagined someone about to give an instruction. Ruth's account offered another possibility.

"So our picture wasn't wrong," Tessa said. "The details were right."

"Our explanation was too sure," Maya answered.

They did not redraw the hand or the chin. They changed the caption. It would describe the visible details and identify Ruth's later recollection separately. A photograph could show how a person stood without revealing every thought behind the pose.

Maya remembered her first drawing, the platform and the enormous banner. She took it from her notebook and set it beside the photograph. Leo asked if she wanted to throw it away now.

"No. It reminds me what I added."

She wrote Imagined scene, made before reading the letters on the back. That label would stay with the drawing even if it never went on the wall.

They spent the afternoon checking the display from the doorway. The first line welcomed a visitor who knew nothing about the tin. The photograph introduced the family writers. The public timeline and the family timeline stood beside each other. Between them, a small label explained that the letters were one family's account, while the historical sources described the larger voting-rights struggle.

Tessa tested the paper pocket. Leo tested the stand. Maya tested the introduction by reading it without pointing to the board. She caught herself saying proved where the evidence only suggested. This time she changed the word before anyone else asked.

Her father listened from the kitchen. "How did you decide where to stop?" he asked.

Maya looked at the pile of unused copies. She had wanted to include everything so the work would look complete. Now she had chosen the details that helped explain the people, the work, and the questions. Other details remained in the folder, available if a visitor wanted more.

"We stopped where the display could still be followed," she said. "We didn't stop asking."

She liked the answer, but she wrote it down afterward to check whether it sounded grander than the work. It seemed to fit. They had made choices, and they had kept a record of them.

Near the door, Grandmother's old coat hung above the empty space where the tin had stood. Maya glanced at it as they carried the boards upstairs. The shelf looked ordinary again. She wondered how many ordinary-looking places contained work nobody had explained yet.

She did not announce that there must be a treasure in every box. Some boxes really did hold buttons. But she wanted to ask before deciding what mattered. Ruth had become more interesting as the grand claims fell away: a person who misplaced addresses, listened to a neighbor, joked with her sister, and kept working.

For family Saturday, Maya would tell the part she could support. She would leave enough room around it for a person to remain larger than a display.

## Installment 17: What the visitors need

On Friday evening, Maya read the introduction to Grandmother while Leo timed it with the kitchen clock. She had expected it to take a minute. By the time she finished explaining how the tin came downstairs, Leo had raised two fingers. By the time she reached the uncertain initial, he had stopped counting.

"People might want supper before you get to the vote," he said.

Maya put the paper down. Every sentence seemed necessary because she remembered the work behind it. But the audience had not asked for an account of every afternoon. They needed enough context to understand what they were looking at.

Grandmother asked her to choose one opening event, one central question, and one reason a visitor might look closer. Maya wrote three lines on a card. She found the tin while looking for buttons. It held letters about work for the vote. The letters had made her ask what a family account could establish.

The new version took less time. It also sounded less like a person trying to prove how hard she had worked.

"I miss the part about the chair," Leo said.

"That can be a question you ask afterward."

He grinned. "I already know the answer."

"Then ask a different question."

They tried the introduction again. This time Leo asked why the display had two timelines. Maya answered by pointing to the public history and the family letters. When he asked who Annie was, she described the work the letters attributed to her and explained the surname they could not read. He did not look bored. Maya realized that leaving room for a question could make the explanation more interesting, not less complete.

Tessa arrived carrying the purple paper pocket. She had made a small label for it: Sources and Copies. Maya asked whether the word sources needed an explanation. Tessa suggested Places we got our information underneath. The two lines could sit together, giving the exact term and a plain way into it.

They arranged a source list beside the pocket. Each historical page had a title and an address. The family letters had dates where they could read them and descriptions where they could not. Maya wrote who had copied each passage so a later reader could ask about a mistake.

Leo wanted to add a giant CHECKED stamp to every card. Grandmother asked what checked would mean to a visitor. Checked against the original? Checked for spelling? Checked by a second reader? The stamp seemed to promise more than any one mark could explain.

They put the stamp aside and kept their small notes. Maya could still show that she had worked carefully. She needed to say what the work had been.

After supper, her father asked whether she wanted to invite her classmates to see the display. Maya thought about the crowded hallway and shook her head. This was a family gathering. A wider audience would need a different plan, and she liked knowing who would be there.

"Maybe I'll tell them about the tin," she said. "I don't have to bring everybody into this room."

Grandmother thanked her for asking. Some family letters were meant to stay within the family. The display used passages Grandmother was willing to share with the relatives coming Saturday. Finding a paper did not mean Maya could decide every use of it alone.

That question made Maya look again at the letter about Elsie's cough. The sentence added warmth to the story, but it was not needed to explain the notices or the meeting. She left it in the folder instead of putting it on the board. She could value a detail without making it public.

The decision felt different from leaving out the cake. The cake had not fitted the short summary. The cough belonged to a person's private life. Both choices affected the display, but for different reasons.

Maya made a small card for comments, then changed its heading from Did you like it? to What did you notice or wonder? She wanted the visitors to enjoy the display, but praise alone would not tell her which parts they could follow. Leo asked whether a person could leave the card blank. Maya said of course. Coming to supper did not mean agreeing to do homework.

Tessa tried the card first. She wrote that the two timelines helped her follow the dates, but the little signature note was difficult to read from the doorway. Maya moved that note closer to the photograph. She kept Tessa's comment with the earlier plan so they could remember why the arrangement had changed. This was feedback they could use: a reader named a particular feature and explained its effect. Maya thanked her, then checked the smaller writing from the doorway herself. She did not need to guess how far away Tessa had stood. Tessa showed her the place.

Tessa tested the hallway route using a tray as though she were carrying food. At the corner, the tray nearly touched a board. They moved the stand several inches back. Leo tried the route while looking at the timeline and bumped into Maya. They agreed that visitors could stop beside the wall instead of in the middle of the passage.

Maya had expected the final evening to be about choosing the perfect words. It was also about where people stood, what they could see, and who needed to get past with a plate. The people reading the display had bodies as well as questions.

When everything was arranged, Tessa asked Maya to read one troublesome name again. It was not Annie's label but a different note, addressed to someone whose first name might be Ellen or Elsie. The date was clear. The greeting was not.

Maya wanted it to be Elsie because that would fit their sequence. She had placed it among Ruth's letters to her sister. Yet the unclear name had not improved merely because the rest of the display was ready. A deadline could end an evening's work. It could not finish a word on an old page.

She took the note out of the main sequence. Tessa helped make a smaller card for the Questions folder. Leo asked whether a visitor would notice that something was missing.

"Perhaps," Maya said. "Then we can explain why."

She looked once more at the clear date beside the uncertain name. Those two details could remain different kinds of evidence. One could be copied with confidence. The other could wait without turning the whole page into a mystery.

Maya compared an uncertain name with a clear date. She asked a relative to read it, then kept the uncertain name out of her final label.

## Installment 18: The afternoon opens

Family Saturday began with a spilled bag of rolls, a missing serving spoon, and two little cousins who wanted to crawl beneath the display stand. Maya had imagined a quiet line of visitors reading every label. Actual relatives arrived in a tumble of coats and voices.

She moved the extra copies onto a low table beside the wall. Leo guided the cousins toward the cardboard boat, which proved more exciting than the old tin. They looked through its empty windows and asked whether it could travel under the sofa. Maya said it could, as long as they brought it back before supper.

The first visitor to stop at the display was Uncle Ben. He read the title, looked at Ruth's photograph, and said, "I remember that picture. I thought her name was Rose."

Maya's stomach tightened. They had spent so long checking Ruth. Was the whole display about to come apart?

Grandmother joined them. "I used to wonder that too. Look at this later letter."

She showed him Ruth's account of the chair and the photograph. Uncle Ben read it, then nodded. He could not remember why he had thought Rose. Maya wrote his recollection on a separate card with his name and today's date. It was information about what he remembered, not a reason to erase the letter's evidence.

"Will that go on the wall?" he asked.

"In our notes for now," she said. "We can compare it with other things later."

He seemed pleased rather than offended. Maya had worried that careful questions would sound unfriendly. Perhaps tone mattered as much as the question itself.

She gave her short introduction to a small group near the stairs. One cousin asked whether Ruth had written the amendment. Maya said no; the letters described local organizing. Another asked if the family knew Harry Burn. Maya said the display did not establish that. The history page helped place Ruth's letters in time, but it did not turn a nearby date into a family connection.

Then Aunt Marisol asked what Maya liked best about the letters.

Maya had prepared for questions about evidence. This one took longer.

"The way Elsie asks questions," she said at last. "She isn't in the same room, but she changes what Ruth notices."

She showed the letter about Mrs. Bell helping choose a time. Aunt Marisol read the paragraph twice. "Someone has to ask the question the plan forgot," she said.

Maya wrote that sentence in her notebook with Aunt Marisol's permission. It belonged to today's conversation, not Ruth's letter. Keeping its source straight did not make it less useful.

At the low table, Leo was demonstrating the foldout card. A younger cousin opened it and read Progress and Unfinished work. He asked which one was the answer.

"Both belong in the explanation," Leo said.

Maya listened while he pointed to the legal change and the archive account of continuing barriers. He did not ask the child to imagine being excluded or assign anyone a part to act out. He used the words on the page. When the cousin wanted to return to the boat, Leo let him go.

Tessa had made a small display of their changed labels. Visitors seemed especially interested in the one with the crossed-out Everyone. Maya had expected that card to embarrass her. Instead it gave people an easy way to ask how the project had developed.

"You changed your mind," Uncle Ben said.

"I changed the sentence," Maya answered. "And then I understood why my first idea needed changing."

That was close to the explanation she had been trying to write all week. A correction could be visible without becoming the whole story. It could show the work of thinking.

Tessa brought the comment cards over before supper. Two were blank. One visitor had drawn an arrow toward the photograph and written Chair story. Another had asked whether Elsie ever attended a meeting herself. Maya could not answer that from the selected letters. She put the question into the notebook rather than turning it into a new scene for the display. The cards showed what these readers noticed. They did not speak for every guest. Maya had wanted feedback, and here it was: a few specific responses, each attached to a person who had chosen to leave it.

Near supper, Grandmother asked whether the children wanted to put the display away. They had been standing beside it for much of the afternoon. Maya's legs were tired, and her voice had become scratchy. She said she wanted to sit with the family and eat.

They left the boards in place but put the original papers back upstairs. Copies remained in the pocket for relatives who wanted to read after supper. Leo carried the tin with both hands while Maya cleared the table. Tessa followed with the source folder, keeping the little question cards together.

Upstairs, the lamp made its familiar yellow pool. The room seemed quiet after the hallway. Maya looked at the places where the originals had lain and felt an unexpected sadness. A finished display meant their long afternoons might be over.

"We could keep reading another day," Tessa said.

Maya glanced at her. She had not said anything aloud.

"You look like someone took away your last piece of purple paper," Tessa explained.

Maya laughed. She could keep the notebook. They could keep the questions. The project did not need to continue every afternoon to remain something they had done together.

Before going downstairs, they checked the last label Tessa had made. It quoted a line about Annie helping choose a meeting time. Maya compared the copy with the letter and noticed that a word had changed during copying. Tessa had written that Annie chose the time alone. The letter described her asking neighbors first.

They had nearly turned listening into deciding. Maya showed Tessa the difference, and Tessa reached for a fresh card. Neither needed to defend the old one.

Maya checked her cousin's label against the letter. A word did not match, so together they corrected it before adding it to the display.

## Installment 19: Beside the tin

After supper, Grandmother asked Maya to bring down the folder containing the torn note. Uncle Ben wanted to see the handwriting. Maya carried the folder flat and set it on a cleared table away from the food.

### Reserved moment E1-b

The original remained between its clean sheets. Maya placed a readable copy beside the protected original for visitors. She pointed to the place where the two torn pieces joined, then stepped back so Uncle Ben could read.

### One more reader

He bent over the copy. "I can make out this word now. I couldn't on the little card."

Maya wrote his reading in her notebook and asked him to point to the letters he saw. Some were clear; one remained doubtful. She put a small question mark beside that part, ready to check it in better light another day.

Across the room, Tessa was telling the younger cousins about the cardboard boat. Leo had made a paper bridge tall enough for it to pass underneath. The boat still had no buttons, but nobody seemed troubled by the empty windows.

Maya watched it travel along the rug. For a moment she wanted to explain how it had led her to the tin. Then she let the children keep their game.

She had an audience for the display and a place in the game. She could choose to sit down without losing either. Grandmother took the notebook while Maya held one end of the bridge. The boat moved beneath it, and the paper legs stayed upright.

## Installment 20: The next line

When the last relatives left, the hallway seemed wider. A scrap of purple paper lay beneath the stand. Maya picked it up and put it into Tessa's pocket of spare letters.

Grandmother asked what should happen to the display. Maya wanted the boards kept for a little while, then the copies stored with their source notes. The originals would return to their folders. The family could decide together about any wider publication.

"What was the impact of your work?" Leo asked, reading from a question card.

Maya considered the afternoon. "Some relatives read the letters. Uncle Ben offered a memory. We changed a label. I can't tell you what everyone will remember."

"That sounds like Ruth," he said.

Maya smiled. She had not become Ruth, and their work belonged to different times. But she recognized the effort to name an action without pretending to know every result.

She wrote a final note about the community of readers who had helped her investigate. The source of each useful detail mattered. So did asking a relevant question. The formal register of the display had helped its audience follow the account, while the jokes at supper had belonged to their own conversation.

There was still an unreadable name in the folder. It did not spoil what they had learned. Maya looked at the tin's worn blue lid and remembered wanting a story with no holes.

Maya left a blank below the final label. "For the next question," she said. She set a pencil beside the tin instead of locking it away.
