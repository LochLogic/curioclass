## Reading notes

Whole original verse novel, read in eight parts plus two short checkpoint entries. Meaning: observation, responsibility, audience and a changing willingness to write; later pet loss is one part of a larger school-year story. Structure: dated first-person entries, nested drafts, recurring objects and contrasting later judgments. Language: literal and figurative descriptions, deliberate line breaks, an idiom and two sayings considered in context. Knowledge: classroom routines, a collaborative display and choosing what to share. No outside biographical knowledge is needed.

Use the opening gate draft and revision only as an ungraded day 1 hook; part 1 is first read whole on day 2. Read the whole assigned part, not a simplified summary. Parts take about nine minutes at 120 words a minute, leaving room for rereading and discussion. On days 4 and 6 revisit the Williams and Rossetti responses in the already-read parts. On day 8 revisit the December sayings as needed alongside the separate comparison story. Day 9 first reads The Tray; day 13 first reads A Whole Afternoon. Keep those entries out of advance packets and do not rehearse their answers. All other entries in the assigned parts are taught readings. Days 16, 17, 29 to 33 and 37 to 38 select already-read entries as evidence for writing and presentations. Day 35 uses separate cold companions, not an untaught fragment of this novel.

Content note: the narrator is a Black boy; classmates have their own voices and choices. An early episode involves writing about a classmate without permission and repairing the harm. Part 7 introduces an old, unwell cat; part 8 plainly names the cat's death, without an accident or medical detail. Preview this before reading. Students may pass in public talk and choose a neutral subject for their own writing. Never request an account of personal loss. Do not present the pending sensitivity or text read as completed.

## Text

## Part 1: September, The blank label

### September 4: A book with my name

Ms. Ortiz,  
you gave everyone a notebook  
with an empty square on the cover.  
I wrote BEN in mine,  
then made the B into a door  
and the E into three shelves.  
You said the square was for a title.  
I closed the cover with my elbow.  
A name should be enough  
for a book that has nothing inside.  

You asked us to write  
something we noticed on the way to school.  
Here is my first try:  

There was a gate.  
It made a noise.  
I went through it.  

It is true.  
It takes up very little room.  
Perhaps that is a useful kind of writing.  

You asked what kind of noise.  
I said the noise a gate makes.  
You waited.  
The room filled with pencil sounds.  
I could have waited too,  
but I remembered the loose latch  
clacking against its metal post.  

Here is the next try:  

The loose latch clacked twice.  
I caught the gate  
before its third complaint.  

That gate does not really complain.  
I know that.  
It is easier to show you  
than to explain why the second try  
sounds more like this morning.  
Please do not put it on the wall yet.  
I have only just opened the door.  

### September 7: The noise shelf

There is a shelf in our classroom  
that has no books.  
Its brackets are strong,  
its paint is chipped,  
and its left corner points at my desk.  
Omar says it is waiting for a trophy.  
Mina says it could hold plants.  
I say nothing,  
because I have an idea  
that sounds odd outside my head.  

At home I build shelves from blocks.  
I decide what belongs together:  
small wheels, smooth stones,  
the blue pieces nobody can name.  
A shelf makes room  
without telling each thing  
what it ought to become.  

On the way home I tried  
to collect noises in my pockets.  
The crossing button clicked.  
A suitcase rattled over cracks.  
Someone shook a wet umbrella  
beside the bakery door.  
I had no way to bring them back.  
By dinner the suitcase was gone.  
Only the umbrella stayed,  
a small indoor rain  
falling where the floor was already dry.  

I wrote that last bit  
on the back of an envelope.  
Grandma asked if the envelope  
was a bill she needed.  
I said no.  
Then I said maybe,  
because I had used the wrong envelope.  
We found another piece of paper.  
She kept the bill.  
I kept the rain.  
The shelf in my mind  
was beginning to look crowded.  

### September 11: My left hand

When you asked for volunteers,  
my right hand stayed on my notebook.  
My left hand held my right wrist.  
I was making absolutely sure  
that neither hand misunderstood.  

Leila read about her brother's boots.  
There was a line about mud  
leaving little islands on the stairs.  
I could see those stairs.  
I have never been inside her house.  

You asked what helped us see.  
Omar said boots.  
Mina said islands.  
I wanted to say  
that the mud moved from outdoors  
into the place where people live,  
and that this made the brother seem real.  
But the sentence in my head  
had too many corners.  

I wrote it instead.  
You read it while we worked.  
Beside it you wrote,  
Which words made you think that?  

I drew an arrow back to the islands.  
An arrow is less frightening  
than a whole explanation.  
It can point  
while I keep my mouth closed.  

At lunch Leila asked  
why I had not said anything.  
I told her I liked her stairs.  
She said the stairs were ordinary.  
That was the strange part.  
She had made ordinary stairs  
appear in our classroom  
without carrying a single board.  
I put a small star beside my arrow.  
No one needed to see that either.  

### September 15: A visitor on the page

My cat Tansy stepped on this notebook.  
Her print is not a letter,  
though it has four small dots  
and one larger mark below.  
She came from the wet porch  
and walked straight across my first poem.  

Dad said to wipe her feet.  
Tansy said nothing.  
She has never thought  
that being told to do something  
is a reason to do it.  

I wanted a fresh page.  
Then I noticed  
how her print sat between  
loose and latch,  
as if a tiny paw  
had caught the gate too.  
I left it.  

I do not want to write  
that she is the best cat in the world.  
That sentence could belong  
to anybody's cat.  
Tansy pulls one string from my sock  
and leaves the other strings alone.  
She sleeps beside a cardboard box  
instead of inside it.  
When Dad opens the back door,  
she looks at him first  
as though he should explain the weather.  

Those things belong to her.  
They are not a prize  
or a mountain  
or a journey across an ocean.  
They are barely anything.  
But when I wrote them down,  
I knew which cat I meant.  
The paw mark is drying now.  
It looks like a period  
with a few extra thoughts.  

### September 20: A title that can wait

We were supposed to title our notebooks today.  
Mina has The World at My Elbow.  
Omar has Machines I Wish Existed.  
Leila wrote her title sideways  
so she could keep adding words.  

I still have BEN.  
You asked if I was stuck.  
I said I was deciding.  
You said those can feel alike.  

I tried Book of Noises,  
but that left out Tansy's paw  
and Leila's invisible staircase.  
I tried Everything,  
but everything would not fit  
between these two thin covers.  

At home Grandma was sorting buttons.  
Some were plain white circles.  
One was shaped like a green leaf.  
She did not throw away the plain ones.  
A plain button can hold a coat shut.  
The leaf button cannot do that better  
just because it looks surprising.  

I set my notebook beside her tin.  
I wrote down the soft rattle  
when she tilted the buttons into her hand.  
Then I wrote what the buttons did.  
They held things together.  
That seemed useful too.  

For now I have put a tiny shelf  
under my name on the cover.  
It has room for a title later.  
The book does not look finished.  
I am beginning to think  
that might be the correct way  
for a notebook to look in September.  

## Part 2: October, What a small thing holds

### October 2: Red, then white

Ms. Ortiz,  
today our class read the little poem  
with the wheelbarrow and the chickens.  
The poem was already finished  
before I had settled into my chair.  
I waited for the part  
where something happened.  
You asked us to read it again.  

I thought a short poem  
would be easy to hurry through.  
But the lines kept separating things  
I would usually leave together.  
Part of an object waited  
until the next line to arrive.  
I knew the word,  
yet I had to look at it twice.  

At recess I watched  
our yellow doorstop  
hold the classroom door.  
It is scratched along one side.  
Someone has written a tiny number on it.  
Without that small wedge,  
the door would keep swinging shut  
while people carried things through.  

I started a poem about it.  
I copied the shape of the wheelbarrow poem  
so exactly that my doorstop  
seemed to be wearing someone else's coat.  
I could see the borrowed sleeves.  

I crossed out the first two lines.  
Then I listened to the door.  
It knocked once against the wedge  
and stayed open.  
That was where my own poem began:  
not with what the other poet said,  
but with what his poem  
had helped me stop overlooking.  

### October 5: A wedge

The door tries  
its old argument.  

The little yellow wedge  
holds its ground.  

Through the opening come  
three wet backpacks,  
a cart of paper,  
Mina carrying something  
with both hands.  

I put space after argument  
because the door seemed to wait.  
I put space after ground  
because the wedge did not move.  
You said a reader might hear it differently.  
That had not occurred to me.  
I had imagined the page  
was a set of instructions  
that every voice would obey.  

Omar read it fast.  
The door sounded angry.  
Leila read it slowly.  
The wedge sounded patient.  
Both readers used my words.  

I wanted to tell Omar  
he had read it wrong.  
Then I asked him to show me  
where the anger came from.  
He pointed to argument.  
I had put that word there myself.  

Now I am wondering  
whether I want the door to argue.  
It might simply be heavy.  
The wedge might be stubborn  
instead of patient.  
I did not expect  
a scratched piece of yellow rubber  
to make this much trouble.  
I have left the poem open  
on my desk,  
which is exactly what the wedge  
would have done with the door.  

### October 9: The empty shelf again

Mina carried a tray to the windowsill.  
We are preparing a class display  
called Things We Almost Missed.  
There will be drawings, labels,  
and some objects on our empty shelf.  
Nobody has decided  
whether a sound can be an object.  

I suggested a collection of noises.  
Omar asked where we would keep them.  
I said in writing.  
He said that was a collection of writing.  
I said yes,  
although I had wanted it  
to sound more impressive.  

Mina suggested leaving spaces  
for things visitors noticed themselves.  
I liked the idea  
until I imagined someone writing  
something better than my gate poem.  
Then the shelf in my head  
became a very narrow shelf.  

I drew a plan anyway.  
An open box could hold folded poems.  
A card could say where each sound belonged.  
Visitors could unfold one,  
read it,  
and put it back for someone else.  

You asked whose sounds we would use.  
I said everybody's.  
You asked whether everybody  
had agreed to that.  

I had not asked.  
A plan looks complete  
when it is only boxes and arrows.  
People do not fit in boxes  
as neatly as I draw them.  
I added a question mark  
beside each name.  
There was suddenly more work to do  
and a little more room on the page.  

### October 12: The price of a good line

I heard Leila speaking quietly  
near the coat hooks.  
She had forgotten something at home  
and was worried about telling her aunt.  
Her hands kept pulling  
at the cuffs of her sweater.  

I wrote a line about sleeves  
swallowing her fingers.  
It was a good line.  
I knew it immediately.  
I wanted to read it aloud.  

Then Leila saw her name.  
She put her hand on the notebook.  
Please do not use that,  
she said.  

I said it was only a poem.  
The word only  
was doing a lot of work  
for such a small word.  
It was trying to make  
her worry smaller  
and my choice less important.  

I crossed out her name.  
She said that was not enough.  
Everybody had seen the sweater.  

So I crossed out the whole entry.  
There is a rectangle of pencil  
where a good line used to be.  
I can still remember the line,  
but remembering it  
does not mean I get to keep it  
on the class shelf.  

After lunch I asked  
whether she would help make the labels.  
She said maybe tomorrow.  
That was not a yes.  
I wrote tomorrow in the margin  
and left the rest of the space  
entirely hers.  

### October 16: The repair

I told Leila I was sorry  
without reading my explanation first.  
I had written a long one  
about how I was learning  
and how I had meant the line kindly.  
It made me the main character  
in what I had done to her.  

Instead I said  
I should have asked.  
I would not use the entry.  
She did not have to help  
with my display.  

She nodded.  
We went to reading.  
Nothing dramatic happened.  
No music told me  
whether the apology had worked.  

Later she drew a label  
with a little folded corner.  
She said visitors could lift it  
if they wanted to read more.  
A closed flap could mean  
that some things stayed private.  

I asked whether she wanted  
her name on the drawing.  
She said yes.  
I asked how to spell her last name.  
She wrote it herself.  

There are three questions now  
on the inside cover of my notebook:  
Did I notice it?  
Did I understand it?  
Is it mine to share?  

I cannot answer all three  
just because a line sounds good.  
Sometimes I have to walk  
across the room  
and listen to someone  
whose answer I have not chosen.  
The rectangle of pencil  
is still on the earlier page.  
I have decided to let it stay visible.  

## Part 3: November, Stopping and going

### November 1: A window at closing time

Ms. Ortiz,  
Grandma asked me to meet her  
outside the community room at five.  
We were carrying two empty boxes home.  
She had been sorting donated coats,  
and the boxes were for our display.  

On my way I passed  
the repair room beside the laundry.  
The owner had arranged old clocks  
in the window.  
None showed the same time.  
One had no hands at all.  

I stood there imagining  
a town where every clock  
could choose its own afternoon.  
School might end before it began.  
Dinner could wait for people  
who were still having breakfast.  
The clock without hands  
would never tell anyone to hurry.  

By the time I reached Grandma,  
she was sitting beside the boxes.  
She did not look angry.  
She looked cold.  
That was harder to explain away.  

I told her about the clocks.  
She said she would like to see them  
another day,  
when we had agreed to stop.  

On the way home  
one box knocked my knee  
with every step.  
The imagined town was still in my head.  
So was Grandma waiting on a bench.  
I could keep both pictures.  
I could not keep her waiting  
just because one of them  
would make a better poem.  

### November 3: The rider in the woods

The poem we read today  
has a rider who stops by woods.  
I thought of my clock window.  
Omar thought of a person  
who wanted a longer journey.  
Mina thought the rider was tired.  

We looked for what the poem said.  
It did not give us  
all the things we wanted to know.  
No one named the promise.  
No one told us  
what waited at the end of the road.  

At first that seemed unfair.  
A writer has the whole page.  
Why leave out the answer?  

Then I remembered  
that I had written about Grandma's bench  
without saying how I felt.  
The cold bench and the knocking box  
had said something for me.  
I had not meant to make a puzzle.  
I had been trying to get it right.  

I read the rider's last lines twice.  
The second time,  
the road seemed longer.  
The words had not changed.  
I had stayed with them longer.  

Here is a note for my own clock poem:  
Do not say  
I was careless and then I learned.  
Show the window.  
Show the bench.  
Let the empty box  
knock against my knee.  

I have written the note  
on a folded scrap  
so I can move it  
instead of pretending  
I will remember everything  
just because I understood it once.  

### November 7: The first box

Our display box has arrived.  
It held coats before it held poems.  
One corner is dented.  
There is a small tear  
where Grandma gripped it on the stairs.  

Omar wants to cover every side  
with silver paper.  
Mina wants visitors to see  
the old folds and repaired corner.  
Leila puts a strip of blue paper  
along the edge  
and waits for us to look.  

I say the box looks unfinished.  
She says we are still making it.  
This is a difference  
I keep having to learn.  

We agree on a plain outside  
and a bright inside.  
Omar makes a handle from cloth.  
Mina writes a question  
for the lid.  
Leila makes the letters large enough  
to read from the doorway.  

I have been writing  
our noise cards at home.  
There is the hallway cart,  
the zipper that catches,  
the pencil sharpener winding down.  
Each sound has its own folded card.  

When I put the first card inside,  
the box looks almost empty.  
I want to add everything immediately.  
Mina says an empty space  
can be an invitation.  
Omar says it can also be empty.  
We laugh.  
I like that he said it.  
Not every useful sentence  
has to carry a secret meaning.  
Sometimes somebody needs to say  
that the box still needs more cards.  

### November 12: More than a bird

The happy poem today  
kept comparing a heart  
with things that were not hearts.  
A bird, a tree,  
a shell bright with color.  
The feeling seemed to outgrow  
one comparison after another.  

I tried writing  
that our display box was like a treasure chest.  
That sounded fine  
until I looked inside.  
There were folded cards  
and a spare piece of tape.  
Nobody had crossed a dangerous sea  
for our pencil sharpener.  

I tried a window instead.  
A visitor could look through words  
at something from our room.  
But a window stays in one place,  
and our cards could travel.  

Then I tried a pocket.  
The box was a classroom pocket  
full of things we had nearly lost.  
I liked that best,  
although Omar said  
he would never put a classroom  
inside his trousers.  

We put the three comparisons  
beside the box.  
The treasure chest made it seem rare.  
The window made it seem open.  
The pocket made it seem close.  

I had wanted one correct comparison  
that would settle everything.  
Instead I had three choices,  
and each choice  
moved the reader a little.  
I kept pocket for my poem  
and window for the display label.  
The same box could do  
more than one kind of work.  

### November 18: The sound we could not keep

A cart rolled down the hall today  
with one wheel squeaking.  
Omar ran to find his notebook.  
By the time he returned,  
the cart was at the other end.  
We tried to remember the sound.  

He said it went eek, eek.  
I said it was more like a question  
that never reached its final word.  
He asked how to spell that.  
I did not know.  

We waited beside the door  
until the cart came back.  
This time I watched the wheel.  
It squeaked when it leaned  
under the cart's weight,  
then went quiet  
on the smoother part of the floor.  

Omar tapped the rhythm  
against his leg.  
I wrote a long line  
followed by a short one.  
We had been arguing  
about a sound neither of us  
had listened to carefully enough.  

The adult pushing the cart  
asked why we were staring.  
We explained the display.  
She let us describe the cart,  
but asked us to leave out her name.  
We wrote only what we had heard  
and what she had agreed to share.  

The card says:  

A wheel asks something  
at every crack.  
The smooth floor  
lets it forget.  

Omar still prefers eek, eek.  
We kept his version on the back.  
A visitor can turn the card over  
and hear another person's cart.  

## Part 4: December, A place to begin again

### December 2: The broken handle

Ms. Ortiz,  
our cloth handle came loose  
while we were carrying the box.  
Nothing heavy was inside.  
The poems slid onto the table  
and under three chairs.  
One landed inside Omar's open bag.  

He said we were back to square one.  
I looked at the box.  
It was still painted.  
The cards were still written.  
I said we were nowhere near square one.  

He said that was an expression.  
He knew the box had not turned  
into a square on a game board.  
It meant that the work  
felt as though it had to start again.  

I was annoyed,  
because I had understood that.  
I simply did not want it to be true.  

We picked up the cards.  
Leila checked the folds.  
Mina found the loose end of cloth.  
Omar wanted to make a stronger handle.  
I wanted to set the box down  
and stop carrying it anywhere.  

You asked what the display needed  
to do for its visitors.  
That question was irritating too.  
It made us think about visitors  
when I wanted to think  
about the handle that had failed.  

We decided the box could stay  
on the empty shelf.  
People could come to it.  
For one afternoon,  
starting again meant changing  
what we thought the finished thing  
was supposed to do.  

### December 5: A bird that stays

Dickinson's hope has feathers.  
I drew a bird in the margin,  
then erased its feet.  
I had drawn it standing  
on a perfectly ordinary branch.  
The poem had put it  
somewhere I could not draw so easily.  

I asked whether hope  
had to feel cheerful.  
You asked us to listen  
to the weather in the poem.  
There was trouble in that weather.  
The bird did not wait  
for a bright, easy day.  

That afternoon  
I fixed a crooked card holder.  
It came loose again.  
I fixed it once more.  
I was not cheerful.  
My hands smelled like paste,  
and I wanted to be outside.  

Still, I moved the holder  
farther from the edge  
and pressed it until it stayed.  
I did not sing.  
I hardly even spoke.  
But I had not quite given up  
on the box or the visitors  
or our stubborn little shelf.  

I wrote a poem  
about a light left in a hallway.  
The light was not a bird.  
It did not belong to Dickinson.  
But her bird had made me think  
about something that remains  
when a person has stopped  
feeling particularly brave.  

I left my first drawing in the margin,  
with its erased feet.  
It shows where I began  
before I understood where to look.  

### December 9: Grandma's basket

Grandma saw me making  
six new display cards at once.  
All six were on my lap.  
All six needed finishing.  
Tansy wanted the warm patch  
where the papers were stacked.  

Grandma said not to put  
all my eggs in one basket.  
I told her there were no eggs.  
She gave me the look  
that means she knows  
I know what she means.  

If the basket falls,  
every egg can break,  
she said.  
It is a warning about depending  
on one plan for everything.  

I said my plan was excellent.  
Tansy chose that moment  
to climb onto the papers.  
Six cards slipped to the floor.  
Grandma did not say anything.  
She did not need to.  

We made a second plan:  
finish two cards,  
leave room for other people,  
and keep a plain copy  
of the labels in my folder.  

I wrote her saying down.  
It was not a rule  
that every basket must obey.  
It was a picture  
that helped a warning stay in my head.  

My page also has  
a picture of Tansy  
sitting inside a basket  
with no eggs in it.  
That is not the meaning.  
It is just a picture  
I wanted to draw  
before she moved somewhere less comfortable.  

### December 13: A saying with a crack

Someone told Omar  
that practice makes perfect.  
He was trying to write  
letters for a new sign.  
His third O looked like a potato.  
His fourth looked like two potatoes  
trying to be one.  

He said the saying was wrong.  
He had practiced four times.  
The sign was worse.  

I wanted to say  
that four times was not enough.  
But I remembered my six cards  
and decided to look first.  
He was pressing harder  
with every attempt.  
The pencil was making a groove.  

Leila showed him  
how she made a light circle  
before darkening the line.  
His next O looked rounder.  
It was still a little lopsided.  
He liked it anyway.  

A saying can hold  
something people have learned,  
I wrote in my notebook,  
without being a promise  
that every try will work.  

Omar said I should put that  
on a much larger sign.  
We laughed again.  
The afternoon felt easier.  

I made a new card  
about the sound of erasing:  

A small gray cloud  
under my hand.  
The page comes back,  
but keeps a little weather.  

There are smudges everywhere  
on this December page.  
I used to think smudges  
were proof that I had failed.  
Now I can sometimes see  
where a different choice began.  

### December 19: The card that waited

We set the box on the shelf  
for a trial visit.  
Three students from another room  
came to look.  
I stood so close  
that nobody could open the lid.  

You asked whether I could step aside.  
I moved one shoe.  
Then the other.  
The shelf looked strangely far away.  

One visitor unfolded my gate poem.  
She read it twice  
and put it back.  
She did not laugh  
or clap  
or announce that she understood.  
I wanted to ask her what she thought,  
but she was choosing another card.  

The next visitor wrote  
about a squeaking shoe.  
The third drew a rectangle  
with a small dark corner.  
He said it was the classroom window  
just before the light came on.  

We had left blank cards  
because Mina thought  
empty space could invite people in.  
I had agreed without believing her.  
Now there were two new notices  
that none of us had planned.  

After they left,  
I read the gate poem myself.  
It was still the same poem.  
It had survived being read  
by someone I did not know.  

I put it back in the box  
with the new window drawing beside it.  
For the first time,  
I did not place my own card on top.  

## December 20: The Tray

I tipped the tray; the seeds fell down.  
They dotted earth, so small and brown.  
I hid my hands beneath the desk.  
"Did anybody see?" I asked.  
Then Mina knelt beside the tray.  
"I'll help you save the seeds," she said.  
I took my hands out, gathered seeds,

## Part 5: January, Who gets the space

### January 6: The box without me

Ms. Ortiz,  
I was absent yesterday.  
When I came back,  
there was a card in our box  
that I had never seen.  
It described the low hum  
of the classroom light.  

Nobody had asked me  
where the card should go.  
Nobody had waited  
until I could choose its place.  
The box had continued  
being our box  
while I was at home.  

I asked Mina who wrote it.  
She said the name was on the back.  
I asked whether she liked it.  
She said I could read it myself.  

It was a good card.  
It had a long line  
that narrowed into a short one,  
like someone turning down a sound.  
I wanted to know  
why I had not thought of that.  

Then I turned it over.  
There was Omar's name.  
He was sitting three desks away,  
working on something entirely different.  

I told him I liked the ending.  
He said he had tried it  
three different ways.  
I asked whether he still had the others.  
He opened his folder.  

I had been treating other people's poems  
as things that arrived finished,  
like visitors at the door.  
His crossed-out lines  
looked a great deal like mine.  
We spent the rest of reading time  
listening to where the light  
went quiet on his page.  

### January 9: A dancer on the page

The Hughes poem begins  
with a body moving into space.  
Arms open.  
Turning.  
A place in the sun.  
Then there is rest  
under a tree.  

I wanted to read the dancing quickly  
and the resting slowly.  
In the second stanza,  
the short commands  
made my voice hurry almost by itself.  
At the end,  
the words softened again.  

The speaker says  
that the dark night is like him.  
He says it with tenderness.  
I am Black too.  
I liked hearing Black  
at the end of the poem  
without a voice shrinking around it.  

At home I read it to Dad.  
He asked which line stayed with me.  
I opened my arms  
instead of answering immediately.  
He moved the bowl on the table  
before I knocked it over.  

Then we talked about the rest.  
A person in a dream  
can want room to dance  
and room to be still.  
I had noticed the movement first.  
Dad had noticed the tree.  

I wrote about our kitchen afterward.  
There was no tree in it.  
There was a chair pulled back  
so my arms would have room.  
I did not write that Hughes  
had described my kitchen.  
He had helped me notice  
how much space a small movement  
can ask another person to make.  

### January 13: The chair pulled back

Dad moves the bowl.  
I move my chair.  

The kitchen opens  
one small square.  

I turn.  
My sleeve brushes the air  
where a spoon had been.  

Dad laughs,  
then waits.  

There is room  
for the turning  
and the stopping.  

I read this one  
with my hands on the desk.  
It did not need me  
to act out every word.  
Mina said she could see  
the space around the chair.  
Leila said the waiting  
mattered as much as the laugh.  

Omar asked whether I could add  
what kind of bowl Dad moved.  
I tried green bowl.  
I tried chipped bowl.  
Neither changed the feeling  
I wanted the poem to carry.  
I left bowl alone.  

That was a new kind of revision:  
not adding a better word,  
not hunting for a louder one,  
but deciding that the plain word  
already held its place.  

I put a note below the poem:  
The short lines slow my turning.  
The empty spaces give Dad time to wait.  

The note sounds more certain  
than I felt while writing.  
I will keep the first draft  
beside it in my folder,  
so nobody thinks the spaces  
arrived in exactly the right places  
on the very first try.  

### January 17: A plan for visitors

We are opening the display  
for the neighboring class next month.  
You asked how visitors  
would know what to do.  
I said we could tell them.  
You asked who would tell them  
when we were busy helping someone else.  

Omar drew a machine  
with six pointing hands.  
Leila drew a sign  
with three clear steps.  
Mina asked whether the sign  
needed three steps at all.  

I wrote an invitation:  
Choose a card.  
Unfold it.  
Notice something new.  
Leave a card if you wish.  

That was four steps.  
It also seemed to tell people  
what they had to notice.  
We read it aloud.  
The invitation sounded like homework.  

Mina tried a question instead:  
What almost escaped your attention today?  
Leila thought escaped  
might make the sounds seem dangerous.  
Omar said we could draw little cages.  
Nobody took that suggestion seriously,  
including Omar.  

In the end the sign said:  
Here are things we noticed.  
You may read, listen or add your own.  

It did not explain everything.  
A visitor could still ask a question.  
That was all right.  
The sign was supposed to open  
a conversation,  
not finish one before it began.  

I wrote my longer version  
in the back of the notebook.  
Somewhere I needed a place  
for the sentences I had learned  
not to put on signs.  

### January 24: The invitation declined

I asked an older student  
whether he wanted to add a poem.  
He said he did not write poems.  
I started telling him  
that everybody notices things  
and a poem can be short  
and he would not have to rhyme.  

He said no again.  
This time I heard him.  

It is embarrassing  
how easily an invitation  
can grow a little handle  
that I keep trying to pull.  
I had wanted him to say yes  
because yes would mean  
our display was worth joining.  

Later he stopped at the shelf  
and read three cards.  
He did not leave one.  
When he walked away,  
I noticed that I was still waiting  
for him to turn around.  

You asked how the visit went.  
I said he had not wanted to write.  
You asked whether he had read.  
I said yes.  
You asked whether reading  
was one of the choices on the sign.  

I had written those choices myself.  
Somehow I had still expected  
all the arrows to point  
at the answer I preferred.  

I put a small empty card  
beside this entry.  
It does not stand for a failure.  
It stands for a space  
that belongs to somebody else,  
even when I have made  
very careful plans  
for what could fit inside it.  

## Part 6: February, Listening past my own voice

### February 3: The rehearsal

Ms. Ortiz,  
we tried the display  
with four visitors today.  
I had practiced what to say  
in front of the bathroom mirror.  
The mirror never asked questions.  
It made an excellent first audience  
and a terrible last one.  

Our first visitor asked  
whether she could keep a card.  
I had not thought of that.  
Our second wanted to draw.  
Our third wanted someone  
to read a card aloud.  
Our fourth stood at the back  
where he could see  
without being asked to do anything.  

I forgot my introduction.  
I began telling everyone  
how we had made the handle.  
Omar gently turned the box  
so the sign faced the visitors.  

Afterward I said  
I had ruined the rehearsal.  
Mina asked which part was ruined.  
The cards had been read.  
Two visitors had added drawings.  
One had asked to come back.  

I pointed at my forgotten speech.  
She pointed at the shelf.  
We were looking at  
two different versions  
of the same afternoon.  

I practiced again at home,  
this time with Dad  
interrupting me with questions.  
He asked whether the box  
could hold a poem about a spoon.  
I said it could.  
Then I asked what he had noticed.  
That answer was easier to remember  
because I was listening for it.  

### February 6: The other telling

We read a story today  
in which a child could not finish a poem.  
One version let the child say I.  
Another stood outside  
and used the child's name.  
The same pencil moved  
between the same two people.  
But I knew different things  
about what the child was hiding.  

I tried that with our rehearsal.  

Ben stood beside the box.  
He looked at the shelf.  
Mina asked a question.  

Then I tried:  

I stood beside the box,  
trying to remember my speech  
and hoping nobody noticed  
that my fingers were shaking.  

Both tellings were true  
to what had happened in my invented version.  
Only one let the reader hear  
what I had kept inside.  

Omar suggested adding  
that Mina secretly thought I was foolish.  
I asked how I knew that.  
He said it would make the story interesting.  
I said it would also make up  
something about Mina.  

In a made-up story,  
we could invent a whole new Mina.  
In an account of our actual rehearsal,  
we had to ask her  
or leave her thoughts unknown.  

She said she had been wondering  
whether the shelf was too high  
for the youngest visitors.  
None of our drafts  
had guessed that.  
We moved the box lower  
before trying to improve  
any more sentences about it.  

### February 10: The quiet corner

The neighboring class arrived  
just as chairs scraped  
in the room beside ours.  
Their teacher closed the door.  
The scraping grew softer,  
but our room filled  
with another kind of sound:  
voices asking,  
paper unfolding,  
somebody laughing near the sink.  

One visitor stepped away  
and covered his ears.  
I almost called him back.  
Then I remembered  
the fourth visitor at rehearsal.  
Watching had been a choice too.  

We put a few cards  
on the table by the window.  
Leila wrote Quiet reading here.  
Omar stood at the box.  
Mina brought a chair  
without making a speech about kindness.  

The visitor sat at the window  
and unfolded the cart poem.  
He read Omar's sound on the back,  
then my version on the front.  
He tapped one finger  
against the edge of the table.  

I could not hear his rhythm  
from where I stood.  
I did not need to.  

At the end of the visit,  
he pointed to the quiet sign  
and asked if it would stay.  
I said I would ask the class.  
This time I remembered  
that something could be a good idea  
without already belonging  
to me to promise.  

### February 14: A visitor's card

One of the new cards says:  

The door closes.  
The hall keeps talking  
on the other side.  

There is no name on it.  
The writer chose  
the box marked no name, please.  
We had added that choice  
beside the blank cards.  

I wanted to find out  
who had written it.  
It sounded like something  
I wished I had written.  
Omar said perhaps  
that was not the point  
of the no-name box.  

I left it alone.  
But I read it again.  
The hall in that little poem  
seemed full of people  
I could not see.  
The door made a boundary  
without making the people disappear.  

I tried reading the three lines  
as one long sentence.  
It moved faster.  
I tried a long pause  
after closes.  
The quiet lasted longer.  

A short poem can hold  
a choice the writer made  
without printing the reason beside it.  
A reader can notice the choice  
and still have a question.  

I wrote down my question:  
Does the speaker want  
more of the hall,  
or less?  

I could not settle it  
from those three lines alone.  
I left both possibilities  
under the copied poem,  
with a space between them  
wide enough for a door.  

### February 21: What the shelf held

We counted the cards  
because the box was becoming crowded.  
I wanted an impressive number  
for our next sign.  
Mina asked what the number  
would help a visitor understand.  

I said it would show  
that lots of people had joined.  
She said that was true.  
Then she asked whether  
one careful visitor's card  
would matter less  
if there were fewer cards around it.  

I dislike questions  
that make me set down  
the answer I was enjoying.  

We sorted the cards by place instead:  
window,  
hall,  
playground,  
home,  
somewhere imagined.  
A few belonged in two piles.  
We made a little marker  
that let them stay between.  

When we finished,  
our classroom sounded different  
in every pile.  
The window pile held rain,  
chairs,  
quiet,  
and one impatient fly  
from a story somebody had invented.  

We left the total count  
in our planning folder.  
The sign showed the places.  
Visitors could follow  
whichever path interested them.  

I used to think organizing  
meant putting everything  
in exactly one place.  
This afternoon it meant  
making the places clear enough  
that someone could choose a way through,  
and leaving enough room  
for a card whose sound  
had more than one home.  

## March 1: A Whole Afternoon

<!-- N13: first read day 13; no advance practice. -->

I used to write "nothing happened"  
under a whole afternoon.  
Today Mina said my line  
about brown seeds let her see the floor.  
I thought poems needed mountains.  
I crossed out "nothing"  
and wrote the scraping of her chair,

Before the bell I asked,

## Part 7: March and April, The pages behind the poem

### March 4: Three pages on the table

Ms. Ortiz,  
you asked us to explain  
where one of our poems began.  
I put three pages on the table:  
the kitchen poem,  
my first notes about Hughes,  
and the version with green bowl  
crossed out in the middle.  

I wanted to say  
that the poem came from happiness.  
That was true,  
but it did not tell a reader  
why these words  
had ended up on this page.  

So I began with the chair.  
Dad moved it.  
I remembered it.  
The poem about dancing and resting  
made me listen to that memory  
a second time.  
Then I chose the spaces  
that gave the movement room.  

I had to look at the pages  
to put those things in order.  
In my head,  
understanding seemed to arrive all at once.  
On the table,  
there were arrows,  
wrong turns,  
and a green bowl  
that had never belonged  
in the final poem.  

Omar asked whether my explanation  
would be another poem.  
I said probably prose.  
There were things I wanted  
to state directly this time.  

He looked disappointed.  
I showed him the crossed-out bowl.  
The prose could explain a choice  
without needing to become  
the choice itself.  
He said the bowl  
had finally found a job.  

### March 11: The hallway light again

I found my December light poem  
behind a stack of labels.  
At the time,  
I had thought it was finished.  
Now the last line  
seemed to tell the reader  
what to feel.  

The light shines bravely,  
I had written.  
The light had no idea  
whether it was brave.  
It was just a light  
above the coat hooks.  

I went to look at it again.  
There was a shoe beneath it  
with one lace untied.  
A paper leaf from autumn  
still clung to the wall.  
The light made a little bright place  
around both of them.  

I wrote:  

One lace waits.  
One paper leaf  
keeps its crooked place.  

Above them,  
the hall light stays on.  

I liked the ending better.  
It did not explain  
that I was thinking about hope.  
It gave the reader  
something that remained.  

Leila asked why I used waits  
for the lace.  
I said I imagined someone  
coming back to tie it.  
She said the shoe  
could be forgotten.  

That made the poem feel  
less comfortable,  
and more interesting.  
I left waits.  
The December poem  
and the March poem  
are together in my folder now.  
Neither one erases  
the afternoon that made the other.  

### March 20: The grown-up question

A visitor asked  
who taught us to write poems.  
I pointed at you.  
You pointed at the shelf.  
The visitor looked confused.  

I said we had read poems  
by people we had never met,  
and we had listened  
to people in our own room.  
We had also tried things  
that did not work.  

He asked which poet  
was my favorite.  
I wanted to choose one name  
that would sound like  
I understood a great deal.  
Instead I said  
that different poems  
had helped with different problems.  

One made me look  
at a small useful object.  
One made a repeated line  
feel heavier the second time.  
One gave a feeling  
more than one bright comparison.  
One kept a bird singing  
through difficult weather.  
One made space  
for dancing and rest.  

That list was not my whole notebook.  
It was a set of doors  
I had learned to open.  
Through them came  
a yellow wedge,  
a cold bench,  
a cardboard box,  
a hallway light,  
and Dad moving a chair.  

The visitor said  
he had expected a shorter answer.  
I said I had expected  
to give him one.  
We stood beside the shelf  
for a moment,  
looking at all the small doors  
that had taken us somewhere else.  

### April 7: Tansy's place

Tansy has been choosing  
the low chair by the window.  
She used to leap  
onto the back of the sofa.  
Now Dad lifts her blanket  
to the lower seat.  
She circles twice  
and settles with one paw uncovered.  

I wrote a poem about it  
and made every line sad.  
Then I looked at her.  
She was watching a loose thread  
on my sleeve  
with exactly her old suspicion.  

I had decided what the poem  
was supposed to mean  
before I had noticed  
what she was doing.  

I started again.  
This time there was a blanket,  
a lower chair,  
a suspicious look,  
and one paw  
refusing to stay tucked in.  

Dad read it at the table.  
He touched the line about her paw.  
Then he told me  
that she was old and unwell.  
The veterinarian was helping us  
keep her comfortable.  
We did not know  
how much time she had.  

I asked whether I had to change  
the poem again.  
He said the poem  
could be about this afternoon.  
It did not have to know  
what tomorrow would bring.  

I sat beside her chair  
with the notebook closed.  
For a while,  
keeping company was the only thing  
I wanted my hands to do.  

### April 18: A copy for the shelf

We are making a small booklet  
for visitors to borrow in the room.  
Everyone may choose  
whether to include a page.  
Leila wants her window drawing  
and one poem about chalk.  
Omar wants two poems  
that look like machines.  
Mina wants a page  
with more space than words.  

I want everything,  
which is my usual first idea  
before I begin thinking.  

Then I read through the notebook.  
There is the crossed-out rectangle.  
There are questions I still cannot answer.  
There are private notes to you  
that would make no sense  
on the visitor shelf.  

Choosing a page  
is not the same  
as hiding all the others.  
Some pages did their work  
by helping the next page happen.  

I choose the gate,  
the wedge,  
and the kitchen chair.  
I write a short note  
about the poems that helped me.  
It names their writers  
without claiming  
that my poems say the same things.  

There is a blank square  
at the top of the booklet cover.  
A title is needed again.  
This time I do not close it  
with my elbow.  
I leave the square open  
while we finish arranging the pages.  
There will be time  
for the right words to arrive.  

## Part 8: May and June, What stays on the shelf

### May 6: The last visit

Ms. Ortiz,  
the smallest class visited today.  
Their feet did not reach the floor  
when they sat at the window table.  
Omar brought a lower box  
so the cards were easier to reach.  

I began our introduction  
without the speech from the mirror.  
I said what the shelf held  
and showed how a card unfolded.  
Then I waited.  

One child wanted a poem  
about a loud sound.  
One wanted a quiet one.  
One asked if we had  
anything about a blue truck.  
We did not.  
He drew one for us.  

I was going to ask  
what sound the truck made.  
Then he pointed at the wheels  
and said they were very tired.  
I asked if he wanted  
those words beside his drawing.  
He nodded  
and watched me write them.  

A truck with tired wheels  
was not a fact  
about a real truck.  
It was his way  
of making the picture move  
inside another person's mind.  
I understood that  
without making him explain  
what tired really meant.  

After the visitors left,  
I set the drawing  
between the hall and imagined piles.  
The wheels belonged to both.  
There was no trophy on the shelf.  
There was a blue truck  
that our first plan  
had never made room to expect.  

### May 19: A small book

The booklet is finished.  
We called it Things We Almost Missed,  
like the display.  
The title had been there  
since October.  
We had needed months  
to understand what it held.  

I read my printed gate poem  
and found a tiny mistake.  
One letter had been left out.  
For a moment  
I wanted to take every copy back.  

We corrected the copies  
with a small mark.  
No one had to begin  
an entirely new year.  
The poems were still there.  
So were the readers.  

I thought about the square  
on my notebook cover.  
It still had my name  
and the little shelf beneath it.  
I wrote a title above them:  
A Notebook of Small Things.  

Small does not mean easy.  
The small yellow wedge  
had made me argue about a word.  
A small space after a line  
had changed the pace of a kitchen.  
A small question  
before using someone's name  
had changed what I could share.  

I wanted to add that  
under the title,  
but there was not enough room.  
Perhaps a title does not have to carry  
everything a book has learned.  

I left the explanation inside.  
The shelf on the cover  
looked a little crooked.  
I did not redraw it.  
It was the first shelf I made  
when I still thought  
I had nothing to put there.  

### June 2: The chair by the window

Tansy died on Saturday.  
Dad told me plainly.  
He sat beside me  
while I asked the same question twice.  
The chair by the window  
held her folded blanket.  

I do not want a comparison today.  
I do not want to say  
she became a star  
or a song  
or anything that is not Tansy.  
She died.  
I miss her.  
Those sentences are enough  
for this page.  

Dad asked if I wanted  
him to put the blanket away.  
I said not yet.  
He said all right.  

On Monday you asked  
whether I wanted company  
or quiet at reading time.  
I chose quiet first.  
Later I sat beside Mina  
while she worked on a drawing.  
She did not ask me  
for a story about what happened.  

I brought a poem about the gate  
to our rehearsal.  
It was something I could read  
without explaining the chair at home.  
You said I could choose  
which pages to share.  
I was glad  
that the choice was still mine  
when I wanted it most.  

At home I opened the notebook  
to the dried paw mark.  
It had not changed  
into a message.  
It was a mark  
she had made one wet afternoon.  
I remembered the afternoon  
and left the notebook open beside me.  

### June 9: The sock thread

I found a sock  
with one loose string.  
For a moment  
I expected Tansy to catch it.  
Then I remembered.  
The remembering came twice:  
first the tug,  
then the empty space.  

I wrote a very long poem  
that listed everything about her.  
It was too much to read aloud.  
It was not too much  
to keep in my notebook.  

The next day I chose  
one part of it:  

One thread lifts  
from the sock.  

My foot waits  
for a tug.  

The thread falls.  
I pull the sock on  
carefully.  

I am not going to put this poem  
in the class booklet.  
Dad has read it.  
That is enough for now.  
It does not make me  
stop missing her.  
It gives the missing  
a place to stand  
for a little while.  

Mina asked whether she could  
write about something cheerful  
at our final sharing.  
I said of course.  
Then I wondered  
why I had said it  
as though she needed my permission.  

There can be a sad page  
in my notebook  
and a cheerful page  
on the desk beside it.  
Neither page has to prove  
the other one is wrong.  
The room is large enough  
for different things to be true.  

### June 16: Dear Ms. Ortiz

You said we could keep our notebooks.  
I thought you might collect them  
and put them in a cupboard  
with the other finished work.  
But this book is mine.  
You have written questions in it,  
and other writers have helped  
change the way I use it.  
Still, I decide  
which page comes next.  

For the final sharing,  
I read the kitchen poem.  
I explained the chair,  
the spaces,  
and the other poem  
that helped me notice them.  
My left hand  
was not holding my right wrist.  
It was holding the notebook open.  

Omar asked a question.  
I looked at the draft  
before answering.  
There was no need  
to pretend I had always known  
what the finished poem would do.  

When we cleared the classroom,  
we left the empty shelf in place.  
The cards and the box  
went to the reading corner.  
Next year somebody else  
may have another use for the shelf.  

I am taking the notebook home.  
There are blank pages at the back.  
On the first one  
I have written today's date  
and one sentence:  

The latch clacked twice.  

Below it,  
there is room  
for what I notice next.
