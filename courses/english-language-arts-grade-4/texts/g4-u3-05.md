## Text

### The Boundary Stake

#### Cast and setting

Ada: a child who notices changes to familiar places.

Ben: Ada's brother, eager to send the family's apples farther.

Mother: a farmer who wants evidence before making a judgment.

Mrs. Reed: a neighbor who brings a copied passage from the canal memorial.

The family and their farm are invented. The scenes take place near a proposed canal route in 1817. Their concerns do not represent a Native Nation's experience or land rights. The map, stake and official response in the play are fictional.

#### Scene 1: A Mark Beside the Tree

[A wooden stake stands beside an apple tree. Ada kneels near it, looking without touching. Ben enters with a small wooden boat.]

BEN: There you are! Mother thought you were collecting fallen branches.

ADA: I was. Then I found this.

[She points to the stake. Ben puts his boat on the ground beside it.]

BEN: Perhaps the canal will come here. Think of our apples going all the way to a city.

ADA: Think of the tree.

BEN: I am thinking of it. A whole boat of its apples!

ADA: You see the apples leaving. I see the roots staying here.

[Mother approaches with an empty basket. She stops between the children and the stake.]

MOTHER: Has anyone told you what this mark means?

BEN: It must mark the canal.

MOTHER: Must it? That is a stronger word than our information allows.

ADA: Someone put it here. That is information.

MOTHER: Yes. We know where it stands. We do not know exactly what it marks.

[Ada picks up the basket. Ben reaches for the stake.]

MOTHER: Leave it in place. We can ask about a mark we can still locate.

[Ben withdraws his hand. He picks up the boat instead.]

BEN: Then let us ask soon.

ADA: Before someone answers with a shovel.

#### Scene 2: What Ben Is Counting

[In the kitchen, Ben is arranging wooden blocks beside his boat. Ada watches from the doorway. Mother is sorting apples.]

BEN: Each block is a load. One trip, two trips, three trips.

MOTHER: Are those measured loads or imagined ones?

BEN: Imagined. But a boat can carry things. That part is real.

ADA: A boat on our table is not a canal beside our tree.

BEN: You think I only care about the boat.

[He gathers the blocks into a pile.]

BEN: I am counting apples we might sell. We work to grow them. What if carrying them away costs almost everything we receive?

MOTHER: That is a reason people support the canal: cheaper transport could help sellers. We still need evidence about the cost.

ADA: What if the apples have nowhere to grow?

[Ben looks toward the window. For a moment, he says nothing.]

BEN: I was thinking about a journey. You were thinking about its beginning.

MOTHER: Both belong in our questions.

ADA: Are you still counting three trips?

BEN: I am counting no trips until I know what the blocks stand for.

[He sets one block beside the boat and leaves the others in a pile.]

#### Scene 3: Along the Fence

[Outside, a fence runs from the house toward the apple tree. A worn path leads beside it. The stake stands beyond a shallow ditch. Mother carries a paper map.]

ADA: On the paper, that bend is no wider than my fingernail.

MOTHER: Stand beside it and look again.

[Ada walks along the fence. Ben follows on the path.]

ADA: From here, the line passes near the tree. From the house, it looked farther away.

BEN: The ground slopes beyond the ditch. Does the paper show that?

MOTHER: Some maps show heights. This copy does not give us enough detail.

[A gust lifts one corner. Ada catches it with both hands.]

ADA: I thought the paper would tell us what happens next.

MOTHER: It helps us ask where. It does not answer every question about how.

BEN: We could put our own marks on a copy. The tree, the fence and this path.

ADA: Not across their marks. Beside them.

[Mother folds the map along an old crease. Ada continues looking toward the tree.]

MOTHER: We will describe the places we mean. That is better than asking whether the canal comes somewhere near our house.

BEN: Along the fence. Beyond the ditch. Beside the tree.

ADA: Small words can point to large questions.

#### Scene 4: A Neighbor's Paper

[Back in the kitchen, Mrs. Reed puts a copied passage on the table. Ben moves his boat aside to make room.]

MRS. REED: This is from the memorial supporting the canal. Its writers expect transportation to become cheaper.

ADA: Does it say where they will dig?

MRS. REED: This passage does not.

BEN: Does it say how much the canal will cost?

MRS. REED: It reports an estimate. Listen to the difference between estimate and final expense.

[She traces a line of writing with one finger.]

MOTHER: Their estimate can help us understand the plan. It cannot tell us the final cost of work that has not been finished.

BEN: Their reason is larger trade. Their information includes an estimate.

MRS. REED: And their purpose is to persuade the legislature to build.

ADA: Then their paper is asking a question too, even if it sounds like an answer.

MOTHER: It is asking for action. We are asking what that action would mean here.

[Ada slides a blank sheet toward the copied passage. Ben puts two apples beside it.]

BEN: Two apples, and two kinds of paper. Their proposal and our questions.

ADA: Leave their words where we can check them.

[Mrs. Reed moves the copy back to the center of the table. Everyone leans toward it.]

#### Scene 5: A Longer View

[The map is open. Ada has marked the tree on a separate slip. Mother sets the slip beside the map rather than pasting it down.]

BEN: If they move the line away from the tree, will you support the canal?

ADA: I might stop worrying about this tree. That is not the same answer.

MRS. REED: The memorial speaks confidently about what the connection could bring.

MOTHER: We should read that confidence carefully. A promise of trade is not an answer to every question about land.

BEN: Could they move the line onto someone else's field?

MOTHER: That is exactly why asking only about our tree is too small.

[Ben picks up the marked slip, then sets it down again.]

ADA: I was making our tree the whole map.

MRS. REED: A map can leave people out even when it includes their land.

MOTHER: We can ask about our farm without pretending our questions are everyone's questions. A family concern does not give us authority to speak for a Nation.

BEN: Then we need to know where the line goes beyond our fence.

ADA: And whose answer is missing from the papers we have.

[Mother turns the map so all four can see it.]

#### Scene 6: Questions [P98]

[In the family kitchen near the canal route, Ada folds a route map so the apple-tree mark is hidden. Ben places a wooden canal boat beside it.]

BEN: The boat could carry our apples farther.

ADA: And what carries the tree if that stake marks its place?

[Mother sits between them.]

MOTHER: We have a line on a map, not an answer about our tree. What must we ask?

[Ada opens the map. She writes Questions above the tree mark. Ben moves the boat aside.]

ADA: First, exactly where would they dig?

BEN: Next, what happens to a family whose field lies there?

[Ada slides the list toward Mother.]

#### Scene 7: What a Letter Can Change

[Several days later, a folded reply lies beside the map. Mother reads it silently. Ada waits with a pencil. Ben turns his boat over in his hands.]

MOTHER: The reply says the stake marks a place to examine. It does not settle the final line beside the tree. They ask for our description of the field.

BEN: Then the tree is safe.

ADA: That is a stronger word than our information allows.

[Ben nods at Mother's earlier words returning in Ada's voice.]

MOTHER: We can answer the request. That is a decision we can make today.

ADA: I want to carry the description myself. I am afraid they will read tree and see only a dot.

BEN: I will come. I can carry the map while you point.

ADA: I was guarding the paper as if folding it could protect the roots.

[She smooths the map with her palm.]

ADA: Now I want it open. I want them to see what we mean.

MOTHER: Write what you know, then mark what you still need to ask.

[Ada begins writing. Ben sets the boat beside the door rather than on the map.]

#### Scene 8: The Open Map

[That evening, the kitchen table is clear except for the map and the completed description. Mrs. Reed returns with the memorial passage. Ada puts her pencil down.]

MRS. REED: Have you settled your opinion?

BEN: I still want the apples to travel farther. Now I want to know what must happen before they can.

ADA: I still want the tree here. Now I know hiding its mark does not answer a question.

MOTHER: Tomorrow we can deliver this description and keep a copy. The reply may answer one question and raise another.

MRS. REED: So the papers stay together?

ADA: The map, the memorial and our questions. With their names on them.

[Ben sets the wooden boat on the windowsill. Ada leaves the map open.]

BEN: A boat needs somewhere to go.

ADA: And somewhere to leave from.

[Mother places the copy of their questions beside the map. No one moves the stake outside. The family gathers its papers for the next morning.]

MOTHER: We can care about an answer enough to keep asking carefully.

[The light fades on the open map.]
