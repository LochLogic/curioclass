## Text

### A. Porcupine: Quills

Source: National Park Service, Porcupine, updated April 11, 2025. Excerpt: the paragraph beginning "It has a stocky build"; unchanged prose. Read selected portions on days 40, 43, 45, 47 and 49 to 55; the day 44 digital pairing uses this excerpt for the first time alongside its separate cold companion, not as a new excerpt.

It has a stocky build with a dense, coarse fur coat and, most notably, quills that cover its back, tail, and sides. These quills, which are modified hairs, are the porcupine's primary defense against predators. When intimidated, porcupines turn their backs towards the threat and flare their quills. The quills are sharp, barbed, and loosely attached to the animal's skin, making them easily detach when a predator comes into contact with them.

### B. Armadillos: Identification

Source: National Park Service, Armadillos, updated December 11, 2019. Selected unchanged sentences from Identification; omitted sentences marked below. Editorial context: scutes are the bony plates of the covering. Use with the armadillo article on days 53 to 55 and in later research rereading.

The nine-banded armadillo can have anywhere from seven to eleven bands. [...] These scutes help protect them from many would-be predators.

Contrary to popular belief, the nine-banded armadillo is not capable of curling completely into a ball for defense.

### C. Striped Skunk: Defensive spray

Source: National Park Service, Mammal Descriptions, 2016 web edition. Whole Striped Skunk entry, unchanged prose. The source page is about animals in Arkansas; "statewide" refers to Arkansas. Use with the skunk article on days 53 to 55 and in later research rereading.

The striped skunk is found statewide. Identifying features include two broad white stripes running down its back. It is known best for its putrid smell. Around the size of a housecat, they are omnivores that feed on insects and small rodents.

They can spray a putrid smelling liquid used as a defense mechanism with surprising accuracy, with jets that may reach up to 15ft.

### D. Octopus: A replacement companion card

Curio Class original, 2026. Rights: ours. This card replaces the unavailable library excerpt; it is not federal prose. Facts come from the two octopus sources below. Use with the common octopus article on days 53 to 55 and in later research rereading.

An octopus can change the appearance of its skin. Tiny structures contain pigment, a colored substance. When muscles expand pigment sacs, more of their color shows. Skin texture can change too. Raised projections can make a surface look rough rather than smooth. Together, these changes can help an octopus blend with a rocky background.

The animal remains present even when it is difficult to notice. Camouflage depends on appearance against surroundings. A rough, patchy surface can make an outline difficult to follow beside an uneven rock. That same outline might be easier to see elsewhere.

A common octopus can also release ink and jet away. Its muscular mantle draws in water, then forces water through a siphon. A jet of water helps move the animal. Ink can confuse a predator during the escape. Camouflage, ink and movement are different possibilities, not a guarantee of safety.

### E. Reserved for day 56 only: Woodland Box Turtle

Source: National Park Service, Species Spotlight - Woodland Box Turtle, 2025 web edition. Thinking Inside the Box: first paragraph, then first two sentences of its third paragraph; intervening paragraph and remaining sentences omitted. Unchanged prose. Withhold this entire card until day 56. Do not display it with the teaching excerpts or expert-group menu.

Box turtles are so named because they can not only retreat into their shells at the first sign of danger, but mostly seal off their legs and head within a hinged plastron (under shell) creating a formidable armor that few predators attempt to breach. The yellow/orange blotched upper shell, or carapace, consists of a combination of rib bones and scales.

[...]

Much like fingerprints, every turtle has unique orangy yellow head and shell markings. The effect helps them blend in with a sun-dappled forest floor.
