## Instructional article, days 127 to 128

A short explanation can focus on one person’s reason. A later history account can follow an event across different records. Both can concern the same vote while giving readers different information.

In 1920, Tennessee’s lawmakers considered whether to ratify the proposed Nineteenth Amendment. To ratify meant to agree formally to the amendment. Congress had already passed the proposal in 1919. The next part of the process depended on the states.

Harry Burn was a member of Tennessee’s House of Representatives. On August 18, he voted in favor of ratification. On the following day, he gave an explanation that became part of the House Journal. The vote and the explanation were related events on different days.

Burn’s introductory line tells readers that he is explaining why he changed his vote. His first numbered reason calls suffrage a right. The focus is his justification, the explanation he offered for his own choice. It is testimony from someone who participated in the decision.

This article has another focus: placing the decision in a sequence. Its writer was not in the room in 1920. The account draws on an archive’s historical page and a federal biography. That distance allows the writer to assemble a sequence from records. It does not allow the writer to know every private thought of the people involved.

A useful comparison asks who made each account, what that person could know, and which details the account supplies. An early date alone cannot answer all three questions. A later account also needs sources, not just confidence.

## C1 version, reserved for first reading on day 129

Tennessee supplied the thirty-sixth state’s ratification in August 1920. Agreement from three-fourths of the states was required. Certification followed on August 26. This explains the ratification procedure; it does not tell us each legislator’s private reasons.

## G1 version, reserved for first reading on day 151

Before the vote, Burn faced pressure from political colleagues to oppose suffrage. His mother urged support. He voted for ratification. The later account places competing influences beside the decision; it cannot measure the private weight of each influence.
