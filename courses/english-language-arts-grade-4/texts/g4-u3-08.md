### Anansi Wins the Stories

Akan tradition, from West Africa, including Ghana and C?te d'Ivoire. Anansi is a spider trickster in Akan storytelling. This version follows an older printed telling collected by W. H. Barker and Cecilia Sinclair. It keeps two tests and omits the source's third test involving injury. It is one adaptation, not every Akan telling.

1. In this telling, the stories people told belonged to Nyankupon, chief of the gods. Anansi the spider wanted those stories to bear his own name. Wanting them was easy; obtaining them would require a bargain. Anansi went to Nyankupon and asked for the right to call the tales Anansi stories. Nyankupon set conditions that seemed difficult for a small spider to fulfill. Anansi would have to bring him a jar of living bees and a great snake. Neither creature was likely to follow a spider merely because he asked. Still, Anansi agreed. He left with a promise to keep and began considering how to complete the tests without relying on strength.

2. Anansi took an earthen jar to a place where bees gathered. He did not begin by chasing them. Instead, he started a loud argument with himself. Could all those bees fit inside the jar? Perhaps they could. Perhaps they could not. He kept changing sides until the bees noticed the noise. They came closer to ask what he was arguing about. Anansi explained that there had been a dispute over whether the jar could hold them. He presented their size as a question that needed settling. The bees wanted to prove what they could do.

3. To settle the argument, the bees flew into the vessel. Anansi waited until they were inside, then closed it. The bees had answered a question about the jar, but they had not understood the purpose of the question. Anansi sent the filled jar to Nyankupon. He had completed one condition through a trick rather than a contest of strength. Yet one success did not finish the bargain. The great snake remained, and Anansi could not expect a snake to behave exactly like a gathering of bees. He needed another plan suited to the next creature.

4. The next day Anansi carried a long stick to the place where the snake lived. Again he began talking as though an argument were already under way. Was the snake as long as the stick? He said it was, then spoke the opposite claim. The snake heard him and wanted to know why anyone doubted its length. Anansi offered a simple test: lie straight beside the stick so the lengths could be compared. When the snake stretched out, Anansi tied it to the stick. What looked like a measuring task became a capture. He sent the snake to Nyankupon, completing the second condition through another deceptive question.

5. Nyankupon now had the things required by this telling's bargain. The small spider had succeeded where a direct struggle might have failed. Nyankupon granted the request: the old tales could be called Anansi stories. That ending explains a name as well as completing a sequence of tests. Anansi gained what he wanted, although the bees and snake had been deceived. A reader can notice both his resourcefulness and its cost to others. Cleverness in a story need not be a rule to imitate. This shortened adaptation ends when the bargain is fulfillled; other tellings include more tests and different details. It does not claim that one printed version contains every Akan account of Anansi.
