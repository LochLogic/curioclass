### Count the Cost

This is an original modern reconstruction of an opposing argument about the 1817 decision. Its speaker is invented. It is not a historical quotation or a transcript.

Neighbors, a canal may carry goods more cheaply. I do not dismiss that hope. I ask whether a hope is enough to begin a project of this size. We should delay committing to the full work until its expense and means of payment are clearer.

First, an estimate is not a finished bill. The memorial reports an estimate of no more than five million dollars. Its writers look to similar works for guidance. That comparison deserves attention, but this route will have its own obstacles. Ground that is easy to draw across may be difficult to dig. We need to know what the estimate includes before relying on it.

Next, borrowing creates obligations before every promised benefit arrives. The memorial's own example says that spending five hundred thousand dollars in the first year would require thirty thousand dollars in annual interest. It proposes future income to meet the expense. Yet proposed income and money already received are different things. Ask what will pay the interest while work continues and traffic is still limited.

In addition, a useful connection must be complete enough to serve its purpose. An unfinished crossing cannot carry a boat merely because other sections are ready. We should require clear reports on obstacles, spending and the parts still to be finished. A shorter working section may teach us something. It cannot answer every question about the full route.

I therefore favor a delay in committing to the whole project. Publish the estimates and explain the risks. Show how the state would respond if construction took more than expected. The possibility of cheaper carriage deserves investigation. It does not require us to treat every promise as a result.

### P99: Storage Before Service

This separate, invented argument concerns a later decision about beginning service. It is not an actual historical proposal.

Delay the canal opening until covered storage is ready at the landings. Imagine a farmer unloading sacks of grain just as rain starts. The boat has not arrived, and the sacks must wait beside the water. Wet grain can spoil before it reaches a buyer. A roof over the waiting goods would address that problem. Proposed sheds cannot protect anything until they are built. Ask each landing to show that its storage is ready before boats begin regular service.
