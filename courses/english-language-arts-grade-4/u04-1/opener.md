# Letters and changing judgments

Start with the question of who gained voting rights and who still faced barriers. Name racism and unequal access before reading. Keep the law, the archive's account, and the novel's invented letters distinct. Invite reading and discussion without asking students to speak from personal experience or reenact exclusion.

An inference is an unstated conclusion supported by source details. Locate the exact words first. The narrator is the teller of a story. Ask whose words a reader is hearing when the story shifts to a letter. Compare one feature of a picture or one pause to the written words that support it.

Print the authorized reading leaves, caption strip, portrait, half-page tickets and private records. Draw the three described portrait features on the board if the picture cannot be displayed. Read the oral version live when no recording is available. Keep the independent check in a separate packet until day 123.

Review the discussion moves chart: mark a detail, listen, ask for detail, link, explain what became clearer. In ordinary pair talk, allow two 45-second turns. Hear a rotating six while everyone writes a located detail. In day 124 conferences, give each child one 45-second evidence explanation. Separate the original response, conference note and unjudged revision.

Use the individual rereading record for the five due readers. Each has two minutes within the 15-minute partner block. Use matching observations from the previous ten days when available. Keep accuracy, phrasing, meaning and contextual correction visible. A paper note shows what a child wrote. Use observed turns for speaking and reading evidence.

Return written work privately. A detail can support an inference without proving it. If a claim goes too far, mark the actual words and test a different claim. When delivery does not match the text, repair one pause or emphasis on a different line.
