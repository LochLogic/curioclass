# Reading land and labor histories

A record can answer its own question well and leave other questions open. Identify who made it, when, and for what purpose. When a source describes a legal finding, identify the question it answers. Use Nation-authored writing when reading about a Nation's relationship to its homeland. Keep each account attributed. Children work as present-day readers. Use published sources for Native perspectives and keep all work seated.

Review **main idea** as a whole sentence with two details joined by a link word. Use one weak detail for inspection. Review **chronology** as information organized by order in time. Use a single short span to distinguish a time signal from the relationship it helps show. Hall describes what he saw or reports during later travel. The history describes the work that created the canal. Teach children to locate the source that has evidence for the question they are asking.

Keep the authorized history and traveler leaves separate and save the first source-labeled notes in dated folders. Allow time to explain older language, including Hall's use of words for settlements and people born in a place. Before the reading, tell students it includes land loss, worker illness and accidents, and a brief period reference to alcohol. Keep descriptions factual and non-graphic.

For a statement that exceeds its source, underline the exact claim and ask which words support it. For a topic given as the main idea, ask what the text explains about that topic. Give one written move in the named return part, using a different taught example for the retry.
