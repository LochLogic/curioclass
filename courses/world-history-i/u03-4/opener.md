# Section 3.4 opener: mapping power and defending a claim

Read this once before day 42. It holds what every day of the section assumes, so the day pages stay short.

## What the section does

Five days, 42 to 46. Each student maps one kingdom or empire and explains it: Ghana, Mali and Songhai; the Swahili
city-states with Axum before them; or the Mongol Empire (days 42 and 43). Day 44 brings the map back with your
written feedback for revision, then a discussion and a closing claim on the unit's question: was a ruler's power in
this age won mainly by trade, by faith or by the sword? Day 45 is the end-of-unit assessment; day 46 responds to it
and reviews units 1 to 3 for course-level check 1.

## How the days run

Every day is 50 minutes. Days 42 and 43 open with a five-minute warm-up that repairs the last exit; days 42, 43, 44
and 46 close with a five-minute exit ticket judged against its look-fors. Mark exits the same evening; more than a
third missing one look-for means the next warm-up repairs it.

## Feedback before scoring

Nothing in this section is scored before a student has read your written feedback and revised.

- **The map.** Collect the folders after day 43. Write one line each on dated geography, politics, economy, culture,
  every link (trade, alliance, war, exchange) and the limit of a source used. A missing alliance with a note that
  no source records one is correct; an invented alliance is not. Day 44 gives five minutes to read and ten to
  revise; the revised map is scored as A2 on day 45.
- **The claim.** Collect the closing claims after day 44. Write one line each on the weighing of trade, faith and the
  sword, the change from Chinggis to Khubilai, the Silk Roads consequence with the harms kept, the counterclaim and
  the limit. Day 45's first five minutes revise it; the revised claim is scored in A2d.

Plan for two evenings of marking, about 30 students at 3 minutes each, an untested estimate.

## The sources, and how to handle them

Every source is named on its sheet with who made it, when, and its rights line. The full list, with citations, is
data/plans/world-history-i/sources.csv. The day 45 packet is reserved: do not show it before the assessment.

- **Outsiders, all of them.** Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo, Rubruck and Ibn al-Athir wrote for readers at home. Ibn
  Battuta dictated his book in Fez in 1355, years after his journeys; Polo's was written down by Rustichello; Ibn
  al-Athir did not see the conquest he describes. Ask every day: who made it, when, for whom, and what can it not
  show?
- **Translators' words.** Gibb's 1929 translation says "negroes" and "blacks" for the people of Mali; Browne's 1902
  translation says "Tartars" for the Mongols. They stay inside the source, glossed once. In your own words, name the
  people: the people of Mali, the Mongols, enslaved people.
- **Slavery named plainly.** Sources A, C and P1 show enslaved people: armed guards at the court, people taken in
  Kilwa's raids, salt miners at Taghaza. Name the force; never treat people as one more good. Their own voices do
  not survive in these sources; say so.
- **Conquest named plainly.** Source E records the killing of women, men and children. Read it once, without
  dramatic reading, and give no death total the sources do not support.
- **No role play.** No one plays a ruler, a conqueror, a trader or an enslaved person, and the discussion is argued
  from sources, never in character.
- **Living peoples.** Mali, Timbuktu's manuscript families, Swahili speakers, the Shona and the people of Mongolia are
  present today. Some students may have family ties to these places; do not ask any student to speak for them.

## The errors to expect

| | The error | What you see | First move |
|---|---|---|---|
| L | A court source proves consent | "The ceremony shows everyone loved him." | Sort what the source shows and cannot show. |
| I | Wealth without labour | "The ruler was rich because the river was there." | Add a producer and a cost to the arrow. |
| B | Modern borders | Mali drawn with today's borders. | A dated map beside a present-day map. |
| H | One period | "Zheng He sailed for Khubilai." | Separate dated bands on the timeline. |
| K | Foreign founders | "The Swahili towns were Arab colonies." | Sort finds into made here and brought here. |
| N | The ruler alone | "Musa made Timbuktu a place of learning." | List everyone the source shows taking part. |
| D | Trade cancels conquest | "Safe roads mean no one was harmed." | Harms and gains in two columns. |
| E | The writer saw everything | "The atlas maker visited Mali." | Ask: could the maker have seen this? |
| G | Naming is investigating | "I would search for more facts." | Open the card and copy one detail. |

## What the teacher needs to know

- Ancient Ghana lay inland, in today's Mauritania and Mali, not in the modern country of Ghana, which took the name
  in 1957.
- Mali's wealth came from taxing trade and from gold fields its rulers did not mine themselves; salt was dug at
  Taghaza by enslaved people. Ibn Battuta saw Mali in 1352 and 1353, under Mansa Sulayman, Musa's brother.
- The Swahili towns were African towns with their own rulers, trading across the Indian Ocean; imported porcelain
  and glass show trade, not foreign builders. Gibb's translation says Kilwa was built of wood; its surviving Great
  Mosque and palace are coral stone.
- Chinggis Khan's conquests destroyed cities and killed very many people; Khubilai ruled China as the Yuan emperor;
  paper money, protected roads and patronage of the arts came with the same rule. Both are true and stay separate.
- The Catalan Atlas (1375) shows Mansa Musa with gold half a century after his pilgrimage made him famous in Cairo
  and Europe; its maker worked from reports.
