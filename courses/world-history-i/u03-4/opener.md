# Section 3.4 opener: mapping power and defending a claim

## What the section does

Five days, 42 to 46. Each student maps one kingdom or empire and explains it: Ghana, Mali and Songhai; the Swahili
city-states with Axum before them; or the Mongol Empire (days 42 and 43). Day 44 brings the map back with your
written feedback for revision, then a discussion and a closing claim on the unit's question: was a ruler's power in
this age won mainly by trade, by faith or by the sword? Day 45 is the end-of-unit assessment; day 46 responds to it
and reviews units 1 to 3 for course-level check 1.

## How the days run

Every day is 50 minutes. Days 42 and 43 open with a warm-up that repairs the last exit; days 42, 43, 44 and 46
close with an exit ticket judged against its look-fors. If more than a third miss one, the next warm-up repairs it.

## Feedback before scoring

Nothing in this section is scored before a student has read your written feedback and revised.

- **The map.** Collect the folders after day 43. In short phrases, about 5 minutes a map, write on dated
  geography, politics, economy, culture, every link (trade, alliance, war, exchange) and the limit of a source used.
  A dated note that no source records an alliance scores 2; an invented alliance scores 0. Day 44 gives five minutes
  to read and eight to revise; the folders then stay with you, and you score A2 from them that evening.
- **The claim.** Collect the closing claims after day 44. Write six lines: the weighing, two explained details, the
  counterclaim, the limit, Chinggis to Khubilai, and the Silk Roads consequence with harms kept. Day 45's first five
  minutes revise it; the revised claim is scored in A2d.
- **Day 45.** Score every item, one record-sheet cell a component, 2, 1 or 0: about 3 hours for 30, an untested
  estimate. Groups for day 46 come from this table, at most four; others work in pairs from the written move. Give
  groups on slips; marks stay private.

## The sources, and how to handle them

Each sheet names who made each source, when, and its rights; citations are in
data/plans/world-history-i/sources.csv. The day 45 packet is reserved: do not show it before the assessment.

- **Who made each source.** Ibn Battuta and Rubruck were visitors writing for readers at home; Ibn Battuta dictated
  his book in 1355, years after his journeys. Polo told his book to Rustichello for European readers. Ibn al-Athir,
  a historian in Mosul, wrote for Muslim readers about a disaster to his own world; he did not see these events and
  heard them from people who fled. Ask every day: who made it, when, for whom, and what can it not show?
- **Their own histories.** Griots' songs, the Tarikh al-Sudan, the Kilwa Chronicle and Ezana's inscriptions are
  named, not quoted: we found no free English text to check; say so as those sheets' limit. The Secret History of
  the Mongols has one; option d quotes it as Source K.
- **Translators' words.** They stay printed. Reading aloud, read each once, say what it means and why we do not use
  it, then use its substitute: "blacks" and "negroes", the people of Mali; "Negrolands", the lands of Mali;
  "slaves", enslaved people; "Tartars" (outsiders' name for the Mongols), the Mongols.
- **Slavery named plainly.** Sources A and P1 show enslaved people: armed men at the court and salt miners at
  Taghaza. Source C records raids on inland neighbors; raids like these often took captives, but the passage does
  not say so. Never treat people as one more good; their own voices do not survive here.
- **Conquest named plainly.** Source E records the killing of women, men and children. Read it once, without
  dramatic reading, and give no death total the sources do not support.
- **No role play.** No one plays a ruler, conqueror, trader or enslaved person; argue from sources, never in
  character.
- **Living peoples.** Mali, Timbuktu's manuscript families, Swahili speakers, the Shona and the people of Mongolia
  are present today. Do not ask any student to speak for them.

## The errors to expect

| | The error | What you see | First move |
|---|---|---|---|
| L | A court ceremony, or a visitor's account of one, proves consent | "The ceremony shows everyone loved him." | Sort what the source shows and cannot show. |
| I | Wealth without labour | "The ruler was rich because the river was there." | Add a producer and a cost to the arrow. |
| B | Modern borders | Mali drawn with today's borders. | A dated map beside a present-day map. |
| H | One period | "Zheng He sailed for Khubilai." | Separate dated bands on the timeline. |
| K | Foreign founders | "The Swahili towns were Arab colonies." | Sort finds into made here and brought here. |
| N | The ruler alone | "Musa made Timbuktu a place of learning." | List everyone the source shows taking part. |
| D | Trade cancels conquest | "Safe roads mean no one was harmed." | Harms and gains in two columns. |
| E | The writer saw everything | "The atlas maker visited Mali." | Ask: could the maker have seen this? |
| G | Naming is investigating | "I would search for more facts." | Open the card and copy one detail. |
| P | Gains shared by all | "The market made the khan rich, so every trader gained." | Name who gained and who paid. |

## What the teacher needs to know

- Ancient Ghana lay inland, in today's Mauritania and Mali, not in the modern country of Ghana, which took the name
  in 1957.
- Mali's wealth came from taxing trade and from gold fields its rulers did not mine themselves.
- The Swahili towns were African towns with their own rulers, trading across the Indian Ocean; imported porcelain
  and glass show trade, not foreign builders. Gibb's translation says Kilwa was built of wood; its surviving Great
  Mosque and palace are coral stone.
- Chinggis Khan's conquests destroyed cities and killed very many people; Khubilai ruled China as the Yuan emperor;
  paper money, protected roads and patronage of the arts came with the same rule. Both are true and stay separate.
- The Catalan Atlas (1375) shows Mansa Musa with gold half a century after his pilgrimage made him famous in Cairo
  and Europe; its maker worked in Majorca, far from West Africa, from reports.
