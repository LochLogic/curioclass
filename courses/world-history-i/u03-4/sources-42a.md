# Days 42 to 44 sources, option a: Ghana, Mali and Songhai

Keep this sheet in your folder until day 46; the option b and d sheets are there for links to other regions. Ibn
Battuta was an outsider writing for readers at home. His 1929 translator's words stay as printed: "blacks" for the
people of Mali, "slaves" for enslaved people. In your own writing, name people as people.

**Their own histories.** The peoples of Mali kept their own history. Griots (jeliw) still sing the epic of Sundiata;
UNESCO lists the Sosso-Bala, the balafon kept by the Kouyate griots of Niagassola, as their heritage. Timbuktu's
scholars later wrote chronicles, among them the Tarikh al-Sudan, kept in family libraries today. We have no free
English text of them we could check, so this sheet quotes only visitors: that is its limit.

**Fact card (ours).** Ghana (Wagadu), founded by Soninke people, traded gold north across the Sahara and was named
in writing by the 800s; its capital, Koumbi Saleh, lay inland, in today's Mauritania, far from the modern country of
Ghana. Its power faded in the 1100s and 1200s.
Mali rose under Sundiata Keita about 1235; Mansa Musa ruled about 1312 to 1337 and made his pilgrimage to Mecca in
1324, staying in Cairo on the way. Timbuktu, Djenne, Gao and Walata were its trading and learning towns. Songhai,
from Gao, took Timbuktu in 1468 under Sunni Ali, grew under Askia Muhammad (ruled 1493 to 1528) and fell to a
Moroccan army in 1591. Across the desert went gold, and enslaved people forced to make the crossing; salt, copper,
cloth, horses and books came south. These were the empires of Soninke, Mande (Maninka) and Songhai peoples; their
descendants live in Mali, Mauritania, Senegal, Guinea, Niger and beyond today.

Where it lies: Koumbi Saleh, north of the Niger's upper course; the Mali heartland, on the upper Niger; Djenne, on
the inland delta; Timbuktu, at the Niger's northern bend; Gao, downstream to the east; Walata, on the desert edge
west of Timbuktu; the Sahara to the north.

**Source A. Ibn Battuta, the sultan of Mali holds an audience, 1352 to 1353.** Ibn Battuta, a judge from Morocco,
dictated his travels in 1355; the translation is H. A. R. Gibb's (1929). Public domain.

> On certain days the sultan holds audiences in the palace yard, where there is a platform under a tree, with three
> steps; this they call the "pempi." It is carpeted with silk and has cushions placed on it. [Over it] is raised the
> umbrella, which is a sort of pavilion made of silk, surmounted by a bird in gold, about the size of a falcon. The
> sultan comes out of a door in a corner of the palace, carrying a bow in his hand and a quiver on his back. ... The
> sultan is preceded by his musicians, who carry gold and silver guimbris [two-stringed guitars], and behind him come
> three hundred armed slaves.

Gloss: *audiences*, meetings where people come before the ruler; *pavilion*, a canopy; *quiver*, a case for arrows.
The sultan was Mansa Sulayman, Musa's brother.

**Source B. Ibn Battuta, what a traveller in Mali carries.** Same book and translation. Public domain.

> A traveller in this country carries no provisions, whether plain food or seasonings, and neither gold nor silver.
> He takes nothing but pieces of salt and glass ornaments, which the people call beads, and some aromatic goods.
> When he comes to a village the womenfolk of the blacks bring out millet, milk, chickens, pulped lotus fruit, rice,
> "funi" (a grain resembling mustard seed, from which "kuskusu" [couscous] and gruel are made), and pounded haricot
> beans.

Gloss: *provisions*, food for the journey; *aromatic goods*, spices and perfumes; *millet*, a grain.

**Source H. Ibn Battuta, the poets at the sultan's festival, 1352 to 1353.** Same book and translation, pp. 323 to
335. Public domain.

> ... the poets come in. Each of them is inside a figure resembling a thrush, made of feathers, and provided with a
> wooden head with a red beak, to look like a thrush's head. They stand in front of the sultan in this ridiculous
> make-up and recite their poems. I was told that their poetry is a kind of sermonizing in which they say to the sultan: "This pempi which you occupy was that whereon sat this king and that
> king, and such and such were this one's noble actions and such and such the other's. So do you too do good deeds
> whose memory will outlive you."

Gloss: *thrush*, a songbird; *sermonizing*, giving moral advice; *pempi*, the royal platform in Source A. These were
griots (jeliw), who kept Mali's history in words; Ibn Battuta called their dress "ridiculous", a visitor's judgment.
