# Days 42 to 44 sources, option b: the Swahili city-states, with Axum before them

Keep this sheet in your folder until day 46; the option a and d sheets are there for links to other regions. Ibn
Battuta was an outsider writing for readers at home, and his 1929 translator's words stay as printed. In your own
writing, name people as people.

**Their own histories.** Kilwa kept its own history of its kings, the Kilwa Chronicle, which survives in an Arabic
version and a Portuguese one of about 1552. Centuries earlier, Axum's King Ezana recorded his reign on stone, in
Greek, Sabaean and Ge'ez. We have no free English text of them we could check, so this sheet quotes only a visitor:
that is its limit.

**Fact card (ours).** Axum, in today's northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, was a kingdom of the first centuries CE to
about 900, trading through its Red Sea port of Adulis and minting its own coins; its king Ezana became Christian in
the 300s. It is the earlier comparison. The Swahili towns of about 1000 to 1500, among them Mogadishu, Mombasa,
Malindi, Kilwa and Sofala, stood along the Indian Ocean coast, each under its own ruler. Swahili is a Bantu language,
spoken today, with many Arabic words. Ancient DNA shows the coast's people had both African and Asian (mostly
Persian) ancestors, mixing by about 1000, while their language stayed African. Gold from the interior, where the builders of Great Zimbabwe (about 1100 to
1450) were ancestors of today's Shona, reached the coast at Sofala and was traded through Kilwa; ivory went out too,
and some enslaved people, taken from the interior and sold across the Indian Ocean; cloth, glass beads and Chinese
porcelain came in.

Where it lies: Mogadishu in the north; Malindi and Mombasa, on today's Kenyan coast; Kilwa, an island off southern
Tanzania; Sofala, far south on today's Mozambique coast; Great Zimbabwe, inland, west of Sofala; Axum, far north,
inland from the Red Sea.

**Source I. Ibn Battuta, Mombasa, about 1331.** Dictated in 1355; translated by H. A. R. Gibb (1929), pp. 112 to
113. Public domain.

> We came to Mambasa [Mombasa], a large island two days' journey by sea from the Sawihil country. It possesses no
> territory on the mainland. They have fruit trees on the island, but no cereals, which have to be brought to them
> from the Sawahil. Their food consists chiefly of bananas and fish. The inhabitants are pious, honourable, and
> upright, and they have well-built wooden mosques.

Gloss: *Sawihil* and *Sawahil*, the coast (Swahili comes from this word); *cereals*, grain; *pious*, devout.

**Source C. Ibn Battuta, Kilwa and the gold of Sufala, about 1331.** Same book, pp. 112 to 113. Public domain.

> We stayed one night in this island [Mombasa], and then pursued our journey to Kulwa, which is a large town on the
> coast. The majority of its inhabitants are Zanj ... I was told by a merchant that the town of Sufala lies a fortnight's journey [south] from Kulwa and that
> gold dust is brought to Sufala from Yufi in the country of the Limis, which is a month's journey distant from it.
> Kulwa is a very fine and substantially built town, and all its buildings are of wood. Its inhabitants are
> constantly engaged in military expeditions, for their country is contiguous to the heathen Zanj. The sultan at the
> time of my visit was Abu'l-Muzaffar Hasan, who was noted for his gifts and generosity. He used to devote the fifth
> part of the booty made on his expeditions to pious and charitable purposes, as is prescribed in the Koran ...

Gloss: *Kulwa*, Kilwa; *Sufala*, Sofala; *contiguous to*, next to; *Zanj*, Arabic writers' name for East African
peoples; *heathen*, his word for people who were not Muslim; *Koran*, the Qur'an; *booty*, what raiders seized; raids
of this time often took captives, but this passage does not say who or what was taken. Kilwa's surviving mosque and
palace are built of coral stone, not wood.
