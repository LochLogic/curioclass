# Days 42 to 44 sources, option d: the Mongol Empire

Keep this sheet in your folder until day 46; the option a and b sheets are there for links to other regions. Who
made each source: Polo told his book to Rustichello for European readers. Ibn al-Athir, a historian in Mosul, wrote
for Muslim readers about a disaster to his own world; he did not see these events and heard them from people who
fled. Browne's 1906 translation says "Tartars", a European name that linked the Mongols to Tartarus, the underworld;
Browne himself notes it. The Mongols called themselves Mongol. In your own writing, name people as people.

**Their own history.** The Mongols kept their own account of Chinggis's rise and his son Ogodei's rule, the Secret
History of the Mongols, written in Mongolian in the 1200s, its author unknown. A free English translation exists, and
Source K is a short piece of it; every other source on this sheet is by outsiders.

**Source K. The Secret History of the Mongols: Ogodei Khan names his deeds.** Ogodei, Chinggis's son, was Great
Khan from 1229 to 1241. Translated by Igor de Rachewiltz, shorter version edited by John C. Street (Western
Washington University CEDAR, 2015), paragraph 281. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0: quoted word for word, unchanged.

> Ögödei Qa’an said, ‘This I have done after I sat on the great throne of my father:
>
> ‘I campaigned against the J̌aqut people and I destroyed them.
>
> ‘As my second deed, I had post stations set up so that our messengers could ride in haste all along the way; and
> for that purpose I had all necessities conveyed to the post stations.

Gloss: *Qa’an*, Great Khan; *J̌aqut*, the translator's note says the people of north China; *post stations*, stops
with fresh horses for the khan's messengers. Limit: written for the khans' own family, it tells their deeds their way.

**Fact card (ours).** Temujin was proclaimed Chinggis Khan in 1206 and died in 1227; his armies took much of north
China (the Jin state fell in 1234, after his death) and, after the Khwarazm shah's side killed Mongol envoys sent
about trade, destroyed the cities of Khwarazm in Central Asia (1219 to 1221). Skilled captives from conquered cities
were carried off to work for the khans; Rubruck met one, a goldsmith from Paris, at Karakorum. Chinggis's grandson Hulegu sacked Baghdad in 1258. By the
1260s the empire had split into four khanates: the Golden Horde, the Chagatai khanate, the Ilkhanate in Persia, and
the Yuan in China, where Khubilai Khan ruled from 1260 to 1294, named his dynasty Yuan in 1271 and conquered the Song
by 1279. In 1287 the Ilkhan sent Rabban Sauma to Europe to seek an alliance against the Mamluks of Egypt. Yuan rule
ended in 1368, and the Ming dynasty followed.

Where it lies: Karakorum, the first capital, on the Mongolian steppe; Khanbaliq (today's Beijing), Khubilai's
capital in north China; Samarkand and Bukhara, in Khwarazm's lands of Central Asia; Baghdad, on the Tigris in
today's Iraq; Mosul, upstream on the Tigris.

**Source D. Marco Polo, how the Great Kaan makes paper pass for money, about 1298.** Written down by Rustichello of
Pisa from Polo's telling; translated by Henry Yule (3rd edition, 1903), book 2, chapter 24. Public domain.

> With these pieces of paper, made as I have described, he causes all payments on his own account to be made; and
> he makes them to pass current universally over all his kingdoms and provinces and territories, and whithersoever
> his power and sovereignty extends. And nobody, however important he may think himself, dares to refuse them on pain
> of death. And indeed everybody takes them readily, for wheresoever a person may go throughout the Great Kaan’s
> dominions he shall find these pieces of paper current, and shall be able to transact all sales and purchases of
> goods by means of them just as well as if they were coins of pure gold. ... Furthermore all merchants arriving from India or other countries, and bringing with them gold or
> silver or gems and pearls, are prohibited from selling to any one but the Emperor.

Gloss: *the Great Kaan*, Khubilai Khan; *pass current*, be accepted as money; *whithersoever*, wherever; *on pain of
death*, or be killed.

**Source E. Ibn al-Athir, the coming of the Mongols, about 1220 to 1231.** A historian in Mosul (in today's Iraq),
writing of events he did not see, from people who fled them; translated by Edward G. Browne (1906). Public domain.

> For indeed history doth not contain aught which approaches or comes nigh unto it.
>
> ... these [Tartars] spared none, slaying women and men and children ...
>
> But these Tartars conquered most of the habitable globe, and the best, the most flourishing and most populous part
> thereof, ... in about a year; nor did any country escape their devastations which did not fearfully expect them and
> dread their arrival.

Gloss: *doth not contain aught*, holds nothing; *nigh unto*, near; *habitable globe*, the lived-in world;
*devastations*, destruction.

**Source J. Mongke Khan's letter to King Louis IX of France, 1254.** Mongke was the Great Khan at Karakorum; at
his court Christians, Muslims and Buddhists debated before the Khan's secretaries (Rockhill, pp. 228 to 235). The
friar William of Rubruck wrote down the letter's sense "as well as I could understand through an interpreter";
translated by W. W. Rockhill (1900), pp. 248 to 249. Public domain.

> The commandment of the eternal God is, in Heaven there is only one eternal God, and on Earth there is only one
> lord, Chingis Chan, the Son of God ... those who shall have heard my commandments and understood them, and who
> shall not believe and shall make war against us, shall hear and see that they have eyes and see not ...

Gloss: *Chingis Chan*, Chinggis Khan; *eternal God*, Rubruck's words, through an interpreter, for Eternal Heaven
(Tengri), whom Mongol rulers worshipped.
