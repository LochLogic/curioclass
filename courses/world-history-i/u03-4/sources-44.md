# Day 44 sources: trade, faith or the sword?

Use these two sources with the day 42 option sheets in the discussion and in your closing claim. Both writers were visitors.
Ask of each: who is speaking, through whom, and for which readers?

**Source F. Mongke Khan speaks to William of Rubruck, Karakorum, 1254.** Mongke was the Great Khan at Karakorum
when Rubruck came; at his court Christians, Muslims and Buddhists debated before the Khan's secretaries (Rockhill,
pp. 228 to 235). Rubruck, a Franciscan friar sent from France, wrote his report for King Louis IX; the Khan's words
reached him through an interpreter. Translated by W. W. Rockhill (1900), pp. 235 to 236. Public domain.

> "We Moal," he said, "believe that there is only one God, by whom we live and by whom we die, and for whom we have an
> upright heart." ... "But as God gives us the different fingers of the hand, so he gives to men divers ways."

Gloss: *Moal*, Rubruck's spelling of Mongol; *one God*, Rubruck's words, through an interpreter, for Eternal Heaven
(Tengri), whom Mongol rulers worshipped; *divers*, different. Mongke's letter to King Louis is Source J on your
option d sheet: read it beside this.

**Source G. Ibn Battuta, justice and prayer in Mali, 1352 to 1353.** Dictated in 1355; translated by H. A. R. Gibb
(1929), pp. 323 to 335. Public domain.

> They are seldom unjust, and have a greater abhorrence of injustice
> than any other people. Their sultan shows no mercy to anyone who is guilty of the least act of it. There is
> complete security in their country. Neither traveller nor inhabitant in it has anything to fear from robbers or
> men of violence. ... They are careful to observe the hours of prayer, and assiduous in attending them in
> congregations, and in bringing up their children to them.

Gloss: *abhorrence*, hatred; *assiduous*, careful and steady; *congregations*, gatherings for prayer. In the same
chapter Ibn Battuta also condemns customs he thought wrong. His praise and his blame both measure Mali by his own
training; Mali's Muslims, scholars among them, practised Islam in their own way beside Mande customs.

**Card: from Chinggis to Khubilai, and the Silk Roads (ours).** Chinggis Khan, proclaimed in 1206, united the
steppe peoples and conquered by the sword; his heirs took north China (the Jin fell in 1234) and Baghdad (1258).
The empire split into four khanates by the 1260s. His grandson Khubilai (1215 to 1294) sought to rule, not only
conquer: he named his dynasty Yuan in 1271, set up a government for China, supported farming, trade and crafts, and
funded Buddhist, Confucian, Muslim and Christian houses of learning and worship, while his armies took south China
by 1279. Under Mongol rule merchants, envoys and
missionaries crossed the Silk Roads more safely than before, carrying silk, paper money, ideas and faiths, but the
same conquests had destroyed cities and killed many people.
