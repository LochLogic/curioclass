# Section 3.3 opener: picture maps, models and keys

## What the section does

Days 24 to 26 answer "How can pictures stand for places?" On day 24 children read a picture map of places near school beside a photograph, and learn that a small picture on a map stands for a real place and that a boxed key says what each picture means. On day 25 they build a block model of a few things seen in the classroom or playground and make a picture graph of the trees and rocks the class counted. On day 26 they revise the model and graph from written feedback and draw a first map of a familiar place with a picture key. Each day is 20 to 30 minutes, and nothing is read by children.

## How the days run

Day 24: you show an Our Place Record photograph of the playground beside a picture map of the same place drawn from above, and ask who took the photograph, when and why, and what the map leaves out. The key shows a heart for the playground, a hexagon for the library and a flag for the park. You trace from a symbol to the key and back, say "it stands for", and model a claim with because. Pairs place symbol cards and say what is next to what. Day 25: the class counts a small bounded patch of ground aloud. You make a two-row picture graph from a tray, one picture for each thing and a labeled place for none, and build a block model of the reading corner with a key card for each block type. Each child builds a small model of two or three seen features and makes a graph of the trees and rocks counted. These first products are practice. That evening you write one line of feedback on one feature, position or count for each child. Day 26: a teacher map with two similar key pictures shows what an unclear key is. Children revise the model and graph from the feedback (these revised records are scored), then draw a first map of a place from the class list, two features and their key pictures.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

A photograph shows what one camera saw from one spot. A map is drawn from above and leaves things out, so every map is a choice by its maker. A symbol stands for a place and is not the place (error C). A key is the maker's agreement about what each picture means, so a symbol's meaning is never guessed (error H). A block model is a small version made from what was seen: draw and build only what was seen, never an invented pond (error D). The graph has two groups, one picture for each counted thing and a labeled place with no marks for zero (error L). A claim names a detail after because (error G). The feedback chain: a first product is practice, it gets feedback on one feature, position or count, it is revised, and only then is it scored. No scale, no compass directions, no neatness marks. A place near school is chosen from the class list (playground, library, entrance, a place the class visited); never a home.

## The errors, in plain words

- C: "The symbol is the real place." Hold the printed symbol beside the real place or its photograph.
- H: "I can guess what a symbol means." Trace to the key and back.
- G: "It helps because it helps." Name a detail from the source.
- L: "A mark can mean any number." Count aloud against the marks; zero keeps a place.
- D: "A good map has what I like." Draw only what was seen.

## Supplies

Our Place Record photographs, picture maps of places near school with a boxed key and movable symbol cards, a tray with a few small things, blocks, blue paper, cloth or clay for a pond or hill that was seen, key cards for the block types, a pre-printed graph card with the row names, a page with a border and a boxed key area, crayons, a feedback card for each child. Tactile string for paths and borders, raised stickers for symbols, contrasting patterns and not color alone. A child who cannot draw small directs an adult to place pieces. Outdoor observation follows the school's own supervision policy; otherwise use a window view and the classroom model.

## A room with nothing

Room objects, chalk lines on the floor, hand-drawn key boxes on scrap paper.

## Exit tickets

All three are new cases printed "invented for practice" and never used in teaching. Every child marks a picture; the spoken part is heard from a rotating group of six each day.
