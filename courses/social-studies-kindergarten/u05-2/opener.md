# Section 5.2 opener: what symbols tell us, and how we know

## What the section does

Days 45 to 47 teach three national symbols and what the Pledge words mean, with the supporting question *What do symbols tell us, and how do we know?* Day 45 is the flag, day 46 the bald eagle and day 47 the national anthem. Each day also takes one group of Pledge words in plain meanings: day 45, a promise of loyalty to the country the flag stands for; day 46, one nation, together; day 47, freedom and fair treatment for everyone. The class explains the Pledge. Nobody is asked to say it, sing, stand or put a hand anywhere; a child who stands quietly is respected, and the school's own practice goes on as the school directs. Each day is 25 minutes and no child reads. The read-aloud is a bound book we made, "Symbols We Ask About"; each page is read aloud and each page's maker, time and purpose are said.

## How the days run

Each day reopens with one repair or retrieval from the day before, then you read the day's page. There are two picture bins: an old paper (the record says) and a speech bubble (people say it means). You say the dates aloud ("long, long ago, in 1777"); no date and no statement is printed on a child's page. Pairs place at most two statements in the bins with tokens, then ask the page one question and point to its answer. Hear the rotating six, one minute each. Every day ends with a picture mark and a spoken ask.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

A symbol is a picture or thing that stands for something, as the key on a map stands for a place. A flag stands for a country. The thirteen stripes are the number of the first states; each star stands for one state, fifty today. The law of 1777 gave no reason for the colors; people say what colors mean, and the colors were given meanings for the country's seal in 1782: red for courage, white for purity, blue for justice. Never say the law said red means bravery. The bald eagle was picked for the Great Seal in 1782 and lives only in North America. Francis Scott Key wrote the words of the anthem in 1814 after seeing the flag still flying; a law made the song the anthem in 1931. A song does not become the anthem by being liked. Saying the Pledge is a choice (the Supreme Court said so in 1943), and fair treatment is for everyone.

## The errors, in plain words

- C: "The stripes are the states we have now." Today's stars are the states now; the stripes are the first ones.
- J: "The law said red means bravery." The old law gave no reason; later people say what the colors mean.
- B, D, I: a symbol is more than a pretty picture; leaders picked the eagle and the page says so; ask a question, then find the page.
- K, E, L, N: a law chose the anthem; learning the words and saying them are different; fair treatment is for everyone; say what the words mean, not only the words.

A puppet says the wrong idea. A child's answer is never the example.

## Supplies

The book "Symbols We Ask About" (teaching pages only), the two sort bins, tokens, a practice flag, puppets, and the record sheet. A raised-line flag and seal, with spoken descriptions, for a child who cannot see; a flag shown by pattern as well as color.

## A room with nothing

A flag drawn on chart paper with thirteen stripes, a traced eagle, two bins from scrap paper and folded-paper puppets.

## Exit tickets

Every exit is a new case printed "invented for practice", with its own pictures, never used in teaching. Every child marks a picture; the spoken part is heard from a rotating group of six each day. The marking scale is one: shown, shown with help, not yet seen.
