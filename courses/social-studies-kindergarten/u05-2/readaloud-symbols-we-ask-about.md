# Symbols We Ask About: teaching pages

A bound picture book we made, read aloud. These are the teaching pages for days 45, 46 and 47 only. Exit pages are in the day 45 to 47 exit cards and assessment pages are in their own file, kept apart. Large pictures, one or two sentences a page, no date and no full statement printed for a child. Say on every page who made it (us, in 2026), and why (to help us ask a page a question). The marking scale on the class sheet is one: shown, shown with help, not yet seen. One actor in each scene. A puppet speaks one line at a time.

## Page 1 (day 45): the flag

A large print of the whole flag on a wall: thirteen stripes, red and white, and a block of stars on blue. Two stickers: *first states* by the stripes, *states now* by the stars. Read: "This is a symbol. A symbol is a picture or thing that stands for something. The flag stands for our country. Long, long ago, in 1777, a law said the flag has thirteen stripes. Thirteen is the number of the first states. In 1818 another law said to add a star for each new state. Today there are fifty stars, one for each state." Name the sources: the 1777 resolution (as quoted in a reference article), the 1818 act (the Statutes at Large) and a reference article on the flag, all read 2026-11-19.

**The sort (two statements).** Put "long, long ago, in 1777, a law said thirteen stripes, red and white" in the old paper bin. Put "people say red means being brave" in the speech bubble bin. Say: the old law gave no reason for the colors; people say what colors mean. Say only red aloud: people say red means being brave. The other colors are not taught here, and nothing about the colors is printed. Never say the law said red means bravery.

**Ask the page.** Model: "My question for the page is: what does a star stand for?" Point to the stars and the sticker and read the answer.

**Pledge begun.** A picture of a child and a heart with a flag behind it. Read: "Pledge means a promise. 'Pledge allegiance' is a promise of loyalty to the country the flag stands for. We explain these words. We do not say them as a class." Source: the Pledge text on a federal civics page, read 2026-11-19.

## Page 2 (day 46): the bald eagle

Our drawing of a bald eagle with a round seal behind it, and a card of an old paper dated 1782. Read: "Long, long ago, in 1782, leaders picked the bald eagle for the Great Seal. The bald eagle lives only in North America." Source: the report of 1782 in the State Department's Great Seal booklet and the Fish and Wildlife Service bald eagle page.

**The sort.** "Leaders picked it for the seal" in the old paper bin. "People say the eagle shows strength" in the speech bubble bin.

**Ask the page.** Model: "Who picked this bird?" Find the answer on the page and point to it. Each child then asks the page one question about the eagle and points to its page.

**Pledge continued.** A picture of many children standing together inside one big outline of a country. Read: "One nation, together. The words mean one country, together, not split apart."

## Page 3 (day 47): the anthem

Our drawing of a fort at sunrise with a big flag still flying over it, a ship far off on the water, and a man on the ship with a paper and pencil. Read: "In 1814 a man named Francis Scott Key watched a battle near a fort. In the morning he saw the big flag still flying. He wrote words about it. Much later, in 1931, a law made the song our national anthem. The class does not sing it." Sources: the National Park Service page on the anthem and a reference article, read 2026-11-19. Say that a puppet's guess is not a source.

## Page 4 (day 47): the Pledge's last words

A picture of three children sharing one box of paints and a smaller picture of one child standing quietly to one side, with an adult next to the box saying "everyone is welcome". Read: "Liberty and justice for all means freedom and fair treatment for everyone. Saying the Pledge is a choice. A child who stands quietly is respected." Source: the Supreme Court said in 1943 that a school cannot make a child say it. Do not read the court's words to children.

## Puppet wrong ideas for day 47 (the class answers from the page)

The puppet says: "The song everybody cheered at the game is our anthem." The class answers: a law chose the anthem. The puppet says: "Only children who say the words may use the art table." The class answers: fair treatment is for everyone. The puppet says: "I can say it fast, so I know what it means." The class answers: say what it means in your own words.

## Key (teacher)

Stripes: the first thirteen states. Stars: one for each state, fifty today. Colors: the law gave no reason; people say what they mean. Eagle: leaders picked it for the Great Seal in 1782; it lives only in North America. Anthem: Key wrote the words in 1814; a law made it the anthem in 1931.
