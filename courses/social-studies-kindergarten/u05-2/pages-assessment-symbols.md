# Symbols We Ask About: assessment pages (reserved for day 50)

The assessment pages of the bound book, kept apart from the teaching and exit pages and not shown before day 50. Printed "invented for practice" where a case is made up. The teacher reads every prompt aloud and supplies no explanation. The marking scale on the class sheet is one: shown, shown with help, not yet seen. A child is heard on one symbol in full and gives a one-sentence why for each of the others; an unheard part is recorded not yet observed.

## The pages

1. **Parade flag.** A new drawing of a flag held by a child at a pretend parade, shown for E6 to E8: "Tell what the stripes are for." "Tell what the stars are for." "What do the colors stand for?" Follow up separately for any missing feature.
2. **Open music book.** A new picture card of an open music book labeled The Star-Spangled Banner, for E9: "How did these words become our country's anthem?"
3. **Flag beside a speech bubble.** A new original picture, for E10. The teacher reads the current Pledge in short phrases and says saying it is a choice: "What do these words mean?" The child is not asked to say it or for each phrase.
4. **Question page.** After the child's full explanation: "Ask a question about one symbol you have already explained. Let us find its page. What does this page help you answer?" Read the page the child's question leads to, adding no explanation.
5. **The Great Seal Hand Press photograph** (E5) is a federal museum photograph kept in its own envelope: National Museum of American Diplomacy, early 1900s, a hand press diplomats used to emboss the Great Seal on official papers. Read the museum, the date and the use aloud; do not give the eagle's reason; do not ask how the press works. Confirm before printing that the eagle is legible; if it is not, use a second public-domain Great Seal image from a federal page.

## Key (teacher)

E5: leaders chose the bald eagle, a bird of North America, for the Great Seal, and this press uses the seal. E6: thirteen stripes, like the first thirteen states. E7: one star for each state, fifty today. E8: the law gave no reason for the colors; people say what they mean. E9: Key wrote the words in 1814 after seeing the flag still flying, and much later, in 1931, a law made the song the anthem; no date is needed. E10: a promise to be loyal to one country, together, with freedom and fair treatment for everyone. E3 and E4: a relevant question and an answer from the page, with the child pointing to the evidence.
