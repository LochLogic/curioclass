# Section 2.3 opener: what story characters show us

## What the section does

Days 15 and 16 answer "What do story characters show us?" Children listen to two stories and point to what a character did: who is fair, caring, just, responsible or in charge, and where a rule is made and where it is followed. Each day is 20 to 30 minutes, and nothing is read by children.

## How the days run

Day 15: teach fair, caring, just (putting an unfair thing right) and rule with pictures, read The Paper Workshop (six scenes) with a pause at each, model pointing with faces covered, then pairs hold a scene card and say "The ___ did ___. I know because ___." Day 16: teach responsible (does what she said) and promise, tell The Lion and the Mouse (Aesop, retold in our words), ask "What did the mouse say she would do? Did she do it?", then go back to the workshop for in charge and for the rule scenes laid side by side: one makes the rule, one follows it.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

Characters show their conduct by what they do, not by how they look (error D). A rule is made when people agree; it is followed when someone acts under it (error E). Just means putting an unfair thing right. In charge means someone whose job it is to decide for the group, like a teacher; a strong character is not necessarily in charge. Name each source's maker and purpose every time: these are made-up or very old stories, and our own records (Our Jobs Chart, Our Decision Record) are different. Fable attribution is stated plainly: Aesop, old, told by many.

## The errors, in plain words

- D: "The biggest or prettiest one is the good one." Cover faces, point to an action.
- E: "Following the rule is making it." Lay the two scenes side by side.

## Supplies

The two stories, two sets of six scene cards (twelve), word pictures, crayons. Scenes can be held, pointed to and shown by touch (raised outlines).

## A room with nothing

Stick-figure scenes on scrap paper, told aloud; the fable told from the page text with a hand-drawn card for each part.

## About the fable

The lion's hold on the mouse is told calmly, with a pause after it, and is never acted out. The lion is not shown as a rightful leader. The moral, "A kindness is never wasted," is the old story's own.

## Exit tickets

Both are new cases printed "invented for practice" and never used again (the spare apron; the class book). The spoken "why" is heard from a rotating group of six.
