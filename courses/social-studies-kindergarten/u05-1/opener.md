# Section 5.1 opener: days we remember, and whom they honor

## What the section does

Days 41 to 44 open the unit "Days we remember", whose question is *Why do we remember some days and some people?* The section has four days, each 20 to 30 minutes. Days 41, 42 and 43 are taught on the day itself, weeks apart: Indigenous Peoples' Day or Columbus Day as your community observes it, Veterans Day, and Martin Luther King Jr. Day. Day 44 adds Presidents' Day, puts the four holidays on the school-year strip, and adds three picture cards: Independence Day, Memorial Day and Juneteenth. No child reads. Everything you teach is a picture card or a page read aloud, and the child answers by pointing, circling, sorting, drawing or speaking.

## How the days run

Each day shows one holiday card from the wall calendar and asks who or what the day helps us remember. On day 41 you read the day's name as your school's calendar names it and say that some places use another name. You show a card about the Aquinnah Wampanoag, who live today on Martha's Vineyard in Massachusetts, with their name said as they say it and their page credited. On day 42 you read "A Thank-You Wall" (an invented class), say what a veteran is, and tell what the record says about the harvest meal of 1621. On day 43 two pages show a pretend town where a posted rule keeps some people apart, and neighbors working together to change a rule. On day 44 you read "Two Presidents" and the seven cards go on the strip, earlier on the left. Every day ends with a picture mark that every child makes and a spoken ask heard from a rotating six.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

A civic holiday honors people or events. To honor is to show thanks and respect and to learn what the people did. A day off is only sometimes true and is never the purpose; say so plainly and without scorn, because many children have no school that day. Praise ("they were the best") is not a reason; a detail from the page is. Veterans Day thanks everyone who served in the armed forces; Memorial Day remembers people who died while serving. The law names the day Washington's Birthday; many people call it Presidents' Day. The law names the second Monday in October Columbus Day; many places observe Indigenous Peoples' Day instead or as well. Say the sources aloud: who made it, when, and why.

## Care

Native nations are named by their own names, in the present tense, and no one acts out Native people, the harvest meal or the separated people of the unfair-law page; the pictures are looked at, never acted. The unfair laws are named plainly at kindergarten level: some laws kept Black and white people apart. No child is asked what their family does on a day, who in the family served, or what the family believes. A child may pass.

## The errors, in plain words

- A: "Every holiday is just a day off." Set a day-off picture beside the card and ask what the card remembers.
- G: "We remember them because they were the best." Say a praise sentence and a detail sentence from the page side by side; the class picks the one the page supports.
- H: "Memorial Day is for all the veterans." Veterans Day thanks everyone who served; Memorial Day remembers those who died while serving.

A puppet or a teacher card says the wrong idea. A child's answer is never the example.

## Supplies

The wall calendar and a today marker, large picture cards, a paper strip, Our Holiday Cards (one card a holiday, kept on the wall), a record sheet, crayons and tokens. Large print and high contrast on every card; raised-line cards for a child who cannot see, with each picture described.

## A room with nothing

Hand-drawn cards on scrap paper, a calendar page, a folded sheet for the strip, and the nation's card read aloud with one sketch.

## Exit tickets

Every exit is a new case printed "invented for practice" and never used in teaching. Every child marks a picture; the spoken part is heard from a rotating group of six each day, and the rest are recorded not yet heard. The marking scale is one: shown, shown with help, not yet seen.
