# Section 6.3 opener: symbols, words and songs

## What the section does

Four days, 62 to 65, of 30 minutes. Unit 6 asks: what holds a country of so many different people together? Day 62: four national symbols and the record of what each one is, kept apart from the meanings people give it later: the flag, the eagle on the Great Seal, the White House. Day 63: the Statue of Liberty, a gift from France dedicated in 1886, and its limit as a sign of welcome. Day 64: the motto "E pluribus unum" (Out of many, one) and the words of the Pledge, and that saying the Pledge is a choice. Day 65: two songs, "America the Beautiful" and "The Star-Spangled Banner", their tunes, words and what a short excerpt means.

## How the days run

Day 62: return and repair (5), retrieval (3), teaching (7), supported work (9), exit (3), look back (3). Day 63: return and repair (5), teaching (8), timeline (4), supported work (8), exit (3), look back (2). Day 64: return and repair (5), teaching (8), supported work (10), exit (3), look back (4). Day 65: return and repair (5), teaching (8), supported work (10), exit (3), hum and listen (4). Every child marks the exit card; the teacher hears the spoken part from one rotating six.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A symbol stands for something else.** It is not the thing. The record says what it is; people add meanings. Say both, and keep them in two bins.
- **The flag:** thirteen stripes for the first states, a star for each state, fifty stars today (sources 62B, 62E).
- **The Great Seal** was adopted in 1782. Its eagle holds an olive branch and arrows: peace and the power to defend (62A, which calls it the American Eagle). The bald eagle has been the national bird by law since December 23, 2024 (62D). The seal's banner carries "E pluribus unum".
- **The White House** is the home and workplace of the president. It stands for the president's work (62C).
- **The Statue of Liberty** was a gift from France, dedicated October 28, 1886. Her torch stands for liberty and enlightenment. A poem on its base, written by Emma Lazarus, welcomes newcomers (63A, 63B). It is a hope of welcome and not proof that everyone was treated the same: for example, in 1882 a law kept Chinese workers from coming for ten years (63C).
- **"E pluribus unum"** is Latin for "Out of many, one". For the Great Seal it joins the thirteen states into one nation (62A). People also say it means many people, one country.
- **The Pledge** is read once as a text (64A). Its words are about loyalty to the country and liberty and justice for all. Saying it is a choice: in 1943 the Supreme Court decided in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette that students cannot be forced to salute the flag or say it (64B). "Under God" is part of the text; people believe different things and no one is asked to agree.
- **The songs:** Katharine Lee Bates wrote the words of "America the Beautiful" after a trip in 1893 (65A). Francis Scott Key wrote "The Star-Spangled Banner" in 1814, and a law made it the national anthem in 1931 (65B). Lyrics are public domain. Singing is a choice; any child may listen. No score is printed in this course; the adult hums the tune.
- **Held out of teaching:** the exit pictures and phrases, and the end packet's views and phrases. The cards for each day say only what that day teaches.

## The errors, in plain words

- **D:** naming a symbol explains its meaning. Link a meaning to a detail.
- **K:** the statue proves everyone was welcomed the same. Say the hope and say the limit.
- **C and E:** belonging needs the same beliefs; belonging needs saying the words. Saying the Pledge is a choice.

A wrong answer is voiced by Pip the puppet or shown on a teacher card, never by a named child.

## Care

Nobody is asked to say the Pledge, stand, sing or hum. The teacher says on each of days 62 to 65: "Saying or singing is a choice. You may listen." Immigrant stories are told through the statue and the poem, not through any child's family. No child is asked where their family came from.
