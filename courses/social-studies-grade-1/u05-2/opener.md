# Section 5.2 opener: capitals, why places grew, continents, oceans and waters

## What the section does

Four days, 48 to 51, of 30 minutes. Unit 5 asks: why did people build towns and cities where they did? Day 48: a capital is the city where a government meets, for the nation (Washington, D.C.) and for our state; it is a city, not a building, and not always the biggest. Day 49: a harbor, a river crossing and a seat of government each helped a place grow, and a map and an account are used together; the Lenape people, whose nations live today, were here before the ships came. Day 50: continents and oceans are met by name on a globe and a world map, and our town, state and country sit inside one another. Day 51: rivers, lakes and mountains, and why people settle near water.

## How the days run

Day 48: return and repair (5), teaching (11), supported work (10), share (2), exit (2). Day 49: return and repair (5), teaching (14), supported work (9), exit (2). Day 50: return and repair (5), teaching (11), supported work (9), share (3), exit (2). Day 51: reopen (5), teaching (11), supported work (9), share (3), exit (2). Every child marks the exit card; the teacher hears the spoken part from one rotating six.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A capital is a city where a state or nation's government is centered.** The building where officials meet is in the capital; it is not the capital. The biggest city in a state is often not the capital.
- **A map suggests a reason; an account supports it; some things neither says.** A harbor shows where ships can stop. The Seaport account says ships brought goods, and travelers came by ship. Neither one says who lived here first; the Lenape nations' own pages do.
- **Lenape people lived here before the ships, and Lenape nations live today.** Colonists took land, and many Lenape people were forced away. The land was never empty and the removal was never voluntary. Nations are named by their own names: the Delaware Nation, the Delaware Tribe of Indians and the Stockbridge-Munsee Community. We read about them and never act them out.
- **A continent is a very large land area; an ocean is a huge connected body of salt water.** The count taught in the United States is seven continents and five oceans. Naming and placing them is grade 2's. Today children point only to ours.
- **A river flows from higher land toward lower water; a lake has land all around it; a mountain is very high land.** People settle near water because they need it. A map shows where water is; it does not say the water is safe to drink.

## The errors, in plain words

- **G and L:** the biggest city is the capital; the capital is a building. Put the star by the caption; circle a building in a city.
- **F, H, K and M:** a map alone proves the reason; no one was here first; shops grew because people like shops; the Lenape are gone. Join a map clue to a record detail; read the nations' accounts again; point to the clue first.
- **Q, D and E:** a state is a country; a continent is a country; blue shapes are all rivers. Use nested cards and picture cards.
- **J:** the map proves the water is safe. A map shows where water is.

A wrong answer is voiced by Pip the puppet or shown on a teacher card, never by a named child. On day 49 the sentence "Are the Lenape gone?" is read once, as a question, and answered at once from the nations' own accounts.

## Care

A sensitivity read of day 49 is owed before it is taught. No settler or Native role play, no costume, no invented speech for Native people, no guessing about any child's family. No child's address is asked. A child's own state is found on the state's government page by the teacher.

The United States locator and state outline cards, the Harbor Map and its Lenape layer, the two read-alouds, claim cards and tokens, the Cattail Crossing card, nested place cards and world views, the Heron Valley map and a low paper relief, word label strips, the printed exit cards, the repair cards and the unit 5 record sheet.

A hand-drawn state outline with a star and a dot; a hand-drawn river, lake and mountain; the spoken read-alouds.
