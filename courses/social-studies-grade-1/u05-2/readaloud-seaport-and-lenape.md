# Day 49 read-alouds: the harbor and the Lenape nations

Read these aloud; no child reads them. Before each, say what the source is, who made it, when, why, and how it can help. The words below are ours. No source is quoted. A sensitivity read of this piece is owed before day 49. No role play, no costume, no invented speech.

## Read-aloud 1: the seaport (about 70 words)

*Source to name:* a museum's page about its seaport, made by the museum, to tell the history of the harbor. It helps because it says what the harbor was used for. It does not say who lived here first.

"New York has a harbor. A harbor is a sheltered place by the water where ships can stop. New York has a natural harbor, so ships can stop there. Long ago, ships came to the harbor and docked beside warehouses and markets. They brought goods from many places, and travelers came by ship too. That is one reason a city grew beside the harbor."

*Picture cards to match:* a ship, a dock, a warehouse and a market square, each with a one-word label.

## Read-aloud 2: the Lenape nations (about 110 words)

*Source to name:* the nations' own pages, made by the nations, to tell their own history in their own words. They help because Lenape people tell about their own homelands.

"Lenape people lived in this place long before the ships came. Their homelands include the land around New York Bay, which they call Manahatta, and villages along the rivers and the coast. Later, colonists took land, and over many years many Lenape people were forced to move far away, to the west. Many were forced to leave. Lenape nations live today. They are the Delaware Nation, the Delaware Tribe of Indians and the Stockbridge-Munsee Community, which includes Munsee people, who are Lenape. Each tells its own history on its own pages."

*Say the name:* Manahatta, Man-uh-HAT-uh. After the sentence "Many were forced to leave," say: "That was not fair." Stop for a breath, then ask the question.

*Picture cards to match:* the word "Manahatta" on a card; a map of arrows leading west labeled "forced to move"; three name cards, one for each nation, each labeled with the nation's own name.

## If a child asks, "Are the Lenape gone?"

Read the question aloud once, as a question for the whole class, and answer at once: "No. The Lenape nations live today, and they tell their own histories." Point to the three name cards. Do not guess about any child's family. Do not act out anyone.

## Teacher key

The harbor account tells why ships and docks mattered; the map shows where the harbor and docks are. The nations' accounts tell who lived here first, what became of their homelands and that they live today. Nothing here names a village site; the map marks only the trail and the name, both approximate. Three nations are named because the sources name them; there are other Lenape nations too.
