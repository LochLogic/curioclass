# Section 6.2 opener: united and different

## What the section does

Three days, 59 to 61, of 30 minutes. Unit 6 asks: what holds a country of so many different people together? Day 59: how Americans are united, through a shared history, the Constitution, laws and shared holidays, and the idea that neighbors can share these and still celebrate in their own ways. Day 60: how Americans are diverse, in languages, birthplaces, beliefs and traditions, and the Native nations who were here first and live here now, with the Cherokee Nation's own pages. Day 61: a claim joined to a detail, "The source shows ___. This supports ___ because ___."

## How the days run

Day 59: return and repair (5), retrieval (3), teaching (7), supported work (10), exit (3), look back (2). Day 60: return and repair (5), teaching (9), supported work (10), exit (3), look back (3). Day 61: return and repair (5), model a sourced claim (5), supported work (12), exit (3), co-write (5). Every child marks the exit card; the teacher hears the spoken part from one rotating six.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **United means joined together as one.** Americans share a national history (the Declaration of Independence, dated July 4, 1776, and the Constitution, signed in September 1787), a Constitution that says how the government is set up, and laws, which are rules for everyone in a place. Sources 59A and 59B.
- **Neighbors need not celebrate alike.** A shared holiday remembers one event; each neighbor keeps it in their own way.
- **Diverse means different from one another** in languages, birthplaces, beliefs and traditions. Beliefs are described and never practised.
- **A count shows some things and not others.** A ten-square strip shows how many neighbors have a mark, "some" or "many" (many is more than three squares of ten). It cannot show what any one neighbor believes, and it is never used to guess a person's belief or citizenship.
- **Native nations were here first and live here now.** The Cherokee Nation is a nation with its own government, constitution and laws, and a language department that works to keep the Cherokee language (sources 60A and 60C). In 1838 the United States sent soldiers to force the Cherokee people from their homelands; it was not a choice (source 60B). The teacher says this plainly in two sentences and adds nothing graphic. Kindergarten's Wampanoag in Massachusetts is a review.
- **Not everyone came by choice.** Enslaved Africans were brought here by force and kept from the freedoms the country promised. Say it plainly in one sentence if a child asks how neighbors came; never ask a child to guess.
- **Some passages are held** for the exit and the end items. Teach only what each day lists.
- **Every town is invented** (Hazel Town, Holly Run, Rowan Town, Tansy Town) and the teacher says so.

## The errors, in plain words

- **C:** belonging needs the same cake, the same hats, the same words. Compare shared government with different languages.
- **F:** Native nations are gone. Read the nation's own account of today.
- **J:** everyone arrived by choice. Name forced removal plainly.
- **L:** a number tells what a person believes. A strip cannot.
- **G:** "I like it" is evidence. Point to a detail on the card.
- **M:** one detail shows both sides. Name a separate detail for each.

A wrong answer is voiced by Pip the puppet or shown on a teacher card, never by a named child.

## Care

No child is asked about their language, where they were born, their beliefs, their family or their citizenship. A made-up town does the asking. The Cherokee Nation is named by its own name, in the present tense. Show no costume picture. No reenactment, no invented speech for a Cherokee person.
