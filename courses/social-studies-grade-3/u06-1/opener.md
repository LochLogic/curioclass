# Section 6.1 opener: why independence, and what the writers believed

## What the section does

Three days of 40 minutes. Day 125 opens the unit question, "What does it mean that government needs the consent of the governed?", with a hook, the word consent, who else cares, the six supporting questions and a four-box record. Day 126 explains why the colonists felt treated unfairly and places the Declaration on the strip. Day 127 reads the equality and consent lines.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **The Declaration is a letter of reasons.** A committee of the colonists' leaders, with Thomas Jefferson as the drafter, wrote it, and Congress adopted it on July 4, 1776 (archives.gov, sources.csv U6B). It gave reasons to the king and the wider world. Read it only in short pieces.
- **Consent means agreeing to how decisions are made**, by voting or by choosing people to speak. It does not mean liking every law (I) and it is not kindness (Q). A government that no one agreed to has no consent (T).
- **The colonists wanted a voice, not no rules** (C). The taxation line is "imposing Taxes on us without our Consent" (U6A). The 1774 harbor card, on the exit, is a second example of unfair treatment (U6Q).
- **Who "all" meant.** In 1776 many people, including enslaved people and women, were not treated as equal. Say it plainly on day 127; day 130 asks who.
- **Symbols.** A flag stands for the country; a chair does not (A). An eagle's meaning comes from its caption, not from naming the bird (B).
- **No room performance.** The room does not sing, salute or recite. Learning about a symbol is enough.

## The errors in plain words

A is thinking a school chair is a national symbol. B is saying "that is an eagle" and calling it a meaning. C is thinking the colonists wanted no rules. H is spacing cards evenly whatever the years. N is thinking only rulers care about the question. O is thinking rights are gifts a ruler can take back. Q is thinking consent means a kind leader. T is thinking whoever is in charge needs no agreement.

## Unsettled facts for the day writers

- Maker and date of the Declaration: a committee of leaders; adopted July 4, 1776 (U6B). Audience is worded "the king and the wider world".
- The 1774 harbor card is an original summary of an act named in the Resolves (U6Q). Do not name the act more exactly than "a law that closed the harbor of Boston to shipping".
- Do not read the Declaration's grievance about dissolved Houses or the passage on altering a government; both are in packet A on day 135. Never read the frontier grievance.
- Interim review: unit 2's two ways to be fair and unit 5's source-sourcing are named as earlier work; a day writer claims them as review only if the ledger lists them at stage written, otherwise teaches them as new and lists that. Grade 1 national symbols are tested by the readiness page, not claimed.
