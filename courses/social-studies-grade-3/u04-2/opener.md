# Section 4.2 opener: whose homelands are here, and why people settled

## What the section does

Two days of 40 minutes. Day 75 names the three Native nations of our region by their own names: the Penobscot Nation, the Narragansett Indian Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah). Children learn a three-mark map legend and use it. The homeland is shaded, a square marks a place the nation holds today, and a state border is another map. Day 76 teaches a chain: feature, practice, place. People did something, so they lived near something.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **Each nation is named by its own name** and lives and governs today. State names (Maine, Rhode Island, Massachusetts) are set apart from nation names.
- **A homeland is where the nation's own page puts it.** A state border is another map. The shading is approximate and has no hard edge.
- **A square marks a place the nation holds today.** It does not cover the whole homeland.
- **A feature and a practice together explain a place.** The river alone did not decide. People used the river for food and travel, so they lived near it. A seasonal camp is a place people stay in one season.
- **Our regional pages say only what they say.** The Narragansett Early History page speaks of a winter home and a summer home on the shore. Where a page is silent, the child writes "the page does not say".

## The errors in plain words

A is naming a state as a nation. B is taking a state line for the homeland edge. T is taking the whole shading for lands held today. C is saying the river decided. L is listing a feature with no practice. J is saying every nation does the same.

## Supplies

Three nation cards, the three-mark legend card, a fresh nation-free legend map, the Penobscot teaching map, the chain strip, two page summaries (Narragansett, Penobscot), the exit maps and the Alder Bend card.

## A room with nothing

Draw the legend on the board. Read the three nation cards aloud. Children point to a shaded patch, a square and a border on a hand-drawn map. A drawn arrow or a spoken chain is a full answer.
