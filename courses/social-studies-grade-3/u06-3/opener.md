# Section 6.3 opener: who was left out, three questions and what symbols promise

## What the section does

Three days of 40 minutes. Day 130 shows voting was set by each state, teaches the New Jersey rule and its end in 1807, reads a short account of Elizabeth Freeman, and names the three questions: citizenship, voting and equal rights. Day 131 sorts fresh cases into the three questions. Day 132 reads five symbol captions and asks whether a symbol proves everyone was free.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **Voting was set by each state.** In most states the voters were white men over 21 who owned property (Massachusetts framework, sources.csv U6G). Enslaved people were not voters and were enslaved. Say plainly that many people were left out.
- **New Jersey was different for a time.** The 1776 constitution gave the vote to "all inhabitants" of full age with enough property who had lived in their county a year; some women and free Black men voted. In 1807 the legislature limited the vote to white male citizens who paid taxes (U6F). Do not give a count of voters.
- **Three questions.** Citizenship is whether a person is counted as a member; voting is who may vote; equal rights is who is treated the same under the law. A denial of the vote does not settle citizenship (D).
- **Native nations govern themselves.** A nation's own decision is not a state election (U). The Oneida Indian Nation is named by its own name, in the present tense, from its own page (U6H). No nation, village or leader is made up.
- **Elizabeth Freeman** (U6J): born into slavery about 1744, she went to a Massachusetts court and a jury decided for her in 1781 (no birthplace or place of enslavement is printed; U6J gives none). Perseverance is something a person does (S). Never tell the overheard story or any abuse. The page says its details come from white voices; say that once.
- **Symbols show an ideal; a dated record shows what happened** (F). Two ways to study a symbol, and no one has to salute, sing or recite (L; Barnette 1943, U6S). The Liberty Bell was named in 1835 by an anti-slavery publication (U6K); the Statue of Liberty was dedicated in 1886 with a broken shackle at its foot, and its meaning was not yet real for African Americans then (U6L).
- **The anthem's adoption year is cut** (not on the page, U6M). The 1777 flag resolution is read from the Journals of the Continental Congress, quoted on a last-resort page (U6O).

## The errors in plain words

D is thinking no vote means not a citizen. F is thinking a symbol proves everyone was free. J is thinking no woman ever voted. L is thinking everyone must salute or sing. S is thinking a virtue is a feeling or a title. U is thinking a nation governing itself means its members voted in state elections. B is thinking a monument's name explains it.

Paper, pencils, the strip with the New Jersey band, three-question pockets, caption cards, the five-row symbol card with a word bank. Cards: cards-day130.md, cards-day131.md, cards-day132.md; exit cards: cards-exits-130-132.md; key (teacher only): key-exits-130-132.md.

## A room with nothing

Copy the cards large by hand. Use three paper pockets labeled citizenship, voting and equal rights, and a fourth for "none of the three".

## Unsettled facts for the day writers

- Packet C's New Jersey account is held for day 135; teaching uses a free Black man in 1802 and a woman in 1810, both made up. Packet C has no invented persons.
- Day 130 and 131 are the sensitivity days: who was left out is stated plainly; no child is asked about home, family, money or religion; no student role play.
- Interim review: grade 2 country reports are named as earlier work; claim it only if the ledger lists it at stage written, otherwise teach as new and list.
