# Cards for day 130

Print "made up, for practice" on every invented card. The teacher reads each card aloud once. Sources: Massachusetts framework (U6G), New Jersey Historical Commission (U6F), Oneida Indian Nation (U6H), National Park Service (U6J).

## Voting was set by each state

Each state set its own rules. In most states the voters were white men over 21 who owned property. Enslaved people were not voters, and they were enslaved. Many people were left out.

## A nation's own path

Maker: the Oneida Indian Nation, on its own page, which is there today. The Haudenosaunee Grand Council let each Nation choose its own path. The Oneida Indian Nation is a nation today and tells its history in its own words.

## New Jersey

New Jersey wrote its own voting law in 1776. It said "all inhabitants" who were grown, had goods or land worth fifty pounds and had lived in the county twelve months could vote. It did not say man or white. Some women and Black men who met those rules did vote. A law of 1807 changed that: only white men who were citizens and paid taxes could vote.

Two cards (made up, for practice):
- Isaac, a free Black man, 1802. He is of full age, owns land worth more than fifty pounds and has lived in his county for two years.
- Hannah, 1810. She is of full age and owns land worth more than fifty pounds.

Who could vote? Say why, and what changed in 1807. On the strip, draw a band from 1776 to 1807 under 1787 and 1791 (item [[s06-P130b]]).

## Elizabeth Freeman (National Park Service)

Elizabeth Freeman was born into slavery about 1744. In 1781 she and a man named Brom went to a Massachusetts court, and a jury decided that they were free. She took the name Elizabeth Freeman, later did paid work and had her own home. Note for the teacher: the park's page says some details come from white voices. Ask: what did she do? Perseverance is something a person does. (Item: [[s06-P130c]])

## Three questions (student page)

Citizenship: is a person counted as a member? Voting: who may vote? Equal rights: who is treated the same under the law? One worked case each (made up, for practice):
- Citizenship: Omar moved to the state as a child in 1796 and was counted as one of its people.
- Voting: a 1794 rule says whoever has lived ten years in the county may vote.
- Equal rights: a 1797 rule lets some neighbors use the town well free and makes others pay.

Write one line for each question. Item: [[s06-P130a]]
