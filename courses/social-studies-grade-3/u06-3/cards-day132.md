# Cards for day 132

The teacher reads each caption aloud once. No child has to sing, salute or recite; learning about a symbol is enough (Barnette, 1943). Sources: National Park Service (U6K, U6L, U6M, U6N); Journals of the Continental Congress, quoted on a last-resort page (U6O).

## Five captions (4, 4, 3, 3 and 3 minutes)

1. Liberty Bell. Ordered in 1751 for the State House in Philadelphia. It rang to call lawmakers together. Later, people working to end slavery took inspiration from it, and in 1835 an anti-slavery publication named it the Liberty Bell. Later meaning, not the first purpose.
2. Statue of Liberty. A gift from France, first proposed by a French abolitionist, dedicated in 1886. A broken shackle and chain lie at its foot. In 1886 its meaning was not yet real for African Americans, as the Black press said.
3. National anthem. Francis Scott Key wrote the words after the September 1814 attack on Fort McHenry, when he saw a huge flag.
4. Independence Day. Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence on July 4, 1776. Americans have celebrated the day since.
5. The 1777 flag resolution. On June 14, 1777 Congress resolved that the flag of the thirteen United States be thirteen stripes, alternate red and white, and that the union be thirteen stars, white in a blue field, representing a new constellation.

## The talk

Does a symbol prove everyone was free? Frame: "The symbol says ___; the record says ___." For the Statue: the symbol says liberty; the 1886 record says it was not yet real for African Americans. Two ways to study a symbol: read its caption, or draw it and say what it stands for. Anyone may decline to sing, salute or recite.

## Five-row symbol card, fresh captions (student page)

All five captions are made up, for practice.
1. The Pine Ford Bell, ordered in 1850 to call townspeople to meetings.
2. The Pine Ford banner, 1900: a blue wave for the river.
3. Pine Ford Day, every May since 1860, remembers the first town meeting.
4. The Pine Ford song, written in 1920, is about the river.
5. The Pine Ford charter, 1840: a paper listing the town's rules.

Put the five rows in order by the year each was made, oldest first. Pointing and dictation count. Item: [[s06-P132a]]
