# Section 5.7 opener: fishing, ships, trade and ports

## What the section does

Four days of 40 minutes. Day 113: fishing, shipbuilding and barter, with a link card. Day 114: Atlantic and Caribbean trade, including the trade in enslaved people, on a simplified diagram. Day 115: how two real harbors, Salem and New Amsterdam, shaped the work around them. Day 116: tracing a documented route on a map.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **Fishing and ships.** Fishing was a major trade, and the colonies sent fish, meat, flour and lumber to the Caribbean islands (Britannica, Triangular trade, sources.csv U5AB).
- **Barter** is a trade of goods or services with no money, from grade 3 unit 3. That coins were scarce is not on a page read, so it is not taught as a fact; the made-up cases give a reason (a person has no coins).
- **The trade in people.** Poorer cod went to Caribbean plantations to feed enslaved people. Rum made from Caribbean molasses was sent to West Africa, in the trade that forced Africans onto ships (U5AB). Salem merchants provisioned Caribbean plantations with fish, livestock and lumber, and ships returned with sugar, molasses and coffee produced by the forced labor of enslaved people. The schooner Pembroke in 1764 came back to Salem from Dominica with sugar, coffee and molasses (National Park Service, U5S); the page does not state the outbound port, so none is taught. Say plainly that Africans were taken from their homes and forced onto ships, and that people are never goods. No graphic detail.
- **One triangle?** The name "triangle trade" came from writers in 1866 to 1887, and a historian cited calls the idea essentially incorrect. No New England trader is known to have completed a full triangle (U5AB). The class draws a simplified network, labeled so, and asks what it leaves out. Do not teach "Triangular Trade" as the name of a fixed circuit.
- **Two harbors.** Henry Hudson reached New York Bay in 1609. In 1624 the Dutch set up a fur trading station on Governor's Island, and in 1625 they founded New Amsterdam in Lower Manhattan (National Park Service, U5T). Do not say anything about slavery at New Amsterdam (the page is silent).

## The errors in plain words

I is thinking barter needs coins. AD is not linking the builder, the fisher and the seller. T is mixing up goods and people. U is thinking ships all ran one triangle. W is not seeing why people and goods gathered at a harbor.

## Supplies

A harbor map, the link card, a simplified diagram labeled so, a printed map and tracing paper, a legend with two marks (goods, a trip). Cards: cards-days113-116.md. Exit cards: cards-exits-113-116.md. Keys: key-exits-113-116.md. No tool.

## A room with nothing

Draw the harbor and the arrows on the board. Hold the map and tracing paper to a window.

## Unsettled facts for the day writers

- Shipyard crafts such as sailmakers and ropemakers are not on a page read. The shipyard card says only that the yard builds boats from timber.
- The Salem page does not describe the harbor or who built the wharves. Take the harbor from the map.
- The plan's "documented return route" on day 114 is the Pembroke voyage of 1764 on day 116; day 114 uses the historians' caution instead.
- No made-up Native nations appear. Invented ports and islands are labeled made up.
