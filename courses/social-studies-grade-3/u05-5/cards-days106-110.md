# Days 106 to 110 cards

Print "made up, for practice" on invented cards. Read every line aloud once.

## Day 106 repair

K (5 minutes): two made-up colony bands on the wall strip, Colony T 1590 to 1640 and Colony U 1620 to 1650. Pairs say which was there when the second began. Response: each child fixes the one cell the teacher's note names, in coloured pencil.

## Why Puritans came

The Winthrop Fleet carried about 1,000 colonists to Massachusetts in 1630. Puritans wanted their religious practices free from the king and the Anglican Church. By the mid-1640s more than 20,000 colonists lived there. (Britannica, Massachusetts Bay Colony, U5Z.) Pilgrims are a different group (day 100). Belief is described, not judged.

Post "What undermined it?" on the question board.

## How they spread

Groups of families asked for land. New towns spread from the coast inland. (U5Z.) The Massachusett and Wampanoag nations lived there and live today. Trace one arrow: tail at Salem, head inland. Say its direction.

## Cause chart: four effects, four causes

- Disease. Disease wiped out whole villages. Cause: contact with newcomers. (Aquinnah page, U5F.)
- Land loss. Land shrank after English arrival. Cause: new towns claimed land. (U5F.)
- Conversion. The minister John Eliot set up praying towns, villages for Native people who had converted. The towns followed Puritan law and had schools that taught English. (Britannica, John Eliot, U5AA.) The Massachusett Tribe at Ponkapoag: its praying town was self-governing from 1657, and the Tribe governs itself today. (U5H.)
- War. After King Philip's War, survivors fled north to live among the Abenaki. (Wabanaki essay, U5G.) Name the war once. No battle detail.

Sort each card: a choice, a pressure or a seizure (F). One cause to one effect (N).

Record frames: "Puritans came because ___." "They spread to ___." "One effect was ___ because ___."

## Ousamequin's life

Ousamequin was the Great Sachem of the Wampanoag in 1620. English records call him Massasoit. Sachems cared for their people, settled disputes and oversaw talks with other groups. In summer 1621 two colonists visited his town of Pokanoket to cement an alliance. (Pilgrim Hall, U5I.) Boxes: before, action, after. The Wampanoag live today.

## Pairs: Winthrop and Metacom

Card 1. John Winthrop. Colony of Massachusetts Bay. Role: led the first wave of 1630, governor 12 of the first 20 years. (U5Z.) Answered to: the colony; the king's charter.
Card 2. Metacom, also called King Philip. Wampanoag. Role: sachem of the Wampanoag from 1662 to 1676, son of Massasoit. Decision: led his people through the war of 1675 to 1678, which is named for him. Why: our page does not say, so that box stays blank. (Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe, U5AC.) Answered to: his people, as a sachem.

Each pair fills a card for each leader: role, a decision, interest, whom he answers to. They find the two led in different years and did not talk with each other: Winthrop in the 1630s, Metacom in the 1670s.

## Who made it?

Three questions: who made it, when, why. A reconstruction is made now from evidence. A painting is a later picture. An artifact is an object made and used long ago.

## A house

The houses at the museum village are timber-framed re-creations of what the first houses may have looked like. (Plimoth Patuxet, U5W.) Caption: a reconstruction is later evidence of one home. The class marks what the caption shows and what it cannot show: other families' homes (G).

## School and learning

Boston Latin School was founded in 1635. A 1642 law required parents to see that children learned to read. Most children learned with a hornbook, a printed page on a thin board under a sheet of horn. Schooling was aimed mainly at boys. Girls were not shut out of every town. (Paul Revere House, U5X.) Three columns: who, where, with what. One line on what the summary does not tell.

Record: draw and label one thing in a colonial home. One sentence on how children learned. No child describes their own home.

## Enslaved people

Slavery was legal in Massachusetts. Enslaved people were held against their will and denied freedom. In Boston they worked in homes, shops, farming, shipbuilding and at sea. Before 1700 most enslaved people in Boston were Native American. (City of Boston Archaeology, U5R.) Fill the same table. Compare it with Ned's line.
