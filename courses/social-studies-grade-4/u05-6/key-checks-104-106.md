## C2-E104-1

Weigh the proposed arrival claim using Source A's date.

Answer and look-fors: The claim fails its date test: 14,550 years ago is earlier than 13,000. People were present before the claim allows.

Samples: People were there earlier than the claim allows: 14,550 reaches farther back than 13,000. | 14,550 is later, so the claim fits.

## C2-E104-2

Explain what Source A leaves unknown about the journey.

Answer and look-fors: The find does not trace a journey. It cannot settle inland versus coastal travel or exclude a Beringia crossing before coastal travel.

Samples: The tools show presence, but no complete route. | These tools prove everyone arrived by boat.

## C2-E104-3

Record Source B's original writer and document date.

Answer and look-fors: Christopher Columbus; February 15, 1493. The university is a later host.

Samples: Christopher Columbus, February 15, 1493. | The university wrote the original letter in 2026.

## C2-E104-4

Infer an intended audience from a clue inside Source B.

Answer and look-fors: Royal decision makers are an intended audience beyond the immediate recipient. The request for help from the rulers, or promise of gold to them, is the clue. Accept a royal intermediary only with this clue.

Samples: The rulers: he asks for their help and promises them gold. | Tourists, because islands are nice.

## C2-E104-5

Infer Source B's purpose from a clue inside it.

Answer and look-fors: To persuade royal backers to give further support by presenting the voyage as profitable. The promise of gold if they help supplies the clue. Accept reporting results plus seeking support.

Samples: He wants more help: he promises gold if the rulers help him. | To entertain children; he asks for nothing.

## C2-E105-1

Compare what Sources C and D each add about Cabot's voyage.

Answer and look-fors: C, a primary legal grant, records royal permission and profit rules before the voyage. D, a later historical interpretation, summarizes the voyage and landing uncertainty. Distinguishes intended authority from a reconstructed outcome.

Samples: C records what the king allowed. D describes the voyage and uncertainty about its landing. | Both are eyewitness accounts of the landing.

## C2-E105-2

Explain whether the two sources prove the exact landing place.

Answer and look-fors: No. C grants permission without recording a landing. D says the precise landing place is uncertain. Permission cannot establish an outcome or residents consent.

Samples: No. C is permission; D says the precise place is uncertain. | The king gave permission, so the landing place is proved.

## C2-E105-3

Explain a cause of the movements on Map E using Timeline F.

Answer and look-fors: Atlantic voyages carried plants and animals between hemispheres. The 1493 transport precedes later crop growth, with ships providing the movement mechanism. An earlier date alone is not a cause.

Samples: Ships carried living things across the ocean in 1493, before the later changes. | Sugar mills caused the earlier voyage.

## C2-E105-4

Explain one crop effect shown by Timeline F.

Answer and look-fors: Transported sugarcane grew on Hispaniola by the late 1490s; mills followed in the early 1500s. Accept either growing or processing change linked to introduction, without claiming equal benefits or free labor.

Samples: Introduced sugarcane became a growing crop; mills later processed it. | Mills existed there before the crop arrived.

## C2-E105-5

Explain one animal effect using Map E and its note.

Answer and look-fors: Pigs carried west to the Caribbean could establish populations there, including wild populations. The note describes introduced animals thriving, not every person benefiting.

Samples: Pigs went west to the islands, where introduced animals could thrive in the wild. | Pigs went east from the Caribbean on this arrow.

## C2-E106-cause

Made-up record: a cart carried clay through a narrow gate. The gate closed, blocking the cart. Potters waited, and the clay stayed outside. Explain the cause of these two effects.

Answer and look-fors: The closed gate blocked the cart: potters waited for clay, and clay remained outside. Connect both effects to blocked passage.

Samples: The closed gate blocked the cart: potters waited for clay, and clay remained outside. Connect both effects to blocked passage. | The waiting potters caused the earlier gate closure.
