Teacher note. A fictional story, told by one bumblebee ("I"). Read it aloud whole on day 104; children join in on the repeated lines. What the bee does is checked against the articles in g2-u3-01 (the five steps of pollination, bees carry pollen on purpose, pollen sticks to hairy legs and body, a mother bee mixes pollen with nectar and feeds her young, a bumblebee can shake pollen loose by buzzing). What the bee thinks, feels, says and names is imagined: real bees are not known to think these things, and the class says so. The picture lines show the facts only; the bee's thoughts are in words. For day 112, the model shows how a bee-view story goes: where it is, what it wants, what it does in order, what it notices with its body, and a closing that answers the start.

## Text

[page 1]
Let me tell you about my morning. I am a bumblebee, and I have a job. I find food for the little bees at home in our nest.
Picture: A round, fuzzy, yellow-and-black bumblebee hovers above a sunny garden of yellow flowers with brown centers. Dew glints on the leaves.

[page 2]
The sun was warm on my wings. I thought, "Today is a good day for flowers."
I flew over the fence, over the path, and over a wooden bench where a girl sat very still. She held a notebook. She did not wave her arms. I liked her for that.
Picture: From above, a path, a wooden bench with a girl of about seven with brown skin, braids and a notebook on her lap. A bumblebee flies over her, high above her hands.

[page 3]
Then I saw yellow. A whole patch of yellow! Each flower had a brown button in the middle.
"That one," I said to myself. I dropped down and landed on the first flower. It was a soft landing.
Picture: The bee lands at the middle of the first flower, its legs spread on the brown button and the yellow petals around it.

[page 4]
I put my face in and drank. The nectar was sweet. It tasted like a warm, sunny day.
Thin little stalks around the button brushed against my fuzzy body. They were the anthers. Yellow powder rubbed off on me. Pollen!
Picture: A close view of the flower center. The bee's head is down at the middle. Thin stalks tipped with yellow dust touch the bee's furry back.

[page 5]
I scraped up pollen with my hairy legs. I mixed it with nectar. I packed it in neat little bundles, one on each back leg.
"This is for the babies," I said. "They will be hungry."
But not all the pollen went in the bundles. Some stayed on my fur, like yellow sprinkles. I did not even notice.
Picture: The bee's two back legs hold round yellow bundles. Yellow dots speckle its furry back and sides.

[page 6]
One flower was not enough. The little bees at home are many. So I lifted off the first flower with a buzz and went looking for another.
I thought, "Two flowers are better than one."
Picture: The bee rises from the first flower, wings blurred. In the distance, a second flower of the same kind shows over a leaf.

[page 7]
I flew across the patch. I felt the breeze on my fur. I smelled something sweet and minty. I found the second flower, the same kind of yellow, and I landed.
Picture: The bee glides across the patch of yellow flowers. A bench is far off in the background.

[page 8]
I dropped my head in to drink. And as I stepped, my body brushed against the sticky top in the middle of the flower. That was the stigma.
Some of the pollen from my fur rubbed off. It stayed there on the stigma.
Picture: The bee walks across the center of the second flower. Yellow dust from its fur is on the sticky pale stigma.

[page 9]
I gather pollen on purpose, for the babies. But I did not plan that rub on the stigma. I did not even know I had done anything. Still, pollen from the first flower had reached the second flower, and my body had carried it.
That is how a bee helps. I just do my job, and the flower gets help too.
Picture: The bee, from the side, at the center of the second flower. Faint yellow dots drift from its fur to the stigma.

[page 10]
I felt full of sweet. I wanted to rest, but the babies were waiting for their food. I lifted up and flew back over the bench. The girl was writing something in her notebook.
"A bee," she whispered. "A bee on a flower, and then another."
I thought, "She was watching. Good. She sees what I do."
Picture: The bee flies away above the bench. The girl looks up with a pencil in her hand, and the notebook shows a small drawing of a bee and two flowers.

[page 11]
Many days later, I flew back to the patch. The yellow petals had dropped. In the middle of the second flower, something had changed. A round brown head stood there, full of seeds.
I thought, "That started with my visit."
Picture: The same patch, with yellow petals scattered on the ground. One flower has a round, brown seed head. The bee hovers near it.

[page 12]
The seeds would fall. Some would grow. Next summer there would be more flowers for more bees.
One bee. Two flowers. And a whole new patch to come.
Picture: Wide view of the garden, the seed head in the foreground, small green sprouts at its base. In the distance, the girl and a grown-up pull a small cart. The bee is a speck in the sky.
