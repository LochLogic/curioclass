# School Days

## 02-a. Learning Together

Japan is a country in East Asia. It is made of islands.

[map: A world map with Japan marked by a red dot, a small arrow from the dot to the caption "Japan, a country of islands in East Asia." Label the sea to the east and the large continent to the west only as "land" and "sea".]

Pupils in Japanese elementary schools learn reading, writing and math. They also learn to take care of their school together.

Look at lunchtime. In many schools, pupils serve the meal themselves. A few pupils take turns as the helpers for the day. They help bring the food to the classroom and serve their classmates. When the meal is over, the pupils clear up too.

Pupils also clean. In many schools, pupils clean their classroom and other parts of the school as a group. Each pupil has a part to do.

Why do schools do this? Teachers and school leaders say that cleaning helps pupils learn to **cooperate**. To cooperate means to work together to get something done. They also say that cleaning helps pupils learn to respect the places that everyone shares.

Lunch has a reason too. Pupils learn about food, and they learn to thank the people who grow it and cook it.

So in many Japanese schools, a school day is not only for learning from teachers. It is also a day for learning together.

[photograph, to be staged by Curio Class, no children: A classroom with desks in tidy rows and a cart by the door. On the cart: a covered pot, a stack of bowls and a ladle. Caption: "Bowls, a pot and a ladle wait for the day's helpers." The photograph adds how a lunch for a whole class is carried and served, which the article does not describe.]

Words to know. **cooperate**: to work together to get something done. **helpers**: the pupils whose turn it is to do a job.

Teacher line (checked 2026-11-19). The official education website of Japan's ministry of education published a visit to a Tokyo elementary school on 24 March 2026 and says pupils serve lunch and clear up themselves, and that cleaning builds cooperation and respect for shared places. Practices differ from school to school.

## 02-b. A Place to Read

Finland is a country in northern Europe. [map: A world map with Finland marked by a red dot and the caption "Finland, a country in northern Europe."]

In Finland, children start school in the year they turn seven. A lesson is usually 45 minutes, and then pupils have a 15-minute break. Every pupil is offered a free meal on every school day.

What else do Finnish pupils do each day? They read.

A **library** is a place where people borrow books and read. In Finland, libraries are a free service. Anyone can borrow books at no cost.

In 2025, a town called Riihimäki started a reading time in its schools. At about ten o'clock each school day, pupils stop and read a book for ten minutes. Teachers and other adults read too, so the whole building is quiet and busy at once.

Why read for ten minutes every day? The town says that reading gets better when people read often and practice. A little reading every day adds up.

One school in the town has a weekly visit from volunteers. A **volunteer** is a person who helps without being paid. These volunteers sit with third and fourth graders and listen to them read. Children who come earn stickers for a reading passport.

So a place to read can be a library, a classroom at ten o'clock, or a chair beside a visitor who has time to listen.

[photograph, to be staged by Curio Class, no children: A corner of a school library with a low shelf of picture books, a cushion on the floor and a lamp. A sign above the shelf reads "Library". Caption: "A quiet corner of a library, ready for readers." The photograph adds the cushion and lamp, which show a place made for comfortable reading, a detail the article does not give.]

Words to know. **library**: a place where people borrow books and read. **volunteer**: a person who helps without being paid.

Teacher line (checked 2026-11-19). The European education information network describes the Finnish school year, lesson length, school start age and the free daily meal; a Finnish government site calls libraries a free basic service; and the town of Riihimäki described its daily ten-minute reading time in April 2025. A web news check on 2026-11-19 found Finnish towns updating school rules in autumn 2026 (for example on phones) but nothing that changes these facts. School practices change, so say "in 2025" as the article does.

## 02-c. Following a School Day

Brazil is the largest country in South America. [map: A world map with Brazil marked by a red dot and the caption "Brazil, a country in South America."]

Some schools run in parts of the day. Pupils may go in the morning or in the afternoon. A group that goes at one part of the day is called a **shift**. One building can hold two shifts: a morning shift in the morning, and an afternoon shift in the afternoon.

Some schools keep pupils for the whole day. These are full-day schools.

A day has a **schedule**. A schedule is a plan that tells what happens and when. A diagram can show a schedule.

Look at the diagram. It shows three example school days, made up for practice. In the example, the morning shift and afternoon shift each have lunch and a snack at school. The full-day pupil has four meals at school: breakfast, lunch and two snacks.

Why does a school serve meals? In Brazil, a national program pays for meals in public schools. Part of the food is bought from family farms.

So following a school day means asking which shift a pupil is in. Then you can follow the lessons, the meals and the walk home.

[diagram, drawn by Curio Class: A wide bar showing one day, with "morning" on the left half and "afternoon" on the right half and a small school building icon below the bar. Row 2, "Afternoon shift": a bracket under the afternoon half, with two meal icons labelled "lunch" and "snack". Caption: "An example made up for practice. A shift is the part of the day when a group is at school." The diagram explains the schedule's order and shows that one building can serve two groups, which the article states only in words.]

Words to know. **shift**: the part of the day when a group of pupils is at school. **schedule**: a plan that tells what happens and when. **diagram**: a drawing that explains something with labels.

Teacher line (checked 2026-11-21). Brazil's national school meals program, run by its national education fund (FNDE), serves students in public schools and must spend at least 30 percent of its federal money on food bought directly from family farms. The shifts and meal counts in the diagram are an example made up for practice, not a count for Brazil: say so aloud before children read it.
