# Fossil Clues

## Article A: What Is a Fossil?

[page 1]
### Left From Very Long Ago

A **fossil** is what remains of a plant or an animal that lived very long ago. Fossils are found in rock. Some are bones or teeth. Some are shells. Some are leaves.

### Signs of What Animals Did

A fossil can also be a **trace**. A trace is a sign of what an animal did. A footprint pressed into soft mud is a trace. A tunnel an animal dug is a trace. If the mud turns to rock, the trace may be preserved for a very long time.

### Why Fossils Are Rare

Very few living things become fossils. Hard parts, such as shells, bones and teeth, last longest. Soft parts usually rot away. A plant or an animal also needs to be covered quickly by **sediment**. Sediment is small bits of mud, sand or clay. A quick cover keeps the body safe from other animals, wind and weather.

### What Fossils Show

Fossils are clues. A fossil shell shows that a kind of animal once lived in a place. Some parks in dry places hold fossils of sea animals. That is evidence that a sea was there very long ago. A fossil footprint shows how an animal moved.

## Article B: How a Bone Becomes a Fossil

[page 1]
### Five Steps

A fossil bone does not form in a day. It forms in steps, over a very long time. This article tells the steps in order.

### Buried Fast

First, an animal dies near a river. Mud and sand cover its body. The sediment hides the bones. This helps the bones last, because it keeps away wind, rain and hungry animals. As more sediment piles on top, it makes a **layer** over the bones.

### Minerals Move In

Next, water seeps down through the layers. The water carries tiny bits of **minerals**. A mineral is a hard material found in the ground. The bits of mineral fill the tiny spaces in the bone. Slowly, the bone turns hard, like stone. The bone keeps its shape.

### Rock Wears Away

Much later, wind and rain wear away the rock above. Little by little, the layers on top are gone. The fossil bone comes close to the surface. A hiker might spot a piece of it.

### Uncovered With Care

Last, scientists come. They uncover the fossil slowly. They use brushes and small tools. They do not hurry, and they do not pull. They write down where they found it. Then they take it to a museum to study.

## Article C: Molds, Casts and Tracks

[page 1]
### Shapes in Stone

Sometimes a fossil is not a bone or a shell at all. It is the shape of one. Think of a seashell on the bottom of a sea. It sinks into soft sediment. The sediment buries it. Over a very long time, the sediment hardens into **sandstone** or other rock.

Then the shell may wear away. A hollow shape is left in the rock. The hollow shape is a **mold**. If new material fills the hollow and hardens, it makes a **cast**. A cast is a copy of the shell's shape. Both are fossils.

Caption: The middle picture shows the mold. The last picture shows the cast.

### Tracks and Traces

Fossils can also record actions. A **footprint** left in soft mud or sand is a trace. If the print is covered by new sediment and turns to rock, it may become a fossil. Many fossil tracks are molds or casts.

A track tells where an animal stepped. It can show how an animal moved. A track does not always tell us exactly which animal made it. A **riverbed** or a **hillside** may hold many tracks that nobody has seen.

### Scientists at Work

A **paleontologist** is a scientist who studies fossils. When a paleontologist finds a fossil, called a **specimen**, the work is careful. A group of scientists brush the rock gently. They take photographs. They write notes in a **notebook**. They draw what they see. They note the **location**, the place where the specimen was found.

Scientists ask themselves questions. What was this animal? Where did it live? What did it eat? Fossil teeth can suggest what an animal ate. Fossil feet can suggest how it moved. A fossil fish tells us a river or sea was there. Each clue connects to other clues.

Scientists also tell visitors a rule. Fossils on public land belong to everyone. In many places, you may not take them. Rules are different in different places. If you find a fossil, leave it where it is, take a picture, and tell a ranger or a museum. They will know what to do.

### Glossary

- **cast**: a copy of a shape made when material fills a mold
- **location**: the place where something is
- **mold**: a hollow shape left in rock by something that is gone
- **paleontologist**: a scientist who studies fossils
- **specimen**: one fossil or sample kept to be studied
- **trace**: a sign left by an action
