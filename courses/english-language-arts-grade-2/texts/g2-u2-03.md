Teacher note. An invented story; say aloud that Amara, Dr. Lee and the fossil park are made up, though the science in it is real. The teacher reads it aloud on day 33 (children listen for what the text tells and what it leaves unsaid, such as what kind of shell it was). Children read it with support from day 34 on (grades 2 to 3 band). The beginning (pages 1 to 3: the class arrives, the mark is found, Amara wonders what it is) introduces the action; the ending (pages 10 and 11: the question is answered, the clues are recorded, Amara tells a friend) concludes it. Each page is one scene with one main actor, and the middle is a short run of ordered steps marked First, Next, Then, At last, so it serves as the narrative model for days 45 and 59 to 63. Amara's thoughts and feelings are named in words ("thumping", "disappointed", "excited") and her actions are exact. The story contains the unit's fossil words (fossil, discover, notice, examine, wonder, careful, clue, evidence, sediment, trace, remain, preserve, patient, paleontologist, specimen, group, gentle and gently, compare, detail, mold, layer) and a few multiple-meaning words for day 37 (pick, brush, fan, mark). The fossil park is a place where collecting is allowed; say aloud that many places do not allow it and that children should ask first. Earth's age is only ever "very long ago". Not in the story, because they are writing terms and not story words: introduce, conclude, sequence, elaborate, action, thought, feeling, temporal, closure, rehearse, draft, revise, edit.

# The Mark in the Stone

[page 1]
On a cool, bright morning, Amara's class went to a fossil park. A guide named Dr. Lee met the group at the gate. Dr. Lee was a paleontologist, a scientist who studies fossils.

"Today you are fossil hunters," Dr. Lee said. "Walk slowly. Look closely. These rocks hold clues about the sea that was here very long ago."

Each child got a soft brush and a paper bag.

Picture: A group of children of different looks, in jackets and sun hats, stand at the gate of a rocky park. A guide in a wide hat holds up a flat gray stone. Each child holds a small brush and a paper bag.

[page 2]
Amara walked to a pile of broken gray stones. Most of them looked plain. She did not pick the biggest stone. She picked up a small, flat one and turned it over.

"What is that?" she whispered.

A curved mark was pressed into the stone. It looked like a tiny fan.

Picture: Amara, a girl with braids and a yellow jacket, crouches by a heap of gray stones. In her palm lies a small flat stone with a curved, fan-shaped mark.

[page 3]
Amara's heart began thumping. Was the mark a leaf? Was it a crack? Was it only a scratch? She wanted to shout for everyone to come and see. But first she wanted to be sure. She held the stone still in both hands and took a deep breath.

Picture: A close view of Amara's face, eyes wide, and the stone held close to her chest in both hands.

[page 4]
First, Amara looked at the mark carefully. Thin lines curved out from one small point. Every line was nearly the same distance from the next.

"A crack would not be so neat," she thought. That was her first clue.

Picture: A close view of the stone. Thin lines fan out from one point, like the ribs of a paper fan. Amara's finger hovers above it without touching.

[page 5]
Next, Amara brushed the stone gently. Dust fell away. She was careful not to scratch it. A second fan, smaller than the first, showed beside it. Now she had two marks that matched. That was her second clue.

She grinned. "This is evidence," she said to herself, "not just a guess."

Picture: Amara sweeps the stone with a soft brush. Two fan-shaped marks, one large and one small, show clearly. Dust floats in the sunlight.

[page 6]
Then Amara carried the stone to Dr. Lee. Her hands shook a little. "I think I discovered something," she said.

Dr. Lee held up a magnifying glass and examined the stone for a long time. Amara waited. Waiting was hard. She tried to be patient.

Picture: Dr. Lee kneels on the ground and studies the stone through a magnifying glass. Amara stands beside her, hands clasped, rocking on her toes.

[page 7]
At last Dr. Lee smiled. "Good eyes," she said. "Let's compare it with something."

She took a picture card from her pocket. It showed a seashell with thin lines curving out like a fan. The lines matched the marks on Amara's stone, detail for detail.

Picture: Dr. Lee holds a picture card of a ribbed seashell next to the stone. Amara looks from the card to the stone and back.

[page 8]
"This stone began as soft mud at the bottom of a sea," Dr. Lee said. "A shell lay in the mud. It pressed its shape into the sediment. Later, the mud turned into hard rock in layers. The shell wore away, and no trace of it remained. But its shape stayed behind. That shape is a mold, and a mold is a fossil."

Amara stared at the stone. "A shell was here," she said slowly, "very long ago."

"Yes," said Dr. Lee. "Its shape was preserved for you to find."

Picture: Two small drawings side by side with Dr. Lee and Amara above them. Left: a shell lying in mud at the bottom of a sea. Right: a gray stone with a shell-shaped hollow and no shell in it.

[page 9]
"What kind of shell animal was it?" Amara asked.

Dr. Lee studied the lines again. "I can't tell you for sure," she said. "I will send a picture of this specimen to a scientist at the museum. She may be able to name it. A fossil can show us a lot, but not everything."

Amara felt a little disappointed, and a little excited, both at once.

Picture: Dr. Lee takes a photograph of the stone on a flat rock with a camera. Amara watches with her chin on her hands.

[page 10]
Dr. Lee wrote the place and the day on a paper label. "Scientists need to know exactly where a fossil was found," she said.

Amara drew her stone in her notebook, with both fans, line by line. Then she wrapped it in soft paper and laid it gently in her bag.

Picture: Amara sits on a log and draws in a notebook with the stone beside her. Dr. Lee writes on a small label.

[page 11]
At last it was time to go home. On the bus, Amara's friend Ben pointed to her bag. "What's in there?" he asked.

"The shape of a shell, in stone," said Amara. "I noticed a mark. I wondered about it. I looked closely and checked my clues. Now I know what it is."

She looked at the little fan in her notebook and smiled. The mark in the stone was no longer a mystery. It was a shell from the sea of very long ago, and she had been the one to find it.

Picture: Amara and her friend Ben sit side by side on a bus. She holds the open notebook with her drawing of the fan-shaped marks. Ben leans in to look. A paper bag rests on her knees.

## Sources

The story is invented. The science in it was checked against these pages.

- National Park Service, "Molds, Casts, and Steinkerns" (a mold is an impression made when a hard part, often a shell, presses into sediment; a cast forms when a mold is filled; a mold is a fossil impression). https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/mold-casts-and-steinkerns.htm, checked: 2026-11-19.
- National Park Service, "How Fossils Form" (rapid burial in sediment; hard parts such as shells last best). https://www.nps.gov/subjects/fossils/how-fossils-form.htm, checked: 2026-11-19.
- Forest Service, "Fossils: A Guide to Casual Collecting on National Forests and Grasslands" (where collecting is allowed differs from place to place; when unsure, leave the fossil and ask; photograph it). https://www.fs.usda.gov/science-technology/geology/paleontology/casual-collecting, checked: 2026-11-19.

Cut from the sources: any age in numbers; the kinds of internal and external mold (too much for grade 2); which shell animal it was (left open on purpose, so the text leaves something uncertain).
