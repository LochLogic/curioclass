## 01-a. Pollen on the Move

**Plants cannot walk to a friend.** A plant stays in one place, rooted in the ground. But many plants need help. They need to share a tiny yellow powder with other flowers of the same kind. That powder is **pollen**. Moving pollen from flower to flower is called **pollination**.

Who moves it? Wind and water can. So can animals. An animal that moves pollen is called a **pollinator**.

**The parts of a flower that matter**

Look inside a flower. The **anther** is a part that makes pollen. It sits on a thin stalk. The **stigma** is a part at the center. It is sticky at the top. Pollen that lands on the stigma can help the plant make seeds. Deep inside the flower, there is also a sweet juice called **nectar**.

**How a bee helps: the steps**

1. **First,** a bee sees and smells a flower. It lands.
2. **Then** it reaches in for nectar. The anther brushes the bee. Pollen sticks to its fuzzy body. We say the pollen *clings*, or *adheres*.
3. **Next,** the bee flies to a second flower of the same kind. It carries the pollen along.
4. **After that,** the bee lands and moves around. Some pollen rubs off on the sticky stigma.
5. **Finally,** the pollen helps the flower start making seeds. Seeds can grow into new plants.

Each step makes the next step possible. If the bee never lands, the pollen never sticks. If the pollen never sticks, it cannot be carried. If it is never carried, the second flower gets no pollen.

**Why does it matter?** The bee gets food. The flower gets help. The plant and the pollinator help each other. Pollinators are part of about one in three bites of food people eat. At least three out of four kinds of flowering plants on Earth are pollinated by insects and other animals.

## 01-b. A Bee Visits

**Bees are champion pollinators.** Bees visit flowers on purpose, for food for themselves and for their young. They are the only insects that look for pollen as food. Pollen is a bee's main protein, a food that helps bodies grow.

**How a bee moves pollen.** A mother bee lands and drinks nectar. She also gathers pollen with her hairy legs and her hairy middle. She mixes the pollen with nectar and carries it to her nest to feed her young. Some pollen stays on her fur. When she lands on the next flower, some of it can brush onto the stigma.

**A special way to shake a flower.** A bumblebee can hold on to a flower and make its body buzz. The shaking makes pollen fall out. This is called buzz pollination.

**Flowers bees like.** Bees like flowers that are sweet smelling, open in the day and have a place to land. Some bees visit many kinds of flowers. Other bees use pollen from only one kind of flower.

## 01-c. A Butterfly Visits

**Butterflies visit in the daytime.** A butterfly probes a flower for nectar. Nectar is its energy food. Adult monarch butterflies sip from many kinds of flowers.

**A butterfly needs a place to land.** A butterfly perches on a flower with its long, thin legs. So it likes flowers with a flat top, like a little platform. It also likes flowers in bunches, and flowers that are red, yellow or orange. Goldenrod is one of them.

**How pollen moves.** A butterfly does not look for pollen. It moves pollen by accident. A little pollen sticks to its body while it sips nectar or rests on the flower. Then the butterfly flies to the next flower, and some pollen comes off there.

**A big difference.** Butterflies pick up less pollen than bees do. They are not as good at moving pollen between plants. But they still help many plants.

## 01-d. A Bat Visits at Night

**Some flowers open after dark.** Bats that drink nectar feed at night. The flowers they visit are often large, pale or white, and strong smelling. These flowers hold plenty of watery nectar.

**A long nose and a long tongue.** Many bats that visit flowers have a long nose and a long tongue. They can reach nectar deep inside a flower. Pollen sticks to the bat while it feeds. Some of it brushes onto the next flower.

**Bats and the desert.** The lesser long-nosed bat flies north each spring. It travels from Mexico into Arizona, New Mexico and Texas. On the way, it visits flowers on desert plants such as saguaro cactus and agave. More than 500 kinds of flowering plants depend on bats. So do fruit plants, such as mango, banana and guava.

## 01-e. A Hummingbird Visits

**A tiny bird that hovers.** A hummingbird is very small. It hovers in the air in front of a flower while it feeds. Its wings beat about seventy times in one second. It drinks nectar. It also eats small insects, which give it protein.

**A long, slender bill.** The hummingbird pushes its long, thin bill deep into the flower. When it pulls back, its face is dusted with pollen. At the next flower, some pollen touches the stigma.

**Flowers a hummingbird likes.** A hummingbird finds flowers by color, not by smell. It likes red, yellow and orange. Its flowers are often shaped like tubes, and they hold nectar down deep. Bee balm, columbine and sage are three of them. On the mainland of the United States, hummingbirds are very important pollinators of wildflowers.
