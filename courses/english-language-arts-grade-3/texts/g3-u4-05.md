# New Well, Shared Work

*An invented story. The village and the people in it are made up. Sand dams and shared wells like this one are real in Kitui County, Kenya.*

[page 1]
In a village in Kitui County, a girl named Mutheu woke before the sun. Every morning, she and her little brother Musyoka walked to a far water point with two jerrycans. The path was long, and the sun was hot by the time they got home.
*Picture: Two children with jerrycans walk on a dusty path past thorn trees at dawn. Far ahead, a small blue water tank stands on a hill.*

[page 2]
Then the village made a plan. "We will build a sand dam," said Mr. Kioko, who led the water committee. "It will hold rain in the sand, and we will dig a well beside it."
Everyone helped. Neighbors carried stones. Grandmother mixed cement. Mutheu passed buckets of sand. The work took many weeks, and the narrator thinks it was the best kind of work, because everyone owned it.
*Picture: Villagers of all ages build a low stone wall across a dry riverbed. A woman stirs cement; two children pass buckets.*

[page 3]
Then the rains came. The river rushed brown and fast. When the storm ended, the water sank into the sand behind the wall, and it stayed there. The sand worked like a bank for water.
*Picture: A brown river pours past the new wall. Beside it, the riverbed is full of pale sand.*

[page 4]
Soon the well was finished. Mr. Kioko stood beside it and held up a paper. "Here is our plan," he said. "Each household gets a turn. The list changes each week, so the same families are not always last."
Mutheu looked at the list. Her family was in the afternoon group, on the fourth day.
*Picture: Mr. Kioko holds up a long list of names beside a new covered well with a hand pump. Neighbors gather around.*

[page 5]
"That is not fair," Mutheu whispered. "We walked the farthest. Grandmother's seedlings are drooping. I should fill my jerrycan first."
But the narrator thinks the plan was fair, because every household got a turn, and nobody had to ask.
*Picture: Mutheu frowns at the list while Musyoka tugs her sleeve.*

[page 6]
For three days, Mutheu watched. She watched Mr. Kioko lower a stick into the well to check the water. She watched neighbors fill their jerrycans and walk home in the morning, smiling.
On the third day, she asked, "Why must we wait? There is plenty of water in the sand."
*Picture: Mutheu watches a man lower a long stick into the well while a line of neighbors waits.*

[page 7]
Mr. Kioko knelt beside her. "The sand holds a lot," he said. "But it is not endless. If a few of us took too much now, the water might run low before the next rain. Because we share, there is water for every household, and it lasts through the dry weeks."
Mutheu did not like waiting. But she understood the answer.
*Picture: Mr. Kioko and Mutheu kneel by the sand dam, pointing at the wet sand.*

[page 8]
On the fourth day, the afternoon group came. Mutheu pumped the handle and the water ran cool and clear. She filled both jerrycans. At home, Grandmother poured water on the seedlings, and the green leaves lifted.
*Picture: Grandmother waters small plants from a cup while Mutheu and Musyoka watch.*

[page 9]
The next week, Mutheu helped Mr. Kioko copy the new list. "A turn is slower than first," she said. "But it keeps the water for everyone."
The narrator thinks she was right. A well that is shared fairly is a well that lasts.
*Picture: Mutheu writes names on a long sheet at a table by the well, with neighbors waiting their turns behind her.*
