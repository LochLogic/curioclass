## Part 1. Latona at the Pool

*A Roman myth, told by the poet Ovid in his Metamorphoses (Book 6). Retold by Curio Class.*

Long ago, in a hot, dry land called Lycia, a mother named Latona walked many miles. She carried her twin babies, a boy and a girl. Her arms ached. Her feet burned. Her throat was dry and rough. Most of all, she was thirsty, and she was too tired to walk much farther.

The sun beat down on the stones. Nothing moved on the road. The babies fussed and whimpered, and Latona hummed to them softly. She kept her eyes open for water.

At last she saw a pool. Its water was clear and cool, and reeds grew along its edge. Latona hurried to the bank.

Some workers were cutting reeds beside the pool. Latona knelt and pleaded, "Please, may I have a drink? My children and I are parched."

The workers looked at her. Then they shook their heads. "This pool is ours," said one. "Go away."

"The water belongs to everyone," Latona said gently. "I only want a small drink. I am not asking for anything else. Please help me protect my babies."

The workers did not help. Instead, they jumped into the pool. They stamped their feet. They kicked up mud. Soon the clear water turned thick and brown, and no one could drink it. The workers laughed.

Latona stood up. Her thirst was bad, but now she was angry too. She raised her hands to the sky.

"You have fouled the water that you would not share," she said. "Now you may stay in it."

At once the workers began to change. Their legs bent and their arms shrank. Their necks grew short and their mouths grew wide. Their skin turned green and slippery. Where the workers had stood, frogs squatted in the mud.

The new frogs leaped into the pool and sank beneath the brown water. When they came up again, they could not shout or laugh. They could only croak.

Latona found another stream farther on. She drank at last, and her children drank with her.

The story says that the frogs never left that pool. People say that frogs still sit at the edge of ponds and croak at anyone who comes near.

## Part 2. The Frogs Who Wished for a King

*A fable credited to Aesop, a storyteller of ancient Greece. This is the telling in The Aesop for Children (1919), with a Roman name for the king of the gods. The text is in the public domain.*

The Frogs were tired of governing themselves. They had so much freedom that it had spoiled them, and they did nothing but sit around croaking in a bored manner and wishing for a government that could entertain them with the pomp and display of royalty, and rule them in a way to make them know they were being ruled. No milk and water government for them, they declared. So they sent a petition to Jupiter asking for a king.

Jupiter saw what simple and foolish creatures they were, but to keep them quiet and make them think they had a king he threw down a huge log, which fell into the water with a great splash. The Frogs hid themselves among the reeds and grasses, thinking the new king to be some fearful giant. But they soon discovered how tame and peaceable King Log was. In a short time the younger Frogs were using him for a diving platform, while the older Frogs made him a meeting place, where they complained loudly to Jupiter about the government.

To teach the Frogs a lesson the ruler of the gods now sent a Crane to be king of Frogland. The Crane proved to be a very different sort of king from old King Log. He gobbled up the poor Frogs right and left and they soon saw what fools they had been. In mournful croaks they begged Jupiter to take away the cruel tyrant before they should all be destroyed.

"How now!" cried Jupiter "Are you not yet content? You have what you asked for and so you have only yourselves to blame for your misfortunes."

*Be sure you can better your condition before you seek to change.*
