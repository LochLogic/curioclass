# The Courtyard Club: A Place for Everyone

## Chapter 1: A Job for Ada

It was a hot week in May, and the whole of Room 6 smelled like sunscreen.

"I have an idea," said Ms. Alvarez on Monday morning. "Friday is our Courtyard Reading Hour. We will take our books outside and read together under the open sky. Ada, you drew a very good plan for our reading corner. Would you draw one for the courtyard?"

Ada sat up straight. She remembered the corner. She remembered the door, the arrow and the folder with two drawings side by side. It had been a long, hard job. But in the end it had turned out well, and the corner was one of her favorite places in the world.

"Yes," she said. "I would like that."

"Seats for twenty-four readers," said Ms. Alvarez. "Some chairs, some cushions and a few blankets. And enough shade, please. The sun is strong this week."

At lunch, the Courtyard Club met on the picnic table in the garden behind Room 6. The beans had grown tall since March and had curled themselves around a string fence. Priya was picking one that was ready to eat.

"I have a new job," said Ada. She held up a clean sheet of paper. "I need to plan the seats for Friday."

"I thought you were done with plans," said Mateo, eating a bean.

"I was done with that plan," said Ada. "This is a new one."

"May I help?" asked Ben.

Ada looked at him. A few weeks ago she would have said no. She would have wanted to draw it by herself, to get it right before anyone could look. But she had learned something from the corner. She looked at Ben, and she did not say no.

"Yes, please," she said. "Can you carry the chairs and count the cushions? I'll draw."

"Good," said Ben. He took out his notebook. "I have three questions already."

"Save them for later," said Ada. She was smiling.

The courtyard was a small square of grass with a big maple tree in the middle. Its leaves made a wide circle of shade, cool and green. A low stone wall ran along one side, and a gate opened to the hall. In the far corner, a wooden bench faced the bean fence. Ada walked around the whole space, looking at it the way a bird would.

She drew the courtyard on her paper. She drew the tree and the wall and the bench. She drew a row of tiny chairs in the shade. She drew a row of cushions in the sun, where the grass was warm. She counted. There were twenty-four seats, exactly enough.

"Do you want to check it?" asked Ben.

Ada had already checked it twice. She had walked from the gate to every chair, and she had counted the chairs three times. She was careful now. She was a person who checked.

"I did," she said. "It's good."

"I'll look at it too," said Ben. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case it needs something," said Ben.

Ada nodded. It was only fair. She handed him the paper and watched him read it, line by line, from the gate to the cushions. He did not find a single mistake.

"It's a good plan," he said at last.

"Thank you," said Ada. She felt proud. She did not know yet that a plan can have no mistakes at all and still be missing something.

## Chapter 2: Two Readers, One Seat

On Wednesday, the club carried the chairs into the courtyard.

There were twelve chairs, six cushions and six blankets. Mateo and Jun hauled the chairs two at a time. Priya shook out the blankets, which smelled like the hall closet. Ben carried the cushions in his arms like a tower of pancakes.

Ada walked ahead with the plan. "Chairs in the shade," she called. "Cushions in the sun. Blankets by the wall."

They put every chair in its place. Ada stood by the gate and looked at the whole courtyard. It looked tidy and bright.

"I like it," said Priya. "Can I try a chair?"

"Of course," said Ada.

Priya walked to the best chair in the courtyard. It was the one right under the maple tree, where the shade was thickest and the breeze came through the leaves. She sat down and sighed with joy. "This is wonderful," she said.

"I want that one," said Mateo.

Priya looked up. "I'm sitting in it."

"I know, but I want it," said Mateo. "It's the best seat in the courtyard. It's cool, and there's a breeze, and I always get the sunny cushion."

"I got here first," said Priya.

"You only got here first because I was carrying the chairs," said Mateo.

Their voices were growing louder. A sparrow flew out of the tree and landed on the wall to watch.

"Ada," said Priya, "tell him I get the seat."

"Ada, tell her I should," said Mateo.

Ada looked from one to the other. This was a new problem, and she felt a funny flutter in her stomach. In the reading corner, the trouble had been a drawing that was wrong. Here the drawing was fine. The trouble was that two readers wanted the same chair.

"There's only one chair there," she said slowly.

"I know," said Mateo.

"Then someone has to move," said Ada.

"It won't be me," said Priya.

"It won't be me," said Mateo.

Ben said nothing. He wrote in his notebook, and he did not look up. Jun, who was standing by the wall, put his fingers in his ears.

Ada thought about what to do. She could ask them to take turns, but that would be slow. She could flip a coin, but that did not seem kind. She could pick one of them, and the other would be sad.

"Here's what we'll do," she said at last. "I'll decide who sits where. I'll put a name on every chair, and nobody will argue. That's fair, because it's the same for everyone."

"Who gets the tree seat?" asked Mateo.

"I'll think about it," said Ada.

"That isn't fair," said Priya.

"It's fair if it's fair to both of you," said Ada.

Priya shrugged. Mateo shrugged. They both looked as if they might not stay quiet for long.

It was an odd feeling. Ada had a plan for the chairs, and she had a plan for the cushions, and she had a plan for the blankets. She did not have a plan for people who both wanted the same thing. She was not sure that a plan could have one.

"Let's put names on every seat," she said again, more firmly. "I'll make labels tonight."

"Okay," said Priya, standing up.

"Okay," said Mateo, sitting down. He sat on the sunny cushion, where he had always sat, and he put his chin in his hands.

Ben closed his notebook. He did not say a word. But he watched Ada for a long time, and the sparrow watched him.

## Chapter 3: Every Seat Has a Name

That night, Ada sat at her kitchen table and made twenty-four name cards.

She wrote each name in clear letters. She put Priya on a cushion in the sun. She put Mateo on a chair in the shade, though not the best chair. She put the tree seat at the center of the courtyard and gave it to Ms. Alvarez, because a teacher is allowed to have the best chair. She put Jun by the wall. She put Nia and Sam, who were coming from the next class, side by side.

When she finished, she looked at her list. Every seat had a name, and every name had a seat. Nobody could argue with a card.

On Thursday, the club taped each card to its chair. Priya looked at hers and frowned, but she did not say anything. Mateo looked at his and sniffed. Then they went to their places, and nobody fought.

"See?" said Ada. "It works."

Ben stood by the gate. He had not taped a card to his own chair. He was holding it, turning it over in his hand.

"Ada," he said, "can I ask a question?"

"Of course."

"What do the quiet readers need?"

Ada looked up. "What do you mean?"

"Some readers like it quiet," said Ben. "Like Jun. He likes to read with his chin on his knees and his eyes half shut. I don't think he'd like to sit beside Nia and Sam, who whisper about their dragon book."

"They whisper quietly," said Ada.

"They whisper a lot," said Ben. "And Jun reads better when it's quiet."

Ada looked at the chairs. She had put Jun near the wall, and she had put Nia and Sam beside him. She had thought about being fair, and she had thought about the names, but she had not thought about quiet.

"I didn't think of that," she said.

"It's easy to miss," said Ben.

"Then what do I do?" asked Ada. "I can't put everyone in the quiet place. There isn't enough room."

"I don't know," said Ben. "But I could ask Jun what he wants."

"Ask him," said Ada.

Ben crossed the courtyard and spoke quietly to Jun. Jun nodded, said something short and pointed to the far bench by the bean fence. Ben came back.

"He said the far bench is a good spot," he said. "It's away from the middle. If a few readers wanted it quiet, they could go there."

"Okay," said Ada. She took a card and wrote QUIET READING on it. She put the card on the far bench. "That's the quiet place."

"What about the talking?" asked Ben. "Some readers like to talk about the book. Like Nia and Sam."

Ada added another card. This one said TALKING PLACE. She put it on the blanket by the wall.

"There," she said. "A quiet area and a place to talk. Now everyone is happy."

She waited for someone to agree. Priya and Mateo were still looking at their own names. The two new cards lay in their places, a little crooked on the grass.

"It's better," said Ben carefully.

"But?"

"But I don't know if it's enough. I don't think we know what everybody wants. We only know what Jun wants, and what Nia and Sam want. There are twenty-four readers."

Ada looked at the cards. She had thought that if every seat had a name, then everyone would be fair. But a name was only a name. It did not say what a person needed.

"I should ask them," she said, almost to herself.

"Ask who?"

"Everyone," said Ada. "I'll ask each reader where they want to sit. I should have done that from the start."

Ben smiled. He put his chair card back in his pocket. "I'll help you," he said.

## Chapter 4: Where Do You Want to Sit?

On Friday morning, Ada began to ask.

She had made a paper list with a line for every reader and one question across the top: WHERE DO YOU WANT TO SIT? Ben carried a clipboard. They walked around Room 6 before the bell, and they asked each person one at a time.

"Where would you like to sit?" Ada asked Amara.

"I'd like the shade," said Amara. "It's so hot."

"Why?"

"Because the sun makes me squint when I read," said Amara.

Ada wrote it down. She asked Tomas, who said he would like to sit by his friend Luis. She asked Luis, who said the same. She asked Nia and Sam, who said they would like a spot where they could talk about their dragon book. She asked Jun, who said he would like it quiet.

She asked Priya, who said, "I'd like the tree, please. Not the middle one. Any shady one."

She asked Mateo, who said, "I'd like the tree too, and I promise I won't argue."

Ben wrote very fast. When they finished, the clipboard was full. They sat on the steps to read the answers.

"Look," said Ben. "Fourteen readers want the shade."

"It's a hot week," said Ada.

"Five want to sit with a friend."

"Three want quiet."

"Two want to be in the sun," said Ben. "Nia wants to feel the warm grass on her toes."

Ada counted them again. It was strange. She had made a plan for twenty-four seats, and every seat had a name. But when she asked, the answers were nothing like the names. Priya had not wanted the sunny cushion. Mateo had not wanted the shady chair. Nobody had even said that fairness was the most important thing. They had said what they liked, and each answer was different.

"I thought I knew what people wanted," said Ada.

"Most people don't know what other people want," said Ben. "That's why you ask."

"I wish I'd asked on Monday."

"Then you'd have a different plan," said Ben. "This one is better."

Ada looked at the clipboard. For once, she was not afraid of a mistake. She was only curious. She wanted to see what the answers could do.

"I'll draw a new plan," she said. "Fourteen readers want the shade, so I'll move nearly everyone under the tree. The friends can sit beside each other. A few readers can have the sun."

"That sounds like a lot of changes," said Ben.

"It is," said Ada. "That's the point."

By the time lunch ended, she had drawn a new plan with a pencil and a ruler. It had the maple tree in the center and a wide circle of shade full of seats. It had a few cushions in the sun for the readers who wanted warmth.

It was a better plan than the first, and it did what she had learned to do. It took what the readers said and tried to make a place for it.

Ada tucked the drawing under her arm and walked into the courtyard to look at the real thing. She did not yet know what Ben was about to say.

## Chapter 5: A Place for Everyone

Ada stood in the courtyard with her seating drawing. "I put everyone under the tree," she said. Ben held up his book. "But I cannot hear my partner when everyone talks there." Ada lowered her pencil. "Where would you choose?" Ben pointed to the far bench. Together they marked one place for partners and another for quiet reading. "A question can improve a careful drawing," Ada said. "And one place need not suit everyone." The readers chose their places.

Ada watched Priya take a blanket beneath the maple and settle in beside Amara. She watched Nia and Sam carry their dragon book to the talking place by the wall, where they could whisper in peace. She watched Jun walk to the far bench. He sat with his chin on his knees, and his eyes began to close.

Mateo came last. He looked at the tree seat, and then at the two places the club had marked. "Where should I sit?" he asked.

"Where would you choose?" said Ada.

Mateo thought. "By Tomas," he said. "We're going to read the same book about a hamster who flies a plane."

"Then sit by Tomas," said Ada.

It was a short answer, and Mateo grinned all the same. He took his cushion over to where Tomas and Luis were sitting at the edge of the shade, and he flopped on it.

By ten o'clock, every reader had a spot. It was a quiet kind of happiness. The courtyard was full of small sounds: a page turning, a whisper, a bee in the beans. A breeze came through the maple. The shadows moved a little on the grass.

Ms. Alvarez came out of the classroom with her own book and a cup of tea. She stopped at the gate. She looked at the tree, the benches, the blankets and the cushions. She listened to the quiet and the talk.

"Ada," she said, "did you plan all this?"

"We planned some of it," said Ada. "The rest the readers planned."

"How did you know where everyone would want to sit?"

"I didn't," said Ada. "I asked."

Ms. Alvarez smiled. She took a seat in the middle of the courtyard, not under the best tree but in the bright part of the grass. "I'd like to sit in the sun," she said. "Is that allowed?"

"Of course," said Ada. "That's the whole idea."

Ben was sitting beside her on the stone wall with his notebook open. He had written a page of questions, and he was reading them over. Ada leaned in to look.

The first question said: What do readers want that I haven't thought of?

The second one said: Who asks the next question?

"The next question," said Ada, "is going to be yours."

"Is it?" said Ben.

"It always is," said Ada. She closed her pencil case. She did not need to draw anything else.

Behind them, the courtyard filled with reading, and everyone had somewhere to be.
