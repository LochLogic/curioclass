# The Courtyard Club: The Missing Plan

## Chapter 1: Two Plans

The Courtyard Club was not a big club. It had five members, one watering can, and a picnic table in the small garden behind Room 6. The members met there at lunch to water the bean plants and talk about nothing in particular.

On Monday, Ms. Alvarez came out to the picnic table with a roll of paper under her arm. "I have a job for you," she said. "Our new books arrive on Reading Day, and they need a home. Will you plan a reading corner for our classroom?"

"Yes!" said Mateo, before anyone else could answer.

Ms. Alvarez smiled. "A good plan has two parts. It shows where the corner goes. It also shows a route, with arrows, so all twenty-four of us can walk from the door to the books without bumping into a single desk. Reading Day is a week from Friday. Please show me the plan before then."

She handed the roll of paper to Ada. Ada was the member who could draw. She could draw a straight line without a ruler and a circle without a plate. She was also a person who liked things to be right the first time. That habit would cause some trouble before the week was over.

Ada spread the paper on the picnic table and held down the corners with four bean pots. Ben sat beside her with his sandwich. Ben was the member who asked questions. He asked them the way other people ate snacks, all day long and without thinking much about it.

"Where should the corner go?" he asked.

"Beside the door," said Ada at once. "That way everyone can find it the second they walk in."

"Good idea," said Priya, who was watering the beans.

Ada drew the classroom first. She drew the long wall with the windows and the short wall with the cupboard. She drew the door, a small gap in the wall at the left. Then she drew the reading corner right beside the door. It had a rug, a tall shelf, a round table and three beanbags. She added the arrows last. They began at the door and led along the wall to the rug. There were four arrows in all, and she was proud of every one.

"There," she said. "Finished."

Jun leaned over to look. "It looks like a real plan," he said.

Mateo nodded. "I want the green beanbag."

Ben chewed his sandwich and looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he stood up.

"Ada," he said, "can I check something?"

"Check what? It's finished."

"I know. I just want to see the door."

Ben walked across the courtyard and into the empty classroom. Ada followed him, because she could not stand to wait. Priya, Jun and Mateo followed her, because they could not stand to miss anything.

Ben stood in the doorway. "Which way does this door swing?" he asked.

"Into the room," said Ada. "Everyone knows that."

"Then watch." Ben opened the door slowly. It swung into the room and across the floor, and it did not stop until it hit the place where Ada had drawn the rug.

Nobody said anything. The door sat there against the beanbag-sized space. A dust speck floated down and landed on the floor, as if it had come to look too.

"Oh," said Ada.

"It would knock over the shelf," said Priya.

"It would flatten the green beanbag," said Mateo, sadly.

Ada's face turned pink. For a moment she looked as if she might tear the plan in half. Then she took a breath. "Fine," she said. "I'll draw it again."

She went back to the picnic table and drew a second plan on a clean sheet. This time she put the reading corner far away from the door, in the sunny corner by the windows. She drew the rug, the shelf, the round table and the beanbags again. She added new arrows that led from the door, along the wall past the cupboard, all the way to the window corner. She drew them carefully. It took her the rest of lunch.

When the bell rang, two plans lay side by side on the picnic table. They showed the same room and the same rug and the same furniture. The corners were in two different places.

"Which one is right?" asked Ben.

Ada looked from one drawing to the other. The first plan was wrong, she knew that. It had a door problem. But the second plan looked right, and the first one had looked right too.

"I don't know yet," she said quietly.

She did not like the feeling. It was a little like being asked a question and finding that her mind was empty.

## Chapter 2: The Old Drawing

At the end of lunch, Ada picked up the first plan and began to crumple it.

"Wait," said Ben. "What are you doing?"

"I'm throwing it away. It's wrong."

"It's wrong about the door," said Ben. "It might not be wrong about everything."

"A wrong plan is no use to anyone," said Ada. She held the ball of paper over the bin.

Ben held out his hand. "Then may I have it?"

Ada gave it to him. She thought he was being silly, and so, if we are honest, did the narrator. A wrong drawing is a wrong drawing, after all. It only takes up room.

Ben smoothed the page flat on his knee, pressing out the wrinkles with the side of his hand. Then he took a folder from his bag. It was a plain blue folder with the word PLANS on the front in his careful, crooked letters. He clipped the old drawing inside the cover. Beside it he clipped the new one.

"Now they're side by side," he said. "If we ever forget why we moved the corner, we can look."

"We won't forget," said Ada.

"Maybe not." Ben shrugged. "But I like to know what changed."

That afternoon, Ms. Alvarez let the club use the last half hour of the day to plan. Ada sat at the back table with the folder open in front of her. She tried not to look at the old drawing. It sat there in the cover, wrong and wrinkled.

A draft is a first try. It is the version that comes before the better one, and it is supposed to be wrong in a few places. That is what a draft is for. Even so, Ada did not enjoy looking at hers.

"I think the new plan is better," said Priya, peering over her shoulder. "The window corner is quiet."

"Quiet is good," said Jun. He liked to read with his chin on his knees and his eyes half closed. "But I'd like a big table so a few of us can read together."

"I'd like more beanbags," said Mateo.

"We can't have everything," said Ada. She chewed the end of her pencil. "The corner isn't big. If we put in a big table, there won't be room for beanbags. And if we put in beanbags, there won't be room for a table."

"What about a small table?" said Ben.

Ada thought about that. She knew where there was one. It was a little round table in the storeroom, with a green top and one wobbly leg. She had seen it on Friday when she carried the paper. It was just the right size for the window corner. Better still, a small table would leave room for the rug and the shelf and three beanbags. Everything would fit. The plan would look tidy and clear and complete.

"I choose the small table," she said, and she wrote it on the plan in large letters: ROUND TABLE (SMALL). "It fits the corner exactly."

"Does it fit two people?" asked Ben.

"It fits the corner," said Ada again.

Ben opened his mouth to ask something else. Then he closed it and wrote in his own small notebook instead. He did that when he wanted to remember a question for later.

By the time the last bell rang, Ada had drawn a neat third copy of the plan with the window corner, the small table and the four arrows. She checked each arrow with her pencil. They led from the door along the wall and past the cupboard to the rug. She did not check the last arrow twice. Why would she? It looked fine.

Ben packed the folder into his bag. He did not say anything about the notebook.

Before the bell, Ms. Alvarez stopped by the back table with a long yellow measuring tape in her hand. "Would this help?" she asked. "A good plan has real numbers on it."

Ada looked at the tape. It was the kind that rolls back into its case with a snap. "I can see how big the corner is," she said. "It is about the size of two beanbags and a table."

"Then the tape will only agree with you," said Ms. Alvarez, and she smiled and set it down.

Ada did not pick it up. Ben did. He measured the shelf, the rug and the round table, calling out numbers while Priya wrote them in the margin. The numbers did not seem important. Ada thought they were a little like the old drawing, something that Ben liked and she did not need. She was wrong about that, as she would soon find out. But she did not know it yet, so she went on feeling sure.

Ada did not say anything about the old drawing either. But as she walked to the bus, she found herself thinking of it, folded in the blue cover beside the better plan. It was wrong, but it had not hurt anything.

That was a strange thought, and she was not sure what to do with it. So she put it away, the way you put away a shoe that does not fit and might fit later.

## Chapter 3: A Small Table

On Tuesday afternoon, the Courtyard Club carried furniture.

It was not easy. The round table came out of the storeroom first, with Mateo pulling it and Jun pushing. Its wobbly leg wobbled all the way down the hall. Priya brought the rug, rolled up like a sleeping cat. Ada carried the shelf's little signs. Ben brought the folder and the beanbags, one under each arm.

"Careful with the green one," said Mateo.

"It's a beanbag," said Ben. "It's very hard to break."

They set the corner up exactly the way the plan said. They put the shelf against the wall under the windows. They unrolled the rug. They placed the round table at the end of the rug, and the beanbags on the floor around it. Ada stepped back to look.

It was beautiful. The sun came through the windows and fell on the green table top. The corner looked like a place in a picture book. Everything was in the right spot and nothing was out of line.

"It's perfect," said Ada.

"It is nice," said Priya, and she meant it.

Ada folded her arms and felt very pleased with herself. "Let's see if it works," she said. "We should each take a book and sit down. Here, I'll pick the first people."

At that moment, the classroom door opened. Two readers from the next class walked in. They were Nia and Sam, who often borrowed a book from Room 6 at the end of the day. Both of them were carrying the same book, a thick one with a dragon on the cover. They had been sharing it all week.

"Is this the new corner?" asked Nia.

"It's the new corner," said Ada. "Please look around."

Nia sat down on the green beanbag. Sam went to sit at the round table. Then Sam stopped.

There was only one chair at the table, and it was the one Ada had drawn. The table was small, and the plan had room for one chair, and one chair was all that fit.

"Where do I sit?" said Nia. She was already holding the book out toward Sam.

"On the beanbag," said Ada.

"But I want to read it together," said Nia. "We take turns turning the pages."

Sam looked at the lonely chair and the small table and the beanbag. "Nobody can read together here," Sam said politely. "There is no place for two."

Ada did not answer. She stared at the table. She had thought of one reader at a time, one person with one book and one chair. She had never thought of two people sharing a book.

"Oh," she said. She felt the way you feel when you are walking quickly and meet a wall that you did not know was there. "My plan hit a wall."

Mateo looked at the wall. "Which wall?" he said. "The wall by the window? It's right where it was."

"Not that wall," said Priya gently. "She means her plan ran into a problem."

"Oh," said Mateo. He looked relieved. "I thought she was talking about the actual wall."

Ben did not laugh. He opened his small notebook and turned to the page where he had written the question on Monday. Then he held it up so Ada could see it. It said: Does the table fit two people?

Ada looked at it. She felt her cheeks go hot. It was only a small question, and it was in pencil. It had been waiting for her all this time.

"You asked me," she said. "Yesterday, you asked me, and I didn't listen."

"I didn't say it very loudly," said Ben.

"It would have helped if you had," said Ada, but she said it with a half smile.

Nia and Sam took turns on the beanbag, reading their dragon book with the cover held open between them. It worked, but not very well. They sat close together, and each had to lean in to see the words. When Sam turned the page, Nia's elbow got squeezed against the shelf.

"It's fine," said Sam. "It's just not great."

"It's a start," said Priya.

But Ada was thinking hard. She looked at the table and the single chair and the cramped beanbags. She looked at the plan in the folder. The plan did not show anything wrong. It showed a neat drawing, with a table and a chair and four arrows.

"The plan is fine," she said slowly, "but the corner isn't."

"Maybe the plan needs a second chair," said Jun.

"Maybe it needs a bigger table," said Priya.

Ada shook her head. "Not yet," she said. "I need to think."

She went to sit by herself on the green beanbag and stared at the ceiling. The rest of the club carried the extra things back to the storeroom. Nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that Ada had a lot to think about.

Ms. Alvarez, passing in the hall, glanced through the door and saw her sitting alone. She did not go in. She only paused for a moment, nodded to herself, and walked on. Some problems are best thought about without help for a little while. Others are not. A teacher has to guess which is which, and Ms. Alvarez guessed that this one would be all right.

## Chapter 4: The Door That Would Not Open

On Wednesday, Ada announced that the corner was ready.

She had spent Tuesday night fixing it. She had drawn a new plan, a fourth one, with a larger round table and two chairs. She had moved the shelf a little to make room. She had even added a sign: READ TOGETHER HERE. She marched into Room 6 before the club arrived and set everything up herself.

When the others came in, they stopped and stared.

"It looks great," said Priya.

"It's ready," said Ada. "Everything is exactly where the plan says. We can show Ms. Alvarez this afternoon."

"May I just try something?" asked Ben.

Ada groaned. "Ben."

"One thing. A small thing." He walked back to the door, took hold of the handle and pulled.

The door opened a little way and then stopped. It would not open all the way. A corner of the tall shelf stuck out into its path, and the door bumped against it with a soft thump.

"There," said Ben. "That's what I thought. The door can't open fully."

"It opens enough," said Ada.

"It opens enough for one person. We have twenty-four people. Ms. Alvarez said everyone has to walk in on Reading Day."

"They can walk in a line."

"Through a gap this wide?" Ben held out his hands. The gap was about as wide as a bookshelf. "Somebody's backpack is going to get stuck."

Mateo tried to squeeze through the gap with his backpack on. He got stuck, as everybody had known he would, and had to wiggle backward. "Ben is right," he said, somewhat muffled.

Ada pressed her lips together. She had spent a whole night on this corner. She had worked on it carefully and had not made a single mistake that she could find. And now here was Ben, standing at the door, talking about a door.

To tell the truth, it was a nuisance. It is always a nuisance to be told about a problem when you thought you were done. A kinder person might say that Ben was only trying to help. The narrator, however, was not feeling kind. The narrator thought Ben could have picked a better moment.

"The plan is right," said Ada. "It's the room that is wrong."

Priya laughed. She could not help it. Then she covered her mouth and said, "Sorry."

"It's funny," said Ben, "but the plan has the shelf here." He pointed at the page. "And the shelf is really there." He pointed at the floor. "They don't match."

"They matched when I drew it!"

"Maybe the shelf moved when you moved it."

Ada opened her mouth and closed it. She had moved the shelf, in fact, at ten o'clock the night before. She had meant to draw the new place on the plan, and then she had been tired and had forgotten.

It was not a large mistake. It was exactly the kind of mistake that a person can make when she is trying hard to be perfect.

"I'll fix it after school," she said. Her voice came out stiff. "I'll bring the plan home and fix it tonight. You don't have to wait."

"We can fix it together," said Ben.

"No," said Ada. "I'll do it."

Ben looked at her. He looked, for a moment, as if he wanted to ask something. But he only said, "Okay."

That afternoon, Ms. Alvarez came by to see the corner. She walked around the shelf and sat in one of the chairs. She asked, "Does the plan show the shelf where it is now?"

"Almost," said Ada.

"Almost?"

"I'm fixing it tonight."

Ms. Alvarez nodded. "Bring it to the club tomorrow and we'll look at it together."

When the bell rang, Ada took the plan out of the folder. She did not take the old drawing, which stayed clipped where Ben had put it. She took only the newest plan, folded it in half and in half again, and pushed it deep into the pocket of her backpack, behind her lunch box.

"See you tomorrow," she said to Ben.

"See you tomorrow," said Ben. He looked at the empty place in the folder. Then he looked at the door, which stood open only a little way, as if it were not sure whether to let anyone in.

Meanwhile, on the bus, Ada was holding her backpack on her lap. She could feel the folded plan through the cloth, as small and stiff as a postcard. She told herself that she would fix the shelf as soon as she got home. She told herself that she would look at the arrows and find nothing wrong. By the time the bus reached her stop, she had told herself so many things that she could not tell which of them she believed.


## Chapter 5: The Hidden Plan

At lunch on Thursday, the Courtyard Club met on the picnic table. Ben opened his blue folder and found the page where the plan should have been. There was only the old drawing, clipped to the cover.

"Ada," he said, "where's the plan?"

Ada did not answer. She was watering a bean plant that had already been watered.

"Ada?"

"I'm busy," said Ada.

"Ms. Alvarez wants to see it today."

"I know." She watered the bean again. The water ran over the sides of the pot and made a small brown puddle.

Ben did not say anything else. After a while, Priya, Jun and Mateo drifted over, and they waited too, and the whole club stood around, looking at Ada's back.

Here is the truth. Ada had taken the plan home on Wednesday night. She had spread it on her bedroom floor and looked at it very carefully. She had fixed the shelf. Then she had looked at the arrows. And the more she looked at the arrows, the less she liked the last one.

Something about the last arrow was wrong. She could not say what. It pointed somewhere that looked right and felt wrong, like a word that you have spelled so many times it no longer looks like a word. She did not know if the arrow was bad or if she was only tired. But she knew one thing. She did not want anyone else to see it until she knew it was right.

So she had hidden the plan. She had put it at the bottom of her backpack. And all morning she had carried it, as heavy as a rock.

That was the whole of the trouble, and here it is again in the order it happened. Ada hid the plan because she feared its arrow was wrong. Ben waited by the empty table. Ada sighed; her plan had fallen flat. "My heart sank," Ada said. Ben asked to see the arrow before she erased it. The narrator says, "Ben was wasting time." Ada finally unfolded the page.

That is the quick way to tell it. The slow way is longer, because the club was slow.

Priya had asked kindly what had happened, and Ada had said the same thing twice. She had looked at the plan last night, and she did not know what to do.

Mateo frowned. "Your heart sank? Is it okay?"

"It's a way of talking," said Jun. "It means she felt bad. Like when you drop something and know it's going to break."

"Oh," said Mateo. "Is that what it means?"

Ada nodded. She had not looked up once. "I think the last arrow is wrong. I think I should erase it and start over. Alone. By myself. Where nobody can see."

Ben's request had been a quiet one, but it made Ada angrier than if he had shouted. "Why?" she said. "It's wrong. I told you. If I erase it, it won't matter."

"It might matter," said Ben. "I'd like to see what's wrong with it. If we don't look, how will we know what to change?"

"That's wasting time," said Ada.

It was the narrator's opinion as well. The narrator thought Ben was wasting time. The lunch hour was nearly half over, the plan was due that afternoon and a person who really wanted to fix an arrow would simply erase it and draw a new one. Looking at the old one was only slow.

Ben did not seem to mind being slow. He sat down next to the bean pots with his folder on his knees. He did not push. He did not ask a second time. He only waited, and he made it very plain that he would go on waiting for as long as it took.

Priya sat beside him. Then Jun did. Mateo, after a moment, sat on the grass with a sigh.

The bell was not going to ring for another twelve minutes. A bee hummed around the beans. Somewhere inside the school, a class was singing.

Ada stood very still. She had never felt so much like a person with a secret. It was a heavy secret, and it was made of a piece of paper.

At last, she knelt down, unzipped her backpack and took out the plan. She held it for a moment against her chest. Then she held it out toward the club, still folded, the way you hold out something that might be too hot.

The page was open at last, and the arrows were all there. The shelf was in the new place. The round table was drawn large and the signs were drawn small. It was neat, and it was careful, and for the first moment nobody could see what was wrong at all.

"I don't see it," said Mateo.

"Neither do I," said Priya.

"That's the problem," said Ada. She sounded close to tears. "I've looked all night and I can't see it either."

Ben leaned forward. He did not look at the arrow first. He looked at Ada.

"Then it's a good thing we're looking together," he said.

Ada let out a long breath. It was the first one she had let out since Wednesday.

## Chapter 6: Signs

Once the plan was on the picnic table, everything felt lighter.

It was strange. The page had not changed. The last arrow was still sitting there, as puzzling as ever. But the picnic table felt friendlier, and the bean plants looked greener, and Mateo found a cookie in his backpack and gave a quarter of it to each member.

"We can't fix it today," said Ben. "The bell is about to ring, and we have a lot to do for Ms. Alvarez. But we can keep the plan out. Nobody will hide it."

"I won't hide it," said Ada. "I promise."

"Then let's do the signs," said Priya. "We can do those now."

The signs were Ada's idea, and a good one. Every corner of a classroom needs a few signs. Without them, a reader who walks in has to guess where to go and what to do. The club had decided to make four, and each one was to be short and clear.

Ada took four cards and a thick marker. She wrote the first sign quickly. It said:

QUIET READERS SIT HERE.

"Good," said Jun. "Quiet readers is a nice phrase. Quiet tells what kind of readers."

"That's an adjective," said Priya. "We did them in school. An adjective describes a noun."

"Readers is the noun," said Jun. "It names the people."

Ada capped the marker and looked at the sign. It was simple, and she was glad. She started on the second card.

THE CAREFUL READER CHECKS THE ARROW. SHE READS SLOWLY.

"Wait," said Ben. "Who is she?"

"The careful reader," said Ada. "She stands for any reader. I use she so I don't have to say the careful reader again."

"That's a pronoun," said Priya. "She takes the place of the noun."

"And checks is the verb," said Jun. "It's what the reader does."

"And slowly tells how she reads," said Ben. "That's an adverb."

Mateo looked from one friend to another. "You are all talking like a grammar book," he said. "I just want to read."

"Then you will like this sign," said Ada. She wrote the third card.

BORROW A BOOK. RETURN IT SOON.

"Short," said Mateo, pleased. "I like short."

For the fourth card, she thought for a long time. Then she wrote the longest sign of the set.

THE READERS PUT THEIR BOOKS BACK ON THE SHELF.

"Hang on," said Ben. "That's not the same as your other sign."

"The other sign was about one reader," said Ada. "This one is about many. See? The readers put. Not puts. When there is more than one reader, the verb changes. And their books, not her books, because the readers are many."

"Nice," said Priya, who liked rules. "The pronoun matches the noun, and the verb matches the subject."

"I learned it last year," said Ada. "I forgot until just now."

They spread the four signs out on the grass to dry. They were not perfect. One of them had a smudge. But they were clear, and a reader who walked up to them would know what to do.

Ada sat back and looked at the signs. She felt something she had not felt all week. She felt calm.

"Why do I like signs so much?" she asked.

"Because they say things plainly," said Ben. "You don't have to guess. You read the words and you know."

"Plans are like that too," said Ada.

"They're supposed to be," said Ben. "But they have arrows, and arrows can say two things at once. A word can't point two ways."

Ada nodded slowly. She put the lid on the marker.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'll look at the last arrow with you."

"Good," said Ben. "But first, you need to show Ms. Alvarez the plan."

Ada's stomach gave a small flip. "Do I have to show her the plan with the wrong arrow?"

Ben thought about it. "You could tell her about the arrow," he said. "You could say that the club is checking it. That's true."

"Would she mind?"

"I don't know," said Ben. "I can ask her if you want me to."

"No," said Ada, and she was surprised to find that she meant it. "I'll ask her myself."

At the end of lunch, she walked back to Room 6 with the plan in her hand, unfolded and flat. She did not hold it behind her back. She held it in front of her where anyone could see it.

Ms. Alvarez looked at it for a long moment. She did not say it was perfect. She did not say it was wrong. She said, "What does the club think of it so far?"

"We like most of it," said Ada. "We aren't sure about the last arrow."

"Then keep looking," said Ms. Alvarez. "That sounds like exactly what a plan needs."

Back at the corner, the club taped the four signs in their places. Mateo walked up to each one and read it aloud in a deep voice, like a news reporter.

"Quiet readers sit here," he announced. "The careful reader checks the arrow. She reads slowly." He stopped. "Hmm. Which arrow?"

"Any arrow," said Ada. "But right now, one in particular."

Mateo looked at the plan. Then he looked at Ada. "The last one?"

"Maybe."

"I could look at it," he offered. "I have excellent eyes."

"You can't find your own shoes," said Priya.

"My eyes are for far things," said Mateo, with dignity, "not near ones."

Everyone laughed, even Ada, and the laugh felt like a window being opened in a stuffy room.

## Chapter 7: The Blocked Shelf

On Friday morning, Ada came to school early. Ms. Alvarez let the club in before the bell, because the corner still needed work and Reading Day was only a week away.

Ben was already there. He was crouched in front of the low shelf with his arm stretched out as far as it would go.

"What are you doing?" asked Ada.

"I'm trying to get a book," Ben said. "It's the red one with the bear. Priya wants it for the corner, and I said I'd bring it."

Ada looked. The shelf was low, and the red book was on the bottom row, near the back. The round table stood in front of it, only a hand's width away. Ben's long arm could not reach under it, and he could not move the table without knocking over the beanbag.

"Ouch," said Ben. He had bumped his elbow on the table leg.

Ada put down her backpack. She knew that table. She had chosen it herself. She had carried it down the hall with Mateo, and she had said, with a lot of confidence, that it fit the corner exactly.

It fit the corner, but not the shelf. It stood right in front of the bottom row, where every reader would have to reach.

She remembered something. On Tuesday, Sam had said that there was no place for two. On Wednesday, Ben had said that the door could not open all the way. On Thursday, Ben had waited by an empty table and asked to see an arrow. Three times, Ben had pointed out a problem. Three times, Ada had been the last person to see it.

This time, though, she had seen it first.

"Ben," she said, "can you reach it?"

Ben looked up. It was a surprising question. Usually he was the one who asked.

"Not really," he said.

"Then I'll move the table." Ada took hold of the edge. "Wait. Where do you want it?"

"I don't know," said Ben. "Where do you think?"

Ada looked at the corner. She looked at the shelf and the window and the rug. She tried to see it as a reader would see it, a reader who needed to stand and reach and sit down with a book.

"Over there," she said, pointing to the other end of the rug. "That way the shelf is clear, and the table is close to the beanbags but out of the way."

They carried it together. The wobbly leg wobbled, but nobody minded. Ben crouched down, reached under the shelf and drew out the red book. He held it up like a prize.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome," said Ada.

She stood back to look at the corner, and her stomach did a funny thing. The corner looked different. It was not quite as tidy as before. The table was off-center now, and the rug had a gap near the window. But it looked like a place where somebody could actually read.

"It's not exactly like the plan," said Ada.

"No," said Ben.

"Do you think I should change the plan?"

"Maybe," said Ben. "Or you could keep both. The first one and this one. Then we can see what we did."

Ada laughed. It was a real laugh, the kind that surprises you when it comes out. "You and your folder," she said.

"The folder is useful," said Ben, with dignity.

When the others arrived, Ada took out a clean card and wrote a short note for the plan. She wrote it in pencil, so she could change it. It said: Table moved so readers can reach the bottom shelf. Ask readers what else gets in the way.

Priya read the note over her shoulder. "That's a good idea," she said. "Ask readers. We have not done that."

"We should," said Mateo. "I know a few readers."

"I know a few more," said Jun.

It was an odd thing, but the corner felt friendlier. Priya's red book sat on the shelf beside the others, and its bear faced forward. Ada looked at the bear for a moment. Then she took out her pencil and added a very small drawing of it to the corner of the plan.

When the others came in, Priya noticed the table first. "You moved it!" she said. "It looks like a different room."

"Is that good?" asked Ada.

Priya turned in a slow circle. "It looks like a place where I could sit for an hour," she said. "Before, it looked like a place where I could sit for a minute."

Ada thought about that all morning. It was a good way to say it. A neat plan could be a plan to look at. A good corner was a place to stay.

A short story might end there, with a table and a book and a friendly corner. But this was a longer story, and Ada still had a last arrow to fix. She had not forgotten it. She only needed one more thing, and it was courage.

She tucked the plan into the folder, next to the old drawing, and closed the cover.

"Monday," she said. "We'll look at the arrow on Monday."

"Okay," said Ben.

"Both of us."

"Both of us," Ben agreed.

## Chapter 8: The Quiet Readers

On Monday, the club held a meeting.

It was an official meeting, with an agenda. Priya wrote it on the back of a sign card. The first item was "Where do the quiet readers sit?" The second was "Beanbag colors." The last was "The arrow," which Ada had asked to put at the very end.

"Quiet readers first," said Jun. He was a quiet reader himself. "Some people like to read near the door because it's easy to leave. Some people like the window."

"The window is nicer," said Priya.

"The door is closer to the hall," said Mateo. "You can see who's coming."

"That's the problem," said Jun. "I don't want to see who's coming. I want to read."

Ada held up her hand. "We can vote," she said. "I'll write the choices."

She wrote two lines on the card. WINDOW. DOOR. Then each member of the club made a small mark beside a choice. Four marks went next to WINDOW, and one went next to DOOR. The group chose the window corner. Mateo's mark was the lonely one by DOOR, and he held up his hands with a cheerful shrug.

"Do I get to say something?" asked Mateo. "It isn't fair that the window gets all the marks."

"It is fair," said Priya. "You voted too."

"I voted for the door."

"And the door lost," said Jun gently. "But you can still sit by it. That's the nice part."

Mateo thought about this. "So losing a vote doesn't mean losing the thing?"

"Not if we share," said Ada. She was surprised to hear herself say it. A week ago she would have wanted her own choice on the plan and no other.

"The quiet readers share a plan now," said Ben.

"They share it with the loud ones," said Mateo. "I like the loud ones better."

"Then you may sit by the door," said Ada. "There's a sign for that, too."

She walked to the shelf to put up the new labels. There were three of them: STORIES, ANIMALS and POEMS. She had written them on Friday in her best handwriting. Now she stuck each one to the edge of a shelf with a strip of tape.

Ben was already walking along the shelves with his finger. He pulled out a book at random from the shelf marked ANIMALS. It was a book of stories about a girl and a robot.

"Ada," he said, "this one isn't an animal book."

"What?"

"It's a story. See?" He held it up. "It belongs on STORIES."

Ada took the book and looked at it. It was a story. Then she looked at the shelf. She saw that her sign, ANIMALS, was taped to the shelf that held the stories. The animal books were on the shelf marked STORIES.

"I switched them," she said. She pressed her lips together. "I switched the labels."

"It's easy to do," said Ben. "They look the same."

"It's the kind of mistake I make," said Ada, slowly. She was not angry, exactly. She was surprised. "I check everything, and I still make mistakes."

"Everyone makes mistakes," said Priya. "Checking is how you find them."

"If we hadn't looked," said Jun, "somebody would have taken home a book about a robot, looking for a rabbit."

"That would have been sad," said Mateo. "I like rabbits."

Ada peeled off the labels and put them where they belonged. This time she asked Ben to hold each one while she pressed the tape. When both of them were sure, she stepped back.

"There," she said. "Now they match."

"Thank you for checking," Ada said to Ben.

"Thank you for letting me," said Ben.

The club finished the meeting with the second item, which was beanbag colors. This took ten minutes and ended with a green one by the window, a red one by the shelf and a yellow one that Mateo carried back to the door "for the loud readers." Everyone agreed. It was a fair plan.

When they reached the last item, Ada stared at the agenda. THE ARROW. She felt her stomach flutter.

"We can look at the arrow tomorrow," she said, feeling like a coward. "It's nearly lunch."

"Tomorrow," said Ben. He did not argue. But he wrote the word in his notebook and underlined it twice.

"The readers who come here will like this corner," said Priya.

"I think they will," said Ada.

She meant it. She stood for a while in front of the shelves. The quiet readers had a place by the window. The loud readers had a place by the door. The labels matched the books. It was a good corner, and it was getting better, one small check at a time.

Only the last arrow remained.

## Chapter 9: Once More

The next morning, Ada stood in front of the shelves and sighed.

"We should check once more," she said.

"Check what?" asked Mateo. He was lying on the yellow beanbag with a book on his face.

"The labels. The shelves. Everything. We found a mistake yesterday. There could be another one."

"We found it and fixed it," said Mateo, from under the book.

"Maybe there are more."

Mateo lifted the book. "Ada, you worry too much."

It is the narrator's opinion that this was true. Ada did worry too much. She worried about arrows and labels and wobbly table legs. She worried when things were wrong and she worried when things were right, in case they were wrong in a way she had not noticed. It must have been very tiring to be inside her head.

"I'm not worried," said Ada. "I'm being careful."

"It's the same thing, only with a hat," said Mateo.

"It isn't," said Ben. He had been quiet, reading at the table. Now he put his finger in his book and looked up. "Yesterday, checking found a mistake. It found the mixed-up labels. That's not worry. That's something useful."

"It's a nuisance," said Mateo.

"Not if you're the person who wants a rabbit book," said Ben.

Priya giggled. Jun, who had been listening from the window, said, "I think both of you are partly right."

"How can we both be right?" asked Mateo.

"Because checking helps," said Jun. "But checking takes time. It's good when it finds a mistake. It's boring when it finds nothing."

"That's true," said Mateo, mollified.

"But we don't know if it will find something," said Ada. "That's the whole reason to check."

Ben nodded slowly. "So the question," he said, "is how much checking is enough."

"How much is enough?" said Priya.

They stood in a loose circle, and each person thought about it. It was a good question, and it did not have one answer.

Ms. Alvarez, who had been marking papers at her desk, looked up. "May I offer an idea?" she asked. "A person who bakes one cake can taste it once. A person who bakes for the whole school should taste it more than once."

"So how much checking depends on how many people are coming," said Jun.

"On how much could go wrong," said Ms. Alvarez, "and for how many people." She went back to her papers.

The club stood quietly. "Twenty-four people are going to walk our arrows," said Ben.

"That's a whole school's worth of cake," said Priya.

"I think," said Priya, "that we check the things that could hurt someone or stop a reader. A wrong label is a small problem. A blocked door is a big one."

"I agree," said Jun. "And I'd say we check the things that are new. The labels were new yesterday, so we checked. The arrows are new too."

"The arrows are old," said Mateo. "Ada drew them on Monday."

"We haven't checked them," said Ben. "Not really."

Everybody looked at Ada. She felt very small and very honest.

"I haven't wanted to," she said. "I'm afraid it's wrong."

"Then it's the one thing we should check," said Ben. "Not because you worry too much. Because it's the one thing we haven't done."

Ada stared at the floor. She knew he was right, and she knew she had been avoiding it for days. It was easier to check labels than arrows. Labels were small and arrows were scary.

She decided that Mateo could think what he liked. He did not have to walk through that door on Reading Day with twenty-four classmates and a plan that did not work.

"Okay," she said. "Let's check the arrows."

Mateo let out a groan. But he sat up, and he put the book down, and he said, "Fine. If it will make you stop saying 'once more.'"

"It won't," said Ben.

"It won't," said Ada, cheerfully. And for the first time in days, she was smiling.

They did not check the arrows that day, though. The lunch bell rang before Ada could find her pencil, and a teacher came to say the club was needed in the courtyard to help with a visiting class. By the time the club got back, it was time to go home.

"Tomorrow," said Ada. She said it firmly. "I promise."

"You said that yesterday," said Mateo.

"I know," said Ada. "This time I'll mean it."

She did not know it yet, but tomorrow would be very different from what she expected. For one thing, it was the day Ben would put the plan on the shelf.

## Chapter 10: In the Dark

On Wednesday, Ben needed the table.

The club had to sort a pile of new books, and the round table was the only flat place big enough. The plan was lying on it, unfolded, with a bean pot on each corner. Ben picked it up.

"Where shall I put this?" he said.

"Anywhere," said Priya. "Just not on the floor."

Ben looked around. Then he reached up and slid the plan onto the top shelf of the tall bookcase by the wall. It was high above the heads of everybody except Ben and sat there like a flag.

"There," he said. "Safe."

The club sorted books for an hour. They put the picture books in one pile, the poetry books in another and the thick books in a third, the biggest pile of all. Mateo found a book about a hamster who flew a plane and refused to put it down.

"It has a map in the back," he said. "The hamster draws a map of every city. He uses arrows."

"Does he check them?" asked Ada.

Mateo turned the page. "He does not. He lands in a lake."

Everybody laughed, and Ada laughed hardest of all. It was a relief to laugh about arrows. She had been carrying that worry so long that she had forgotten it could be funny.

Near the end of the hour, Ada looked up at the top shelf.

"Ben," she said. "I need the plan."

"Okay," said Ben. He started to get up.

"No, wait. Just let me look." Ada stood on her toes and stared. She could see the plan's edge and a few arrows, but they were small and far and tangled, like ants on a distant wall. She stepped to one side. She stepped to the other. She narrowed her eyes.

"I can't follow them," she said. "I'm in the dark."

Mateo glanced at the windows. "It's a sunny day," he said. "It's very bright."

"I don't mean that kind of dark," said Ada. "I mean I don't know where the arrows go. I can't tell which one comes first."

"Oh," said Mateo. "I get it. You're in the dark about the arrows."

"Yes," said Ada. She sounded relieved to be understood. "Ben, could you explain it to me?"

Ben looked pleased. He took the plan down and spread it on the round table. Then he did something nobody had done before. He put his finger on the first arrow and traced it, very slowly, as if he were walking.

"I start at the door," he said. "Here's the first arrow. It points along the wall. So I walk along the wall."

"Yes," said Ada.

"The second one goes past the shelf. That's the one that used to bump the door. We fixed it."

"Yes."

"The third one goes past the cupboard. I turn here." Ben's finger turned. "And then there's the last one." He stopped.

"What?" said Ada.

"The last arrow doesn't go to the rug," said Ben slowly. "It points away from the door. It points straight at the cupboard."

Everyone leaned in. Ben was right. The last arrow, the one Ada had been afraid of for days, was bent at the wrong angle. It was supposed to curve toward the rug and the window corner. Instead, it pointed to the cupboard, a closed brown cupboard with a door that never opened.

She could see it clearly now, with Ben's finger beside it. In her bedroom, alone, she had looked at the page and seen only a blur of worry. Here, with a friend, she saw an arrow, a cupboard and a mistake that was small enough to fix.

"If somebody followed that arrow," said Priya, "they would walk into a cupboard."

"It's my fault," said Ada. She felt her face grow hot, and then she felt something else. It was not shame, and it was not fear. It was something more like surprise. "I knew it was wrong. I just didn't know how it was wrong. I couldn't see it."

"That's because you were looking at it from far away," said Ben. "Or because you were looking by yourself. I don't know."

"Probably both," said Ada. She sat down on a beanbag. "Why couldn't I see it?"

"Maybe it's hard to see your own mistakes," said Jun. "They hide in plain sight."

"Like a missing plan," said Mateo, and then he stopped, because he realized what he'd said. But Ada only laughed.

"Like a missing plan," she agreed.

She took the pencil from her pocket, and then she did not erase the arrow. She held the pencil above it for a while. Then she lowered it.

"Not yet," she said. "I want to see it work first. Or not work. I want to know exactly what goes wrong."

"We could walk it," said Ben. "Tomorrow, before school, when the room is empty."

"We could," said Ada. She looked at the plan, and then at Ben. He was waiting politely, with his notebook in his hand.

"Yes," she said at last. "Let's."

The bell rang. The club put the books back in the piles. Ben took the plan, folded it carefully in half, and slid it into the folder. This time, Ada saw him do it, and she did not feel the old rush of fear.

She felt something else, the way you feel just after you have stood up from a long sit: a little shaky, a little taller, and ready to go.

## Chapter 11: Before Everyone Comes

Ada reached school before anyone else. She stood in front of the classroom door, looking through the glass, and waited for Ben.

He came a few minutes later with the blue folder under his arm and his hair sticking up in the back. "Morning," he said.

"Morning," said Ada.

"Ready?"

"Not really," said Ada. "But let's go anyway."

The classroom was quiet and full of morning light. The desks stood in their rows. The cupboard stood in its place. The reading corner waited in the far corner of the room, with its green beanbag and its yellow one and its wobbly little table.

Ada spread her drawing on the classroom floor. "I kept it hidden because I wanted it right," she said. Ben traced the arrow toward the closed cupboard. "May we try it before everyone comes?" Ada handed him the pencil. They walked from the door to the reading corner, stopping at the cupboard. Ada turned the arrow. "A question can find what a careful drawing misses," she said. When the others arrived, they followed the changed route together.

Ada looked at what she had done. The arrow was a little crooked now, bent where she had turned it. She could still see the pencil line of the old arrow underneath, pale and faint, like a footprint in snow. She did not erase it.

"The old one's still there," said Ben.

"I know," said Ada. "I want it there."

By the time Priya, Jun and Mateo had followed it, the route had a new last arrow, a new neat copy and a new note on the top corner in Ada's best capital letters: PLEASE CHECK ME.

"What does that mean?" asked Mateo.

"It means I'm not the only one who looks," said Ada. "I want each of you to walk the route once more, on your own. Go slowly. If you bump into anything, or if an arrow points anywhere strange, tell me."

"Really?" said Priya. "Anything?"

"Anything," said Ada.

It was a brave thing to ask. Ben, who had been asking questions for years, had never heard her ask for them before. He smiled and wrote it in his notebook.

Priya went first. She followed the arrows, one by one, and told Ada what she saw. "The second arrow is a bit faint," she said. "Could you darken it?"

Jun walked it next. "The beanbag is too close to the third arrow," he said. "Someone might trip."

Mateo walked it last. He took very big steps. "I think the arrows are fine," he said. "But the yellow beanbag is in the way. I'll move it."

"That's three things," said Ada. She held the pencil. "That's three things we wouldn't have known."

It took the club most of the morning, and it was tiring work. It is tiresome to walk a route over and over and look for trouble. Anyone can see that. The narrator thought it was far too much fuss for one small drawing.

But Ada did not mind. She darkened the second arrow. She moved the yellow beanbag. She checked the plan against the room, arrow by arrow, shelf by shelf. And when she had finished, she wrote a short note at the bottom of the page, in pencil.

It said: We checked this together.

"Do you think it will work?" asked Ben.

Ada looked at the arrows. She looked at the door, and at the cupboard, and at the corner by the window. Four people had walked the route, and every one of them had arrived.

"I think it's the best plan I've ever drawn," she said. "And I think the reason is that I didn't draw it alone."

Ben said nothing, but he looked very pleased. He closed the folder, fastened it and gave it back to her. She had never carried it before. It felt lighter than she had expected.

## Chapter 12: Reading Day

On Reading Day, the books arrived at nine o'clock.

Ada had been awake since five. She had checked the plan at breakfast, checked it again on the bus and checked it a third time at her desk, until Priya reached over and gently took the pencil out of her hand. "It's finished," said Priya. "We all walked it." Ada nodded. She knew that was true. She also knew that a person can know a thing and still feel a flutter in her stomach, and that the flutter is not always a warning. Sometimes it only means that something matters.

They came in four brown boxes, carried by a very tall man from the school office. He set them down by the door and waved a cheerful hand. Twenty-four students in Room 6 gathered around the boxes, and a few of them tried to peek.

"Not yet," said Ms. Alvarez. "First, we have a plan."

She looked at the Courtyard Club. Ada, Ben, Priya, Jun and Mateo stood in a row beside the whiteboard. Ada held the plan in both hands.

"Ada," said Ms. Alvarez, "would you tell us what we are about to do?"

Ada's heart gave a small hop. But she took a breath, and she looked at Ben, and she said it. "Our plan has arrows," she said. "They show where to walk from the door to the reading corner. We checked them. Ben checked them, and I checked them, and Priya and Jun and Mateo checked them. We think they are safe."

"Thank you," said Ms. Alvarez. "Would you lead us?"

The club led. Ada went first, holding the plan up like a map. Ben came second, carrying the first box of books with the blue folder balanced on top. Priya, Jun and Mateo followed with the other three. Behind them walked the rest of Room 6, in a long, slow line, following the arrows.

It was quiet. Everybody looked at the floor. A tall boy stepped over a pencil and a girl stepped around a backpack. Nobody bumped a desk. The first arrow led along the wall. The second one led past the shelf. The third one led past the cupboard. And at the last arrow, which pointed neatly toward the window corner, the line turned together, like water in a stream.

It was a very tiring way to arrive. If you want the whole of it in a few lines, here they are. In chapter 10, Ben found that the arrow pointed away from the door. In chapter 11, Ada asked everyone to check a new plan. In chapter 12, they followed it together and reached the reading corner. The narrator calls checking tiresome. Ada calls it useful. She had never been so glad to be tired.

At the end of the route, Ada turned around. She saw twenty-three faces looking at her, and all of them had found the corner without a single bump.

"It works," said Mateo, in a whisper.

"Of course it works," said Priya. "We checked."

"I know," said Mateo. "I just wanted to say it."

Ms. Alvarez waited until everyone had settled on the rug, the beanbags and the floor. "I have a confession," she said. "When I asked the club to plan this corner, I did not know whether it would work. I only knew that planning is a good way to learn. Ada, Ben, Priya, Jun and Mateo made a plan, found a problem, fixed it and checked it again. I could not have asked for a better start."

She held up a book with a bear on the cover. "Now, who would like to read first?"

Twenty-three hands went up. The twenty-fourth hand belonged to Ada, and it was holding the plan, so she lifted her elbow instead. Everyone laughed.

The rest of the morning was a blur of books. Boxes were opened and rows were sorted, and a happy noise filled the room. Nia and Sam came from the next class to find their dragon book. They discovered that there were two copies, and they sat side by side at the round table, one chair each, reading with their heads close. Ada watched them out of the corner of her eye.

"Two chairs," she whispered to Ben.

"Two chairs," he said.

"I fixed that one on Wednesday," said Ada.

"I know," said Ben. "You asked."

Before lunch, Ms. Alvarez sent the readers to the corner in small groups to choose their first books. Ada's group went last. She carried the plan in one hand, a little wrinkled from all the carrying, and Ben carried the blue folder.

The group reached the reading corner. Ben clipped the checked plan beside the doorway. Ada stopped him from putting the first drawing away. "Hang that beside it," she said. "Then we can see what changed." The two pages fluttered together whenever someone opened the door. Ada watched another reader stop to compare them, then take the clear route to the books. She left both pages hanging.

Ben stood next to her. He did not say anything for a while. A cool breeze came from the hall and made the paper rustle.

"You know," he said at last, "I have a question."

Ada laughed. "Of course you do."

"Do you think there might be another plan someday? A bigger one?"

"I hope so," said Ada.

And she meant it, because she knew now what it takes to make a plan: a drawing, a friend, a few good questions and the courage to unfold the page.

THE END
