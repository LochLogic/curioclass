# 1. Frogs Live in Many Places

> **In this book.** Every frog has a body and a home. This book looks at both. It asks how a frog's home helps explain its body, and how its body helps it live at home.

Listen near a pond on a warm evening. You may hear a peep, a croak or a long, low trill. Those sounds come from frogs.

Frogs live almost everywhere on Earth. Only the frozen continent of Antarctica has none. There are about six thousand kinds of frogs. Some live in ponds and streams. Some live in trees. Some live in wet forests. Some live in dry places where water comes and goes with the rain.

The place where an animal lives is its **habitat**. A habitat gives an animal what it needs, such as food, water and a place to hide. Frogs need all of these things. They also need a habitat that is wet enough for their eggs, which dry out quickly.

*Figure: a map of the world with small frog drawings on six continents. A pond frog sits on a lily pad, a tree frog clings to a leaf, and a large frog rests in dry grass.*

[page 2]

Frogs are not all alike. A frog that lives in a stream does not look the same as a frog that lives in a tree. A frog in a dry place does not act the same as a frog in a pond. A frog's body and its home go together.

When scientists study a frog, they ask two questions. Where does it live? What does its body let it do there? The answers help us understand why frogs look and act the way they do.

Here is an example. Imagine a frog with bright green skin. In a green forest, its color may help hide it. On pale sand, the same color would stand out, and a hungry animal might spot it at once. The color is the same, but the home is different, so the color helps in one place and not in the other. This is the kind of idea you will look for again and again.

In this book, you will find out how a frog grows from an egg. You will read about the glass frog of the rain forest. You will also meet two unusual frogs from far away. As you read, look for ways that a frog's body fits its home.

# 2. What Is an Amphibian?

A frog is an **amphibian**. Frogs, toads and salamanders are all amphibians. The word comes from a Greek word that means "double life." That name fits. Many amphibians begin life in water and then live part of their lives on land.

Amphibians have some body parts in common. Most have four legs. A frog has long back legs and shorter front legs. Its strong back legs let it jump on land and kick through water.

Amphibians also have **moist** skin. Moist means a little wet. A frog's skin is covered with a thin layer of slime that keeps it from drying out. Air can pass through this wet skin, so a frog takes in some air through its skin as well as its lungs. This is one reason frogs live near water or in damp places. In a very dry place, a frog's skin could dry out.

[page 4]

Amphibian eggs need water too. A bird's egg has a hard shell, but a frog's egg does not. It has only a soft, jelly-like covering. In a dry place, the egg would dry up. So most frogs lay their eggs in water or in a damp spot.

Grown-up frogs eat other animals. They eat insects and other small creatures. Many animals eat frogs, too. This is why a frog's color and skin matter so much. Some frogs have colors that match leaves or mud, so an animal may not notice them. Some frogs have skin that is poisonous to the animals that try to eat them.

A frog's young need a safe place too. A pond has plants that grow in and around the water, such as reeds and arrowhead plants. These plants give tadpoles cover and protection, so tadpoles can grow up among them.

# 3. From Egg to Frog

A frog begins as an egg. The egg does not look like a frog yet. It is a tiny dark dot inside a clear ball of jelly. A mother frog may lay many eggs at one time. The clump of jelly and eggs is called **frogspawn**.

Frog eggs need water. After a heavy rain, low places in the ground can fill up. If the water stays for several weeks, a mother frog may lay her eggs there. Because the water is still there, the eggs stay wet.

Within a few days, a **tadpole** hatches from each egg. A tadpole is a young frog. It looks more like a little fish. It has a long tail, and it has **gills**. Gills are body parts that take air out of the water, so a tadpole can breathe underwater. Some people call a tadpole a polliwog.

A tadpole swims by waving its tail. It eats algae, the tiny green plants that grow in water. The tadpole eats and grows, and soon it gets bigger.

Then the tadpole begins to change. This change is called **metamorphosis**. The word means a big change in shape. Metamorphosis does not happen all at once. It happens in stages.

[page 6]

First, back legs grow, and then front legs grow. Next, **lungs** form inside the body. Lungs are the body parts that breathe air. As the lungs grow, the gills disappear. The tadpole can now breathe air, so it can leave the water.

At the same time, the tail gets shorter. The body takes the tail in, so the tail becomes smaller and smaller. The young frog is now a **froglet**. A froglet is a tiny frog with four legs and a short tail. It looks like a small adult.

The froglet climbs out of the water onto the land. Its mouth grows wider, and it eats insects and other small animals instead of algae. The froglet keeps growing until it is an adult. A grown frog can lay eggs of its own, and the cycle begins again.

[page 7]

How long does all this take? It depends. Some frogs take a few weeks to change from eggs to froglets. Others take much longer. The time depends on the kind of frog, on how much food it can find, and on how warm the water is. This is why the diagram on this page shows the stages in order but gives no number of days.

A pond is a busy place for a young frog. There is food in the water, and there are plants to hide among. When the froglets leave, the ground around the pond may be full of small hopping frogs. Later in the summer, the grown frogs call at night. The cycle then begins again for the next group.

*Figure: a circle of four drawings with arrows between them, going clockwise. A clear ball of eggs in a pond; a tadpole with a long tail; a froglet with four legs and a short tail on a rock beside the water; and a grown frog on the bank. Each stage has a label. There are no dates or numbers.*

# 4. How Frog Bodies Fit Frog Homes

A frog's body is made for the kind of place where it lives. Look at some body parts and what each part can do.

**Legs.** Most frogs have strong back legs. On land, a frog uses these legs to jump. In water, the same legs kick like a swimmer's. A frog that lives in a pond or stream has an easy way to get away from a hungry animal. It jumps into the water.

**Toes.** Many frogs that live in water have webbing between their toes. Webbing is skin that joins the toes together. It makes each foot wide, like a paddle. Scientists see webbed toes as a clue that a frog spends much of its life in water.

**Eyes.** A few frogs have eyes high on top of their heads. Scientists see this as another clue that a frog lives mostly in water.

[page 9]

**Skin and color.** A frog's skin color is often a match for its home. A frog in the leaves may be green or brown. A frog on a muddy bank may be dull and dark. When a frog sits still, its color may hide it. The color is useful because many animals look for frogs to eat. A color that blends in can keep a frog safe.

**Voice.** Many male frogs call. They call during the breeding season, and the sound tells females where they are. Each kind of frog has its own sound. Some sound high and some sound low. Frogs often call at night.

**Home and body together.** Put the parts together, and you see a pattern. A pond frog has webbed feet for swimming. A frog in the leaves has a color that matches them. A frog in a place where rain comes only in one season has a life that follows that rain. The next sections show three frogs that live in very different places.

**Why the home matters.** If a pond dries up, the frogs that need it must move or wait. If a stream dries up, the tadpoles in it may not grow. A frog cannot choose a different body, so the home has to give it what its body needs. That is why scientists who study frogs also study the place where it lives.

*Figure: three small drawings of frog parts. A webbed foot with the skin between the toes shaded; a frog's head with the eyes high on top; and a green frog sitting flat on a leaf.*

# 5. A Frog of the Rain Forest: The Glass Frog

Some frogs have bodies that are strange and beautiful. One kind has skin that you can see through.

Glass frogs live in warm, wet forests of Central and South America. They are small. Most are a shade of green or brown. Their name comes from the skin on their bellies. This skin is **transparent**, which means you can see through it. Look at a glass frog from below, and you may see its organs and bones inside.

There are many kinds of glass frogs. This section is about one of them, the **grainy Cochran frog**. It is named for its skin, which has a fine, grainy look. Its body is a dark bluish green with black spots. It is sometimes called the crystal frog. It lives in the warm, damp lowlands of Panama, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Honduras.

[page 11]

The grainy Cochran frog lives in trees near streams. It spends most of its time on leaves. It is most active at night, so it is hard for animals to find in the dark.

What does it do by day? During the day, the grainy Cochran frog rests flat against a leaf. Its green color blends into the leaves around it. This makes it hard to see. A frog that is hard to see is less likely to be found by a hungry animal.

At night the males call. Their call is a harsh, creaking sound. A male picks a spot on a leaf above a stream and guards it. If another male comes near, the two may wrestle. They hang upside down from the leaves and push against each other chest to chest. Some of these contests last as long as two hours.

Scientists tell glass frogs apart in many ways. One way is to look at the bones. In the grainy Cochran frog, the bones are dark green. In some other glass frogs, the bones are white. You can see the bones only because the skin is transparent. The see-through skin gives scientists a clue that most animals do not.

[page 12]

The stream is important to the grainy Cochran frog's young. A female lays her eggs on a leaf that hangs over a stream. The eggs are held together in a clump of jelly. The leaf holds the eggs above the water, and the tip of the leaf keeps a steady flow of water running over them.

About two weeks later, the eggs hatch. The tiny tadpoles stay on the leaf until the next rain. Then they drop from the leaf into the stream below. In the stream, they spend several months growing into frogs.

Notice how the frog's home and body work together. Its green color blends into the leaves of its home. Its eggs rest on leaves where the stream is close. Its tadpoles grow in the water below. Take away the leaves or the stream, and the frog could not live this way.

*Figure: a cutaway picture of a rain forest stream. A green frog sits flat on a leaf. A jelly clump of eggs hangs on a leaf tip over the water, and a tadpole swims in the stream below.*

# 6. Two More Unusual Frogs

**Cranwell's horned frog.** This frog lives in a region of South America called the Gran Chaco. It covers parts of Argentina, Paraguay, Bolivia and Brazil. The land there has dry, thorny forests, palm grasslands and wetlands that fill with water at certain times of year. The frog's life follows the rainy seasons.

Cranwell's horned frog is not shaped like most frogs. It is squat and round, and it has a very large mouth. Its head is wide and flat. Above each eye is a ridge of skin that looks like a small horn, and this gives the frog its name. It is big for a frog, between three and five inches long. A few weigh as much as a pound.

The frog's large mouth lets it swallow big mouthfuls. Sometimes it tries to eat something bigger than its own head! Small hooked teeth help it hold on to its food. Males call during the breeding season, and the call is a repeating sound much like a low horn. In the wild, most of these frogs live only one to four years. The frog is named for Jorge Cranwell, a scientist from Argentina who studied frogs and helped tell this kind from a close cousin. The frog sometimes sleeps with its eyes open.

[page 14]

**Western water-holding frog.** This frog lives in Australia, in places where water comes and goes with the rain. It is a large, solid frog with a flat shape. Its eyes sit on the top of its head, its mouth is wide and its legs are short. There is a lot of webbing between its toes. Its color ranges from pale gray to dark brown. A male can grow to about six centimeters long, and a female to about seven.

Scientists say that the position of its eyes and the webbing on its toes are clues. They are signs of a frog that spends much time in water. Its call is a low "waah, waah, waah." A male may repeat it about eighty times in one minute.

Put the three frogs side by side. The glass frog has a see-through belly and lives in leaves above a stream. The horned frog has a huge mouth and lives where the rains come in a season. The water-holding frog has eyes on top of its head and lives where water is not always there. Each frog's home helps explain its body.

*Figure: three frogs drawn in a row, each at its own home. A glass frog on a leaf over a stream; a round horned frog on dry leaves; and a flat frog with eyes on top of its head at the edge of a pool.*

# 7. Frogs and Toads

Frogs have close cousins called toads. A toad is an amphibian too, and it begins life in much the same way. A mother toad lays eggs in water. Tadpoles hatch, eat, grow and change. But there are differences that you can see.

**The eggs.** Frog eggs are laid in a clump of jelly. The clump sits at the edge of the water, partly under the surface. Toad eggs are laid in a long, double row, like a string of beads. The string winds around the stems of water plants.

**The young.** When young frogs have grown legs, they climb out of the water. They stay near the pond and often return to it. Young toads leave the water and do not come back. They spend most of their lives on the ground.

[page 16]

**The jumpers.** Many frogs are very good jumpers and climbers. A frog that jumps well can leap out of trouble, and a frog that climbs well can reach the leaves above the water. Not every kind is the same, so a reader should check which animal a book is describing.

**What they share.** Frogs and toads are alike in important ways. Both begin as eggs in a damp place. Both start as tadpoles that breathe with gills. Both change into animals with lungs and legs. Both eat plants as young and other animals when grown.

A pond in spring can hold both. The reeds and arrowhead plants near the water give the tadpoles cover. The still water gives them algae to eat and a place to grow. The ground around the pond is where young toads and frogs go next. A pond is a home for the young, and the land around it is a home for the grown.

*Figure: a side-by-side drawing. On the left, a clump of frog eggs at the edge of a pond. On the right, a long double string of toad eggs wound around a water plant. Below each, a young frog and a young toad on the bank.*

# 8. Putting It Together: Homes and Bodies

Look back at the frogs in this book. Each has a home, and each has a body that fits it.

**A pond frog.** A pond gives a frog water for its eggs and food for its tadpoles. A pond frog has strong back legs for swimming and jumping, and webbed toes that make its feet like paddles.

**A glass frog.** A forest stream gives a glass frog a place for its young. The grainy Cochran frog has green skin that blends into the leaves where it rests by day. Its eggs rest on leaves over the stream, and its tadpoles drop into the water below. Its home gives it leaves for hiding and a stream for its young.

**A horned frog.** The Gran Chaco has seasons of rain, and this frog's life follows them. Its round body and huge mouth are unlike those of most frogs.

**A water-holding frog.** This frog lives where water is not always there. Its flat shape, the eyes on top of its head and the webbing on its toes show how it is built.

When you explain a frog, begin with two questions. What does its body look like? What does its home give it? Then link them. The leaves help the glass frog hide, and the stream helps its young grow. That is how a body and a home work together.

# 9. Glossary

**adult** (noun): a grown animal that can have young.

**amphibian** (noun): an animal that can live in water and on land. Frogs, toads and salamanders are amphibians.

**froglet** (noun): a young frog with four legs and a short tail.

**frogspawn** (noun): a clump of frog eggs held together in jelly.

**gills** (noun): body parts that take air from water, so an animal can breathe underwater.

**habitat** (noun): the place where an animal lives.

**lungs** (noun): body parts that take in air.

**metamorphosis** (noun): a big change in an animal's body, such as a tadpole becoming a frog.

**moist** (adjective): a little wet.

**tadpole** (noun): a young frog that lives in water and has gills and a tail.

**transparent** (adjective): clear enough to see through.

**webbing** (noun): skin that joins an animal's toes.
