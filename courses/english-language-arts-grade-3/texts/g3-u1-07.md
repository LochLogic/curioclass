## Text

How Booker T. Washington Learned to Read

Booker T. Washington was born enslaved in Virginia in 1856. Enslaved people were forced to work without pay, and they were not free to leave. In April 1865, when Booker was a boy, he and the other enslaved people on his farm heard that they were free. Being set free from slavery is called emancipation.

After freedom, Booker's family moved to Malden, a town in West Virginia, where his stepfather had a job at a salt furnace. Booker went to work there too, while he was still a child. Often he began as early as four o'clock in the morning.

Booker wanted more than anything to learn to read. Years later, he wrote about it in a book called Up from Slavery. He said the first thing he learned at the furnace was a number, 18, which was marked on his stepfather's barrels. Then his mother found an old spelling book for him. Booker wrote:

"I began at once to devour this book, and I think that it was the first one I ever had in my hands."

To devour means to eat hungrily. Booker means he read the book eagerly. But he had no teacher. He wrote:

"I had learned from somebody that the way to begin to read was to learn the alphabet"

He tried every way he could think of, and in a few weeks he knew most of the letters.

When a school opened for children in Malden, Booker's stepfather still needed him at work, so Booker could not go in the daytime. Booker did not give up. He found a teacher for lessons in the evening, after the furnace work was done. He wrote:

"These night lessons were so welcome that I think I learned more at night than the other children did during the day."

Booker was determined to get an education. In 1872, when he was sixteen, he set out for a school called Hampton Institute. Later he became a teacher himself.
