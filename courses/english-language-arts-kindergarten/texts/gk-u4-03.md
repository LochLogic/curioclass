[page 1]
This is a map of Africa. Africa is a very big continent. Find the country of Kenya on the east side, beside the Indian Ocean. Kenya's capital city is Nairobi. People live, work, farm, and go to school all over Kenya.

Picture: An original, plainly drawn map of Africa with Kenya colored in, the Indian Ocean labeled beside it, and a dot for Nairobi. No people shown.

[page 2]
A woman named Wangari Maathai lived in Kenya. She studied science at schools and universities, and she became a scientist. She cared about the land, the water, and the trees of her country.

Picture: An illustrated adult woman in a bright blouse, labeled Wangari Maathai, kneels on a hillside beside a young tree and looks at its leaves.

[page 3]
Many women in the countryside of Kenya shared the same concerns. Streams near their homes were drying up. It was harder to have enough food. And they had to walk farther to find firewood for cooking.

Picture: Three women of different ages stand beside a dry streambed, talking. One carries a bundle of sticks on her back. Hills and sky are behind them.

[page 4]
Wangari Maathai organized with these women. In 1977 she started a group called the Green Belt Movement. Women across Kenya joined it.
Trees can help with these concerns. Trees hold the soil in place. They catch rainwater. They give food and firewood.

Picture: A group of women in everyday work clothes and sun hats stand in a circle around Wangari Maathai, looking at a young tree held up in a pot.

[page 5]
The women set up nurseries. A nursery is a place where young trees are grown. The women planted seeds and watered them. Day after day, the women watered the little plants. Slowly the seeds grew into seedlings. A seedling is a very young tree.

Picture: Rows of small seedlings in black pots at an outdoor nursery. Women kneel in the rows, planting seeds and watering.

[page 6]
When the seedlings were ready, the women planted them together. They planted in many places across Kenya. The women did the work together, side by side.

Picture: A line of women and a few children carry seedlings in pots across a field, toward a row of fresh holes. A man leans on a shovel nearby.

[page 7]
The seedlings grew, and more trees grew. As a result, more soil stayed in place and more rainwater was held. More people joined in the planting, and the work spread.

Picture: The same hillside shown in two views: bare soil on the left, young trees in rows on the right, and many people planting between them.

[page 8]
Now we can put the story in order. First, women shared their concerns. Next, Wangari Maathai organized with them, and they set up nurseries. Then the seeds grew into seedlings. After that, the women planted the seedlings together. As a result, there were more trees on the land.

Picture: Five small pictures in a row, left to right: women talking beside a dry stream, a meeting circle, a nursery, seedlings in pots, and women planting on a hillside.

[page 9]
In 2004, Wangari Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize. She was the first African woman to win it. She died in 2011. The Green Belt Movement still works today. People in Kenya still grow seedlings and plant trees together.

Picture: A children's drawing style scene: a girl and a boy and an adult plant a seedling at a school in Kenya, with a school building and a Kenyan flag in the background.

[page 10]
Wangari Maathai was a leader. Many women organized and planted together. They showed how people can care for trees, and how trees can help people. When neighbors work together, big things can grow from small seeds.

Picture: A wide view of a hillside with many people of all ages planting and watering young trees together in the sunshine.
