# Day 79: Decisions, places and consequences

## Teacher page

### Before class

Print the listed pieces. Fold Sources D, E and H under; hold the slips. Sort day 78's exits by look-for, counting misses on the Proclamation's reach.

### The day

#### 5 min. Warm-up

Return day 78's practice (s05-P78-1, s05-P78-2) and read the order, Sources A, D, B, C, with each aim; students correct their timelines. An aim that never changes is error J. Return day 78's exit; students answer **Ask.** [[s05-P79-1]] alone. Over a third missed the reach (error C)? Read day 78 Source C's gloss list (loyal slave states, Tennessee, excepted parishes and counties); else a small group does. Victory alone (error L): that group rereads day 78's Source D on who reached Fortress Monroe and what Butler did.

#### 10 min. Teaching first

Three offices: Davis, Confederate president; Lee, commanding the Army of Northern Virginia; Grant, Union general in the West, later of all Union armies. Seneca leader Ely S. Parker, Grant's military secretary from late 1864, helped draft the Appomattox surrender terms in his hand. The Confederacy was founded to protect slavery; its armies fought for that. Read Sources A and B aloud with source lines. Davis signed the 1862 draft law to fill the armies; Lee told Davis the time was right to enter Maryland, crossing before Davis replied.

Read Source F's rows. Antietam (September 1862) turned Lee back, giving Lincoln his moment for the preliminary proclamation. Gettysburg turned back a second invasion, Lee's last. Each cost thousands of lives; none alone ended the war.

Source G photographs Lincoln and McClellan at Antietam, no dead shown; per its description, Lincoln came to press McClellan, whose slowness soon cost him his command, to attack. It shows a meeting, not either man's thoughts.

#### 10 min. Worked example

Read Source C aloud. Students read Source F's Vicksburg row (on the Mississippi) and attempt **Ask.** [[s05-K79-1]] before the model:

[[steps:s05-K79-1]]

Grant cut loose from his supply line; Vicksburg surrendered July 4, 1863. With Port Hudson's fall on July 9 the Union held the whole river, splitting the Confederacy. Thousands of enslaved people in Mississippi fled to Union lines. Students fill the frame on side 1.

#### 10 min. Alone: the organizer

Hand out the organizer. Students answer **Ask.** [[s05-P79-2]] alone from Sources A, B, C and F: each leader, a decision and result; each battle, an immediate and a lasting effect. Practice: walk the room, give no answers, collect for comments; they return in day 82's warm-up, never scored.

#### 5 min. The 1862 land laws

Uncover Sources D, E and H; read source lines, passages and glosses aloud. Federal power here: Congress deciding who owned western land. The Railway Act promised the United States "shall extinguish" the titles: the nations still held the land in 1862. The Western Shoshone treaty, made nation to nation, has no article ceding their land. Teacher background, not taught: the US-Dakota War of 1862 and the Sand Creek massacre of 1864 fell in this war; unit 6.2 (Western expansion, Native nationhood) takes them up.

#### 10 min. Exit ticket

Hand out the slip; read Source I's source line and passage aloud, neutrally, without explaining. Students answer [[s05-X79]] alone; scribe dictated answers without hints. Collect tickets.

### Other rooms

Paper is the main path; with no printer, board the sources and organizer tables for students to copy.

### At home

**Swap.** No printer: copy the questions and passages onto paper. Cover Sources D, E and H; keep the slip for the exit.

**Skip.** The warm-up.

- **8 min. Read.** Read Sources A, B, F and G's description. Davis led the Confederate government, Lee its main army, Grant the Union army in the West.
- **8 min. Example.** Read Source C; the child tries [[s05-K79-1]], then follow its steps together.
  **Say.** "Why did the river matter?" Expect: holding it split the Confederacy.
- **8 min. Organizer.** The child fills in the organizer half-sheet alone; talk it over after.
- **5 min. Land laws.** Read Sources D, E and H with their glosses.
  **Say.** "Whose land was it?" Expect: nations Source H names.
- **10 min. Exit ticket.** Hand over the slip; read Source I without explaining it. The child answers [[s05-X79]] alone.

### Shorter or longer

Shorter: Source G's description only. Longer: compare Lee's letter with what Antietam did to his plan.

### Words and supports

Gloss conscription (required service) and propitious (favourable); use the sheet's glosses for "Indian titles", stipulated and alternate section. Name nations, not "Indians": the Western Shoshone (Source H), and the Cheyenne and Arapaho (its gloss), whose land the line crossed. Say "Indian" only reading the law's words; otherwise say "Native nations' titles". Teacher only, their own names: Newe (Shoshone), Tsistsistas and Hinono'ei (Cheyenne and Arapaho). Some families are citizens of these nations: ask no student to speak for a nation; run no land-claim game. Read in chunks; describe places by the river and the states. An unfamiliar word is a language gap, not error D or error K: re-ask plainly before marking.
