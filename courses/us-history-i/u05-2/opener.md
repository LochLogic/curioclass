# Section 5.2 opener: the war, its leaders and its memory

Read this once before day 78. It holds what every day of the section assumes, so the day pages stay short.

## What the section does

Five days, 78 to 82. Students analyze how Lincoln's aims changed and what the Emancipation Proclamation did and did
not do (day 78); what Davis, Lee and Grant decided and what Antietam, Gettysburg and Vicksburg changed, with the
1862 land laws on the home front (day 79); who served and how we know, from a journal, a letter, a photograph and a
cartoon, with Spotswood Rice's letter on the exit (day 80). Day 81 is a writing day: essay 1, draft 2. Day 82 tests a Lost Cause claim against the record
of 1861 and asks what question follows.

The unit's question frames every day: why did the Civil War come, and did Reconstruction fail or was it overthrown?
This section supplies evidence for the first half and the method students need for both: who made a source, when,
for whom and why, and what it cannot show.

## How the days run

Every day is 50 minutes: a five-minute warm-up that returns earlier work, teaching or a worked example, practice,
and an exit ticket judged against its look-fors.
Mark exits the same evening; more than a third missing one look-for means the next warm-up repairs it.

Each teaching day prints a source sheet beside the student page. Sources are short (100 to 200 words), glossed, and
read aloud once before students work alone.

## The sources, and how to handle them

Every source is named on its sheet with who made it, when, and its rights line. The full list, with citations and
what is still to check, is data/plans/us-history-i/sources.csv.

- **Slavery and violence are named plainly.** Say "enslaved people", not "slaves", in your own words; a source keeps
  its own words. Say that the Confederacy was founded to protect slavery, because its founders said so.
- **Racist language inside a source.** Day 82's Source B, Stephens's Cornerstone speech, states white supremacy as
  the new government's foundation. Read it as evidence of what the founders said, once, without dramatic reading.
  Say before it: "This is a hateful claim. We read it because it tells us, in a founder's own words, why the
  Confederacy was made."
- **No battle role play, no reenactment.** Battles are studied by decision and effect, by place and on a facts card.
  Day 79's photograph shows Lincoln at Antietam, not the dead.
- **Confederate symbols only as sources.** None appear on our pages except inside a printed source.
- **The Lost Cause is tested, never balanced.** On day 82 do not ask "which side is right?" Ask "what does the record
  from 1861 say, and does the later claim survive it?" It does not.
- **Native land.** The Pacific Railway Act granted land and a right of way across land Native nations still held,
  among them the Cheyenne and Arapaho on the Platte and the Shoshone farther west, and promised in section 2 that the
  United States would extinguish "the Indian titles". The Homestead Act gave out land the United States had already
  taken by treaty cession, often under pressure, or by force. The Lakota and Arapaho fought the line; in the 1868 Fort
  Laramie treaty they agreed to withdraw opposition to it. Read section 2 and Source H with students. Some students'
  families are citizens of the nations named; do not ask any student to speak for a nation, and run no land-claim
  game or simulation.

## What the teacher needs to know

- The Proclamation applied only to areas in rebellion on January 1, 1863. It did not cover the loyal slave states
  (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri) or Tennessee, which it never named, and it excepted 13 Louisiana parishes, West Virginia and 7 Virginia counties. It
  became real where Union armies went and where enslaved people fled to them. It opened the army to Black men;
  about 180,000 served in the United States Colored Troops.
- In August 1862 Lincoln wrote publicly that his aim was the Union. He had already shown his cabinet a draft
  emancipation order in July and waited for a Union victory; Antietam gave it.
- Davis was the Confederate president; Lee commanded the Army of Northern Virginia; Grant led Union armies in the
  West and from 1864 all Union armies. No single battle ended the war.
- Harriet Tubman guided the June 1863 Combahee River raid; in her own letter she counts 756 people brought to
  freedom. Black soldiers were paid less than white soldiers until 1864, and captured Black soldiers faced death or
  enslavement.
- After the war, Davis and others wrote that slavery was not the cause. The 1861 record, including the Confederate
  constitution and Stephens's speech, says it was. Historians call the later story the Lost Cause.
