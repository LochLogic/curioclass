## Teaching notes

A retelling from the Chinese tradition of Yu the Great. Shun is the ruler who asks Yu to recount the work. Gun is Yu's father. Yi and Ji are Yu's collaborators, called Yih and Tseih in Legge's translation. The account belongs to a living storytelling tradition. ## Yu and the Way to the Sea

When the ruler Shun asked Yu to speak about the flood, there were people in the hall who had never seen water higher than the bank of a river. One man sat close to Yu so he could hear how high the water had come. Yu raised his hands to show the height of a hill. Then he thought of the families who had carried baskets up that hill and sat down only when there was no farther to climb. He rested his hands on his knees. To tell Shun what had changed, he would have to start a long way from the hall.

In the years of the great flood, people went uphill with what they could carry. Some took a pot, and some took the seed they needed for the next crop. They carried these things close to their bodies. Looking back, they saw water gather in the hollows where their houses had been. It spread so far that hills they usually saw across a stretch of fields looked like islands. From one of those hills a person could call across the water without ever finding out whether somebody heard.

Yu's father Gun had been asked to hold that water back. He put his strength into walls of earth. There was a good reason for the first wall. Behind it people could keep a piece of ground dry long enough to bring in food. When the water rose, the people brought more earth. They put it where Gun pointed, bank on bank. Then the river went around a wall, and then it broke through another. Gun kept people working where they could still get to the banks. After years had passed, the families were still on the hills. The work passed to Yu.

At first Yu went from wall to wall. A break in one was easy to see. With a stick he drew the shape of the missing earth in the mud. A woman who had come to find the place where her house stood pointed past the break. She showed him where the water had spread when it first came through. The next bank was far enough away that he had to climb a little to see it. From there he could make out part of a former streambed, shallow and choked with wood, that bent away below the old village. He had been trying to see where the wall could hold. He followed the streambed to see where the water could go.

Yu took that question with him through the flooded country. He traveled on land and in boats. Where the ground gave way under his feet, he found other means to cross it. At the end of a day's journey he asked the people he met where the streams had once gone. Some remembered bends he could still see. Others pointed to ground with no line left in it. He began to understand why one bank could stand while the water was spreading onto another village's fields. He wanted a plan that reached all the way to the sea.

Some of the people Yu asked to help had worked for Gun. One knew where stones could be taken out to make an old channel passable. Another knew where water from a smaller stream joined the larger one. Yu sent people to different places with parts of the same plan. Here they could clear a passage. There they could deepen a channel so that the water would reach a river instead of lying among the houses. At a place where the route led through the hills, Yu had to wait while a party went ahead to tell people on the far side what was coming. The workers could shout to one another on a hillside. Across the flooded land they could not. More than once, a person had to make the whole journey just to bring back an answer.

There was work to do while the channels were still being opened. In some places the water lay over the fields so long that there was no grain to harvest. Yi helped the people find food while the ground was still covered. Ji helped them sow when they could reach land again. Yu asked each place what it could spare. A settlement with stores of grain could trade them for what it lacked. People who could make the journey carried food for some who could not. When Yu went to see how the digging was getting on, he sometimes found an empty basket near the work. He asked where it had come from before he let anybody put earth in it.

Once he asked Ji how soon people could let go of the seed they had carried uphill. The earth at the bottom of a slope had only just appeared. A person could stand on it at one end. At the other, there was still a sheet of water over it. Ji put a little of the soil in his hand and looked at the land above it. There was a higher patch people could tend while they waited for the rest. Yu had brought word about when a channel would be ready. He carried back word about a field.

Season followed season. One day a messenger came to say that the water had reached an open river by the way the workers had prepared. There was still water over the farther fields. Yu sent the messenger back to find out where it went from there. Only after the people downstream had seen the water pass did he come to look again. He walked along a bank with people who had known it when it was deep under the flood. A little above them, on ground that could be reached on foot, someone was bringing seed out of a basket. Yu waited while they marked off a place to sow. In another village people were still carrying food up a hill. He had more questions for the next messenger.

Gradually there was dry ground enough for families to build and plant again. The streams joined rivers that carried the water onward to the sea. The people whose first baskets had gone uphill full of seed could use their baskets for a harvest. Some stayed near the places where they had waited out the flood. Some went back downhill. Along the way there were neighbors to thank for food or work, and people who could show a traveler how much of a route they had opened.

In Shun's hall, Yu named Yi and Ji and told how people had shared what their own land could give. The man who had moved closer listened for the height of the flood. He heard how far the water had gone and how often a person working at one place had needed news from the next. He heard how long families had waited for grain to grow. When Yu had finished, he looked down at his hands. There was no measure he could make with them for the distance the people had worked together. He began to tell Shun where the first of the streams met the next one.
