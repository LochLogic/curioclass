## Teaching notes

Bellwether, its characters, and its visits, maps, and rebuilding decisions are fictional. The story includes a damaged home, displacement, money worries, and an approaching storm. There is no death on the page. Keep discussion about the characters. Offer a quiet pass and keep complete print beside audio.

The close third-person narration follows twelve-year-old Mara Vega. Readers track what Mara expects or overlooks, then infer other people's priorities from their actions and dialogue. Follow the numbered drawer, the back of the notice, and the changing position of the chairs. Decisions depend on different kinds of evidence: reports of where water went, observations about who can get through a space, and money and staffing limits. Compare the demand with finished grade 5 prose and obtain a band measure before teaching.

## Chapter 1. Two Places for a Door

Mara Vega was twelve the spring she learned to draw a door two ways. In the first drawing, the door belonged at the bottom of Mill Street, between the river and the blue house where her grandfather had grown up. In the second, a stranger carried it uphill. Mara had drawn only the stranger's hands. She left the rest of the page white.

“The bottom one is ours,” she told her mother.

“They're both yours. You've got pencil all over your sleeve.”

Mara rubbed her wrist. The second drawing had started as a joke. Grandpa Luis had said that if the town wanted them to move, somebody ought to tell the town how heavy an oak door was. Mara had tried to draw a person having that conversation with the door. Somehow she'd drawn the person getting it up the hill.

Her mother took the pencil from the table and laid it between the two drawings. “You're coming with me if you want. The inspector said we can stand at the front walk while she checks the shop. We may get an answer about the counter today.”

Mara was in her shoes before her mother finished. The counter was their longest piece of furniture, with drawers that stuck in damp weather and a brass strip across the front edge. Her great-grandmother had sold lamp parts behind it. Mara had learned to count change there, sliding each coin against the brass to hear what it sounded like before it reached her palm. By the time she was nine, she could tell a dime from a quarter with her eyes closed. She could tell which family member was working by the way they shut the drawer, too.

“Can Grandpa come?”

“He asked us to take pictures.”

At the other end of Aunt Celia's kitchen, Grandpa was wrapping a piece of toast in a napkin. He folded it with the care he usually saved for something that might break in the mail. His good apron hung from the back of the chair. He had worn it twice all winter, both times indoors while he fixed a neighbor's chair.

“Look under the drawers,” he said, setting the toast beside Mara's mitten. “You might see the little numbers my mother put there.”

“And if it's too far away?”

“Then it's too far away.”

He said it easily. There was a drawer full of forks at his elbow, and he opened it so sharply that everything struck the front at once.

Outside, the morning smelled of the onion skins in Aunt Celia's compost. Mara and her mother set out from the top of Ash Hill. From up here even the longest block of Mill Street looked short. Their roof looked like something laid out on a table. On the left was the peaked roof of the Lows' washhouse. To the right, Patch Lane sloped to the bend. A strip of bare gray earth followed the river through the old playing field. On the far bank, opposite town, a stand of trees hid the river when its water was low. Today Mara saw threads of light moving between their trunks.

Her mother went down the long way on Switchback Road. Mara had thought it got its name because the bus driver had to switch hands on the wheel. Kit had told her it was named for the bends. The road was wide enough for the bus and a sidewalk on one side. At each turn she saw a different piece of the water. Down near Mill Street, beside the town's orange barricade, she could see none at all. A row of damaged buildings stood between them and the channel.

Mara thought the river had been giving them so much to look at that she'd forgotten how often it was out of sight. From beside their shop, before the flood, she could hear it when a truck wasn't passing. That had seemed close enough.

They met the inspector, Ms. Dinh, where the barricade ended. She wore a yellow jacket with a zipper missing its pull. A paper clip hung in its place. Mara kept looking at it. It seemed like a problem she could solve.

“Wait here with your mom,” Ms. Dinh said. “I can show you a view from the front after I'm done. I don't expect you to go any farther than that.”

She disappeared behind the shop. The front display window had been boarded up in the fall. All winter Mara had pictured the board from this side, its three pale knots like eyes. The town had removed it for the inspection. Light entered the old display and stopped at a long black streak on the wall. Mara had planned to take a picture of the streak to bring Grandpa. From the walk, with the bare glass between it and her, the streak seemed smaller than the thing she was afraid to have him see.

“I know what he'd do,” she said. “He'd set up again down here. He'd get a different counter if he had to.”

“Did he tell you that?”

“He keeps saying the name of the street like there won't be one if we go anywhere else.”

Her mother turned the phone over in her hand. “When I say the name of the street, sometimes I mean a person. I might mean Bea with the mending machine, or Mr. Low asking for a shorter hem on one leg. You can ask Grandpa which he means.”

“You want us to go up to the seed store.”

“I want to see if the seed store could work. I saw the rent for the uphill apartment. I haven't seen a figure for fixing our house, except two numbers a builder wouldn't stand behind. If we have to make two decisions, about a home and about work, I want real numbers for both.”

“But we're allowed to rebuild here?”

“Maybe. The rules depend on what's damaged and what would have to change. Allowed, possible, and affordable. Those are three different questions.”

Across the street, the metal shutter on the washhouse struck its frame. It knocked in pairs. Mara used to hear that sound while she waited for her mother to close up. Several other sounds she knew from those afternoons were missing, and the shutter's knocking made the street behind it seem larger. She had never thought quiet could give a place more room.

From the walk, Ms. Dinh pointed her flashlight through the glass. The beam ran over a shelf, a sagging pegboard, and the corner of the counter. Mara saw a froth of peeled finish. Below it, where the panel had split, she glimpsed a pale crosspiece. She raised her phone and looked through the picture instead. In that little square the pale wood and the gray wood seemed like two pieces that had never met.

“I can't clear the building,” Ms. Dinh said. “I'll ask the contractor to look at removing a few items from the front after the supports go in. Send him a short list. Keep it to what you'd most want, because I need him to decide what can come out safely.”

On the walk home Mara held the phone like a cup that might spill. She had a picture of the counter and a picture of the door, though only the lower half of each. Near the first bend she remembered something that wasn't in the pictures. On Saturdays, after the paying jobs were lined up on the long shelf, Grandpa put a little bell on the counter and let anybody bring in one broken thing for a free look. Sometimes the look was all the thing got. Sometimes someone waiting knew enough to help. When Mara was younger, she had believed the bell made the next person appear. The last Saturday before the flood, she had let a girl from school ring it over and over until Grandpa had taken it away and set it in the window.

He'd never charged anybody to ring it once.

“I'm going to put out the Saturday bell again,” Mara said. “We can do that before there's a shop. That would show him we can still do it.”

“What would show him?”

“The people coming.”

Her mother stopped where a tuft of last year's grass stuck out of a crack. Farther up the hill, a car rolled quietly into a driveway. “You can take on the invitation,” she said. “Grandpa and I will decide about tools and repairs. And if you make an invitation, check that the people you're asking can actually get there.”

Mara nodded before she thought to ask who she was asking. In her mind, she could see the same people waiting in the same order on the walk. Each had a round white space above their head to put a name in. She could fill in the first three. By the time she reached the kitchen, she had one more.

At the table Grandpa unfolded the napkin. Mara had forgotten to eat the toast. He let her set the phone down in the middle of it, and they leaned toward the little rectangle together.

“Here's the door,” she said. “And here's where the counter was.”

“That's still where it is.” He enlarged the photograph with two fingers. In the pale crosspiece, nearly out of the light, was a crooked blue four. “Oh, look at that. She used to write them twice. Once on the drawer and once on the slot, so nobody had to guess where it belonged.”

He wanted to look longer. Mara let the picture stay. With her free hand she eased the two drawings out of the way so there would be space to turn the phone.

## Chapter 2. Somebody Else's Saturday

First Mara made an announcement. She found a sheet of paper with one ragged edge, tore off the fringe, and ruled a straight line across the top. SATURDAY FIX-IT, she wrote, big enough to see from the other end of the kitchen. Underneath she drew the bell, twice its actual size. She could almost hear it. She did not write an address. By supper she meant to have one that nobody would have to look up.

Grandpa stood beside her. He had taken the good apron off his chair, and he turned it over in his hands to find which pocket faced forward.

“What are you promising them?” he asked.

“One thing each for a free look. You get to tell them what we can do.”

“Yes. Your mom has a few tools that stayed dry. We can do a little work with those. Ask people to bring something they can carry. Nothing that plugs in and nothing that got wet in the flood. No promises about parts we don't have.”

She added small letters at the bottom. She was going to need a second sheet with a cleaner edge. “Can you get the bell?”

“The actual bell?”

She nodded. He hung the good apron across her knees. In one of the pockets she could feel the edge of something flat. It was a slip from the old order pad, with a customer's first name and the word *hinge*. The paper had gone soft along one fold. She ran her fingers all around the pocket.

“Grandpa?”

“The bell's still at the shop. It can stay there until somebody can get it.”

For a moment the drawing looked ridiculous. Nobody needed a picture of a thing that wasn't coming. Mara rubbed the bell with the side of her thumb until the pencil made a dark hill. Then she drew the outline again, faintly, on top of the hill.

“Would a tap on the table do?” she asked.

“I liked hearing whose turn it was. You could say their name.”

Getting an address turned out to be harder than finding all the people who remembered their old one. In the months since the flood, Ash Hill seemed to have grown a second town over the first. There were two mattresses in the Low family's dining room. The library had four extra folding tables between its shelves, so people could fill out forms while somebody watched their children. Mara's classmate Kit lived over the bus garage with his aunt and his mother, Bea. The garage played a short beeping tune whenever one of the doors opened. Last week, when Bea sent Mara a voice message, the tune had sounded between two syllables of her name.

Bea had rented the little room next to the repair shop. Hemming, patches, and alterations, said the sign before the flood. After school Mara used to sit in there and tell Bea what she should put on the next sign. Quick hems. Secret pockets. All flags fixed. Bea never changed it, but she listened to every version, and once she had taken a customer's coat off its hook to show Mara how a secret pocket would go inside.

Mara called the library. A librarian offered a table by the front window at two on Saturday. All the morning spaces were full. Mara called Mr. Low and asked if she could use the long concrete apron in front of the washhouse if the town permitted it. He said the washhouse grounds were still closed, then offered half his carport on the hill. She could use it in the morning. His brother was coming at noon with a car that had a loose wheel and needed the whole space. All outdoor plans depended on the weather. By the end of the calls she had two addresses, one afternoon and one morning, and no real reason to want either.

She took the announcement to Kit. His mother was out on a job, and his aunt sat upstairs with a wrapped ankle, so the two friends sat on the narrow landing where they could hear if she called. His aunt called anyway.

“You got company?”

“Mara.”

“Then let her have the stool.”

Kit set the stool down on one of the lower steps, where there was no room to sit on it. Mara moved it up to the landing. They both sat on the steps. The stool between them did good service as a table.

“Mr. Low says we can get his carport in the morning,” Mara said. “It feels more like the old time if it's morning.”

Kit looked down over the rail. He could see the nose of the bus from here. “My mom's at the grocery loading dock till twelve on Saturdays. She gets back here about one. How's she supposed to help?”

Mara had counted Bea as a person who might come buy something if they could find a thing to sell, or bring something in because the work was free. She had completely forgotten that Bea used to sit at the corner of the counter when there was mending. “Would she want to?”

“She has a whole sewing basket under the sink because there's nowhere to leave it out. She told me if I bring her one more sock with a hole in it, she'll turn it into a glove so she only has to fix the thumb. Yeah, she'd want to.”

“The library in the afternoon, then.”

“Did the librarian say we could do repairs or put out a sign?”

Mara looked at the announcement. The word FIX was twice as tall as the word IT. She had told the librarian the repairs would be small. She hadn't said anything about a sewing basket or a line or the small tools Grandpa might have in mind. “Probably.”

“Did you ask?”

She laid her palm across the title. “I've been on the phone all morning. I asked lots of things.”

Kit opened his mouth, then turned his head. She waited for the bus door to play its tune. It stayed shut. “When somebody asks you how you know a thing,” he said at last, “you say what you know about a different thing. You did that at lunch Monday.”

“What does Monday have to do with the library?”

“Nothing. That was when you did it last.”

He picked up her pencil and added a question mark after the place where an address should go. She had disliked many of Kit's artworks, especially the one where he had drawn the school principal with gills and given it to the principal. She disliked this one more.

She sent the library a message. By the time she had descended the outside stairs and was waiting to cross the road, the reply was in her pocket. The rule for the big front room was no tools. Even the old sewing club used a room at the back. That room was booked for a private group all Saturday. The librarian could spare an hour on Friday afternoon if they could clear the table by five.

Friday afternoon Mara had promised to help her mother sort winter invoices, and Bea was working. Mr. Low's carport might work if Grandpa ran the whole thing alone. Grandpa would say yes if she took it to him sounding desperate. Just yesterday he had stopped reading a letter from the bank to mend a tear in Aunt Celia's oven mitt. He had spent ten minutes on the little unimportant tear. He had turned the bank letter face down.

At the far curb Mara folded the announcement so small that nobody could read the title. She had thought the trial would be getting people to trust that the old shop was still theirs. In the space of one bus tune it had turned into something more awkward. She was going to need other people to tell her what the shop had been. So far she knew where three families were, two available times, and one good question Kit had asked that she wished he hadn't.

Halfway home, she pulled out the paper again. Under his question mark, in little letters that followed the torn edge, she wrote a second one. *What were you coming for?*

At supper Grandpa asked how many people she had invited. Mara held up the little folded paper as evidence of her morning's labor. He waited. She put it back down. There was no way to make a number come out of it.

“I haven't invited anybody yet,” she said, and found that the sentence became easier toward the end. “I think I have to ask a different question first.”

He moved his plate out of the way and asked to see the paper. Mara unfolded it all the way this time. He held down a corner with the handle of his fork. Nothing on the notice would tell him that she'd already talked to four people and walked halfway up the hill, or that a library could offer a table you couldn't set a sewing basket on. After a minute he turned it back to the blank side. “When you ask,” he said, “this is where you can write down what you hear.” She drew her thumbnail through the biggest crease. It was still there, but the fold lay flat.

## Chapter 3. The Other End of the Table

On Tuesday after school, Mara walked the route of the number four bus with Kit. They had agreed to begin at the first stop west of the garage and work east. Kit had a watch. Mara carried a pencil, the folded announcement and several empty slips Grandpa had torn from his last clean order pad. On the first slip she had written her new question. Kit read it over her shoulder.

“Sounds like you're going to ask somebody why they're on the bus.”

“What would you write?”

He thought for half the block. “‘What's one thing you used to do at the shop?’ They can say they never came. That would tell us something too.”

At the first bench a person in a green cap was peeling an orange into a paper bag. Mara had planned to start with somebody she knew. She sat a little too close and slid along the bench. The person glanced sideways.

“I'm Mara. Did you ever go to our shop on Mill?”

“Your uncle did the chairs?”

“Grandpa. Sometimes I did the tags.”

The person held up the orange, as if Mara had asked whether it was hers. “Your grandpa told me to see Bea for a strap on a walking bag. Then the lady behind me took out two pieces of webbing and wanted to know how long I was going to be in town. It took a minute to figure out that she had been Bea the whole time.”

Mara wrote *Bea* on the slip. “Would you bring something to another Saturday?”

“If it was right by the bus, maybe. I'd need a place to set it down while I wait.”

Mara was first to notice that the bench seat was too narrow to hold a bag and a person side by side. She slid to the very end. Beside her knee there was a round gap where a bolt looked as if it should have been. She let the gap occupy her attention until she could think of a way to ask about waiting without sounding like the person was doing it wrong.

“What would make it easier to wait?” she said.

“I'd like to know about how long. If everybody had a time on a slip, I could come back.”

At the next stop, the driver opened the door to see what they wanted. It was Amel, whose dog had a low wooden chair Grandpa had repaired twice. She knew all the Saturday routes. The bus went up the hill in the afternoon, but between noon and the next circuit the driver had a long break. If a person missed the earlier bus up Switchback, they would wait more than an hour. Mara drew a line between the bus garage and the library. The line felt like the sort of evidence she'd wanted all winter. It also made her carport idea, which she hadn't quite given up, look worse.

At the third stop, a boy in a school sweatshirt asked if the shop could repair a violin neck. Mara didn't think so. He wanted to know whether they could mend the soft case. She said she could ask. They talked for several minutes about the loose strap on the case before the boy admitted he had never been inside the repair shop. He used to look in after his lessons. The counter looked so tall from the window that he'd thought you had to be a grown-up to ask for anything.

After he got on the bus, Mara stood looking at the space his shoes had left. “It was tall,” she said.

“To him.”

She tried to think what the counter looked like when she was four. The top had been above her eyes. Her grandfather had let her set her feet on a box to see over. Then he'd shifted the box a little every time she grew, until one day she'd walked past without stopping to find it. She had assumed any child wanting to see over the counter knew where the box was.

They ended at the bus garage after the last circuit before supper. Amel was checking a form at the open office window. A man had come in with a set of keys for her, and by the way he placed them in her hand Mara guessed he was the person who would have the next shift. As soon as the keys left his fingers he became oddly cheerful.

Kit poked his head in and said Amel should ask her next question to Mara. She said her next question was whether there was a pencil on the floor under the window. There was. Mara returned it and found herself, in the instant between handing over the pencil and stepping away, asking if the garage had anywhere two people could use small hand tools. She expected the office window to close. Instead Amel pointed out a side door and asked if Mara had seen the passenger room.

The room was small, with wide panes that looked toward the lot. A crack in one pane ended in a little field of clear tape. There was a drinking fountain with two heights, a long brown bench and a folding table. A man with a backpack slept in the far corner. Once he woke, Amel would tell him where to catch his bus. There had been no ticket sales in here since she had moved them to the front window. But people still waited here whenever it rained.

“You're asking for a space that's already doing something,” Amel said. “Suppose you leave people somewhere to wait. Suppose the kids can get around the room without having to squeeze past a repair.”

All the way from the first stop, Mara had been trying to infer when it would be easiest to get people over to the repairs. Here the door was open between the room and the bus stop, and people were already getting to it. She crossed the room to its far end, where the table had been turned against the wall. The man with the backpack snored once in the middle of a perfectly ordinary breath. She whispered anyway.

“What if we made the appointments right at the door, and we kept this for bus riders until a person actually wanted a repair? Nobody in the waiting room has to come to us. We could use one end of the table. Put a chair at the other end for Bea so the sewing stays out of the way.”

Amel said she would ask the manager about one trial Saturday, from one to three. There were conditions. No one would handle tools unless one of the adults in charge had okayed it. Nobody would bring lamps or anything with a cord. There'd be no food or money laid out on the worktable, and when they left, the only change in the room should be a few extra sweep marks. The garage could offer a broom.

“What if it's too small?” Mara asked Kit afterward.

“For what?”

It was hard to say. She had been imagining the old counter, Grandpa behind it, the letters from the door above him. Hearing the wait-room rules, she had realized that none of them fit except Grandpa. And Bea, if she had a chair. And other people whose names might just reach her if they could come through the door.

Kit picked up the announcement. He didn't smooth the crease all the way. He opened it on the stairs to his apartment and drew three chairs under the bell. They were the only things he put there.

When Bea got home, Mara told her what they'd asked about. Bea pushed her gloves together, finger into finger, until each glove seemed to share the same hand.

“One end of a table,” she said. “You got me that much?”

Mara started to say the room was Kit's idea. Then she saw that Bea had sat down on the stair, though she was still wearing her work shoes. Bea had put one hand on the inside door handle because there was nothing else beside her to keep her steady. Mara took out the piece of paper with her name on it. “That's how much you want?” she said.

“For Saturday. Then ask me again.”

Mara wrote that down as well. It seemed like a small sentence to save. She still had a clean slip, so she used it.

## Chapter 4. When the Buses Come

The first thing to arrive on Saturday was an empty bag. Mr. Low put it on the table while he took off his jacket, and Mara asked what he had brought. He had come to pick up six spoons from a person on the two o'clock bus. Somebody's silver drawer had broken in the move and they were dividing up the pieces. His bag was in good condition.

“Still, could you look at this?” he said, and turned the bag over to show a seam. It would have taken Bea about a minute. Behind him, though, the next person was already bringing in a doll blanket they'd talked to Bea about on the phone. Mara offered Mr. Low a time for later and pointed to a chair he could use while he waited for the bus. At first he'd looked disappointed. Then he sat where he could see the street and set the bag squarely at his feet.

After a little while he put his jacket back on. “Just in case I step out,” he told Mara. She said she would call his name if the spoons showed up and he was out of sight. In the old shop nobody would have called his name for spoons.

Grandpa worked slowly with the tools he'd brought. He opened and closed the catch of a wooden recipe box until he could tell where it scraped. He showed its owner the faint new line it had cut in the wood. When Mara brought over a paper slip, he asked her to write *catch sticks open*, exactly what the owner had said. The owner asked whether she meant to get rid of the scrape. Grandpa drew the pad closer so he could read the slip himself. The scrape had told him what to try, he said. Afterward the owner could decide if she wanted to see it.

At the table's other end Bea tightened two stitches on a cloth bag before she looked up. “Whose is this?” she asked. There were three hands in the air. It turned out to belong to the only person standing there whose hand was down, a woman helping Mr. Low identify the spoons. Names went on the slips after that, even when everybody was sure they'd remember. Kit tore the strips. Bea wrote the names. Mara called them out, feeling the oddness of each one in her mouth until a person responded.

At two the bus arrived. Twelve coats blew into the room, many of them with people inside who had come for the next bus and knew nothing about the repairs. Mara had expected a string of people going up to the table one at a time. Instead, neighbors came across the room to greet people they hadn't seen. On the other side of this moving screen, Mara saw the person in the green cap who had told her about the strap. The person had a little carry bag and paused in the doorway, watching how the crowd moved. Then the head in the green cap turned back toward the lot.

Mara climbed off her chair. On the table lay her original announcement, folded under a pencil to stop it curling. She could go to the doorway and call out that the repairs were indeed being done, in the room just as the sign said. As she took hold of the sign she saw the three chairs Kit had drawn. Apart from Mr. Low's, two real chairs stood behind the repair table. All the other chairs were near the window. It was hard to see a place to set anything down between the table and the door.

“Kit,” she said. “Would you take this chair?”

“I'm standing.”

She pointed to the gap between the sign and the door. Together they carried two empty chairs to one side of the doorway, keeping the way in clear. She took the pencil off the paper. On its unprinted back she wrote, *Start here*, and carried it to the green cap. She gave the person a time and a place to set the bag down. They stayed. When the bus doors folded shut, there was still a line, but if you came into the room through the door you met a chair and a question before you met the crowd.

While Grandpa put a felt dot where the catch had scraped, Bea mended the strap of the boy's violin case. Kit held the case off his knees to give her room for the last stitch. The boy put the case down where she showed him. “It's different from where it was,” he said, turning it toward the light.

Mara thought that meant he would ask her to move it.

“That's how I can tell where you saw it breaking,” he explained. He reached out to carry the case and found a slip still tied to the handle. A name, a rough idea of the repair, and a time were all there. On the back he drew a crooked bow as small as a fly and gave the slip to Bea. When she tucked it into her apron pocket, she tucked it without folding it, and one white corner showed.

Near the end a little girl came to the table with nothing to mend. She had watched the bus through the window with her face pressed so close that the glass showed four small beads of breath. For several minutes she watched Mara write. Then she held out both hands, apparently offering an invisible thing that needed to be examined.

Mara gave her a spare slip, which was all the invisible thing seemed to require. The girl wrote four lines with a red pencil and showed the pattern to her father. Then she brought it to the girl by the window who'd lent her the pencil. The bus pulled up again. Mara thought one of them might forget the slip, but the girl passed it through the open door to her father before she climbed onto the bus herself. She had made a place for it to go.

At three, while Kit swept, Mara counted eleven items that had come for a look and nine that had left with a repair. One needed a part, and one was too damaged for anyone there to help. She wrote all three numbers in her notebook. Her mother glanced over her shoulder and pointed to the empty line below them.

“What's that for?”

Mara waited until Mr. Low came over with his bag of spoons. He set a spoon down on the table. The bowl was round but the handle didn't line up with it. He wanted them to see something about the pattern, so he turned it in the light.

His brother's hand, he told them, had bent it years ago when he was learning to carry food to the table without dropping it. You could see the shape of a thumb in the bend. He asked if he could keep it just that way. Grandpa slid the spoon toward him, and Mara wrote *one thing left as it was*. There were plenty of things they could have said about the day, but those were the words she wanted in the empty line.

That evening the repair slips lay spread on Aunt Celia's table. Grandpa poured out the loose change from his pocket so they would have weights to keep the papers from sliding into the crack in the middle. Aunt Celia picked up the one with the bow. Bea told her it had come from a boy she'd never seen until that day. She wanted it back when Aunt Celia finished. Grandpa had made a thin scrap of sanding paper into a wedge to quiet the table's rocking. It was a very small change. They heard it only when the table stopped making a sound.

It had seemed to Mara that the trial would end when she could tell Grandpa where everybody used to come. She watched him help Aunt Celia slide the slips into an envelope, so a person could find them without having been there. She knew far more about where some people had been and where they could go next. And Grandpa looked happier now than he had when she had first told him her plan.

“Same time next month?” he asked Bea.

“Ask me what I need next month,” Bea said.

“All right. What do you need next month?”

Bea held her hands apart as far as the seat of a chair. Then she pushed them sideways, making a little more room. “That much,” she said, and smiled at Mara so they both knew it was a beginning.

## Chapter 5. Everything That Can Come Out

On Wednesday the contractor set three boxes on the steps at Aunt Celia's. The biggest said *cabinet*, but there was no cabinet in it. There were two handles wrapped in a sheet of brown paper, a piece of brass folded over at one end, and a drawer. The drawer had a black four on the bottom and a round dark dot where one screw had been. Mara turned it upside down, then right side up, as if it might contain something in one position that it didn't in the other.

“The crew got to the list once the building was supported,” said the contractor, whose name was Nate. He drew a finger over the short scar that ran down the side of the drawer. “This one was already loose. The rest of the frame was coming apart where it stood. I took what I could without bringing the damaged wall with it.”

Grandpa came down a step and picked up the brass. Mara remembered how often he'd warned her against running her thumbnail under the strip because it would leave a black line that a scrub brush couldn't get out. He held it as if she might ask to have a turn with it. She wanted to take the folded end and bend it back to the shape it had been. Instead, she touched the drawer. Damp had lifted the wood around the bottom. It felt like the rough side of a piece of paper, and she could see where the damp had stopped.

“The door?” her mother asked.

Nate unfastened the second box. From it he lifted a chipped enamel number, 18, which he handed to her in both palms. Mara had put the numbers on the drawing without needing to look. Until she saw these ones in her mother's hands she would have said they were the size of her head. They were smaller than the letters on her school sweatshirt.

“We left the door in place. I'll show you why in the pictures.” Nate went through the short list with a red pen. One mark for each thing his crew had brought. A different mark for the five he had left. Next to *counter* he drew both marks.

In the smallest box was something wrapped in a towel old enough to belong to their family. Mara recognized the orange stripe. Nate pulled back a corner so she could see the bell. The base had a shallow dent at the rim. When he set it down, it rested on two sides of the dent and rocked if you touched it. Until now she had pictured it lying upright in the wreckage, waiting to be pressed. Nate's photo showed it upside down between a board and a broken flowerpot.

“Does it ring?” she asked.

“Didn't try.”

Grandpa put the brass down. He waited for Mara to press the bell. She moved her thumb toward it twice before she let it go the whole way. The sound was clear but thinner than she remembered. Then Grandpa tried it over the open drawer. The wood caught the sound and threw it back at them, and the note was closer to what she'd expected. Not quite. She tried again without the drawer. The note seemed to strike the bottom step and fall through.

“It works,” her mother said. “Why do you look so mad?”

Mara was angry at the step. The step was in the wrong place, and so was the rest of the porch and the hedge and the house. There were three boxes beside her with all the things she'd be able to carry in one trip. The things she couldn't carry weren't going to come after her. She stared at Nate until he looked back. She'd forgotten he didn't know what she was going to say.

“Thanks for the bell,” she said. He nodded and brought the empty box sides up so the wind wouldn't tip them. Once he left, Grandpa took the drawer along the walk to wash a lump of loose mud out of the corner, thinking later about whether there was a use for it. Mud slid down the bucket and stayed in a shape like the mouth of the shop entrance.

That evening Mara climbed the library stairs to see the town's display. There were three walls to walk around, each with maps and short blocks of type. Near the door stood a container of yellow slips and a woman called Ruth who worked in the town office. The slips asked people to mark sites they might consider for a temporary workspace. Both the old seed store and a shed off Patch Lane had been offered for proposals. The shed stood across from the washhouse and its back opened toward the playing field. Six months ago Mara could have walked there from her own house in the time it took to heat a piece of toast. She could have gone there by sound. Grandpa's last jobs moved uphill in boxes from that direction, and here was a chance to go almost all the way back.

The red line on the printed map bypassed the shed's front steps. *Limit of surveyed water*, said the caption. Inside the closed line was the center of the playing field and all of the old shop lot. The shed's rectangle lay just outside. Mara colored the little square beside *Patch Lane shed*. It seemed unnecessary to shade the space beside the seed store, too. She felt as if the color might pull the two buildings toward each other.

“What's the difference between these open circles and these dots?” she asked Ruth. There was an example of each in the key, but the words by the open circles were squeezed into a little tab.

Ruth came around the table. “An open circle means nobody could check that spot. The survey crew stayed on the public road here. Between the shed and the bend is a stretch they couldn't see, so look for the dotted part of the outline. We joined two observations across that gap, but they're far apart.”

“How far?”

They bent to the map. Ruth pointed out a pole at one end of the dotted section and the wall of the washhouse at the other. Mara put two fingertips down where her great-grandmother might have put two coins. It was ten easy steps between her fingertips, though on the ground the space held the entire mouth of Patch Lane. “So it could have gone in there.”

“The building owner says she had a dry floor. That's another piece of evidence. We're asking about the road too.”

Mara followed the road with the side of her pencil. A car might leave the shed from this corner, but even a tiny pencil couldn't get to the other end of the street without crossing the colored area. She had sat on a little step at that corner waiting for the school bus for five years. A white line on a map gave her a shorter way to remember it. The colored area gave her a reason to keep looking.

Ruth went to show another visitor where he could get a larger print. Mara stayed at the map. Nobody stood at her elbow. She put her finger on the place where the old shop's step bent toward the house and thought of Ruth telling her she'd have to get off the public road to check a certain spot. She knew which half of every step was steady. To the person making the map, the step had been a small bend in a line. On a second map she'd have to put the iron railing where it was. People needed it to take a step down from the sidewalk, even if they never went in the door. She'd also need to make a place for Mr. Low's bag somewhere off the street. His bag had been in perfect shape on Saturday. People came to a place like this to ask something even when they had nothing broken.

In the corner Ruth was helping the visitor hold his bigger map flat. He put a little circle around an address. When he asked her for one other piece of paper, Mara carried it over from the pile. He folded the two maps in different directions before he took them away. One was folded around his own circle. The other opened first at the key.

By the time her mother arrived, Mara had drawn a light circle around the seed store. She didn't turn in the yellow slip. She kept it in her hand while they went around the other two walls of the display. On one panel she read a heading about buildings. On the next she found office hours for people who needed to ask about money. Near the door her mother collected a slip from the pile and shaded in both squares.

“You still think the shed is possible?” Mara asked.

“I think I need to look before I agree to a lease. We can ask for a visit and take this map along.”

Outside Mara folded her own slip with the printed squares facing outward, so she could read both names without opening it. She took the long way home because her mother wanted to know where the road changed from white to red on the drawing. At the first bend they came to a piece of temporary fencing that a crew had moved away from the curb. Beside it was a hole where the fence had stood. Mara pointed to the other road as well as the fence.

“The bus used to turn there,” she said. “Count that part too.”

## Chapter 6. A Clear Floor

Mara's mother arranged to see the Patch Lane shed on a bright Saturday when the dry grass clicked against the cuffs of their pants. Its owner, Ms. Idris, met them on the lower end of Switchback Road so they could take the open sidewalk together. She had a little folding rule on a loop in her pocket and gave it to Mara to open. It popped to attention a section at a time. It was too short to measure even the smallest room in the shed.

“I thought you'd like how it bends,” Ms. Idris said. “My sister gave it to me. She says you only need to know the rest if the first six inches fit.”

Mara carried the rule unfolded. The shed stood on a rise modest enough that a person driving past might not have named it a rise. This was what she used to like about Patch Lane. Her house seemed to be just a little bit higher than the water. You could find a few more feet to stand on without feeling like you had walked away from anything. Ms. Idris showed them a yellow line she had painted below the sill to remind whoever cut the grass where the ground rose. The shed door opened toward the playing field. The field had one white goal left. Twin white cups, the mounts for the other, still showed at the far end.

Inside, the pale wood floor was so clean Mara would have taken off her shoes for it. There were four long rectangles where the sun had gone around old cabinets and made the rest of the boards darker. A broken corner of chalk was still balanced over the narrow window. It made her want to write an address on the floor big enough to walk around in. Her mother took off her jacket. It was the first room they'd looked at for work since the flood where anyone took off a jacket.

“We moved the cupboards out last summer,” Ms. Idris said. “The roof is in good shape. With a short lease and a deposit we can leave the monthly cost where it is. You could start with a worktable and figure out what else you need.”

“Can we see what happened at the road?” her mother asked.

Ms. Idris understood at once. She turned her head to look at the field before she moved her feet. “By the willows. You won't be able to tell from the window.”

The three went back to the public sidewalk. Mara had expected to see a bare place where the town could lay new stones and make a fresh road with a bright white edge. Instead, the edge of the road rose into an ordinary bank. At the far end of the bank, one of the pipes crossing beneath the road ended past the ground that used to hold it. The air beneath its mouth was the same color as the air everywhere else. Once Mara had seen it, she couldn't look anywhere but there.

“I'm dry in the shed,” Ms. Idris said. “But I can't promise the road. For weeks afterward I went the long way round to check it.”

Mara shook out the library map. The turn from the shed was marked with three dashes and the word *closed*. She had seen the three dashes at home and thought of them as a short delay on the way to a room with a clean floor. The second page from the library display was a copy of a flood explanation with a paragraph about rapid flooding. It explained that water can rush into low ground far from the rain that set it moving. At home she'd thought of that as a fact about weather somewhere else. Here the pipe opened into a strip of blue sky, and she could imagine trusting the same blue on her way to work. She ran her finger from the building across the place where the town still had a barricade.

“Where would we go if this way closed while we were working?” she asked.

Ms. Idris pointed back the way they'd come. “The sidewalk to Switchback is open today. We don't have an all-weather route from here. I'd tell anybody who rented the shed to follow road closures and leave if officials said to leave. Before you sign anything you'd want to ask them about access from the rest of town.”

Mara set the folding rule down on the map. It made a straight short line between the shed and the washhouse. Toward the beginning of the visit she might have drawn a dotted path there and marked it *shortcut*. Now she wondered how much of her picture of a working shop consisted of people getting there just in time. The boy with the violin case had come early because his mother could only drive him then. At the bus garage, a covered chair and the repair table had been in the same room. There was nothing between a chair and the shed except the low stretch of road and that folder of sky between the bottom of the pipe and the earth.

“It may be a lovely place for something,” her mother said later, when they'd walked back up to Switchback and stood looking at the curve. She hadn't answered Ms. Idris about the lease. “We can hold on to the numbers while we work out the rest. Did you see something you want to put on the list?”

Mara turned the rule over in her hand. She was still carrying it. “The floor.”

“Is that enough?”

She looked back at the shed. At this angle she could see the slope, which was clear enough to put in a drawing. What she couldn't see was the piece of empty air under the pipe. “I want to put down a question instead,” she said. “If you drew everyone's whole trip, where would it go?”

Ms. Idris came back to get her folding rule. Mara bent it at two of the hinges before she gave it back. “Like Switchback Road,” she said. “We want to ask about roads and the hill room before we pick.”

“Can I leave you the price for a week?” asked Ms. Idris. “If you decide against it early, let me know. I've got somebody who wants to store empty crates. They wouldn't have to have the public coming through.”

Mara could think of worse things to have on that floor than rows of empty crates with dry wood all around them. The crates could wait until somebody came for them. Several customers she had met at the bus stop couldn't wait that long.

They took the long way home again. In the time it took to climb the first bend, Mara thought of three things she'd still like to do in the shed. She would put all the chairs where she could see them from the door. She would make one shelf from the wall to the low window. She would stay late just once and watch the last light leave the playing field. Before she said any of it, she told her mother what Ms. Idris had offered about the other tenant. Her mother sat flat on a dry stone and called from the bend. Mara waited on the walk with the map folded in her hand. She felt bad and relieved before her mother had even said hello.

Her mother said into the phone that they wouldn't take the offer right now. She listened. A truck engine coming up the hill almost covered her answer. “No, the room looked good. And I'm glad you showed us what's happened to the road.” She was quiet for such a long time afterward that Mara looked over to see whether the call had ended. Her mother had leaned her head on one shoulder. She held the phone against her as though she were trying to keep it dry. When she got up, Mara asked if Ms. Idris had sounded disappointed.

“She asked whether anyone could come see her as a customer once we get a new place. She's got a rocking chair she'd like to keep using.”

Mara started to say to bring it. Then she thought of the front door and the fact that they'd still have to find one. “Can we ask what happened to it?”

“She thinks it's missing a pin. She wanted to know when we might be able to look. I said I'd find out.” Her mother held out the phone. “She's sent a picture already. Can you get it bigger?” Mara enlarged the picture. At the edge of it was a perfectly smooth place in the carpet, where the chair curved up above the floor.

## Chapter 7. Three Columns

“We can afford it if nobody gets sick, nothing needs to be delivered, and all the customers pay exactly when they'd say they would,” Mara's mother said. “Which of those would you put on a sign?”

She spoke to Grandpa, but it was Mara who looked up. At dinner, a week after the shed visit, they had covered half the table with numbers. Aunt Celia was cutting an apple on a board she had set across two bowls so they wouldn't run out of flat places to put things. Her table was made for six. Four people and a pile of forms made it seem smaller than a coffee table.

“That's for bringing the Mill Street shop up to what the code office asks?” Grandpa pointed to a number for work on the foundation. He had set his reading glasses on his head. He leaned far enough to make them slide to his nose by themselves.

“And we'd still have a house to decide about. Even in this estimate, the shop is closed a long time. Also, the old shops that brought people down the street are going to be scattered for a while.”

Grandpa studied the estimate. “We can open at a different address and still have to wait for customers to find us. Remember how quiet it got before school let out?”

“I remember you moving a coffee cup four times so it couldn't be empty.”

Mara's mother kept at it. She drew three columns on butcher paper: *repair the old shop*, *rent the shed*, and *use the seed store*. In each column, under the price, she wrote what would have to happen before a customer could walk in. There was a mark by the shed. Mara had brought home the folded sketch of the closed road and held it against the paper next to that mark. The shed was still on the table because Grandpa hadn't been to see it and wanted to know why the others were ready to set it aside. When they had gone around the numbers once, Grandpa wrote *access unresolved* beside it himself.

The last column hardly had anything in it. The town owned the seed store. It had an unfinished room, an empty stockroom, and a deep covered entry above Switchback Road. There was a hard floor in one of the rooms and green linoleum in the other. Ruth had told Mara that the town would take proposals from businesses that could use the space and help pay for the building. She hadn't promised that there would be any money left for making it pretty. Upstairs was storage, and it would stay storage. An apartment on Ash Hill sometimes came up for rent. It was across the street and ten doors down, in a different building the owner had kept in good repair. Mara could feel her mother making a place on the paper for the things she could pay for and the things she couldn't.

Kit stopped by with two clean aprons from Bea, who had washed them after the free repair afternoon. He left the door open for a minute while he unwound the straps from his wrists. Evening light showed at floor level. He picked the aprons out of their bag and handed one to Grandpa. The other was Grandpa's, too. In winter Bea used the spare whenever she needed to carry a pencil and pins at the same time.

Kit examined the three columns. “Where's my mom's?” he asked.

Mara checked herself before she answered. Bea had rented her own rooms down on Mill. Ordinarily if she needed room somewhere else she'd ask for it. Bea's Saturday chair had been assigned before Mara first heard about the seed store. The only measurements Mara had ready for Bea were the distance between two legs of a folding table. She pulled a chair out for Kit.

“We talked to Ruth about the building,” said Mara's mother. “Other people can put in a proposal. The form has a space for one lead tenant and room to attach other names. I don't know how much Bea can put toward it. Has she talked to you about it?”

Kit sat sideways, as if the moment his mother emerged in any answer the chair would need to go back. “She's got three names in her phone for desks in other towns. One wants money just to look, so she won't call that one. One's in the town where the grocery's warehouse is. She could go with her work ride. She hasn't told me what the third is yet.”

Mara wrote *Bea* on the bottom of the paper where the grease spot from the apple board began. “Ask her how much room she's thinking.”

Kit put both hands on the chair seat and turned his body around. “You can ask her. She might say she wants a door she can shut. She might say she doesn't want something that takes you two hours to set up and put away each week.” He looked over at the laundry bag. “I left something in the other pocket.”

In the spare apron was the strip with the bow. Grandpa held it up for Mara to see. One of the corners was worn thinner than the rest, and there was a new mark at the bottom, a little purple line. Grandpa asked Kit if he'd made it. Kit shook his head. Bea used those lines to mark jobs she had finished. She'd thought of the violin case again after she saw the boy waiting outside his lesson. He'd brought the case to her on a weekday, this time for a new zipper. He had found her by asking around near the garage. She had had to tell him to come back when there was a table she could work at.

In the space for the seed store, Mara added *ask who has to fit*. She could see the column from her place even when her grandfather leaned back. It looked like a short list. That was because she hadn't learned the names or the dimensions yet. She left room in the margin and around the apple board where they might go.

“Does the shed get its name on the paper if we're not taking it?” Kit asked.

“It's so we can remember we looked,” said Grandpa. He took the paper about repairs to the old shop, moved it to Mara's end of the table and lined it up with the big sheet. “We can read them in the same light.”

The next day Mara found a little line on the page where he had listed the parts of the counter he thought he could use. She'd thought that Grandpa would need somebody to convince him to come uphill. Now, turned sideways in a square no longer than her finger, the line said *brass for a smaller edge*. His letters came to a point on the word *small*. They always had, in room or out of it.

That Saturday, an old customer named Hal dropped off a stuck drawer. He had asked where the shop was going, and Mara's mother had said there were a few possibilities. If Hal had asked a second question, it might have been how anybody could keep coming if they were never told where. Instead he turned the drawer upside down on the porch rail. He had brought it by bus with the knob poking through a hole in a paper sack. The knob had worn through the sack when the bus made its last long turn. He picked up the paper with two fingers to show Mara.

“I'd have tied a string on if I'd known,” he said. “Next time I'll have to bring the whole cabinet.”

Grandpa put his glasses on and looked at the gouge where the drawer had caught. He asked Hal if the cabinet was something one person could carry. Hal counted out the pieces for him. There were enough for three trips and two people. He sat on the porch step while they talked. He wanted to know who would be free at four on a weekday. A big repair could take most of the space an adult could sweep in an afternoon, and Hal hadn't planned to set it up in his own hall. They agreed to wait until somebody knew where there would be a bench. Hal left the drawer on a damp spot he hadn't seen. Mara moved it into the light and told her mother what parts of the trip would be his to make.

## Chapter 8. Cart Before the Key

Ruth took an iron key out of her pocket on the first morning the town opened the seed store for visits. It was longer than the key to Aunt Celia's house and about half an inch shorter than the line Mara made for it in her notebook while she waited. One clump of visitors had gathered on the level landing. Another stood near the side windows, trying to see how far back the dark went. Mara's friend Kit had a rolled paper tube under his arm. Bea had brought a low cart with the machine she used every weekday, and she stood beside it in her work shoes.

“We can't take the cart inside unless Ruth clears it,” said Mara's mother.

“I only want to try the entrance,” Bea said. She made sure she'd been heard before she put her hands on the cart. “A room's a room. I'd like to see whether I can get from the stop to it without unloading the cart on the sidewalk.”

There were two ways to the store from the bus stop. One followed the sidewalk and its long, level turn into the covered entry. The second went straight across a side lot and up two shallow steps. From the stop a person could see the steps, the front windows, and a sign that said *Seed and Feed* from a long time ago. The round lettering had caught the morning light. The sign for the level way in was small, at the angle where the sidewalk turned. The corner of a roof downspout hid half of it. Mara could tell which way the cart would go before Bea pointed a wheel down one route. She put the shape of the sign in her notebook.

Mara herself had come by the steps. The door on the landing opened toward the visitors, and as Ruth turned the big key each of them stood back another inch. There was just enough room for everybody who was there if you counted only the people. The cart stopped at the turn from the sidewalk. It couldn't pass the group until there was a reason for everyone to move. Ruth stepped ahead of it and made the reason herself. “First five can see the front room, then we'll switch. Please leave space for this wheel.”

The front room smelled very faintly of paper. There was a shallow line where a row of shelves had leaned against the green wall. Kit held the tube against the line and looked down it as if he expected to see a little passage through to the old store. Mara kept hearing one heel clack along the floor much louder than the other. It turned out to be her own. One of her soles had picked up a piece of plastic from outdoors. Until she pulled it off she could hear which places she'd walked back over.

Bea tried the cart in from the level entrance once Ruth said she could. The cart's rubber wheels made two gentle tracks in the dust. One track turned toward the front room, where the table might go. The other stopped a little short of the wall. There was a rough strip of board lying across the threshold into the stockroom. A corner had lifted. It touched the cart, and the cart began to turn without Bea telling it to. Bea stopped it, backed up, and asked Ruth if the repair budget could cover the threshold. Ruth took a photograph with a short ruler next to the strip for scale.

Mara pulled out her own picture of the room. In it the table occupied the sunniest part. The bench Grandpa would need had one good wall to go against. There was a square for the bell. She had marked the shallow steps but not the level entrance, which she hadn't seen until Bea took it. Moving her pencil from the bus stop all the way to the bench brought her right through the largest number on the page. It was the total space her family planned to ask for. She set the notebook on top of Kit's tube.

He tugged the tube free. “This is a door,” he said. “Or it will be if you borrow my tape before it gets cold.”

He had taped three sheets of wrapping paper together to make a full-size outline of a door. At home he had compared it with the entrance to their apartment, which he was always having to hold open with his foot while Bea got the cart up the outside stairs with help. The paper had two slits where a hand might push at the hinge side and the handle side. He stretched the whole thing flat with care and asked his mother to stand on the inside and imagine she had a shoulder against one end of it.

“Why are we making a door if there's one already?” one of the visitors asked. He sold fruit at the market, and he had come to see whether he could keep boxes in the stockroom. He was known for having strips of paper and a marker ready in his vest. For once he had neither.

“It's the rest of the space around it,” Kit said. “When you hold it open with your foot, what have you got left?”

The fruit seller put one foot flat on Kit's drawing. With his other foot he tried to take a step from the handle side. His knee swung into the place where his hand would have been holding a box. He tried again, and the paper slid. “You could stand on the quiet side and hold it for somebody,” he said. He took two steps around the paper and found himself in front of the old shelves, where Mara had meant to put a repair bench.

She hadn't thought the man would want to stand there. In her drawing there would have been a wall between him and the bench. That was a way to give a worker quiet. In the storefront, somebody needed to come through with boxes. Somebody else needed to sit down before they offered to run the desk. Mara wondered how many people they could ask to stay in motion while they waited, and what sort of things they'd have to put down along the way.

In the front room a small shelf still stuck out over the old baseboard. It was at about the height of a cane handle. Kit asked Ruth if it had been left there for a particular reason. Ruth showed them how the shelf ended right below a window that used to open. From outside the window, on the side toward the stairs, the same piece of wood would be level with a person's chin. Somebody had once set money on it from one side and taken it from the other. Mara got a coin out of her pocket and pushed it to the far edge. From here, she could move her hand farther than the coin would go. She imagined standing outside with only the slot to tell her whether the room was warm.

“Would we keep it?” Kit asked.

“It hasn't shown up on anybody's plan,” Ruth said. She waited. Kit took two photographs from the floor, one looking at the shelf and the other from below it toward the window. Looking over his shoulder, Mara could only see where the shelf went when she could look at both pictures at once. She put a question mark on her own drawing in the place where she'd thought she had only a wall.

Ruth told the visitors about a second visit, after the town cleared a loose piece of the doorway and arranged marks for the proposed spaces. They would be able to walk from the main room to the stop with somebody who knew which side of the road the bus would use. If anyone wanted to try a different layout, Ruth could bring removable tape. Mara borrowed a pen and signed up her family's name. She left a line below it. Then she slid the page to Bea and asked the fruit seller to wait while she got another.

That afternoon they took Kit's paper door over to the kitchen table. They unrolled it just enough for the edge to hang low beside a chair. The rest had to gather in soft loops to stay on the wood. Grandpa pressed each loop down with his palm and let it rise again. He asked if the bell would fit on the bench he could see in Mara's notebook. She told him how the cart got almost to that spot.

“You want the bench any farther from the door?” she said.

“I want to see who walks through,” Grandpa said. He looked up with his hand still on the loop of paper, and she could tell he thought that was a measurement. She turned to a clean sheet and wrote exactly what he said.

## Chapter 9. The Way Around

For the second visit Ruth brought measuring tape, a roll of blue tape for the floor, and a clipboard with three sheets of paper that kept trying to get away. Amel came from the garage in the sweater she wore on her days off. She could tell, even without the bus, how much time a bus would need to make a turn. From the upper stop, she pointed downhill to what was left of a white line painted across the pavement. The bus used to come up from that direction after serving the low streets. The new schedule sent it toward the hill by the west approach first, and the uphill stop was served less often on Saturdays. That was one way to run fewer stops without sending buses onto roads that hadn't reopened.

“Can they put a stop at the bottom of the sidewalk?” Mara asked.

“They can look. Send the town a request and tell them whose trip would be easier. I can show you the stops we actually have.”

Amel held a timetable at arm's length. There was a town address at the bottom for comments about transit. Above it two separate rows began at the same place and ended at different ones. In the Saturday column the last afternoon bus was close enough to the end of the repair hours that somebody would have to choose between having a thing fixed and getting home with it. Mara had experienced that kind of choice exactly once. She and Kit had wanted to watch the part of a game that might go into extra time. They had left watching the scoreboard over their shoulders, and their walk had seemed to last longer than a quarter. For Kit's mother and others who took the bus to work, a missed trip made a different kind of trouble.

Mara took a picture of the timetable to send to Mr. Low. The shaded part on the paper came out white in the picture, and the boundary between Saturday and the rest of the week was hardly visible. She erased the message before she sent it. She copied the Saturday row into her notebook, reading each number aloud so Amel could hear if she got one wrong. Amel pointed out a tiny mark at the end. On market Saturdays, the first bus used the stop on the other side of the garage. There would be people sitting at the right time in the wrong place unless somebody told them which side to stand on.

“What if they never send any comments?” Mara asked as she copied that part.

“Then we have the count when they get on.” Amel tapped a pocket. “The person counting doesn't see who went home to wait for a ride.”

A car went by with a flute case on the shelf behind the back seat. Mara knew the shape from the boy who had come to the garage. She and Kit had counted the boy because he'd made it into the room. At the next table she wanted to ask fewer questions that began with *how many came*. First she'd have to get a good way to tell which direction a person had wanted to come from.

“If we start half an hour earlier, could the people from down by the garage get up here and have time?”

Amel showed her when the first suitable bus reached the stop. “Just about, if the last few appointments stay short. Get somebody to make that trip before you print it on your paper.”

Inside, the fruit seller was already marking his proposed space with strips of brown paper while he waited for the tape. His name was Terry, and he needed to bring boxes in early while the fruit was cool. Mara had known him as a man who wore the same plaid vest in every kind of weather. Hearing him ask about hours in the same tone in which he had once let her mother buy two bruised pears, she began to suspect there were reasons for the bruised pears being available just when she got to the market. He counted one box, one box more, and a passage about as wide as he could reach. He kept going back around to see if he had boxed himself in.

Mara's mother marked the repair space in blue. The old counter had been seven feet long. The new one could be four, with a bench behind it that an adult would move into place. Grandpa stood where the bench would go. He used his hands to mark the distance he had to reach for his tools. He could just fit one cabinet and a narrow stand for waiting jobs. If they hired anyone else in the summer, that person would have to go around him one way to get a tool and the other way to take it back. He called it a puzzle and ran a finger down the air between them.

Bea measured the stockroom and drew a line down the middle of a copy of the plan. She wanted to work where she could close the door when a customer had to try on clothes. She also wanted a table that was wide enough for her to turn a heavy coat, which meant it couldn't be the same table Terry used for boxes. She would be willing to trade regular hours at the public desk for some room to keep the heavy machine where she used it. Mara's mother pointed out the building's other users would have to agree to it, but a schedule might make that possible. When Mara's mother said *public desk*, Bea asked which of the blue squares was meant to be public.

Mara pointed to the front. She would keep a bowl of slips there, she explained, and a child could leave a broken thing without reaching above their head. She had practiced this part at the kitchen table. The thing she hadn't practiced was the question Bea asked next.

“Who would pay for the part the public uses?”

Mara's mother reached for the clipboard. The town would charge rent for the square footage and pay for certain repairs required to open the building. Someone would have to take the rooms on a lease. The town would also consider a proposal with a public room, but it had asked for named users, a budget, and hours, even for that. Somebody still had to pay for the lights and the heat. Anyone who had put their name down as a business might have to pay a share. No one had signed to do so yet.

“If we put the desk here,” Mara said, pointing again, “we could start there while people are waiting. Use the same table for Saturday.”

Grandpa made his cabinet shape one more time. “That's where a customer would come to leave something on a weekday.”

“It still could be.”

“A paid repair can sit for two weeks. I want to be able to get to it without moving somebody's sewing or a bus rider trying to warm up.”

Mara's voice went low. “We know how to put out a table.” She was thinking of the old counter and the person in the green cap in the same instant. She had expected the front of the new shop to have little more in common with the old one than a few things that had come out of a box. At some point during this conversation she had put Grandpa into the room exactly as he'd been before. The cabinet moved with him. She saw no way to leave both the cabinet and the person trying to get around it where they'd been.

“Would you like to try it?” said Ruth. “Put chairs where the big things would be and walk the route from the door.”

They tried it with a chair stack for the counter and the cart for the cabinet. Terry had to wait in the doorway while Grandpa went round to the side he could reach. Bea stood beside the cart, and even though she could see where she would need it, she couldn't move it there without interrupting everybody. In the excuse-filled truce that followed, three people stepped out onto the landing. A cooler smell came in from the trees. Looking from the doorway to the place where Terry stood, Mara realized he was the only one who had actually reached what he had marked on the floor.

Kit fetched his paper door. He had made a small new slit along the bottom so that it could bend at the corner without tearing. He set it flat right where the chairs and the cart came closest. “Same problem as before,” he said. He turned it over. On the blank side his mother drew an arrow through the biggest open part of the room. The arrow stopped where Mara's square for the bell began.

“There's no place for me to go from there,” Bea said.

“There's the stockroom.”

Bea put down the pen. “Give me a day before you put that on a plan.”

At the uphill stop afterward, the advertised bus arrived early enough to idle for a minute. Kit and Bea got on. Mara stood where she could see the warm backs of their two seats through the side window. Kit drew a line on the glass where the morning had fogged it. There were several directions it could have gone. It went straight down and stopped at the bottom seal.

Mara wanted to wait for the next bus and make the same trip as they had. She would have to wait a while. She could see the time now without looking at the schedule. Instead she walked down the level path all the way to the curve and turned back to the store. Near the landing Kit's tape had left a little bright rectangle where someone had picked up a piece of paper off the ground. She lifted it with her fingernail and stuck it in the notebook. It would mark the first version of a room that they'd already tried to walk through.

## Chapter 10. In Plain View

Bea's word came on Monday. She could pay for part of a workroom if she had certain weekdays and a place that could close while she pinned a hem. She would contribute to the shared front desk if somebody listed how much it would cost, what access it gave her, and who had the authority to change her hours. Under those conditions, she might be able to leave the loading dock shift in time. Otherwise she would talk to the person in the other town. The grocery wasn't forever, but she couldn't keep lifting sacks in the morning and hoping that somebody's kitchen had room for her machine that night.

The statement was all there on one piece of notebook paper. Bea had written a second one for Ruth. She handed the first to Mara in the garage loading area outside the passenger room, standing just clear of the NO PARKING letters. “You can take that home and read it,” she said. “That paper is what I want you to show anyone else.”

For weeks Mara's mother had been penciling her own name into the town's form as the person to call. Somebody had to write first, she'd said, and she knew she could answer the phone. Mara read Bea's piece of paper three times. On each trip down its lines she saw something she could put beside the Saturday chair. At the bottom she found that there was a question she couldn't answer using just that chair.

At dinner she pulled out the newest plan. The blue square she had reserved for the bell had grown to include the counter, two places to wait, and the small strip of continued floor where a person might set down a sack. There was an outer line around it all and a name at the top of the line: VEGA REPAIR. The bottom of the letters nearly touched the top of the seat she had drawn. For one day she had been carrying it around as if it were a picture of something already seen.

She laid next to it the paper Bea had given her. Here was a person asking to spend her own money to work alongside their shop. On the other sheet, the words VEGA REPAIR took up more room than that. Three weeks earlier Mara had used the name of the shop to stand for all the people who'd waited at the old counter. People at the bus room had arrived with plans she hadn't drawn. She tried to think of one of them standing in the spot that she'd colored blue. She could fit the person in without effort, but when she tried to get someone else to that same spot, she ran out of decisions she could make herself.

“Mom, could you come here before you make a copy?”

Her mother stopped with the new form in her hand. At the edge of the table was the small printer they'd borrowed from Aunt Celia. Just last night Mara had loaded it with ten fresh sheets. The first one shone at her from inside the tray. She set both plans where her mother could see them together.

“Can we take our name off the front room?”

“You mean our last name?”

“Our space. Could the room open to the sidewalk for people coming to all the places? Could they sit while they wait, even if people are using the rooms at the back? We could ask for a smaller shop with a door of its own. Bea and Terry could pay for the spaces they actually need, too.”

Her mother put the form down. “Who takes care of the bigger room?”

Mara thought about how long Mr. Low had held the empty bag on his lap. She could see the way his jacket had made a pillow behind his elbow. “I don't know,” she said. Then she waited to see what would happen to the answer once it was there in the room.

“I can ask the town office for a meeting. We can see who would be willing to keep the room open and how the bills get paid. When you take something away from the part we rent, you might have to take something away from what Grandpa can do.”

“He doesn't need a big counter.”

“Can you ask him what he needs? He'll tell you more than that.”

Mara looked at the sketch. She had rarely asked her grandfather anything whose answer she was secretly hoping not to get. Grandpa said he would come after he finished washing the pan. She heard three hard taps when he put it in the rack to dry. Then he set it on the stove instead. She had time to watch a drop slide down the inside of the kettle before he came to the table.

“If I leave you a bench behind the front room, how much of it needs to be yours?” she asked him.

He examined Bea's paper, then the drawing. It took him a while to work out the position of the level entrance. Mara wanted to point to it. He had the plan upside down to himself, and his finger kept going right where the side steps were. She let him turn it when he realized, and he came to the part where she had marked the old blue square.

“Where am I going to put things that wait?” he asked.

Mara could explain what she had meant, and for a while she did. If the person working out front kept track of customers, the bench didn't need to do that, which meant he could store the waiting jobs closer to the back door. He pointed out that he couldn't see everything balanced on a shelf above his shoulder. She tried another version. He could take a look at a job while the customer was there and refuse it if it was too big, then somebody would carry it away. She could hear the word *refuse* getting loud in her own voice, though she wasn't saying it loudly.

“I can work on smaller things,” he said. “I have better fingers for those anyway. Your mom will have to see if the small jobs bring in enough. I would like to know the size before I tell you where the bench goes.”

“You could tell me where one end goes.”

He moved the map over to the spot where they could both get at it. Above the rectangle with their name, his thumb covered most of the view. The nail had a thin white line where it had split against the pan two days ago. He ran his thumb down until it reached the doorway. Then he lifted it. Mara rubbed the name until there was a field of gray where it had been. At first her sleeve picked up some of the pencil. She rolled it back and kept rubbing. She could still see the name when she tilted the page, suspended in the paper by the pressure she had put into each letter. She drew a box smaller than the first one and left spots for two doors. In the open space she wrote, *Whose job is it to open this room?*

Before she went to bed, Mara used the little scissors from the kitchen drawer to cut the flat shape of the counter out of a cereal box. It was wide enough to hide the level entrance on the new plan. She made a second shape the size of the bench. They looked so nearly the same without the drawing beneath them that she turned them over twice trying to remember which was which. She laid them on the plan, turned one sideways, and put three coins by the main doorway for people. She could have made them stand in a neat row. She put one by the door and two side by side against the wall. That left one wide stripe of paper for anyone who needed to pass.

Grandpa came through for a cup of water. He had to hold the handle with the end of two fingers because his thumb had a bandage on it. When he saw the coins he set the cup on the workroom. “Where's your sink?” he asked.

The plan had only the public sink, across the room by the entrance. She took one coin out of the way so he could get to it. The cup was still on the workroom. She drew a little line to the sink and put his name beside it, as one trip they'd try to make before they settled on the furniture.

At breakfast she and her mother sent the town both copies, the old outline and the one with gray across the top. Her mother added a note that asked Ruth to invite Bea and Terry to a meeting in their own names. Mara almost asked whether there was a way to send just the second copy without telling the town about the first. Kit had made a line on the bus window during the first visit. She thought about which direction the line had gone and tapped *send* while her mother sat beside her.

## Chapter 11. The Cost of an Inch

The consequence arrived in an email before Mara had gotten her coat all the way off on Thursday. Ruth had a plain answer and a complicated one. The plain answer was that a shared front room was possible. The complicated answer began with two questions. How many hours did people have to use it to make it worth heating? Who would leave their own work to staff it on an ordinary Tuesday when there were no volunteers? Mara's mother read the questions twice. Beside her elbow, in a little loop of cord, was a paper tag from one of the winter jobs. That tag was still waiting for somebody to come for it. The house had been hard to find at first, and that customer had already gone to Mill Street by mistake.

Ruth had sent the same questions to Bea and Terry, who had said they would meet at the library on Saturday. After supper Mara tried possible answers on Grandpa. Every answer that had the word *we* in it took a while to explain. Sometimes *we* meant the three shops. Sometimes it meant whoever arrived first. At first Mara thought whoever arrived first could ask for a volunteer. Grandpa wrote the time by her answer and asked when that person would open the door on a weekday. When she said eight, he shifted his attention to a patch of the newspaper and began reading about girls' basketball. Before he looked back, Mara had already worked out that the two of them were going to have noticeably different answers about eight.

“Before school I can come up from Aunt Celia's,” she said. “It takes nine minutes if I can walk across the side lot.”

“Are you on the lease? Can you be at the desk all day if your teacher wants you in class?”

“No.”

“Good. I want to hear what the other people there would do for an hour, a day, a week. Keep your part small enough to do it twice.”

Mara didn't want to make her part smaller. Each time she'd asked a question and gotten one good answer, she had learned to believe that they were getting close to the finished room. Now the change she had been proudest to draw required three other people to give up time they might need. Suppose they drew the first plan again, but took her name off it. Would that do any of the same work? She tried to ask Grandpa, but he had slipped into the next column of the newspaper and was reading aloud about a game that went into extra innings. She stopped to point out that he'd crossed over to a different sport.

Then she set up a desk on an empty stretch of table and asked her family to pretend to arrive. Aunt Celia wanted tape and had to wait for Mara to finish telling her mother what the drill was called. Before Mara could ask Grandpa what he wanted, the potatoes boiled over. Aunt Celia went to turn the heat down. Mara's mother got a towel, and Grandpa cleared the table of anything that might get wet, including the piece of paper that said which person was taking the next shift. When they came back, Mara couldn't see what she'd been doing last. She had the tape in her own pocket. She wrote down how many minutes had passed. It was less than she thought and longer than she'd want to stand there if all she needed was a piece of tape.

“Could you give me that even if my turn's over?” Aunt Celia asked. Mara put the tape where her aunt could reach it. Next to it were the piece of paper and the pencil. Her aunt borrowed the pencil too, put three dots on the schedule, and went back to the stove. By the first dot she'd written her name. The second was a time she could offer. The third was where someone else would have to write something.

Kit came to the library with his mother on Saturday. She set a cloth tape on the table even though people would be talking about money. Kit had once made its yellow foot and a half into spectacles. When he moved the ends apart to wear them, the drawn numbers crossing his forehead became small enough for Mara to count. She thought he might do that again. He laid the tape straight beside his mother's notebook instead and began to write down what people said about hours.

Terry explained that his kind of work was early. By midafternoon he wanted the cart and boxes out because that was when he picked up his children. He could staff a desk before nine on two days, but if people needed the room at two, he couldn't promise to come back and do it. Bea wanted fixed hours so customers would know when to drop off and return. Mara's mother could have the repair shop closed Monday so she could find parts in town with her father. If they could afford somebody to cover the shared room, the Monday problem would be different. Ruth made a note for every version. She had two colors of chalk and left a white dash wherever she wanted a person to think of something that wasn't written yet.

“Here's what I can offer,” Bea said. “I can do one afternoon for the whole room. I need the other hours at my machine. That room takes the time it takes, just like mine does.”

Mara opened her notebook to the last page where she had made the corner into a little square. She had been saving the square for a number she could put on the last version of the plan. Instead, she wrote *one afternoon* in it and then boxed those words in again. The meeting had taken an hour to produce a real size for part of a job. She could see the attachment of each line to the one underneath it. Some lines were too thin for what she'd asked them to carry.

Near the end Ruth asked her to explain how they'd staffed the garage room. Mara could have said that everyone pitched in. She counted the jobs instead. Amel had unlocked the door and checked it. Bea and Grandpa had the skills to handle the repairs. Mara had been at the desk taking names, and Kit had kept the way clear and swept at the end. The fruit seller raised his head at the last detail. He said that after a day of moving boxes he would need ten minutes to sweep and half an hour to take the boxes away. He had not thought of doing them in a shared room. The room would work if nobody had to forget that he was there. He could bring his own broom.

Kit read aloud a line from his notes. “Ruth will ask Bea when she can open the room.” Ruth asked who he meant by *she*. Kit had been looking at Bea when he'd written it. Next to them, on a bench in the library, a person had been waiting for Ruth. The person had lifted her head when she heard the line. Kit sat a little straighter and read it again with Bea's name where it mattered. This time Bea gave him an answer.

On the way back to the bus, Kit told Mara he'd write up the hours and include the blank spots. “Are you taking notes for your mom?” she asked.

“I'm taking notes for the people who come.” He opened the page and showed her a line from the old stop through the door to a chair. It wasn't a plan or a diagram really. He had just made a circle, started a line, and written *sit*. The letters were big enough that they didn't belong to his mother's notebook. They could have been read from the next table.

He went on without her to catch his bus. Mara cut up the hill past the place where the paper drawing of the door had first brushed the dust. Grandpa had taken a different route and reached Aunt Celia's porch before she did. He held up a little cord with the old apron on it. He had washed the apron, and the pocket with the folded order slip was still turned inside out. He drew the corner open with one finger and said he thought he'd taken everything out first. Mara helped him turn the pocket the right way. There was a tiny piece of paper in the crook of one stitch. It caught under Grandpa's nail and stayed there, nothing on it that either of them could read.

“We can keep working here for a little while,” he said. “I called my old customers about the bigger things. Some will have to find somebody else, whichever room we end up with.”

Mara stood a few seconds with the damp waist strap resting on her hand. “Can I help you call the rest?” she asked. He moved aside so she could see the folded paper on the porch rail. Four people had a mark beside them. There were nine people left. She didn't ask if they would call them all tonight. Some wouldn't be home. Some could come get a chair. Whoever she reached, she would have to say the chair was ready before she told them how far up the hill it was.

## Chapter 12. The Second Side

Once there were people to call, it seemed there might be something Mara and Grandpa could put a number to. The first evening they reached three customers and made an appointment with two. The third had lent a table for the repair work before the flood and wanted to know whether she could have it back. Grandpa found its tag, and he and Mara wrote down what the table looked like so they could tell it from the other tables being stored by the moving crew. They would find out if it had come up the hill. Mara waited for someone on the phone to get angry. Nobody got angry that night. One person was so tired that she forgot to hang up. Mara listened to the sound of dishes a moment before she did it for her.

Emails were different. A parent wrote that his son needed to get a violin case fixed before a concert. He had asked around at the bus stop and didn't know whom to call. Mara sent the note on to Bea as it stood, with the date and the subject line *case*. Bea wrote back that she needed to see what kind of zipper it was. If she could borrow a machine someone had offered for the afternoon, she could look at it in the garage room at one on Saturday. Otherwise she would need another week. It took four more messages and a phone call to be able to write one simple answer for the boy's father. Mara wrote it twice. In the first version she had said *we will fix it*, which was a taller thing to set on the table than Bea had promised.

On Monday Ruth asked how a single point of contact had worked in the garage, now that they were trying to imagine one at the store. “I think we had three,” Mara told her. She sent a copy of the old hand-drawn announcement and a picture of Kit's chairs, and then she remembered to send how they'd used the other side. Ruth wrote back about a line in the town's form that Mara hadn't noticed. A group proposing a public room could ask the town to keep a part of the building in its own use for a trial period. That would help with light and heat if the group could cover staffing. The town already offered a quiet place to wait at the library. It could agree to another at the bus stop for a few hours, if the tenants could offer a schedule, a budget, and a reasonable way to find out whether anyone needed it.

Mara wanted to show Grandpa the line immediately. He had just been told he'd need someone to drive him across town to look for the table that might still exist. She told him about that line first. At the end he said, “Me and your mom can go over together. You can call ahead for us if you'd like.”

He said *me and your mom* the way he would say *here we go*, as if they were already moving. In a text asking when the shed could be opened, Mara wrote, *My mother and grandfather can come on Wednesday.* It came out tidy and plain. Then she read Grandpa the message from Ruth about the public room, and he pulled out a chair to hear it one more time. It took him less time to understand the idea than she had expected. The town was willing to keep a hand on one end of the table. Everyone else would still need a way to pick up the other end.

At the next visit to the store they brought a version of the bench much easier to carry than planks. Mara and Grandpa had worked out a rectangle on the back of Kit's paper door. There was a mark on it for four shallow trays. The bottom tray started just above the floor and was easy to pull all the way out. Grandpa himself had drawn the tray handles big enough to know what they were from across the room. He wanted to be able to reach inside without running his wrist against the top. Between the bench and the doorway, Mara had left the width of the cart and some space beside it.

Kit asked if they had room to write his name on the paper. “It's ours to work on now,” Grandpa told him. “You gave it to Mara and me so we could use both sides. But you're the one who made the door. Go ahead and sign.” Kit wrote along the hinge in letters light enough to go over later if they wanted.

Bea showed them what she needed in the second half of the room. She changed the side where she'd put her chair when she realized she could turn the machine and have the light come over her other shoulder. Terry moved his shelf away from the seam in the floor where they had been marking the line between spaces. He could keep an extra stack of crates there if he could reach it from both sides. Once, his end of the paper slid under the cloth tape, and it looked as if all the fruit and the tables and the machines would fit by crossing a single crease. Mara held the paper down with her shoe until he could get a weight for it. Nobody had to squeeze past a chair to come and help.

Her mother looked at the new bench. “You've got fewer trays than before,” she said to Grandpa. His old cabinet had so many drawers that they had marked all the ones below the counter from underneath. There's always another tray, she thought he might say. He stuck one hand in his pocket for an actual slip. He turned it over and wrote a name and a date on the back. Then he let it lie across one of the trays on the pencil drawing.

“Four jobs I can actually find. Hand me that, would you?”

Mara handed him the slip. Grandpa asked her to put it somewhere and show him where to look. She laid it down at the drawing of the front door. “I can bring you the thing from here, but we'd need the public desk open.”

With Ruth and her mother, Mara took an inventory of the hours they had. They could guarantee Wednesday and Friday afternoons if the town kept the front room on its own books for the first three months. Bea could be there Wednesday. Mara's mother could be there Friday until the paid jobs picked up. Saturday depended on volunteers. Grandpa said he could use the Saturday hours once a month, like at the garage. If nobody came on a Friday they could decide after a month whether another afternoon would make more sense. They wrote these lines down in ink. Monday and Tuesday remained blank for the public room. The repair shop, Bea, and Terry could open by appointment at other times if they arranged it with one another. While the public room was closed, they could still let customers through at their individual appointments.

“There won't be a sign saying OPEN every day,” Ruth said. “There can be a sign saying when. If there isn't a person there to answer your question, where does the sign tell you to go?”

Mara thought of the violin case. “There's a phone number that takes an answer and forwards it to the right person.”

“There could be,” her mother said. “I'll find out how much that costs.”

The group was about to roll up its plans when Kit said he wanted to try leaving the room. He'd noticed they only ever practiced getting into it. He picked up Bea's empty cart, stationed Mara and Ruth near the table as waiting customers, and made a turn toward the outdoor light. There was no room at the front for the handle to go straight. He stopped and asked Ruth to take a pace away from the table. She did, though if she'd been looking for her coat and there'd been three more people in line she might not have heard him. The second time they put the table at a different angle. Both times the wheels made it out. The second time the rest of the room could stay where it was.

Mara made a little note out by the door. Whatever was on the table needed to stay put while they moved it, so slips hadn't better sit loose on top. Kit took a coin off their sketch. In the absence of a paperweight, he tucked the corner of the sketch into the gap beneath the cart's handle. Then he had his hands free to move the cart. She saw him use the same gap for the coin when they carried the papers out.

Then Grandpa asked Mara to hold his pencil for a while. He had made a hole the size of a grain of rice near the corner of the sheet, pressing in the same spot more than once. He didn't need it, but he had been using it to mark the place for the numbered drawer. Mara put the stub in her pocket where it wouldn't keep poking at the paper. She waited until everybody had finished, and then she wrote a note in the margin. *Two afternoons and one Saturday each month to start.* It was a small promise. She could name a person who could keep each part of it. There was still some room around it to change the hours when they learned who needed the room and when those people could come.

## Chapter 13. What the Price Is For

The shop plans had a new place on the kitchen wall, held by two magnets that looked like yellow suns. In the space where Aunt Celia usually left her grocery list, Mara's mother hung a sheet about an apartment on Ash Hill. It had two bedrooms and a small place beyond the kitchen where a person could put a chair in the sun. Two little photographs were printed inside a larger photograph, one of the kitchen and one of a window. The window looked directly at a wall. It took Mara a while to realize she was seeing the other side of the building, across a courtyard. There were people out of the picture who looked back at that window every time they went to wash a dish.

“It's the one I asked about before,” said her mother. “The owner is willing to show it to us. There's a deposit and the rent. Once we commit, the deposit and the first month are due the same week.”

“What happens to the house?” Mara asked.

“We would still be paying for the work that's left and the money we owe on it. We can ask the town what their offer would cover if we decide to sell, but we don't know the amount yet. The place isn't available until we can stay in it, and for a while no one can.”

Mara looked from the window picture to the blue house in the photograph on the fridge. A cousin had taken that one in August from the street while her grandfather brushed sand off the welcome mat. She remembered thinking the blue paint in the sun was too bright for a house that had always looked that way. At the apartment, she wouldn't have to decide to sit in the sun. The sun would come over a little courtyard and tell her when it was tall enough. She expected to lie awake at night and hear the people on the other side go to sleep. She wasn't sure how far she wanted another person to be away from her, even when all they did was sit down.

She went with her mother and Grandpa to see the apartment. At the entrance from the street they heard somebody hitting a tennis ball against the wall. There was just enough time to guess where the ball would hit next before it did. The entrance from the courtyard didn't have steps. The owner showed them which doors had keys and what belonged to everyone, including a worktable in the laundry room that folded flat against the wall. Grandpa sat in the sunny corner while the others asked about rent. The owner said she would keep the apartment open until the end of the week. She needed to know by Friday whether she could invite the next people on the list to look.

On the way back they passed the bus stop, where two pages about the seed store had just gone up in the clear case. What Mara saw first was the name Vega in small type under *repair workroom*. Bea had signed her own proposal for fixed days at her sewing machine. Terry's part of the form said that the shared front room mattered to him even if he took most of his fruit to the outdoor market. There were dates for the town to consider whether to open the building, and a paper for comments that Ruth would pin there for anybody who wanted one.

Mara and her mother were the only ones to stop. Grandpa walked another few yards, leaving little half-moons from the soles of his shoes in the dry grit. He had told her once that a person bending over a long time needed to find a tree they could see at eye level and give themselves a reason to stand. She watched to see which tree he chose. He was looking straight across at the apartment they had just left.

“I don't have two deposits,” her mother said quietly. She had waited for Mara to come close enough to hear without letting anybody who passed hear. “I have almost one, and about half of the other. I could get to the rest if Grandpa agrees to sell the big floor drill that's in storage. We can't use it in that new room, but he's been holding on to it in case we found a bigger place.”

Mara thought of the floor drill's tall handle and the mistake she'd made the first time she had drawn it. She had thought something with a handle that large ought to have wheels. The base was iron, and in all her visits it had moved once, when three people came to take it off its platform before the water got into the shop. It belonged to afternoon work with the door propped open and the sound coming and going between the rooms. It belonged to the man with the worn apron who was now trying the distance from a bus stop to a room with a chair in it. She followed her mother across the road to ask him about the drill.

Grandpa listened on a bench. At first he wanted to know what replacing it would cost. Her mother said there were three kinds of repair work she could think of. There was work they could take for pay in a small room, work they could arrange for somebody else to do, and work that was gone from them for a while. If he thought there was a fourth kind, they could ask. He looked down at his palm. “The drill itself is mine,” he said finally. “The bench it sat beside was the shop's. I can look for a buyer. Can you help me find out what the drill's worth?”

Mara's mother could. She put the address of the storage workshop in her phone. They walked on in a row three people wide until the sidewalk narrowed. Grandpa let Mara pass, and her mother stepped back after them both. She caught up again at the first driveway. None of them said anything about the fact that they could have reached their old house along a straight piece of sidewalk in half the time it took them to get to Aunt Celia's.

The man running the storage workshop called that afternoon with the name of a person who repaired farm gates. She had come to see another piece of equipment he'd been storing. If she could look over the drill the next morning, she might take it before Friday. Mara's mother and Grandpa spoke to her on the phone. When Grandpa took his turn, he began by naming the one part of the machine he always had to wipe down twice. The woman asked whether the part needed replacing. She wanted to know where the bolts came up through the stand and whether the base would fit her cart. Grandpa sent her a photograph, then another with a measure against the base. While he waited for her to say if the wheels would go under it, he put the phone on the table and drew the base with the three holes that could be seen from its front.

“I should draw the fourth on the back,” he said. “Only sometimes you forget there's one on the back.” He picked up the phone and called her again. Mara had never heard him tell anybody to come and get a thing in the same voice in which he asked them if they wanted to care for it. That evening he put the drawing by the door so he could take it to the workshop in the morning.

That evening Mara sorted the things from the little boxes. The number 18 had a sharp enamel corner. She wrapped it and put it back on the bottom of a box. An old photograph of four people standing in front of the house had a dot on the face of each person where the paper had worn. She asked Grandpa which year it had been taken. He thought it was thirty years before the flood. In the picture, he was bending his knees to let a child put something in his shirt pocket. The child was Mara's mother, and the thing was a penny. She said she'd promised to let him keep it safe while she ran across the road to see something that couldn't wait. He'd put it in a bowl on the counter when they got inside, and another penny had already been there. Over supper they'd argued about which was hers. He said he'd never known, even when he picked one out and gave it back.

Mara put the photograph in an envelope. The envelope had an address on it for a store that was long gone. She crossed out the address and wrote *for the kitchen*, because she didn't know a number for the new kitchen yet. Then she copied the rent and the date they had to decide into the notebook beside the three columns she'd drawn for the shop. For the first time there was a reason to take the house and the shop off the same page. She turned to two new pages. On one she wrote *where we can live*. On the other she wrote *what we can do for work*. The spaces she left underneath were quite different sizes, and both of them needed answers.

The next day the owner of the Patch Lane shed called back. She was moving ahead with the crates, and Mara's mother thanked her for the visit. Mara wanted to tell her that the clean floor had mattered. She ended up saying she hoped the person with the crates would leave some room for the light. Ms. Idris laughed. The sound came out sharp and then let go like her folding rule, and she said she'd write that in the lease if Mara wanted. After the call Mara sketched the shed on a piece of brown paper lying nearby. She drew it without a door. From a little distance, you could see it had been pretty much all floor.

## Chapter 14. The Open Hours

On the evening of the meeting, a boy at the library asked Mara why a town had to have so many copies of a drawing. She was helping Ruth put copies on the back table in stacks. One showed the original plan, with one business using the front room. The next showed how the front room might be shared. The third showed the same plan with the hours and the proposed budget beside it. Every pile rested on a different color of paper. The boy was using a pink copy as a bookmark in a book about fish.

“So you can ask about something we crossed out,” Mara said. She handed him the one they had crossed things out on. There was only a trace of the old letters, copied at the wrong angle. She could have pointed to each without leaning closer. The boy followed her hand as she moved it down the page toward the new room. He wanted to know where a person would put a library book if they came over with one. She said the public room should have a wide table. He put his book down and went to get his own chair.

The town council sat in a shallow half circle. Ruth spoke first. The lease would be temporary while the tenants tried the hours. The town would pay for entrance repairs and would keep the shared room through the trial if the group could come back with a schedule and name grown-ups responsible for each opening. She went through the rents and walked two fingers around the room in the diagram so the people farthest from it could see which entrance the repairs concerned. Transport staff had been asked for a report. There wasn't a bus change yet. Two people had offered to meet people at the stop during the first Saturday, and the printed times lined up with the scheduled buses. Those were the pieces of the plan everyone in the room could use tonight.

It was difficult to know which person would speak next. Some people took a deep breath as though they had been asked to stand up. An older man in the second row, who had sung at the library when the shelves were first cleared for forms, asked whether the town would pay to heat a room when it was closed. Ruth explained what they had budgeted and where the estimates were. A school aide asked if more businesses could apply. Terry said he was only one person with a short lease. They would have to ask Ruth how someone else could get their name down if he left. Somebody near the back wanted to know if the Saturday work meant the town would now repair all flood-damaged equipment. Mara could hear Grandpa clearing his throat. She had gone over that part of the announcement with him three times in Aunt Celia's kitchen. She waited, and after he put in his answer, she set down the first page and turned to the one where the phone number belonged.

Bea stood up holding her paper straight down at her side. She talked about her hours, what they paid for, and the way she could go straight from the machine to the desk on Wednesday. A person in a purple sweater raised a hand and asked if someone could pick up mending when the public room was closed. Bea said she could work out an appointment, and then for a moment neither of them followed it with the next sentence. Mara had heard Bea explain the same thing to the boy's father over the phone. Now, while the person in purple wrote down her name, she could see Bea wait before telling the whole room what she could do, leaving enough time for the person to decide whether to write it down.

Mara's name was on the list for the next comment. She had set out two lines on a card. In the first she meant to name the street the way Grandpa said it. In the second she meant to tell the town it was possible to keep the counter's work even if the counter went away. The man in the second row had lifted the budget page up close to his face. The boy with the fish book was in a chair with his feet just over the edge. Between them was a person Mara remembered from the bus in a green cap, though this evening she saw the cap on the empty chair beside that person. Ruth called her name.

Mara set the card down. “We tried a repair table at the bus garage, because the people who could get there might be coming from all over. One person came in and almost went out again when the room got too full. They needed a place to set down a bag and a time when we could see them. We had a chair. It was behind the table.”

She took a breath. She had thought she might want to tell people what it was like to see the person turn back at the door. If she did, the person might have to sit through that story in front of everyone. Instead, she picked up the page with the proposed hours and held it between her hands. “Could we put outside hours on the sign, too? Times and a number you can take away. If this room is supposed to be for people waiting, they should have a way to find out how long they'll wait without coming all the way uphill to see if it's open.”

Then she sat down. Kit had left the pencil near the blank part of the schedule. He nudged it toward her with the side of his hand. She slid it back once she had hold of the card. They listened to four people talk about the room. The man in the second row said he could come to an afternoon with his grandson, but he didn't think he could hear a number read from across the room. The woman in purple said people could point to a card or use a printed number. Ruth said she could bring a few copies of the dates to the stop by the garage. The boy brought his fish book up to ask whether somebody could wait in the chairs even if they weren't dropping off a repair. Bea told him she would try the open room with anyone the first Wednesday. Mara thought that was more useful than telling him he was welcome any time, including times that nobody was there.

A woman who sold used books asked whether the town should use the whole front as workroom space and put the extra rent toward sidewalk repairs. She pointed to a mark on a different diagram where people had to cross an uneven patch to reach the library. Mara had stepped over the patch so many times that she did it while she was looking elsewhere. The woman had never been able to get her book cart across it without help. She laid her diagram in the same place where Ruth had set out the cost of keeping the public room. Two council members leaned forward. The woman could make more money at the store, she said, but she would rather see the sidewalk fixed if the town could afford one thing. Ruth wrote down which part of the sidewalk the woman meant.

The chair of the council told them there was a short time to try the room with funds the town already had for opening it. Any longer agreement would have to come back to the council with the costs of keeping it open and results from the trial. The woman asked them to put the sidewalk question on the same calendar. Kit wrote the date where the colored plans ended, all across the borrowed white space.

The vote approved a three-month trial, subject to the door work, signed leases for the back rooms, and a schedule with names. Two council members asked to see counts of visits once a month so they could understand how people used the room. Mara watched Ruth write the requests down. There would be days with ten people, she was sure, and days with two. A person might spend half an hour in a chair without ever saying what she'd come for. Mara wondered whether it was possible to count the service without making anybody talk about why they'd needed it. By the time they climbed up to Aunt Celia's, she had drawn a sheet that asked for a tally and a place to leave a question. Personal names would stay on claim slips and go home with the repaired things.

At the kitchen door Grandpa stood aside. Mara was ready to go in, but he had pointed behind him to the road. The night had reached the hillside. Nobody had put a line in the sky to show just where it was. In the building across the road a rectangle of glass was still lit, and somebody with a broom was sweeping toward the brighter half. “I think we should take the apartment if your mom can manage it,” he said. He kept looking at the light. “You can have a word about where the table goes.”

“We'd stay in Bellwether?” Mara asked. It came out more as a question than she'd meant. Grandpa said the bus going by had their town written on the front, large enough for both of them to read. It was past the bend before she thought to see which way it was going.

## Chapter 15. The Drawer That Travels

Friday came with a small envelope for the apartment and a much larger one for the seed store. Mara's mother took the apartment after the drill sale gave her enough for the two deposits, then she and Grandpa signed for a workroom. The family could afford that room by leaving the largest jobs to other people and sharing the rent of the back space. Paying next month's bills would depend on getting some paid work through the door. Mara carried the apartment envelope without opening it, all the way from the town office to Aunt Celia's kitchen. It had a square corner and a round bulge. The key inside made a weight that slid each time she turned. She held her hand still on the staircase to see whether she could keep the key from moving at all.

Two weeks later she came back up with a box of plates wrapped in towels, and the width of the whole staircase hardly seemed enough. Aunt Celia waited at the bend with a tray she said Mara should keep where they could set it down when she visited. Grandpa had painted his grandfather's name on the tray in short careful strokes. After they put the plates away, they left the tray by the door for keys. When Mara went past she tapped the corner. She had never heard a family address from the sound a tray made. Maybe it took years and a little ordinary dirt on it first.

At the store the town crew finished the doorway work and fixed the sign so a person at the bus stop could see the level route. On their final check, Bea took the cart around the turn. This time both wheels ended at the worktable. She had wanted quiet at the back where the machine was, and once she turned it the window light did come over the other shoulder. Terry started stocking the far room early in the morning, just when he'd said he could. He had four more empty crates than the drawing allowed for. He stacked them two high behind the door and wrote down the time he would come and take them away. He would have to do it before the afternoon. He set his own broom beside the one that had come with the room.

A carpenter named Mae put together the short counter and a low table that fit in the front room. The short counter had enough room behind it for the trays. The low table was just as solid as the counter. Grandpa ran the folded strip of old brass across the new edge. When the light hit it, the bend threw a speck at the ceiling. He could cut the piece short and avoid the bend completely, he said. Or if Mara could find another use for it, he had set aside a plain wooden strip for the tape to go on.

Mara tried the strip beside the drawer the crew had saved. At first it looked like a face with one eyebrow. She took it off. The drawer had been lying in the apartment with envelopes in it for more than a week. She had told Grandpa she was saving it for the counter. Mae said the bank of trays was already installed, and they could mount the drawer to the side of it if they cut it a little narrower. Knocking out the swollen corner would remove the oldest screw and most of the blue number. Mara ran a finger under the wood beside the place where the screw went. That side was nearly the only part Grandpa's mother would recognize if she looked up from a coin.

“Can I have this one for the front table?” Mara asked. “It could hold the slips and the pencils. We can lift it right up if the front room needs the table for something else.”

Mae tested its balance with two fingers and showed her where to add a felt pad so it would sit flat. Grandpa looked inside again. “There's a sharp bit next to the screw.”

“Could we have that smoothed and keep the mark?”

He set the drawer on the counter so Mae could reach it. Mara put the strip of brass on the far end of the low table, where the folded part pointed into the room. The bend gave it a lip that held a piece of paper. Mae took a scrap from her pocket and put it there. When they took their hands away, it stayed. By the time Grandpa set the drawer on the table again, Mara had thought of a note the brass could hold. She put *next opening* on one line of her paper and left the second line white, ready for a time.

That afternoon Aunt Celia came in by the level entrance with a drawing Mara remembered making before the flood. It was the shop seen from the street, with the telephone wire drawn so low that it touched the window. Aunt Celia had found it in a pile of school papers Mara had left at her house years before. She wanted to know where to put it. The front room had an empty wall, but the wall wasn't theirs. Mara tried the drawing beside the schedule to see how much of the schedule it would hide. Later it might go in a frame at the apartment. Aunt Celia put it near the end of the counter and went to pick up the envelope she'd carried it in.

On the pavement two young children had been counting the letters in the old store sign. They came to the door to see if the room had seeds inside. Mara said she didn't think so, then asked them what they wanted to grow. Something that came up high next to a window would be good, the taller one said. Mara drew a blank square at the bottom of an opening-hours sheet where people could leave an idea for the room. By the time the children walked down the path, there was a crooked stalk in the square. Aunt Celia asked for the old drawing back and tucked it in her envelope. “I think we already know where this one goes,” she said.

Late that afternoon a storm rolled along the upper river. From the store the clouds looked farther away than they were, behind the north shoulder of the hill. Amel sent a message to the adults who had offered to meet the bus. The town had closed a low stretch of road to check it, and the garage was holding two buses. She couldn't tell them when the buses would move again. Mara could see the river through the side panes if she stood far enough back. It was the color of folded paper. She wanted to look at the pipe below the shed from near enough to see what it was doing. Instead, she closed the side blinds because that was what Grandpa asked her to do before they left, and got her own things together.

“Will we have to close tomorrow?” she asked her mother while they checked the lock.

“We'll look at what the town says in the morning. If the buses are held again we won't tell people to walk up on their own. We could change the date. Bea has a way to reach the people we gave times to.”

The front room became much quieter once the lock turned, even with people talking on the landing outside. Mara set the notice where it could be read from there. It gave the opening hours and a place to check changes before making the trip. Once she tapped the brass and heard its small note, she moved the time paper deeper under its edge. She wanted to be able to take that piece out without having to move the drawer with it. When she was done, her grandfather put the bell on the low table. The felt pad she'd added under its dent was thick enough to stop the rocking. On this flat table it made a sound that was interesting enough to hear once. Grandpa tapped it once more without calling anybody over.

They came home to find a notice in the courtyard asking everybody to bring their shoes in off the walk. Water had blown in across the concrete, and somebody in the next building was going along the row of doors with a broom. Mara found their wet shoes in a spot where the tennis ball could have hit them and spent some time arranging them inside on a towel. She checked her phone twice before she thought to put it down, and then again when she heard Grandpa's chair move. He had turned it a little away from the window to read. The window still showed him the wall on the other side. She could also see somebody down there closing the door of the laundry room.

The second bus message arrived after dark. The hold had lifted. Amel planned to check the road report again in the morning, then run by the usual high approach if it was clear. Mara had made two passes at a short note for the callers, one with the time and one saying the repairs would wait for another day. In the end she kept them both in the drawer at home, and before she closed it she made marks on the bottoms so she could tell which to send. She did it with the lights on. The two marks might never need to be read in the dark. Still, she made one round and the other square, and she could tell them apart with her eyes closed.

## Chapter 16. Here Is Your Turn

The morning report left the buses running by the high approach. At twelve thirty, half an hour earlier than the first garage repair day, Ruth unlocked the seed store for its first Saturday. Bea had put the little printed cards for the first appointments behind the front desk. Two volunteers in knitted hats stood near the way to the level entrance. Neither of them had to tell anyone to use the steps. Terry had brought a bowl with fruit that was going to bruise if he tried to pack it for the market again. He set it where people could reach it as they left, and at once it had less fruit in it than it had when he carried it in.

Mara stood just outside for the first few minutes, letting the sun reach through her sweater. Kit was across the landing, sticking a paper sign to a plank so the wind couldn't put it over the edge. It said *take a seat*, at a height where a child could see it. On the back he had put a handprint in blue paint. Nobody could see it once he tied the paper up, but they both knew which way it was pointing. Inside, one of the chairs they had set along the wall was turned toward the window. Somebody had wanted to watch the bus come. Mara left it that way. It could still face the table later.

The first customer brought a wooden box with one hinge. She laid it at the corner beyond the drawer the same way she would have laid it at the old counter. Grandpa went to that end to meet her. “I can look at it myself,” he said. “Give me a minute to put on my apron.” He looked in the box with her. Inside the lid she'd penciled the names of her children, and there was a fine line running through the second name. She said she didn't want it covered on the inside. He asked her to mark which parts of the wood mattered. Mara turned the slip over so she could write on it, and the woman drew a small open shape around the place she had fixed her eyes on. On their copy Grandpa wrote *check lid before touching*. Mara slipped the two versions together so they wouldn't come apart before the job was done.

The second visitor brought nothing. He had been on the bus that got there five minutes before his appointment at the library and would go there when the rain left off. Before taking a seat, he asked if he was supposed to be waiting. Mara wondered what she had said that might have made him think it. Then she heard somebody saying the same thing at the far end of the room. A little board beside the entrance still had the word *customers* on the page, left from the paper they had used at the very first visit. Even with all the new signs, that was the one the visitors met first when the door was propped open. She went to the desk for a pencil. Underneath the word she wrote, *and people waiting for the bus*. Then she added *and other neighbors*. When she ran out of space she turned the paper over. *Sit if you need to*, she wrote, large enough for a person outside to read.

A little while later, as promised, the violin case came back. Bea had ordered a zipper. She told the boy she was still waiting for it, and he explained that his father had borrowed another case for the concert. He brought this one anyway because he could carry it on the bus with the strap she'd mended. The rest of the case swung easily at his side. While Bea detached the old zipper to see how a new one would fit, he took a seat by the window and asked Mara which bell was theirs. She wondered how he knew there had been more than one. He pointed to a picture on the wall. It showed the drawer at the old shop with the bell balanced on top, and a second bell, far back by the wall, that had been part of a clock someone brought in. Mara had never noticed the second bell in the picture. She couldn't tell whether Grandpa had too many pieces for the clock or too few. At the edge of the old photograph was a hand waiting to take it away.

“This one here,” she told him. “We used to ring it for each person. You can also take a number.”

He rocked the bell gently with one finger and then reached into the drawer for a number. It was five, which was before the number she thought she had last put out. She checked the stack. Some of the cards were upside down. Somebody had probably set them back that way after reading what they said on the other side. She put them back in order, then made space to leave a few facing both ways. She had begun to get into the habit of writing a name only on a repair slip, where someone needed to know which thing was whose. On her tally she counted one boy who could use a chair next to the window. She could have counted the violin case too. She left a mark for that in another column.

Around one the front room filled. The rain had blown over in half an hour, and one of the people in the knitted hats had gone home. There was no way to get a third row of chairs into the room and still give the cart room to turn. Mara could see the person in the green cap on the far side of the landing. In one hand they were carrying a box tied with a strip of cloth. In the other hand was a book. It had more pages than the person could hold with one finger wedged between them, and the book kept trying to shut. Mara had an open seat beside the low table. She had almost put a box of extra slips on it. Instead she set the box under the table and brought the person a number. There was a place to put a book and a place to rest the box. The person set them both down and carried the book back to their lap. They had come to look at the clasp on the box and had enough time to wait while Grandpa finished the hinge.

Ms. Idris arrived with a rolled photograph of her chair. She asked if Grandpa remembered how much room it took up when she had brought it down to the old shop years ago. Grandpa put his fingers together at the height of its broadest side. There was just that much air between them when he stood in front of the new bench. A whole chair would fit there. A person carrying a large board would need him to move out of the way. He showed her which date he could come and look at it where it stood, so she wouldn't have to get it in her car before he had even seen it. Mara wrote down the street and the day. It was an address beside the playing field. Ms. Idris looked at Mara's slip before she put the photograph away.

“You could come back to the shed across the road if you want,” she said. “The crates are in. I left one place by the window.”

After she left, Mara wrote the address on a second piece of paper. She held it where the light was coming from. She had drawn the playing field from the shed and she'd looked at it on a map. Now she owed somebody down there a visit just to see how the light fell. She let the paper rest on the broad part of the bent brass and went to open the door for the next person.

By closing time people had come and gone for several reasons. Fourteen had turned up for some kind of work. Some had only wanted a price or a place to call. The fruit bowl was empty. Two people asked to be called before the next Saturday because the first bus was too late for them. Grandpa took a tag with no name on it back to the table and asked who had written it. It was Kit's. He had been standing by the door all afternoon, and the open room had given him a view of the bus every time it pulled in. The paper said *print the return trip*. Mara put it on the little stand at the end of the table so anyone leaving could see it. On Monday her mother could ask for the next set of schedules, and they could leave them there once they had them.

Before locking the room she took the paper out of the old strip of brass. The next date was Wednesday. She put it up to read from outside and gave the bell one little push, just until she felt it take the weight of her finger. Then she left it as it was. The room was nearly empty. Kit had come in for his notebook and was waiting by the window with his coat sleeve over his hand. She asked whether he wanted to help make a clean drawing to go beside the schedule. They could start with the bus, make the turn to the level path, and put the front door where a person could see it. She thought this would take two turns of the page. Kit said he'd get her one with more space.

Grandpa and Mara took the bus back to their part of the hill. They got off a stop early and walked as far down Switchback Road as the open sidewalk allowed. Between them and the river, the roof of the blue house showed above a temporary fence. Mara used to count from their porch to the shop in lamp posts. From here she could see the first post and where a patch of bare earth broke the line. The next post was hidden by the washhouse. The new town plan left some of that ground available for study and kept the lowest parcels out of the immediate rebuilding proposals. The family had applied to learn what a sale to the town would mean. There would be an answer someday. For now the numbers on their family door were still in a small box on a shelf upstairs. Grandpa wanted to keep both digits even if the building came down.

Mara let the water have its turn between the places she used to know. She turned her head far enough to find the reflection of the trees on the far bank, where the channel opened up in the bend. She didn't try to draw it. At last she came back uphill with Grandpa and unlocked the apartment. From somebody's kitchen across the courtyard came the brief scrape of a chair. She put out the two drawings she had made in the spring. Beside them she laid a fresh sheet. On it she drew the door at the seed store from the side with the level landing. Then she drew the same door open, with a place beside it where a person could bring in what they had, set it down, and stay.
