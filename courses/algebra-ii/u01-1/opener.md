# Section 1.1 opener: from repeated steps to functions

## What the section does

Five days, 1 to 5, open the course. Students start with a puzzle whose move counts build on one another, then meet
the same idea in folding, in tables and graphs, and in function notation. By day 5 they can say what a sequence needs
to be one definite function: a starting value or values, a rule, and the whole numbers it accepts. Nothing from these
days is scored. The scored attempt is the end-of-unit assessment on day 13, which section 1.3 owns.

- **Day 1.** A puzzle with paper disks, the table of moves, the start-of-year check (ten minutes) and an exit ticket.
- **Day 2.** Folding a strip: the layers double, and a term is told apart from its position. The check gaps are repaired first.
- **Day 3.** A fixed difference or a fixed ratio, in tables and in dot graphs.
- **Day 4.** Practice: one rule applied again and again, tables and plotted points, with prompts that fade.
- **Day 5.** The starting value, the recurrence and the domain, with a rule that needs two starting values.

The family note goes home on day 1.

## How the days run

Every day is 50 minutes. Days 2 to 5 open with a five-minute warm-up that keeps Algebra I function notation and an
exponential rule fresh, with new inputs each day. It is practice and never evidence. Teaching comes first, then a
worked example, then pairs, a short talk and a five-minute exit ticket on paper, unaided. You may read an exit aloud.
Each day's exit tells you what to open the next day with: the error letters below name the repairs. Results go on the
unit's record sheet. No timer is ever shown to a student. A student who prefers to say an answer may dictate it.

## The ideas, at an adult's level

- **A sequence is a function.** Its inputs are the whole numbers from a stated start, usually 0 or 1, and each input
  has exactly one output, the term. The input is the position; the output is the term. If L(3) = 8, the position
  is 3 and the term is 8.
- **Its graph is separate dots.** Nothing happens between stage 2 and stage 3, so a line is never drawn. Mark the
  allowed integers on an input line and the dots follow.
- **Recursive and explicit are two ways to give one function.** A recursive rule starts from a given term and builds
  each term from the one before. An explicit rule gives the output from the input alone. Day 2 gives the explicit
  geometric form, day 5 the recursive form. Translating between them is for days 8 and 9, in section 1.2.
- **A recurrence alone does not define a sequence.** The rule m(n) = m(n-1) + 6 fits 4, 10, 16 and also 20, 26, 32.
  The start is part of the definition.
- **Why 2^0 = 1.** Zero folds leave one layer. Counting from stage 0 means n is the number of steps from the start.
- **A rule that uses two earlier terms is still a function.** List the index and term pairs: no index repeats.
- **The disk puzzle is a recurrence the class finds.** One disk takes 1 move, two take 3, three take 7. The next
  count is twice the one before, plus one. Do not hand out a formula for the count, and do not tell a story about
  the puzzle. A direct formula comes later, in section 1.2.
- **Difference or ratio, over equal steps only.** A fixed difference is the mark of an arithmetic pattern and a
  fixed ratio of a geometric one. Ratios may be fractions or decimals.
- **Function notation is review.** q(3) is the output when the input is 3, not q times 3. It is retrieved in the
  warm-ups, not taught as new.

## The errors to expect

Wrong answers are shown as a student might write them, never with a class member's name. Every invented context says
"invented for practice".

| | In plain words | If many students make it |
|---|---|---|
| A | Allows an input outside the domain: a noninteger like 2.5, or an integer before the start. | Draw an input line with only the allowed integers marked and have students place three proposed inputs on or off it. |
| B | Adds when the pattern multiplies. | Put a table with equal differences beside one with equal ratios and ask for both calculations on each. |
| C | Miscounts the steps from the starting index. | Mark zero steps at the start, then count n minus the starting index on a number line. |
| D | Gives a rule with no starting value. | Make two sequences from different starts under one rule and ask which is meant. |
| G | Reads f(n) as f times n. | Trace one input through a labeled table into the notation. |
| I | Says a rule using two earlier terms is not a function. | List the index and term pairs for the first six terms and ask whether any index repeats. |

## Supplies for the section

Pencils, graph paper and a ruler; paper disks of three sizes (four for the last task); the disk puzzle sheet; paper
strips (at least 25 cm by 4 cm) and the folding table; sort cards A to F; rule cards 1 to 6; situation cards A to D; the record sheet
and the family note. A graphing calculator is optional on day 4; a hand table and plot do the same job.

## A room with nothing

Tear scrap paper into three sizes for disks and draw three spaces on a sheet. Fold strips of scrap. Copy a card onto
the board and let pairs copy it. Do the arithmetic by hand; day 4 keeps decimals to one place. With one projector,
every student predicts on paper before the answer is shown, and keeps their own tables. For one child at home, move
three paper disks together, fold one strip, plot two tables as dots, and write one starting value, rule and domain.
