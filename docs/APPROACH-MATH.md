# The Curio Class approach to teaching mathematics

This page adds what is particular to mathematics to the shared core in [APPROACH.md](APPROACH.md). It does not
repeat that page; the principle numbers below point back to it. The same rules hold: nothing here has been
tried with children, every claim rests on a source read for this page, and the list at the end says how much of
each was read. Evidence ratings (strong, moderate, minimal) are the federal practice guides' own.

**What it means to be good at math.** We use the five strands from *Adding It Up*: understanding the ideas,
carrying out procedures accurately and flexibly, setting up and solving problems, reasoning and explaining, and
seeing math as sensible and worth the effort. The report's main point is that the strands grow together. The
National Mathematics Advisory Panel says the same of understanding, fluent procedures and quick recall of
facts: each supports the others.

**Focus, coherence, rigor.** These come from the K-8 Publishers' Criteria. Focus: most of the year goes to the
grade's major work (the criteria say at least 65 percent of class time). Coherence: each topic is tied to earlier
grades and to the major work. Rigor: understanding, fluency and application, each pursued in the major work, none
standing in for the others. The spine carries focus (its count of major-work days) and coherence ("builds on"
and "where it comes back"). The unit plan carries rigor: each day is marked as understanding, fluency or use.

**The progressions are the backbone.** The standards were cut from written accounts of how each topic grows
across grades (the Progressions). A unit plan reads the progression first. Four that matter for grade 4:

- *Fractions are numbers.* Grade 3 builds every fraction from unit fractions and places it as a point on the
  number line, the same way whole numbers are placed. Grade 4 rests on this for equivalent fractions, which the
  Progressions call hard because the numerals get bigger while the parts get smaller; diagrams and expressions
  have to be tied together.
- *Place value.* A digit is worth ten times what it would be one place to the right. The algorithms depend on it.
- *Multiplicative comparison* ("times as many") first appears in grade 4. The Progressions warn that the language
  is the hard part and suggest tape diagrams.
- *Standard algorithms.* The standards separate strategies from algorithms. Students first compute with place
  value strategies, drawings and area models and explain them; the written method is shortened over time. Fluency
  with the standard algorithm is expected for addition and subtraction in grade 4, multiplication in grade 5 and
  division in grade 6. So grade 4 multiplies and divides with strategies and area models, on purpose.

**Concrete, then pictures, then symbols.** New ideas start with objects or drawings and are tied, step by step,
to the written form. What the evidence shows: connecting concrete and abstract forms helps (Pashler et al. 2007,
moderate); for students who struggle, a well-chosen small set of concrete and drawn representations is rated
strong (WWC 2021, recommendation 3). What it does not show, in what we read: that a fixed three-stage order is
needed, or that more representations are better. The EEF maths report says manipulatives should be temporary and
chosen for a reason, and that too many representations at once can confuse. The Publishers' Criteria add that a
manipulative must be true to the mathematics and tied to a written method; "fluently" never means with blocks.

**The number line.** It is the one picture that holds whole numbers, fractions and decimals together. Using it as
the central picture for fractions is rated moderate, with the panel noting that much of the evidence comes from
whole numbers and decimals (WWC fractions guide, recommendation 2); for students who struggle it is rated strong
(WWC 2021, recommendation 4). The grade 4 spine carries one number line model through all its fraction and
decimal stretches for this reason.

**Fluency.** Fluent means accurate and reasonably fast, with a method that is understood (Publishers' Criteria;
*Adding It Up*). The standards say where it is expected: products of one-digit numbers from memory by the end of
grade 3, the addition and subtraction algorithm in grade 4. The criteria say fluency needs its own practice,
alongside understanding. On timing, the one guide that tests it recommends short timed activities (one to five
minutes) for students who struggle, rated strong, only on material already taught, with feedback, and never as
bare timed worksheets (WWC 2021, recommendation 6). We wanted to say fluency is best built without time
pressure. We could not source that, so we do not say it. Curio Class builds fluency in the daily five-minute
warm-up and in practice tools; whether those carry a timer is an open decision.

**Word problems and problem solving.** The federal guide for grades 4 to 8 recommends teaching students to
monitor and reflect while solving (strong), to use visual representations such as tape diagrams (strong), to
learn and compare more than one strategy (moderate), and to connect problems to the concepts and notation under
them (moderate); its advice to plan a mix of routine and non-routine problems is rated minimal. Deliberate
teaching of word problem types is rated strong for students who struggle (WWC 2021, recommendation 5). Real-world
settings help on similar real-world problems and not on other skills (National Mathematics Advisory Panel), so
they are used for application days, not as a cure-all.

**The eight practices.** The Standards for Mathematical Practice describe how a capable student works: making
sense of problems, reasoning with quantities, arguing and critiquing, modeling, choosing tools, being precise,
using structure, and seeing regularity. A practice is learned inside content. Each unit names the one or two it
builds and the task where students do it.

**Discussion.** A math discussion is planned: the teacher anticipates what students will do, watches for it,
chooses whose work to show and in what order, and connects the methods to the day's idea (the "5 Practices", as
described in the Illustrative Mathematics design principles). Comparing strategies is the part with rated
evidence (moderate, above). The wider case for discussion in math rests on professional guidance (NCTM) and on
shared-core principle 7.

**Errors are teaching material.** Many errors are sensible ideas used where they no longer hold, such as
"multiplication makes bigger". The EEF maths report advises bringing them into the open and planning for them in
advance. Every check lists the common wrong answers and what each one shows, and lessons put a wrong worked
example beside a right one once the right one is known (Deans for Impact).

**Students who are behind stay on grade-level work.** The standards do not say how to help them. The federal
guide for small-group help in the elementary grades rates all six of its recommendations strong: systematic
teaching, precise math language, representations, the number line, word problems, and short timed practice
(WWC 2021). Sorting students into lower sets tends to widen gaps (EEF maths report). In Curio Class the readiness
check finds the gap, support is given inside the stretch and on respond days, and the grade's work goes on.
Curio Class does not yet offer a small-group program of the kind that guide studied.

**What a math tool is for.** A math tool shows why (move a point on a number line, cut a strip, build an array
and watch the product), gives practice with feedback, or checks unaided. An on-screen manipulative has to meet
the same bar as a real one: true to the math, tied to the written method, and dropped when no longer needed. On
whether on-screen manipulatives teach as well as real ones, we read nothing; the one review we know of was out of
reach. The general findings on software are thin (shared-core principle 10).

## What this asks of a math unit plan

Beyond the shared checklist:

1. Cite the progression the unit sits in, and the grade before and after.
2. Name the representation carried through the unit, and the day it is tied to the written method.
3. Say whether a standard asks for strategies or for the standard algorithm, and teach that.
4. Name the one or two practices and the task that builds each.
5. List the expected errors, with the wrong worked example that will be used.
6. State the fluency target, if the standard sets one, and where it is practised.
