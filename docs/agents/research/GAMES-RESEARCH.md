# Games shelf research: skill as the mechanic

Research pass, 2026-11-07. For the planned Curio Class games shelf. Not committed.

## Summary

1. The best evidence (Habgood and Ainsworth 2011) says games teach more and get played far longer when the skill is the core move, not a quiz between moves.
2. Integration is not enough by itself. DragonBox shows a well-loved game can still fail to transfer if it scaffolds too much and never bridges to real notation.
3. Side rewards (coins, badges off the main path) cut play time, and "easy" keeps kids playing but slows learning, so difficulty must be honest and signposted.
4. Classroom casual play works best as 30 to 90 second levels, deterministic rules, juicy feedback, a clean level ramp, and saved progress with no accounts.
5. Build first: Fraction Splitter, Factor Crusher, Number Line Launcher, Robot Route, Graph Shaper, Bond Builder.

## 1. What the research says

### Key findings

**Intrinsic integration works (Zombie Division).** Habgood and Ainsworth built two versions of a division game for ages 7 to 11. In the intrinsic version, dividing was the combat itself: you attack a skeleton by picking the weapon that divides its number. In the extrinsic version, the same problems came as a quiz between levels. Under fixed time, children learned more from the intrinsic version. Given free choice, they played it about 7 times longer.
Source: Habgood, M. P. J., and Ainsworth, S. E. (2011). Motivating children to learn effectively: exploring the value of intrinsic integration in educational games. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 20(2), 169-206. https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/ and thesis https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/10385/1/Habgood_2007_Final.pdf

Habgood's two rules, from the thesis: (a) deliver the learning through the parts of the game that are most fun to play (the core loop), and (b) embody the learning in the game's structure, so the rules of the game are the rules of the subject.

**Endogenous fantasy (Malone).** Malone named challenge, curiosity and fantasy as the drivers of intrinsic motivation, and argued the fantasy works best when it depends on the skill. Later critics say the evidence for the fantasy part is thin. The useful lesson: the theme should not matter as much as the mechanic.
Source: Malone, T. W. (1981). Toward a theory of intrinsically motivating instruction. Cognitive Science, 5(4), 333-369. https://cdgr.ucsb.edu/database/toward-theory-intrinsically-motivating-instruction

**Games help on average, and design matters more than medium.** A meta-analysis of K-16 studies found games beat non-game instruction (g about 0.33). Enhanced game designs beat plain ones (g about 0.34). Simple visuals did as well as or better than rich ones. Enough exposure and real integration into class mattered.
Source: Clark, D. B., Tanner-Smith, E. E., and Killingsworth, S. S. (2016). Digital games, design, and learning. Review of Educational Research, 86(1), 79-122. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/ and commentary https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=16779

**Design heuristics review (Ke).** Ke reviewed 69 studies and found learning sticks when game actions carry the content, when there are moments to reflect and retry inside play, and when support (hints, worked steps) is built into the game.
Source: Ke, F. (2016). Designing and integrating purposeful learning in game play: a systematic review. Educational Technology Research and Development, 64(2), 219-244. https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/maltt/VIP/Ressources/Articles/Ke2016.pdf

**DragonBox: integration without transfer.** DragonBox hides algebra as cards on a board. Kids love it. But Long and Aleven found grade 7-8 students who played about 3.5 hours showed no gain in algebra problem solving, even on problems from the game, while a tutor group did gain. Aleven blamed too much scaffolding. Another study (Dolonen and Kluge) found more engagement but less learning than textbook practice. A large 2023 trial (4,092 grade 7 students) found both DragonBox and From Here to There! can help conceptual understanding.
Sources: https://www.cmu.edu/homepage/computing/2014/spring/fun-and-games.shtml and https://sites.google.com/view/from-here-to-there/project-overview/dragonbox
Lesson: the game must end in the real notation, and the player must do the reasoning, not the interface.

**Refraction (fractions with light beams).** A fraction puzzle from the University of Washington Center for Game Science. Players split lasers into halves and thirds to power targets that need exact fractions. A 7-week pilot with 35 students showed gains on a fraction test and faster solve times. Logs showed children often made moves that made the puzzle unsolvable, so undo and early warnings matter.
Sources: https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera12/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/534474/ and https://educators.brainpop.com/?p=31295

**Side objectives hurt.** In Refraction, off-path coins made four times as many players quit early as played longer. Reshaping the extra goals to reinforce the main goal fixed it.
Source: Andersen et al. (2011). On the harmfulness of secondary game objectives. FDG. https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/fdg2011/

**Easier is more engaging, but learns slower.** In a 10K and a 70K player experiment on Battleship Numberline, easier versions kept kids playing longer, and the most engaging versions produced the slowest learning. Telling players how hard a level is up front helped them choose challenge.
Source: Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi, Koedinger (2013), CHI. https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/chi/LomasPFK13.html and https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:c41dac0c-5700-4404-97f9-40fcf2844290

**Linear number games build number sense.** Preschoolers who played a linear number board game improved on number line estimates, comparison and counting. A circular board did not work. Gains held 9 weeks and replicated.
Source: Ramani, Siegler and Hitti (2012). https://education.umd.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/Ramani%20et%20al.%2C%202012%20%281%29.pdf

**Constraint puzzles (Wuzzit Trouble).** Third graders who played a gear-turning puzzle (reach keys using fixed step sizes) for 10 minutes, 3 times a week for 4 weeks, gained number sense versus a non-playing group. The authors credit juggling several constraints at once, and the try, check, revise loop.
Source: Pope and Mangram (2015), International Journal of Serious Games. https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/view/88

**Fraction slicing (Slice Fractions).** Grade 3 players did about as well as a direct instruction group on fraction items, with some transfer to ideas the game did not target.
Source: https://library.iated.org/view/CYR2016GAM

**Programming puzzles (command-queue robots).** Popular and widely used, but controlled outcome evidence is thin. Teachers report the jump to procedures (functions) loses many students.
Sources: https://courses.cs.duke.edu/compsci101/spring14/assign/01_algorithms/lightbot.php and https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/lightbot-programming-puzzles/teacher-reviews/4030361

**Quiz games are extrinsic, and it shows.** A review of 93 studies found a live quiz platform can lift performance and mood, but some studies show no gain, and there is a wear-out effect with repeated use. That fits the model: the quiz is a wrapper, so novelty carries it.
Source: Wang and Tahir (2020). The effect of using Kahoot! for learning. Computers and Education, 149. https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/39435

### Failure modes to avoid

- **Chocolate-dipped broccoli.** Amy Bruckman's term (1999), popularized as "chocolate-covered broccoli" by Brenda Laurel. Drill with a fun coating. Kids learn to tolerate the drill to reach the fun. Sources: https://the-avocado.org/2021/11/17/thats-edutainment-duolingo-and-chocolate-dipped-broccoli and https://www.toontalk.com/English/easydoit.htm
- **Bypassable skill.** If random tapping or brute force wins, kids will find it. Guard with limited moves, a predict-then-run step, or a score for efficiency.
- **Hidden skill that never surfaces.** The DragonBox problem. The game must show the real symbols by the end.
- **Over-scaffolding.** If the interface does the reasoning (snapping, auto-solving), the player does not.
- **Side currencies.** Coins, gems, cosmetics that pull attention off the skill (Andersen 2011).
- **Unrecoverable states.** Children dig into dead ends (Refraction logs). Always offer undo and restart, and flag dead ends.
- **Easy forever.** Engagement rises as difficulty falls (Lomas 2013). Signpost difficulty honestly and keep the ramp moving.
- **Theme doing the work.** Fantasy alone does not motivate learning (Malone critique). Spend effort on the mechanic, not story.

## 2. What keeps casual puzzle games replayable

| Driver | What it means | Classroom fit |
|---|---|---|
| Short levels | 30 to 90 seconds each, many per session (Juul: casual games bend to any time budget) | Strong. Fits 5 to 15 minute slots. |
| Juicy feedback | Big, fast response to small input: pops, shakes, sound, particles (Jonasson and Purho, "Juice it or Lose it") | Strong, if sound is optional and motion respects reduced-motion settings |
| Clean level ramp | Each level adds one idea; tutorials are levels, not text | Strong. Mirrors a lesson sequence. |
| Mastery goals | Solve, then solve in fewer moves (par), then a hard variant | Strong. Par replaces coins and rewards the skill itself. |
| Honest near-miss | Show how close a skill attempt was ("off by 1/12") | Good. Never fake a near-miss. Faked near-misses are a gambling pattern. |
| Undo and instant retry | Failure costs seconds, not minutes | Strong |
| Procedural or endless mode | Fresh puzzles after the hand-made set | Good for early finishers |
| Daily puzzle | One shared puzzle a day | Good for warm-ups, but no streak pressure |
| Collections and loot | Random rewards, chests, gacha | Reject. Manipulative and off-skill. |
| Timers and lives | Fabricated time pressure, energy limits | Reject lives. Timers only as an opt-in mode. |

Sources: Juul, A Casual Revolution (MIT Press, 2009) https://popmatters.com/120029-jesper-juuls-new-book-a-casual-revolution-2496158014.html ; Jonasson and Purho talk https://gdcvault.com/play/1016487/Juice-It-or-Lose

**Manipulative patterns to keep out.** Radesky et al. coded 160 apps used by kids aged 3 to 5. Most used manipulative design: fabricated time pressure, lures to keep playing, characters that guilt the child, ad pressure. Kids from lower-income homes saw more of it. Curio Class games should have none of these.
Source: Radesky et al. (2022), JAMA Network Open. https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children

**Classroom constraints for the shelf**

- Single HTML file, Canvas 2D or DOM. No WebGL needed. Runs on low-end Chromebooks.
- Works with mouse, touch and keyboard. No hover-only controls.
- No accounts. Progress in localStorage, wrapped in try/catch, with a level-select so a wiped device is not a blocker.
- Sound off by default or easy to mute. Classrooms are loud or silent.
- Each level is solvable in under 90 seconds once understood. A teacher can stop at any time.
- A "show the math" panel at level end that states the real notation (the DragonBox fix).
- No randomness inside the core rule. Random level generation is fine; random outcomes of a skilled move are not.

## 3. Candidate concepts

Grade bands are suggestions. "Why it can't be bypassed" names the guard.

1. **Untangle (graph planarity).** Drag dots so no lines cross. Skill: spatial reasoning; for grades 9-12, Euler's formula V - E + F = 2 if levels ask for face counts. Bypass: pure dragging solves it with no curricular skill. Pitch: "Pull the knot loose." Verdict: fun and cheap, but the skill tie is weak. Only worth it as a discrete-math level pack.
2. **Equal Swap (fraction match-3).** Swap tiles to line up three equal values shown in mixed forms (1/2, 2/4, 0.5, 50%, a pie). Grades 3-6. Guard: a match only counts if values are equal; no gravity cascades, so no luck chains; limited swaps per level. Pitch: "Three of a kind, only if they are truly equal." Risk: kids may match by look, so mix forms in every row.
3. **Bond Builder (chemical bonding match).** Atom tiles show open bonding slots (H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4). Connect atoms on a grid; a molecule clears when every slot is filled. Grades 7-10. Guard: valence is the rule; wrong combos leave dangling slots; par counts molecules made from a fixed tray. Pitch: "Leave no hand unheld."
4. **Slide Plan (sliding-block planning).** Slide blocks to free a piece. Skill: multi-step planning only. Grades 3-8. Bypass: trial and error. Pitch: "Get the cart out of the yard." Verdict: good puzzle, weak academic tie. Skip unless blocks carry a rule (see 5).
5. **Crate Sums (Sokoban with arithmetic).** Push number crates onto pads; each pad row must sum to its target. Grades 2-5. Guard: you cannot pull crates, so you must plan the sum before pushing. Pitch: "Push the numbers home, then they have to add up."
6. **Circuit Run (pipe connect for circuits).** Lay wire tiles, switches and resistors so each bulb gets its target brightness; series vs parallel matters. Grades 6-10. Guard: brightness is computed from real current; par on parts used. Pitch: "Light every lamp, burn none."
7. **Body Defense (tower defense as immune system).** Place cell types against pathogen waves: phagocytes are general, antibodies only bind their matching antigen, memory cells make repeat waves easier. Grades 6-9. Guard: matching antigens is required, but much of the play is generic tower defense. Pitch: "Your body, your defense line." Verdict: fun, costly, and the skill is partly lookup.
8. **Plot Packer (area and perimeter packing).** Fit polyomino gardens into a plot; each order asks for an exact area and a perimeter cap. Grades 3-6. Guard: the order check is exact; the player must compute before placing. Pitch: "Fit every garden, waste no ground."
9. **Beat Builder (rhythm, fractions of a beat).** Fill a bar with note tiles (whole, half, quarter, eighth, triplets) so it sums to the time signature, then it plays back and you tap along. Grades 3-7 music and fractions. Guard: the bar will not play unless it sums exactly. Pitch: "Build the bar, then play it." Risk: Chromebook audio latency for tap timing; keep tapping optional.
10. **Deck Odds (deck building and probability).** Build a small deck so the chance of drawing a needed card meets a target, then draw. Grades 7-12. Guard: score based on computed probability, not the draw. Pitch: "Stack the odds honestly." Verdict: card draws feel like gambling in a classroom; a breeding or spinner theme fits better (see 20).
11. **Robot Route (instruction programming).** Queue commands (step, turn, jump, pick up) with loops and functions so a robot clears a grid; a crowd mode sends several walkers through one shared program (genre of the walker-guiding puzzle). Grades K-8 CS. Guard: no direct control; the program must run; par on instruction count rewards loops. Pitch: "Plan it, press run, watch it work."
12. **Number Line Launcher.** Launch a probe onto a number line to hit a hidden target named as a fraction, decimal, percent or negative. Grades 2-8. Guard: the only input is a position; the error is shown as a number. Pitch: "Name it, aim it, land it." Strong evidence base (Siegler, Lomas).
13. **Balance Box (algebra balance).** A two-pan scale with a mystery box. Do the same move to both sides to leave the box alone. Tiles turn into symbols across levels until the last world is plain equations. Grades 6-8. Guard: unequal moves tip the scale; the endgame is real notation. Pitch: "Keep it balanced, find the box." Lesson from DragonBox built in.
14. **Fraction Splitter (beam or water splitting).** Water flows from a source; splitters divide it into halves or thirds; each garden needs an exact fraction. Grades 3-6. Guard: the target is exact; adders and splitters are limited; undo plus dead-end warning. Pitch: "Give every garden exactly its share."
15. **Dial Lock (step-size gear puzzle).** A dial turns only in fixed step sizes (for example +7 and -3). Collect keys at set numbers in few turns. Grades 2-6. Guard: you must find combinations of steps; par on turns. Pitch: "Crack the dial with only two moves."
16. **Graph Shaper (function routing).** Set slope and intercept (later a, h, k for parabolas) so a rolling marble passes through rings. Grades 8-11. Guard: input is the equation, not a drag of the curve; drag mode shows the equation updating so it teaches both ways. Pitch: "Write the road, then ride it."
17. **Root Forge (morphology word building).** Combine root and affix tiles to forge a word that meets a meaning clue ("not able to be seen"). Grades 3-8 ELA. Guard: the clue is a meaning, so random combos fail; the word list must be curated. Pitch: "Build words from their parts."
18. **Food Web Balance.** Remove or add one species and predict which populations rise or fall, then watch the simulation. Grades 5-9 life science. Guard: you commit the prediction before it runs. Pitch: "Pull one thread, see the web move." Verdict: strong skill, slower pace.
19. **Map Runner (scale and bearing navigation).** Plot a route using map scale and compass bearings to reach checkpoints with limited fuel. Grades 5-8 geography and ratio. Guard: the route is typed as distances and bearings. Pitch: "Get there on one tank."
20. **Trait Breeder (Punnett squares).** Breed creatures to reach a target trait in few generations; the game shows the expected odds, the score uses them. Grades 7-10 biology. Guard: predicting odds earns par; luck alone is not scored. Pitch: "Breed the blue one in three tries."
21. **Factor Crusher (prime factorization).** Numbers drop in; split each into a factor pair; primes shatter and score. Composite numbers that reach the floor stack up. Grades 4-7. Guard: only true factor pairs split; par rewards clean splits. Pitch: "Break every number down to its primes."
22. **Logic Gates (gate wiring).** Wire AND, OR, NOT, XOR so outputs match a truth table. Grades 7-12 CS. Guard: the truth table is checked on all inputs. Pitch: "Make the lamp obey the rule."
23. **Vector Pool (vectors and components).** Set a shot as x and y components (later angle and magnitude) to bank off walls into pockets. Grades 9-12 physics. Guard: no drag-to-aim; input is numeric. Pitch: "Call the shot in numbers."
24. **Sentence Train (syntax coupling).** Couple word cars so the train forms a grammatical sentence that matches a picture. Grades 2-5 ELA. Guard: couplers only fit by part of speech, the picture checks meaning. Pitch: "Hook up a sentence that says what you see." Verdict: workable, but fiddly to make fun.
25. **Ratio Mixer (proportional reasoning).** Mix paint or juice from stock jars to hit a target ratio and a total volume. Grades 6-8. Guard: the mix check is exact; pours are numeric. Pitch: "Mix it exactly, make enough."

## 5. Recommended first six

Picked by score, then spread across subjects and grade bands, so the shelf is not all middle-school math.

1. **Fraction Splitter** (grades 3-6, fractions). Closest proven pattern (Refraction, Slice Fractions). Exact targets, undo, dead-end warnings.
2. **Factor Crusher** (grades 4-7, factors and primes). Arcade energy, tiny ruleset, endless mode is natural.
3. **Number Line Launcher** (grades 2-8, number sense). Cheapest build, strongest research base. Add juice and par to lift fun from 3.
4. **Robot Route** (grades K-8, computer science). Fills the CS gap. Ramp procedures slowly, since that is where kids drop off.
5. **Graph Shaper** (grades 8-11, linear and quadratic functions). Gives high school a real game. Equation input, curve drag as a secondary view.
6. **Bond Builder** (grades 7-10, chemistry). Brings science onto the shelf. Valence is the whole rule.

Strong runners-up: Dial Lock (cheap, proven by Wuzzit Trouble research), Balance Box (DragonBox lesson built in), Logic Gates (high school CS).

## 6. Design rules for every game on the shelf

- The skill is the verb. If you can remove the skill and the game still works, redesign.
- One currency: par (moves, parts or turns). No coins, gems or loot.
- Deterministic core rule. Every outcome traces back to the player's reasoning.
- Undo and instant restart on every level. Warn about dead ends.
- Show difficulty honestly on the level select.
- End every world with a "show the math" card in standard notation, and one level that uses that notation directly.
- A built-in reflection moment: after a fail, show what was off and by how much.
- 30 to 90 second levels, 15 to 30 hand-made levels, then an endless or daily mode.
- No lives, no streak guilt, no timers unless the player turns them on.
- Mute by default or one tap away; honor reduced motion.
