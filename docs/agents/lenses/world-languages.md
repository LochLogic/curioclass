## What good teaching of a world language looks like

**A task a learner can do.** Every lesson aims at something done in the language: ask, tell, understand, show. The learner
leaves able to do one more thing than they could before. Words and grammar serve the task.

**The language is the language of the room.** The teacher and the pages use the target language nearly all of the time,
made clear by pictures, gesture, a model and a frame. English is used for a purpose and said so. Understandable input comes
before output.

**Three modes, every unit.** Interpretive (take in a text or a conversation), interpersonal (a real exchange where each
person has something the other lacks) and presentational (prepared language for an audience). The unit ends in one
linked task across the three. A scripted dialogue is not an exchange.

**Chunks first.** Learners meet whole phrases they can use now, then notice the pattern. A verb chart is a reference,
never the lesson. Errors in form that do not block meaning are tolerated while the task is the goal, and the common
ones are worked on in short, planned responses.

**Real language from real people.** Authentic texts, talk and (for Spanish) audio from several places, at the level, with
help, not rewritten into textbook language. Culture is practices, products and perspectives, not a holiday list.

**The level is a stated target.** The course names its sublevel exit and the course does not stretch past it. Function,
context, text type and control increase in a stated order, and earlier language returns.

**Help fades.** Frames, word banks and pictures are planned per level and withdrawn on a named day. A heritage learner has
a path that builds on what they already know.

**ASL is a language, not a gesture set.** The parameters (handshape, location, movement, palm orientation, non-manual
markers, use of space, classifiers) are taught directly and practised each unit. Face and body are grammar. Deaf culture
and the Deaf community's own voice are part of the course. Materials are drawings and storyboards (no video, no photos of
people: Cory, 2026-10-27), and a Deaf signer checks them.

## Errors reviewers catch

**Course frame and scope and sequence**
- One course for all levels, or a level with no stated target sublevel.
- Units organised by grammar topic or word list instead of a real-life theme with a task.
- A mode missing from a unit, or no linked end task.
- A standard id, level name or can-do reference the file does not hold, or can-do wording copied (paraphrase only, with the note).
- ASL units that omit a parameter, or a unit with no Deaf cultural content.

**Unit**
- An interpersonal task with no information gap, or a scripted dialogue marked as interpersonal.
- The end-of-unit assessment tests recall of lists, or has one mode only.
- Instructions in English with no reason, or a text rewritten into textbook language.
- The error register lists errors with no reason or remedy, or treats home language transfer as a fault.
- No heritage speaker path, or a heritage speaker asked to translate or "teach the class".
- A false claim of effect, or of review or endorsement by a body (never say Curio Class meets or is endorsed by ACTFL,
  NCSSFL or any state).

**Day**
- A word list or conjugation drill as the whole lesson.
- The teacher talks most of the lesson; learners say or sign little.
- A frame that is never withdrawn, or withdrawn on no named day.
- An exit ticket a learner can pass without using the day's structure.
- Spanish: vosotros forms shown as the default (they are off by default, DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-04); a regional variety
  corrected as wrong; a Spanish error in the text (a fresh agent checks every Spanish text).
- ASL: a drawing with the wrong movement or missing facial grammar; English gloss shown as if it were the sign; a
  hearing-centred or deficit view of deafness; voice used during signing practice.
- A product or company name in public copy; stereotype, one country or group carrying all the examples.
