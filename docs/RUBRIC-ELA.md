## How it works

Three gateways. Grades 6 to 12 have no first gateway: the read starts at Gateway 2. Grades 3 to 5 have a first
gateway on foundational skills, used only when the program is designed to teach them. A partial pass of Gateway 1
lets a course go on to Gateway 2. Only a full pass of every gateway that applies lets it go on to Gateway 3. In
Gateway 2, a zero on any one scored indicator stops the course, whatever the total. So a course cannot make up
for a missing piece (say, no research teaching) with strength elsewhere.

Points and pass lines:

- Gateway 1 (grades 3 to 5, where it applies): 44 points, 38 or more passes. Two parts must each be passed in
  full: word reading (22 of 24) and fluency (6 of 8).
- Gateway 2: 56 points, 50 or more passes. The whole gateway can lose only six points.
- Gateway 3: 14 points, 12 or more passes.

## What the reviewers count (grades 6 to 12 evidence guide)

In our own words, for the indicators the expert read in full.

- **2a.** Counts are made on anchor texts only; optional and extra texts do not count. Reviewers note the share
  of whole texts and excerpts and of long and short texts, whether a reason is given for an excerpt, which kinds
  of text are missing, and whether informational texts come from sources that can be trusted. Long texts are
  full-length books, novels and plays.
- **2b.** Three checks for each anchor, and a fourth thing for full marks: a written reason why this text is
  here, in this grade and at this point. Order need not climb by measure.
- **2c.** Supports come before, during and after reading, match what the text record says is hard, and each has
  a note telling the teacher how to run it.
- **2d.** The topic must be specific. A broad or vague theme does not count. Across a year the sets should reach
  into history, science, the arts and literature.
- **2e.** Who is in the texts and who wrote them, and guidance for the teacher on topics that may touch
  students' own lives.
- **2f.** The main path set out plainly, and a schedule that fits a year.
- **2g.** The 80 percent count; tasks that send students back to reread hard parts; speaking as well as writing.

## The pilot read

Settled by the expert. A five-day slice on one anchor is read only on what five days can show.

- **Read in full:** 2b, 2c, 2g, 2h, 2i, 2j, 2k, 2l, 2r, 2u; 3a, 3j, 3k; and 3n in words.
- **Read in part, with a note:** 2a (the quality of the one anchor), 2d (how the slice fits stretch 2's set), 2f
  (five days of pacing), 2m and 2n (only if the slice includes its openers).
- **Not read on the pilot,** marked "stretch or course level": 2e, 2o, 2p, 2q, 2s, 2t, 2v, 3b to 3g, 3l, 3m, 3o,
  3r to 3u.
- **The 80 percent count on the pilot:** every question, task, exit question and end check item in the five
  days. One that can be answered without the text counts against. The count is written on the question set.
- **One short written answer on the theme that cites the text** (RL.6.2 with RL.6.1) is added on day 2 or 3, so
  the slice has something for 2r. It stays inside the pilot's standards and moves no day.
- **The text record uses four headings for what makes a text hard:** meaning, structure, language, and what the
  reader needs to know already.

## How this differs from the math rubric

- The counted line is different: 80 percent of questions and tasks must need the text. There is no
  share-of-the-year line like the math 65 percent.
- Texts are a quarter of Gateway 2 (14 of 56). A zero on any scored indicator stops the course, and the whole
  gateway can lose only six points. So a weak or badly placed anchor cannot be made up for by good lessons.
- The same skill is often two indicators: one for teaching it, one for practising it (words, sentences, the
  writing process, research, discussion). A plan must show both.
- The grade 3 to 5 standards include reading words and fluency (RF.3.3, RF.3.4 and their grade 4 and 5 twins).
  A grade 3 to 5 ELA course that claims every standard cannot be planned as "grade 6, easier".
