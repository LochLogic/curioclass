# Funding prospects

Research passes 2026-10-28 and 2026-11-27. The 2026-10-28 entries came from web search, often aggregator sites:
confirm them on the funder's own page before writing. The 2026-11-27 sections (larger routes, evidence, all 50 states,
near the end) were checked on funders' own pages, with a status on each row. Status column: who has been contacted and when.

## What the research says first

1. **Most big foundations do not take cold emails.** Gates, Lumina and the Horace W. Goldsmith Foundation say they
   do not accept unsolicited proposals or letters of inquiry; Walton's status is mixed (sources disagree). A cold email
   to them will not get read. They are reached through invited programs or warm introductions.
2. **Most grants need a 501(c)(3) or a fiscal sponsor.** A fiscal sponsor (an existing nonprofit that holds the money
   for a fee of about 5 to 15 percent) unlocks most of the list below. Setting one up is step one.
3. **The fastest money goes to individuals:** a few programs fund a person and an idea directly, with short forms
   and quick answers. Start there.
4. **"Who uses it" is the first question.** Before the bigger asks, collect proof of reach without tracking:
   Cloudflare's request counts and a handful of teacher quotes.

## The "someone who wouldn't think twice" donor

That donor exists, but the research is clear that they are reached through people, not cold email. Routes:

- **Warm introductions:** family teachers, school administrators they know, the derby community, LinkedIn
  connections in tech. One forwarded email from someone they trust beats a hundred cold ones.
- **Show, don't tell:** the strongest pitch is a link to one great tool plus a topic page showing tools still to build.
- **Public posts** where tech philanthropists read (a Show HN style launch post, education subreddits) create the
  inbound version of the same thing.

## What evidence funders want (2026-11-27)

One pilot design serves them all if it reaches ESSA Tier 4 now and leaves a door open for Tier 3 later.

| Funder | Wants | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ESSA Tier 4 ("demonstrates a rationale") | A logic model tied to a need, research behind it, and a study of effects under way. **No student outcome data needed** | https://ies.ed.gov/rel-central/2025/01/module-3-chapter-3-requirements-evidence-effectiveness-and-design-standards |
| ESSA Tiers 1 to 3 | Student outcomes; Tiers 1 and 2 need 350+ students across more than one district | same |
| EIR | Mid-phase: moderate evidence (a quasi-experiment); evaluation able to meet WWC standards | FY2025 notices above |
| NSF STEM K-12 | Theory, rigorous methods, "a mechanism to assess success", a data management plan | NSF 25-545 |
| Overdeck, Accelerate | Pre-registered studies published whatever the result; fidelity and adaptation; Accelerate also wants 500+ students and student-level data | https://overdeck.org/blog/our-2026-research-strategy/, https://accelerate.us/research/accelerates-research-agenda-2026-27/ |
| Gates | Evidence for coherent HQIM plus professional learning; grants by staff invitation or RFP | https://www.gatesfoundation.org/our-work/programs/us-program/k-12-education/coherent-instructional-systems |

**Pilot shape that meets Tier 4 and keeps the promise:** a written logic model citing research; pre-registered on OSF;
one signed-off band (science K-3 is ready); teachers across 3+ districts (multisite) for one semester; a 5-minute
log per lesson (taught as written, changed, skipped, minutes, why), short validated surveys (teacher efficacy, the System
Usability Scale for tools, a quality rubric mirroring EdReports' gateways), class-level counts only (share of the class
meeting the exit ticket), 6 to 8 interviews, a CC BY report. Curio Class never receives anything about a student. A
partner district or university can later link its own student data under its own agreements for Tier 3. Sample size
is Cory's call (Desk q-funding-pilot-1): 3 to 5 family and friends teachers is a usability pilot; 24 to 30 is what gives
the numbers weight, and Louisiana districts in the group would also supply the three endorsement letters Louisiana
requires.

## States: OER funding and instructional-materials review, all 50 (researched 2026-11-27)

Checked on each state agency's own site where it opened. Status: **verified** (read on the agency page), **unverified**
(found secondhand, check before acting), **browser check** (the page refused automated reads; opened in Chrome
afterwards where noted). "Fit" is for Curio Class as it is now: free, CC BY, no student data, no legal home yet.

**What it says.** No state has an open K-12 OER grant that an individual or a not-yet-nonprofit can apply for. The money
runs two ways: (1) small OER development grants to in-state districts and nonprofits (Washington is the live example), and
(2) state review lists that steer district purchase money, where listing is free or cheap but needs a complete
year-long core program for a whole grade band, often with an EdReports rating first. Texas is the one place listing
directly unlocks money ($40 per student per year). Louisiana, Oregon, Oklahoma and Indiana take free submissions and
Indiana and Louisiana name OER outright. So the review lists become real once a course band is complete; the near-term
wins are listing in state OER hubs (OERColorado, #GoOpen Michigan, Nebraska, Wyoming, Maine MOOSE) and a Washington lead
partner for the next OSPI round.

## International routes (researched 2026-11-27)

Cory asked that international options always be weighed alongside US ones. Checked on funders' and ministries' own
pages; "browser check" means the page blocked even Claude in Chrome and Cory opens it. The short version: the large
European education foundations take no cold proposals, and almost no foreign government money can be led by a US
group. What works is open-source money, free listings and offline distribution, national platforms that list free
resources, and partner roles in other groups' bids. Legal and tax rules for foreign money are in LEGAL-HOME.md.
