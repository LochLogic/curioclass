## 1. Cardboard Claw Machine

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a large cardboard box (at least 40 cm on each side), a smaller box or cardboard sheets, 3 paper cups or a sheet of cardstock for the claw, string, drinking straws, 2 long pencils or dowels, tape, glue, scissors or a craft knife, a ruler, small prizes (wrapped candy, small toys, folded paper notes)

**Nice to have:** a hot glue gun, clear plastic from food packaging for a window, rubber bands, paper clips

### The big idea
Claw machines are famous for being nearly impossible to win. Yours will be fairer, or just as sneaky, your call. You'll engineer a working claw that opens and closes on a pull string, a gantry that moves it left and right, and a cabinet with a prize chute. Then you get to decide how hard to make it.

### Goals
- A cabinet with a claw that can move, drop, grab and lift a prize.
- A prize chute where won prizes slide out.

### Objectives
- I can build a **tendon-driven** gripper, where pulling a string closes the fingers.
- I can design a sliding mechanism that moves along a rail.
- I can test and adjust a machine until it works reliably.

### Get ready
Clear a big table. Cut away the front of the large box (leave a 3 cm frame) so you can see inside. Gather everything into one spot.

### Steps
1. **The claw** (25 minutes). Cut three fingers from cardstock, each 2 cm wide and 10 cm long. Score each one in two places to make knuckles. Tape a short straw piece along the inside of each finger segment, then thread a string through each finger's straws and tie it at the tip.
2. Tape the three fingers around a paper cup (the palm), pointing down. Gather the three strings through a hole in the cup's bottom and tie them to one pull string. Pull: the fingers curl. Let go: rubber bands on the outside of each finger pull them open.
3. **The gantry.** Push two pencils or dowels across the top of the box, front to back, as rails. Make a cardboard "trolley" with two straw sleeves that slide along a rail.
4. Hang the claw from the trolley on a second string, the lift string, that runs up through the trolley and out the top of the cabinet.
5. **The chute.** Make a sloped cardboard ramp in one front corner leading to a hole in the box's side. Build a low wall around it so prizes don't slide in by accident.
6. Fill the floor with prizes. Test the full cycle: move the trolley, lower the claw, close, lift, move to the chute, open (25 minutes).
7. Tune it. Too weak? Add a fourth finger or rubber grip strips. Too easy? Shorten the fingers or use slippery prizes.

### Make it yours
- Add a coin slot that only lets the trolley move after a coin drops in and trips a cardboard lever.
- Design it as an arcade for a story world: a claw machine on a pirate ship that sells "treasure."
- Make it two-player: two claws on two rails racing for the same prize.

### Stuck?
- The claw won't close all the way: the strings are rubbing. Use wider straws or put a dab of soap on the string.
- The trolley jams: sand or tape the rails smooth, and make the straw sleeves slightly wider than the rails.

### Show it off
Open the arcade after dinner. Each family member gets three tries, and the winner keeps the prize.

### Clean up
Pick up every cardboard scrap and keep the machine somewhere it won't get crushed.

### For the grown-up
This is mechanical engineering: linkages, tendons like the ones in our hands, and iterative testing, all part of engineering and physics. If they use a craft knife or glue gun, it's with your OK and on a cutting surface. **Ask at dinner:** "How does pulling one string close three fingers?"

## 2. Sci-Fi Story 100 Years Ahead

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, a quiet spot

**Nice to have:** index cards for worldbuilding notes, a timer

### The big idea
The best science fiction isn't about lasers. It's about one change and everything it touches. What if sleep became optional? What if every city floated? What if no one could lie? You'll pick one change, follow it 100 years into the future, and write a story about one ordinary person living with the extraordinary result.

### Goals
- A finished short story of 1,000 to 2,000 words set in the year 2126.
- A "consequence web" page that shows how your one change reshaped the world.

### Objectives
- I can extrapolate (follow a trend forward) and imagine first, second and third-order effects.
- I can reveal a world through small details instead of long explanations.
- I can write a protagonist whose personal problem connects to the world's big change.

### Get ready
Get paper and a pen. Write "2126" at the top of a page.

### Steps
1. **One change** (10 minutes). Write 10 "what ifs." Pick the one that makes you most curious, not the coolest-sounding.
2. **Consequence web.** Write the change in the center. Around it, write first-order effects (direct results). Off those, write second-order ones (results of results). If sleep is optional: people work 20-hour days, then beds become antiques, then "sleepers" become a rebel subculture.
3. Pick a character who lives at the edge of that world, not at the center. A kid whose family still sleeps. A museum guard in charge of the last bed.
4. Give them a want and a deadline. "She has to stay awake for a 48-hour exam, but she's never not slept."
5. **Detail bank** (10 minutes): list 10 small, specific things in your world. What do people say instead of "good night"? What's on the breakfast table?
6. **Draft** (45 minutes). Start in the middle of a scene. Never stop to explain the world. Let readers figure it out, the way you'd figure out a foreign city.
7. Revise: cut any paragraph that only explains. Make sure the ending shows your character changed or chose something.

### Make it yours
- Write it as a series of messages, signs and news headlines from 2126.
- Write a companion story set in the same world, 50 years earlier, when the change first happened.
- Make the world better, not worse: write a hopeful future with a real problem in it.

### Stuck?
- The story becomes a tour of the world: give your character a problem in the first paragraph.
- The ending fizzles: decide what your character gives up or gains, and end on that.

### Show it off
Read the first page aloud at dinner, stop at a cliffhanger and see who wants the rest.

### Clean up
Keep your consequence web. You've built a world, and it can hold more stories.

### For the grown-up
Speculative fiction builds the English skills of narrative structure, character and theme, plus critical thinking about technology and society. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the one change in your future, and what surprised you about where it led?"

## 3. Five Egg Dishes

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** about 8 eggs, butter or oil, salt, pepper, a small and a medium pot, a nonstick or well-oiled pan, a spatula, a whisk or fork, a slotted spoon, a bowl of ice water, a timer, plates

**Nice to have:** vinegar, grated cheese, chopped herbs or scallions, toast

### The big idea
Chefs used to say a cook could be judged by how they make an egg. The same ingredient becomes five completely different dishes depending on heat, time and technique. You'll run a five-station egg lab, nail each technique, and serve a tasting plate for whoever is home.

### Goals
- Five dishes: boiled, scrambled, fried, poached and a rolled omelet.
- A tasting plate with a label for each dish.

### Objectives
- I can control heat to change how proteins set.
- I can time cooking to the minute for a specific result.
- I can use the stove safely on my own.

### Get ready
Get a grown-up's OK to use the stove, check for egg allergies, and make sure you can use 8 eggs. Set out everything you need. Fill a bowl with ice water.

### Steps
1. **Boiled** (12 minutes). Lower 2 eggs into boiling water with a spoon. Pull one at 6 and a half minutes (jammy yolk) and one at 10 minutes (firm yolk). Drop them into ice water to stop the cooking.
2. **Scrambled, low and slow.** Whisk 2 eggs with a pinch of salt. Melt butter in the pan on low. Stir constantly with a spatula for 3 to 5 minutes. Pull them off while they still look a bit wet: they keep cooking on the plate.
3. **Fried.** Heat oil on medium. Crack an egg into a cup first, then slide it in. Cook until the white is set and the edges are lacy (2 to 3 minutes). For "over easy," flip gently and cook 20 seconds more.
4. **Poached.** Bring a pot of water to a gentle simmer (small bubbles, not boiling). Add a teaspoon of vinegar. Stir to make a slow whirlpool and slip the egg in from a cup. Cook 3 minutes. Lift out with a slotted spoon.
5. **Rolled omelet** (10 minutes). Whisk 2 eggs. Pour into a buttered pan on medium-low. Stir fast for 10 seconds, then let it set. Add cheese or herbs, tilt the pan and roll it into thirds with the spatula.
6. Peel the boiled eggs under running water, starting at the fat end.
7. **Plate it.** Arrange all five on one big plate, with salt, pepper and a small label for each.

### Make it yours
- Make it a "world of eggs" plate, with one dish seasoned in a different style: chili oil, herbs, cheese or spices.
- Run a blind test: which scramble does your family like better, low and slow or fast and hot?
- Cook a sixth dish you invent, and name it after yourself.

### Stuck?
- The scramble is rubbery: the heat's too high. Turn it to low and pull it sooner.
- The poached egg looks like a cloud: the water's boiling too hard. Keep it at a gentle simmer and use the freshest egg.

### Show it off
Serve the tasting plate to whoever's home, and have them rank all five.

### Clean up
Soak egg pans in warm water right away. Wipe the stove when it's cool. Wash hands after handling raw egg.

### For the grown-up
This teaches technique, heat control and the chemistry of protein, plus real stove independence. Check for egg allergies before they start. Raw egg on hands or counters should be washed off with soap. **Ask at dinner:** "Which egg was the hardest to get right?"

## 4. Puzzle Box with a Secret Compartment

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a sturdy box with a lid (a shoebox or gift box), extra cardboard, scissors or a craft knife, a ruler, a pencil, glue, tape, string, paper clips, a pen

**Nice to have:** small magnets, a hot glue gun, decorative paper or paint

### The big idea
Puzzle boxes have been made for centuries: boxes that only open if you know the secret sequence. Yours will have a false bottom hiding a secret compartment, and a lock that takes three moves in the right order to open. Then you'll hand it to someone and watch them struggle.

### Goals
- A box with a hidden compartment that's hard to notice.
- A three-step opening sequence, plus a note hidden inside.

### Objectives
- I can design a simple mechanism like a sliding latch or a hidden pin.
- I can measure and cut precisely so the parts fit snugly.
- I can design a puzzle that's hard but solvable.

### Get ready
Work on a cutting surface (a stack of cardboard or a cutting board). Measure the inside of your box: length, width and depth.

### Steps
1. **Plan the sequence** (10 minutes). Sketch three steps. Example: 1. Slide a side panel to reveal a hole. 2. Pull a string through the hole to release a pin. 3. Lift a false bottom that's now unlocked.
2. **False bottom.** Cut cardboard to exactly the box's inside size, minus 2 mm on each side. Glue two small "ledges" (folded strips) on the inside walls, 2 cm up from the real bottom. The false bottom rests on them. Make it hard to lift with a fingernail.
3. **Lock the false bottom.** Make a sliding latch: a strip of cardboard that slides through a slot in the side of the box and goes over the false bottom's edge. With the latch in, it won't lift.
4. **Hide the latch.** Glue a thin cardboard panel over the latch's end, so it looks like part of the box wall. Make it slide sideways on two paper-clip rails to uncover the latch (30 minutes).
5. **Add a decoy.** Put something in the upper part of the box to distract: a fake treasure or a confusing note.
6. Decorate the outside so the seams disappear. Use patterns that run across the joints.
7. Test it yourself 5 times. Then hide your real message or treasure in the compartment.

### Make it yours
- Add a magnet lock: hide a magnet inside the box wall, so a paper clip latch only moves when you slide a second magnet on the outside.
- Write a story for the box: it belonged to a spy, and the note inside is the last clue.
- Make the sequence a riddle written on the lid.

### Stuck?
- The false bottom is too obvious: make the real bottom and the false bottom the same color, and add a thin layer of paper on top so it looks like the floor.
- The latch sticks: cut the slot 1 mm wider and rub a pencil on the cardboard edges to make it slide.

### Show it off
Give the box to a family member with no hints and time how long it takes. Offer a hint every 5 minutes.

### Clean up
Pick up scraps and put the knife away with the blade closed or covered.

### For the grown-up
This builds measuring, design, mechanisms and logical thinking, all part of engineering and geometry. If a craft knife is used, it should be on a cutting surface, cutting away from the body. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the trick most people miss on your box?"

## 5. Foam Tray Printmaking

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | High | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** clean foam trays (from fruit, vegetables or baked goods, washed with soap), scissors, a dull pencil or ballpoint pen, washable paint, a flat plate or baking sheet for rolling paint, a small sponge or brush, paper, a large spoon, newspaper

**Nice to have:** a small foam roller or rolling pin, colored paper, thicker paper like cardstock

### The big idea
Printmaking lets you make the same picture again and again. You'll carve a design into a foam tray, ink it and press it onto paper to make a **relief print**, where the raised surface carries the ink and the carved lines stay white. Artists have made posters, book art and protest signs this way for hundreds of years.

### Goals
- A finished printing plate carved from foam.
- An "edition" of at least 5 prints, numbered and signed.

### Objectives
- I can design for printing, knowing the image will come out reversed.
- I can ink a plate evenly and burnish (rub) a clean print.
- I can number an edition the way printmakers do.

### Get ready
Cover the table with newspaper. Cut the curved edges off a tray to get a flat piece about 10 x 15 cm. Squeeze a little paint onto a plate.

### Steps
1. **Sketch** (10 minutes). Draw your design on paper the same size as your foam. Keep it bold: thick lines and big shapes print better than tiny details.
2. **Reverse it.** Any letters must be drawn backward on the foam, because the print flips. Hold your sketch up to a window and trace the back of it to see the mirror image.
3. **Carve.** Press into the foam with a dull pencil. Press hard enough to make a groove, but not through. Go over lines twice to deepen them (15 minutes).
4. **Ink.** Dab or roll a thin, even layer of paint over the whole plate. Too much and the grooves fill in.
5. **Print.** Lay the paper on the plate. Rub with the back of a spoon in small circles over every part. Peel slowly from one corner.
6. Make 5 or more prints, re-inking each time. The first one is usually a test.
7. **Edition.** In pencil, below each print, write the number like "3/5" on the left and sign on the right.

### Make it yours
- Do a two-color print: print the whole plate in yellow, carve more lines, then print in blue over the top, lining up the corners.
- Make a series of four plates that tell a short story.
- Print on something else: a card, a cloth bag, or wrapping paper.

### Stuck?
- The print is blotchy: use less paint and rub harder with the spoon.
- Lines filled in: carve deeper, and wipe extra paint off the plate before printing.

### Show it off
Hang the whole edition in a row like a gallery, and give one print to each family member.

### Clean up
Rinse the plate and plates in water while the paint is wet, and wipe the table. Keep the plate to print again.

### For the grown-up
This teaches relief printmaking, design in reverse, and the idea of an artist's edition, all part of high school art. Use only clean trays from produce or baked goods, not from raw meat. **Ask at dinner:** "Why did your letters have to be backward?"

## 6. Paper Towel Consumer Lab

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 4 or 5 absorbent things from home (paper towel, a napkin, toilet paper, a tissue, a dish cloth or old T-shirt square), a measuring cup, a measuring spoon, water, a ruler, scissors, a plate or tray, coins or small weights, a timer, paper and a pen

**Nice to have:** a kitchen scale, a second brand of paper towel, food coloring

### The big idea
Ads say their paper towel is "the strongest" or "the most absorbent." Do they prove it? You're the independent testing lab. You'll design fair tests, run them three times each, and publish a real consumer report that ranks the products in your own house.

### Goals
- Three tests (absorbency, wet strength, wicking speed) on at least four materials.
- A one-page consumer report with a table and a winner in each category.

### Objectives
- I can design a **fair test** by changing only one variable.
- I can repeat trials and take an average.
- I can turn data into a clear recommendation.

### Get ready
Cut each material into the same size square, 10 x 10 cm. Make 3 squares of each for each test. Draw a data table with materials down the side and tests across the top.

### Steps
1. **Predict.** Write which you think will win each test, and why.
2. **Absorbency** (15 minutes). Pour 100 ml of water onto a plate. Dip a square in for 10 seconds, lift it, let it drip for 5 seconds, and measure the water left on the plate. Water absorbed = 100 minus what's left. Do it three times per material.
3. **Wet strength.** Wet a square, stretch it over the top of a cup and hold it with a rubber band or tape. Add coins one at a time until it tears. Record the count.
4. **Wicking speed.** Hang a strip (2 x 10 cm) so the bottom 1 cm dips into colored water. Time how long it takes for the water to climb 5 cm.
5. **Average** your three trials for each test. Add, then divide by 3.
6. Find the **cost per sheet** if you can read a package price, or skip this.
7. **Write the report**: rank each material in each test, then pick a "best overall" and a "best value."

### Make it yours
- Test a new variable: does warm water absorb faster than cold?
- Make a fake ad for the winner, using your real data as the slogan.
- Test something unexpected: a sponge, a sock, a slice of bread.

### Stuck?
- Results are all over the place: make sure every square is the same size, and that every dip is the same length of time.
- The coins slide off: put them in a small cup on top.

### Show it off
Present your report at dinner and challenge someone to beat the winner in a live test.

### Clean up
Dry the plates, wring out cloths, and throw wet paper in the trash, not the toilet.

### For the grown-up
This is the scientific method in a real-world setting: fair tests, repeated trials, averages and conclusions. It also builds a healthy skepticism of ads. **Ask at dinner:** "Which one won, and was it what you expected?"

## 7. Jingle for a Local Business

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, your voice, a table or pot for a beat

**Nice to have:** any instrument at home, a phone to record for the family, a second singer

### The big idea
A great jingle gets stuck in your head for years after one listen. That's not an accident. It's a **hook**, built with a short melody, a rhyme and a business name placed exactly where the beat lands. Pick a small business near you (the bakery, the laundromat, the bike repair shop) or invent one, and write a 30-second jingle they'd be proud of.

### Goals
- A 30-second jingle with a hook, a verse and a tag line.
- A performance for your family.

### Objectives
- I can write a short, memorable melody using a few notes.
- I can fit words to a beat so the stressed syllables land on strong beats.
- I can write for an audience and a purpose.

### Get ready
Pick the business and write down three things about it: what it sells, what makes it special, and who comes there. Find a spot where you can sing out loud without feeling awkward.

### Steps
1. **Write the message** (5 minutes): one sentence that sums up why people should go there. "The bike shop that fixes it the same day."
2. **Find the hook.** Say the business name over and over, tapping a beat. Where do the strong syllables fall? "Su-ZY'S BAK-er-y" has three strong beats.
3. **Make a melody** for the name using just 3 to 5 notes. Hum many versions. A good hook often jumps up on the name and comes back down.
4. **Write the verse**: 4 lines that rhyme in pairs (AABB), each line about 8 syllables. Put a real detail in: "Fresh at six, the bread's still warm."
5. **The tag**: end with the hook again, plus where to find them. "On Maple Street, by the train."
6. **Structure it.** Hook (5 seconds), verse (15 seconds), hook plus tag (10 seconds). Time it with a clock.
7. Rehearse 5 times, with a beat on the table. Add a harmony or a backing "ooh" if you have a second voice.

### Make it yours
- Write the same jingle in three styles: a slow ballad, a rock anthem and a sea shanty.
- Write a jingle for a business from a fantasy world: a potion shop or a dragon-sitting service.
- Add a sound effect "logo": a bell, a whistle, a pop, at the end.

### Stuck?
- The melody won't come: sing the name the way you'd call a friend across a field. That's already a melody.
- The words don't fit the beat: count syllables, and cut or add little words like "the" and "so."

### Show it off
Perform it at dinner. The next day, ask if it got stuck in anyone's head.

### Clean up
Write the final lyrics and a simple pitch map (up, down, same) in your notebook.

### For the grown-up
Writing a jingle teaches songwriting (melody, rhythm and rhyme) and persuasive writing for an audience. If your teen wants to share it with a real local business, help them decide how. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you sing us the hook?"

## 8. Five-Minute Stand-Up Set

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, index cards, a "microphone" (a hairbrush or wooden spoon), a lamp for a spotlight

**Nice to have:** a stool, a clock to time yourself, a mirror

### The big idea
Stand-up comics make it look like they're just talking. They're not. Every laugh is planned: a **setup**, a **punchline**, a **tag**, and a lot of rewriting. You'll write, polish and perform a five-minute set of clean, original material about your actual life. The living room is your comedy club.

### Goals
- A written five-minute set with at least 3 bits (topics).
- One live performance.

### Objectives
- I can write jokes with a setup that creates an expectation and a punchline that breaks it.
- I can use the rule of three and a callback.
- I can perform with timing, pauses and confidence.

### Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Write "What's weird about my life?" at the top of a page.

### Steps
1. **Mine for material** (15 minutes). List 20 things you've noticed that bug you, confuse you or make you laugh: group chats, little siblings, cafeteria rules, your grandpa's texting style, being the shortest one.
2. Pick the 3 with the most energy. Under each one, write what's true and specific. Comedy lives in details.
3. **Write jokes.** A setup states something everyone believes. The punchline flips it. Setup: "My mom says I'm on my phone too much." Punchline: "She tells me this by text."
4. Use the **rule of three**: two normal things and one surprising one. "My dog has three moods: hungry, asleep, and hungry while asleep."
5. Add **tags**: extra punchlines after the first one, so one setup gets two or three laughs.
6. Plan a **callback**: bring back a joke from early in the set at the very end.
7. Write one word per bit on index cards to keep you on track. Practice out loud 4 times with a clock. Cut anything that drags (30 minutes).
8. Perform it. Pause after punchlines. Never laugh at your own joke first.

### Make it yours
- Do a "character" set as someone else: a time traveler from 1850 who just discovered toasters.
- Write a whole set on one topic, start to finish.
- Add crowd work: a bit where you ask the audience a question and riff on the answer.

### Stuck?
- It's not funny on paper: jokes often look weak until they're said out loud. Test them before you cut them.
- A joke punches down: aim at yourself, situations or the powerful. Never at someone's identity.

### Show it off
Do a "comedy night" with a lamp spotlight. Ask a family member to introduce you.

### Clean up
Keep your joke notebook. Comedians save everything, even the bits that bombed.

### For the grown-up
Stand-up builds writing skills (economy, structure, word choice), public speaking and confidence. Laughing helps. Your teen should keep it clean and kind, and aim jokes at themselves or situations, not at people's identities. **Ask at dinner:** "What was your best joke, and where did it come from?"

## 9. Plan and Hike a Local Trail

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 90-120 min | Outdoors | Low | On your own | 2+ |

**Materials:** a map of a local park, trail or loop (a paper park map, a printed map from a grown-up, or one you draw), a backpack, water (at least 500 ml each), a snack, a jacket, a watch or clock, paper and a pen, a hiking partner (a friend, a sibling or a grown-up)

**Nice to have:** a compass, a small first aid kit, a whistle, binoculars, a phone for emergencies

### The big idea
A real hike starts at a table with a map, long before your boots hit the trail. You'll plan a route, estimate the time like a mountain guide, pack the essentials, leave a trip plan at home, and then go. It's your expedition, with a partner and a plan.

### Goals
- A written trip plan with route, distance, time estimate and turnaround time.
- A completed hike with field notes from the trail.

### Objectives
- I can read a map's scale and estimate distance.
- I can estimate hiking time using a rule of thumb.
- I can pack and plan for safety.

### Get ready

### Stuck?

### Show it off

### Clean up
Bring back everything you took. Wash out your water bottle and hang up wet gear.

### For the grown-up
This builds map reading, estimation, planning and judgment, plus confidence outdoors. Your teen should get your permission, go with a partner, leave you a written plan, stay on marked paths, and be home by the time they say. **Ask at dinner:** "Was your time estimate right? What slowed you down?"

## 13. Balanced Hanging Mobile

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 3 or 4 sticks, straws, wooden skewers or a wire coat hanger cut into pieces (with a grown-up's OK), string or thread, scissors, cardstock or cardboard, markers or colored pencils, tape, a ruler, paper clips

**Nice to have:** a hole punch, colored paper, natural objects like leaves, feathers or shells

### The big idea
A mobile is a sculpture that moves, floats and balances. It was invented by an artist who loved both art and engineering. The secret is **torque**: a light shape far from the center can balance a heavy shape close to it. You'll design a theme, cut your shapes and balance a whole floating world.

### Goals
- A hanging mobile with at least 3 levels and 6 shapes that balances on its own.
- A theme that ties the shapes together.

### Objectives
- I can find the balance point of an arm by moving the string.
- I can explain torque: weight times distance from the pivot.
- I can design shapes that look good from every angle.

### Get ready
Pick a theme: the solar system, birds in flight, sea creatures, an imaginary weather system, or a set of symbols for your family. Clear a table and find a hook or a chair back to hang from while you build.

### Steps
1. **Design shapes** (15 minutes). Draw 6 to 8 shapes in different sizes on cardstock. Color both sides, since mobiles turn. Cut them out and punch or poke a hole at the top.
2. **Build from the bottom up.** Take your smallest arm (a short straw or stick). Tie a shape on each end with strings of different lengths.
3. **Find the balance point.** Tie a string loosely around the middle of the arm and slide it until the arm hangs level. Tape it there.
4. **Check the math.** A shape twice as heavy needs to be half as far from the balance point. Weight times distance should match on both sides.
5. Hang that whole arm from one end of a longer arm. Hang a bigger shape from the other end. Find the new balance point.
6. Repeat for a third level. Hang the whole thing from the top string (30 minutes).
7. Adjust. Make sure no shapes hit each other when it turns. Change string lengths if they do.

### Make it yours
- Make a mobile that tells a story from top to bottom, like a bird's life from egg to flight.
- Use only found natural objects, no cut paper.
- Hang a sound element, like two spoons or keys that touch gently, to make a wind chime mobile.

### Stuck?
- It keeps tilting: slide the balance string a few millimeters toward the heavier side.
- Shapes tangle: use longer arms or stagger string lengths.

### Show it off
Hang it near a window or a doorway and give a short talk on the theme.

### Clean up
Pick up string bits and paper scraps. Put sharp wire pieces in a safe spot or the trash.

### For the grown-up
This combines art with physics: balance, torque and center of mass. If they cut a wire hanger, it needs pliers or wire cutters and care with sharp ends. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you balance the big shape with the small one?"

## 14. Homemade Seismograph

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a sturdy cardboard box (shoebox or bigger), a paper cup, string, a marker or pen, coins or small stones for weight, tape, scissors, a long strip of paper (tape sheets end to end, or use receipt paper), a ruler

**Nice to have:** a sheet of plastic wrap, a stopwatch, a helper to pull the paper

### The big idea
Seismographs record earthquakes by keeping a heavy pen still while the ground moves underneath it. You'll build one from a box, a cup and a marker, then become the earthquake. You'll record small and big "quakes," measure their size and figure out how far they were from the box.

### Goals
- A working seismograph that draws a line on a moving strip of paper.
- A set of labeled readings, from a tiny tremor to a big quake.

### Objectives
- I can explain how **inertia** keeps a heavy hanging mass still while the frame moves.
- I can measure amplitude (wave height) on a recording.
- I can compare readings and connect them to distance and strength.

### Get ready
Stand the box on its side, with the open side facing you. Poke a hole in the top center.

### Steps
1. **Build the pendulum** (15 minutes). Poke two holes near the rim of the cup and tie on a string handle. Poke a hole in the cup's bottom and push the marker through, tip down, so it just reaches the bottom of the box. Fill the cup with coins around the marker.
2. Thread the string up through the hole in the box top. Adjust so the marker tip just touches the box floor, then tape the string.
3. Slide the paper strip under the marker along the box floor so it can be pulled through.
4. **Baseline.** Pull the paper slowly and steadily without bumping anything. You should get a flat line.
5. **Earthquakes** (20 minutes). Pull the paper while someone taps or shakes the table: a soft tap, a medium bump, a big shake. If you're alone, pull with one hand and tap with the other. Label each quake on the strip.
6. **Measure.** Use a ruler to measure the amplitude (highest point to the center line) of each quake in millimeters.
7. **Distance test.** Stomp on the floor 1 m, 3 m and 5 m from the table. How does the reading change? Seismologists use the same idea to estimate how far away quakes happen.

### Make it yours
- Make your own Richter-style scale: classify each quake from 1 to 5 by amplitude.
- Record a "day in the house": leave it near the front door and pull it during a busy moment.
- Build a "shake table" from a tray on marbles and test which block tower designs survive.

### Stuck?
- The marker wobbles on its own: the cup needs more weight, or the string is too long.
- The line is faint: lower the string a few millimeters so the tip presses a little harder, or use a fresh marker.

### Show it off
Lay out your recordings on the floor and challenge your family to guess which quake was which.

### Clean up
Cap the marker and keep the box for future tests.

### For the grown-up
This teaches earth science (seismic waves, measurement) and physics (inertia, amplitude), and shows how real instruments collect data. **Ask at dinner:** "How does a seismograph know the ground moved?"

## 18. Fresh Pasta by Hand

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Indoors | High | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 200 g (about 1 and 2/3 cups) all-purpose flour, 2 eggs, a pinch of salt, a fork, a clean counter or big board, a rolling pin (or a clean, smooth bottle), a knife, plastic wrap or a bowl to cover the dough, a large pot, a colander, butter or olive oil, grated cheese or garlic

**Nice to have:** semolina flour, a bench scraper, fresh herbs, black pepper

### The big idea
Some of the world's best food has only two ingredients. Flour and eggs, plus your hands, become silky noodles in under two hours. Fresh pasta cooks in 2 minutes, tastes completely different from dried, and makes any dinner feel like an event. Today you're the pasta maker.

### Goals
- Fresh, hand-rolled and hand-cut pasta for 2 to 3 people.
- A simple sauce and a served dinner course.

### Objectives
- I can use the well method to mix dough by hand.
- I can knead until the dough is smooth and elastic, and explain what gluten does.
- I can cook fresh pasta to the right texture on my own.

### Get ready
Get a grown-up's OK for the stove and check for egg or wheat allergies. Clean and dry a big work surface. Wash your hands.

### Steps
1. **The well.** Pile the flour on the counter and make a crater in the middle, wide enough for the eggs. Crack in the eggs and add salt.
2. **Mix.** Beat the eggs with a fork, then slowly pull in flour from the inside wall of the well. Keep the wall up until it gets thick, then fold everything in with your hands.
3. **Knead** (8 to 10 minutes). Push the dough away with the heel of your hand, fold it back and turn it. It starts shaggy and becomes smooth, like play dough. If it's sticky, add a pinch of flour. If it's dry and cracking, wet your hands.
4. **Rest.** Wrap the dough and let it sit for 30 minutes. The **gluten** (stretchy protein) relaxes, so it rolls without shrinking back. Plan your sauce while you wait.
5. **Roll** (15 minutes). Cut the dough in half. Roll each half from the middle outward, turning it often, until it's thin enough to see your hand through it, about 1 mm.
6. **Cut.** Dust the sheet with flour, roll it loosely into a log, and slice across into ribbons, 5 mm for thin, 1 cm for wide. Shake them out.
7. **Cook.** Boil salted water, drop in the pasta, and cook 2 to 3 minutes. Taste one. Save a cup of pasta water, then drain.
8. Toss with butter or oil, cheese and a splash of pasta water to make it silky.

### Make it yours
- Shape something new: bow ties (pinch rectangles in the middle) or filled pillows (a spoonful of ricotta between two sheets).
- Color the dough with a spoon of pureed spinach or tomato paste.
- Invent a sauce from what's in the fridge and give it a name.

### Stuck?
- The dough won't come together: add water a teaspoon at a time.
- The noodles stick together: use more flour when cutting, and cook them right away.

### Show it off
Serve it as the main course for the family and explain how it was made.

### Clean up
Scrape dried dough off the counter with a bench scraper or spatula before wiping, so it doesn't smear. Wash the pot and board.

### For the grown-up

### Goals
- A one-page "culture file" for an invented alien people, with at least 5 customs.
- A first-contact meeting played out in character, ending in a signed (or drawn) agreement.

### Objectives
- I can build a believable culture with values, customs and a reason behind each one.
- I can communicate without shared words, using gestures, objects and drawings.
- I can stay in character and adapt when the other side surprises me.

### Get ready
Set the table as a neutral meeting ground: two chairs facing each other, a blank sheet of paper in the middle. Put your trade objects in a bag. If someone at home will play, give them 10 minutes alone with a blank culture file to invent their own side.

### Steps

### Show it off

### Clean up
Return the trade objects and costume pieces. Keep the culture file and treaty together.

### For the grown-up
This is drama with real anthropology and communication ideas in it: how cultures form, how misunderstandings happen and how people repair them. ## 21. Water Clock

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Either | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** two clear plastic bottles or cups (one larger), a pushpin or thin nail, scissors, tape, a permanent marker or strip of tape for markings, water, a clock or timer with a second hand, a tray or towel

**Nice to have:** food coloring, a third bottle, a ruler

### The big idea
Before mechanical clocks, people told time with water. The **clepsydra**, a water clock, was used in ancient Egypt, Greece and China to time speeches, court cases and the night watch. You'll build one, calibrate it minute by minute, and then fight its biggest flaw: water flows slower as the container empties.

### Goals
- A water clock that measures at least 10 minutes with marked lines.
- A test of its accuracy, and an improved version.

### Objectives
- I can **calibrate** an instrument against a known standard.
- I can explain why flow rate drops as water pressure drops.
- I can design an improvement and measure whether it works.

### Get ready
Work over a tray or outside. Cut the top off the bigger bottle to make a collecting jar. Cut the bottom off the smaller bottle to make a funnel-shaped top reservoir, cap still on.

### Steps
1. Poke a small hole in the smaller bottle's cap with a pushpin. Start small: you can make it bigger later.
2. Set the smaller bottle upside down in the mouth of the collecting jar, cap down, and tape it so it's steady.
3. Add a few drops of food coloring to a jug of water. Fill the top reservoir to a line you mark as "full."
4. **Calibrate** (15 minutes). Start the timer as you remove your finger from the hole. Every minute, mark the water level on the collecting jar. Label 1, 2, 3 and so on.
5. **Look at the marks.** Are they evenly spaced? Probably not. As the top empties, there's less pressure pushing water out, so flow slows down.
6. **Test it.** Empty and refill. Have someone time a task (like 50 jumping jacks) with a real clock while you time it with your water clock. How far off is it?
7. **Improve it** (25 minutes). Try a fix: a **constant-head** design where you keep the top reservoir always full (top it up with a cup while it runs), or a third bottle that feeds the reservoir. Re-calibrate and compare.

### Make it yours
- Build an alarm: a light object floats in the collecting jar and tips over a domino when it reaches the 10-minute mark.
- Use your water clock to time a board game turn or a speech for the whole evening.
- Design an "ancient" version with a story: the timer for a court in a fictional kingdom.

### Stuck?
- Water runs out too fast: tape over the hole and poke a smaller one.
- The water stops: there's an air lock. Poke a tiny hole near the top of the reservoir to let air in.

### Show it off
Challenge the family: time a 5-minute challenge with your water clock and a real clock side by side.

### Clean up
Pour the water on a plant (coloring is fine in small amounts), and dry the bottles.

### For the grown-up
This builds physics (pressure, flow rate) and the science practice of calibration and testing an instrument, as well as history. The pushpin is the only sharp object. **Ask at dinner:** "Why do the marks get closer together near the bottom?"

## 23. Paper Piano Chord Lab

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 or 3 sheets of paper taped end to end, a ruler, a pencil, a black marker, tape, a few coins or buttons

**Nice to have:** any real keyboard, guitar or tuned instrument at home, family members to sing a note each

### The big idea
Almost every song you know is built from a few chords, and every chord is built on a simple pattern of steps. You'll draw a two-octave piano keyboard to scale, decode how major and minor chords work, and use your paper piano to build the four-chord progression behind thousands of songs. Then you'll make it sound with your voices.

### Goals
- A two-octave paper keyboard, labeled.
- A chord chart with major and minor chords, and a four-chord progression you can sing.

### Objectives
- I can count **half steps** on a keyboard.
- I can build major chords (4 + 3 half steps) and minor chords (3 + 4).
- I can explain a progression using Roman numerals, like I, V, vi and IV.

### Get ready
Tape paper end to end into a long strip. Clear a table.

### Steps
1. **Draw the keys** (15 minutes). Draw 14 white keys, each 2.3 cm wide and 15 cm tall. Black keys are thinner and shorter (1.3 cm by 10 cm), in groups of 2 and 3. Color them in.
2. **Label.** The white key just left of every group of two black keys is C. Then D, E, F, G, A, B, C again.
3. **Half steps.** A half step is from one key to the very next key, black or white. C to C sharp (the black key) is one half step. E to F is also one half step, since there's no black key between.
4. **Build a major chord.** Start on any key (the root). Count up 4 half steps, put a coin. Count up 3 more, put a coin. C major is C, E, G.
5. **Build a minor chord.** Root, then 3 half steps, then 4. A minor is A, C, E. Notice how one note changes everything.
6. **Chord chart** (15 minutes). Build major chords on C, F and G, and a minor on A. Write each one down.
7. **The progression.** In the key of C, C is chord I, G is V, A minor is vi, F is IV. Play I, V, vi, IV on your paper piano by tapping the chords in order, four beats each.
8. **Make it sound.** With family, each person takes one note of the chord and holds it. Start on a comfortable note for the root and sing "do-mi-sol" for major, "la-do-mi" for minor. If you have a real instrument, check the sound.

### Make it yours
- Write lyrics over I, V, vi, IV and hum a melody that uses notes from each chord.
- Build seventh chords by stacking one more note (3 more half steps) on top.
- Try the progression in a new key, like G, by starting on G and following the same pattern.

### Stuck?
- Counting gets confusing: count the starting key as zero, not one.
- Singing chords sounds off: start very slowly and let each person find their note before the next joins.

### Show it off
Teach your family the I, V, vi, IV chord pattern and see how many songs they can name that use it.

### Clean up
Roll up the paper piano and keep it for practice.

### For the grown-up
This builds music theory (scales, chords and progressions) and pattern thinking, plus a link between math and music. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the difference between a major and a minor chord?"

## 25. Finger Knit a Scarf

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 2 or 3 old T-shirts you're allowed to cut up (or yarn if you have it), sharp scissors, a ruler

**Nice to have:** a large-eye needle, different-colored shirts for stripes, a friend to knit with

### The big idea
You can knit with just your hands, no needles. And you can make yarn from old T-shirts: cut them into a long strip, stretch it, and it curls into a soft, chunky cord. You'll turn old clothes into a thick, cozy scarf, learning a craft that's thousands of years old.

### Goals
- T-shirt yarn cut from old shirts.
- A finished finger-knit scarf at least 100 cm long.

### Objectives
- I can cut a continuous strip of T-shirt yarn.
- I can finger knit using the over-and-under weave.
- I can join new yarn and finish the ends so they don't unravel.

### Get ready
Get permission for the shirts you're cutting. Lay one flat on a table and cut off the hem and everything above the armpits, so you have a tube.

### Steps
1. **Cut the yarn** (20 minutes). Fold the tube in half, side to side, but leave 5 cm unfolded at the top. Cut strips 2.5 cm wide from the folded edge up to (not through) the unfolded part. Open the tube. Cut diagonally across the uncut part, from the first strip to the second, and so on. You'll get one long strip.
2. **Stretch it.** Pull the strip a little at a time. The edges curl in and it becomes "yarn."
3. **Cast on.** Hold your left hand palm up. Tuck the yarn tail between your thumb and first finger. Weave the yarn over and under your four fingers, then back again, so each finger has one loop.
4. **Knit a row.** Weave over and under again, so each finger has two loops. Starting with your pinky, lift the bottom loop over the top loop and off the back of your finger. Do this for all four.
5. **Repeat** (45 minutes). Keep weaving and lifting. Pull the knitting down the back of your hand every few rows. Count rows to keep it even.
6. **Join new yarn**: tie the end of a new strip to the old one with a tight knot, and keep going. Hide the knot on the back.
7. **Bind off.** When it's long enough, move the loop from your pinky to your ring finger and lift the bottom loop over. Keep moving to your first finger. Cut the yarn, pull the tail through the last loop and tighten.

### Make it yours
- Stripe it with shirts of two or three colors.
- Make a matching headband with the leftovers.
- Knit two fingers wide for a thin "rope," and braid three of them into a thicker scarf.

### Stuck?
- Loops are too tight: relax your hand, and pull the knitting down more often.
- The yarn breaks: T-shirt yarn tears at seams. Knot it and keep going.

### Show it off
Wear it at dinner and tell everyone which shirts it was made from.

### Clean up
Sweep up fabric scraps, and save the bits for stuffing a pillow or toy.

### For the grown-up
Finger knitting builds fine motor skill, patterns and patience, plus reuse of old clothing. Scissors used on fabric should be sharp. **Ask at dinner:** "How many old shirts became your scarf?"

## 26. Speed of Light in a Microwave

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a microwave oven, a large microwave-safe plate, something that melts in clear spots (a flour tortilla covered with grated cheese, a layer of mini marshmallows, or a bar of chocolate laid flat), a ruler, paper, a pen, a calculator or scratch paper

**Nice to have:** a second food to compare, a magnifying glass

### The big idea
Light travels about 300,000 kilometers every second. Scientists spent centuries trying to measure it. You can do it in your kitchen with a microwave and a plate of cheese. Microwaves are light waves your eyes can't see, and they leave melted spots exactly half a wavelength apart. Measure the spots, read one number on the oven's label, and you've measured one of the universe's most important constants.

### Goals
- A plate of melted spots, measured to the millimeter.
- A calculated value for the speed of light, compared to the real one.

### Objectives
- I can explain **standing waves**: waves that bounce back and forth and make hot and cold spots.
- I can use the formula speed = wavelength x frequency.
- I can calculate **percent error** and explain where my error came from.

### Get ready
Get a grown-up's OK to use the microwave and check for any food allergies. Find the label on the back or inside the door: it lists the frequency, usually about 2,450 MHz. Write it down.

### Steps
1. **Stop the spin.** Take out the turntable, or set your plate upside down on top of a bowl so it doesn't turn. The food must stay still to show hot spots.
2. **Load it.** Cover the plate evenly with cheese on a tortilla, marshmallows or a flat chocolate bar.
3. **Zap carefully** (5 minutes). Heat for 15 seconds. Look. If no spots melted, try 10 more seconds. Stop as soon as you see 2 or 3 distinct melted spots. Never run the microwave empty.
4. **Measure.** Use the ruler to measure the distance between the centers of two neighboring melted spots. It should be about 6 cm. Measure three pairs and take the average.
5. **Calculate.** The distance between spots is half a wavelength, so the wavelength = 2 x distance. Convert to meters: 6.1 cm x 2 = 0.122 m.
6. **Speed of light** = wavelength x frequency. 0.122 m x 2,450,000,000 per second = about 299,000,000 m/s.
7. **Percent error.** Compare your answer to the real value, 299,792,458 m/s. Percent error = (your value minus real value) divided by real value x 100.

### Make it yours
- Try a second food to see if it changes your result.
- Repeat it three times on fresh plates and see how close you get.
- Write a "lab report" in the style of a 1600s scientist announcing an astounding discovery.

### Stuck?
- Everything melts at once: you heated it too long. Start fresh with shorter times.
- No clear spots: the plate is still moving, or the layer is too thick. Use a thin, even layer.

### Show it off
Show the family your plate and your number, then let them eat the experiment.

### Clean up
Wipe out the microwave, put the turntable back and wash the plate.

### For the grown-up
This is a real physics measurement: waves, frequency, wavelength and error, all in high school physics. Use only microwave-safe plates, no metal, short heating times, and never run the oven empty. Melted cheese and chocolate can be hot. Check for allergies before eating it. **Ask at dinner:** "How did a plate of cheese measure the speed of light?"

## 27. Persuasive Speech Night

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, index cards, a "podium" (a stack of books on a table), a clock

**Nice to have:** a poster or simple visual, a family audience, a ballot box

### The big idea
Great speeches have changed history, and they all use the same tools. You'll write and deliver a 3-minute persuasive speech that tries to change your family's minds on something: a serious idea, a household rule, or something gloriously ridiculous like "cereal is a soup." Then the audience votes.

### Goals
- A 3-minute persuasive speech written on cue cards.
- A live delivery and a family vote before and after.

### Objectives
- I can use **ethos** (trust), **pathos** (emotion) and **logos** (logic).
- I can use a speech structure that builds to a call to action.
- I can deliver with eye contact, pauses and volume.

### Get ready
Choose your topic and your position. It should be something people might disagree with. Write it as one clear sentence: "Every family should have a no-phone dinner every night."

### Steps
1. **Pre-vote.** Ask your audience where they stand before you start writing, and write the count down.
2. **Brainstorm** (10 minutes). List every reason for your side. Then list the best argument *against* you. You'll need to answer it.
3. **Structure the speech** in five parts:
   - **Attention**: a hook, story or surprising fact.
   - **Need**: the problem.
   - **Solution**: your idea.
   - **Visualization**: show the world if they agree (or if they don't).
   - **Action**: tell them exactly what to do.
4. **Add the three appeals.** Ethos: why should they trust you? Pathos: a story or image that makes them feel. Logos: a fact, number or logical step.
5. **Answer the other side.** "Some people say... but..."
6. **Cue cards** (20 minutes). Write one main point per card, with a few key words, not full sentences.
7. **Practice** out loud 4 times with a clock. Pause after your strongest line. Look at your audience, not your cards.
8. **Deliver** it, then hold the second vote.

### Make it yours
- Hold a debate: someone else argues the other side, and the audience votes.
- Give the speech as a historical or fictional character arguing their cause.
- Make it a ridiculous topic delivered with total seriousness.

### Stuck?
- You can't think of reasons: interview a family member about why they'd agree.
- Nerves: plant your feet, take one slow breath, and pick one friendly face.

### Show it off
Make it a speech night: everyone speaks, and the votes are counted after each one.

### Clean up
Keep your cue cards. You'll use the structure again in school.

### For the grown-up
This builds rhetoric and public speaking skills used in English, debate and civics. Respond to your teen's arguments with real questions. **Ask at dinner:** "What was your best argument, and did it change any votes?"

- A working cardboard prototype of an original product.
- A 60-second pitch with a name, a problem and a demo.

### Objectives
- I can interview users to find a real problem.
- I can sketch, prototype and test a design.
- I can pitch an idea clearly and persuasively.

### Get ready
Gather a pile of recycled materials. Make a list of 3 people to ask questions, or, if you're alone, walk through your house noticing problems.

### Steps
1. **Find problems** (15 minutes). Ask people: "What bugs you every day at home?" Or notice: tangled chargers, a sock that always goes missing, a spoon that slides into the pot. Write 10.
2. **Pick one** and write it as "How might I help ___ do ___?" Example: "How might I help my dad find his keys every morning?"
3. **Sketch 5 ideas** in 10 minutes. Go wild. Then pick the best.
4. **Build a prototype** (40 minutes). Use cardboard and junk. It doesn't have to be pretty, but it should actually work, at least in part.
5. **Test it** with someone or by yourself. What went wrong? Fix it once.
6. **Name it** and write the pitch: the problem (15 seconds), the solution (20 seconds), a demo (15 seconds), and the ask (10 seconds): "Will you invest in a test of 10?"
7. Practice the pitch with a clock until it's under 60 seconds.

### Make it yours
- Design for a fictional customer: a busy dragon, a sea captain or an astronaut.
- Make a fake package and ad for your product.
- Create a "Version 2" that fixes your prototype's biggest flaw.

### Stuck?
- No problems come to mind: watch someone cook or get ready for 10 minutes. Count the moments they get annoyed.
- The prototype won't work: build only the most important part.

### Show it off
Hold a pitch night. Family members are investors with 100 pretend dollars each to give out.

### Clean up
Recycle scraps and keep the prototype on a shelf.

### For the grown-up
This is design thinking: empathy, defining problems, building and testing, and presenting, the same process used in engineering and business classes. **Ask at dinner:** "Who is your product for, and what does it fix?"

## 29. Party Game for Six

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, index cards or cut paper, pens, a bowl or hat, a timer or clock

**Nice to have:** a die, colored paper for team cards, a group of friends or family

### The big idea
The best party games are about the people, not the pieces: bluffing, guessing, laughing and accusing. You'll design an original party game for six or more players, with simple rules, a twist, and moments where the whole room shouts at once.

### Goals
- A party game for 6 or more players, with cards and a rule sheet.
- A playtest, even a small one, and one improvement.

### Objectives
- I can design a game around social mechanics like bluffing, guessing or hidden roles.
- I can make rules that scale from 4 to 10 players.
- I can write rules short enough to explain in two minutes.

### Get ready
Get cards, pens and a hat. Think of a party game moment you love: a perfect bluff, a hilarious guess.

### Steps
1. **Pick a core** (10 minutes):
   - **Hidden roles**: one or two players secretly have a different goal.
   - **Word guessing**: describe, draw or act out with a twist.
   - **Bluffing**: players claim things that may or may not be true.
2. **Add a twist.** Example: in a word game, the guesser can only ask yes or no questions, but one player is a secret "liar" who answers wrong.
3. **Make the cards** (20 minutes). Write 30 to 50 prompt cards, or role cards for every player count.
4. **Write the rules**: setup, a round, scoring, how the game ends. Keep it to one page.
5. **Scale it.** What changes with 4 players? 10? Adjust role counts or time limits.
6. **Playtest.** With a group, or by "simulating": write six player names and play through a round, imagining what each would do. Look for dull moments.
7. Revise one rule and give the game a name that makes people want to play.

### Make it yours
- Make it about your family or friends, with prompts only your group would get.
- Add a physical element, like everyone touching their nose when a secret word is said.
- Design a version that works outside, in a park or a yard.

### Stuck?
- The game is quiet: add a moment where everyone acts at once, like a vote or a grab.
- One person dominates: give everyone a turn to be the center of the round.

### Show it off
Bring it out at the next family gathering, sleepover or night with friends.

### Clean up
Rubber-band the cards and keep them in an envelope with the rules.

### For the grown-up
Party game design builds creativity, rule-writing and social awareness. It's a ready-made screen-free activity for the next time friends or family visit. **Ask at dinner:** "Can we play a round of your game right now?"

## 30. Neighborhood History Walking Tour

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 90-120 min | Outdoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a clipboard or notebook, a pen, a hand-drawn map, comfortable shoes, water

**Nice to have:** a long-time neighbor or relative to interview, a camera, colored pencils, a library book about your town

### The big idea
Every street holds clues: a date carved on a building's cornerstone, a faded painted sign on a brick wall, a street named after someone nobody remembers. You're the historian. You'll walk your neighborhood, gather clues, interview someone who's lived there a long time, and build a 6-stop walking tour you lead for your family.

### Goals
- A map of 6 tour stops with a fact or story for each.
- A guided walking tour for your family.

### Objectives
- I can find primary evidence in buildings, signs and street names.
- I can gather an oral history through an interview.
- I can turn research into a short, engaging script.

### Get ready
Tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. Plan a loop of about 1 to 2 km near home that you're allowed to walk. Dress for the weather.

### Steps
1. **Clue hunt** (40 minutes). Walk slowly and look up. Look for:
   - Cornerstones or dates on buildings.
   - **Ghost signs**: old painted ads on walls.
   - Old features: horse rings, carriage steps, a filled-in doorway, a bricked-up window.
   - Street names, plaques and memorials.
2. **Sketch and note** each clue: where it is and what it says.
3. **Interview** (15 minutes) a neighbor you know, a grandparent or a family member who's lived nearby a long time. Ask: "What was here when you were young? What's changed? What's the strangest story you know about this street?"
4. **Pick 6 stops** with the best stories. Order them in a loop.
5. **Write a script** for each stop: 4 or 5 sentences with a hook, the history and a "look at this" detail.
6. Draw the tour map with numbered stops.
7. **Lead the tour.** Walk the family through, using your script.

### Make it yours
- Do a "lost places" tour of things that used to be there but are gone.
- Add a mystery stop: a clue you couldn't explain. Ask your audience for theories.
- Do a "100 years from now" tour, imagining how each stop will change.

### Stuck?
- Your street feels too new: look for the oldest thing you can find, even a tree or a fence. Every place has a story.
- You can't go out much: do a tour of your building or home, with each room's history.

### Show it off
Lead the tour on a weekend for the family. Hand out your map.

### Clean up
Put away your notes, and keep the map: future tours can grow from it.

### For the grown-up
This builds history skills: primary sources, oral history and turning research into a story. Your teen should tell you their route, stay in agreed areas and only interview people they already know. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the oldest thing you found on our street?"
