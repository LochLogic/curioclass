## 1. Cardboard Arcade

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 to 4 cardboard boxes (shoeboxes, cereal boxes, a shipping box), paper cups, scissors, tape, markers, paper, small soft balls (rolled-up socks or crumpled foil balls), a ruler

**Nice to have:** a box cutter (grown-up only), paper towel tubes, craft sticks, a small bell, masking tape for the floor

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Big news! The first arcade in our house opens today, and you are the owner. Every game is made from boxes, every player wins tickets, and you decide the prizes. What games will your arcade have?"

### Goals
- An arcade with at least three working games made from boxes and cups.
- A stack of paper tickets and a prize counter with price signs your child wrote.

### Objectives
- Your child can plan a game and explain its rules out loud.
- Your child can add up points and trade them for tickets.
- Your child can write a game name and a one-sentence rule on a sign.

### Get ready
Clear a strip of floor about as long as a door. Gather the boxes, cups and balls in a pile. Make 3 sock balls by rolling socks into tight bundles. Keep the box cutter up high; you are the only one who uses it.

### Steps
1. **Pick the games** (10 minutes). Share three starter ideas: **Hole Toss** (a box lid with three holes: fist size for 1 point, lemon size for 5, egg size for 10), **Cup Pyramid** (6 cups stacked 3, 2, 1, knocked down from 6 big steps away), and **Tube Drop** (a tube taped at a slant that drops a ball into one of three cups). Your child picks three, or invents their own.
2. **Draw, then cut.** Your child draws every hole and door in marker. You cut them.
3. **Build** (30 minutes). Your child tapes, stacks and props. Ask: "How will the ball get back to the player?" A paper cup taped behind each hole catches balls.
4. **Make the rules.** Put a tape line on the floor for where players stand. Decide how many tries (3 balls is a good number) and what each hole is worth.
5. **Print the money.** Cut paper into strips about 1 inch by 3 inches. Your child writes "1 TICKET" on 30 of them.
6. **Write the signs.** One per game, with a name and one rule: "HOLE TOSS. 3 balls. Small hole = 10 points."
7. **Open the prize counter.** Your child picks 5 prizes from around the house (a sticker, a turn choosing dinner music, a hug coupon) and writes a price for each: "5 tickets."
8. **Test play** (15 minutes). Play every game twice. If a game is too easy, move the tape line back a step.

### Make it yours
- Give the arcade a theme, like a space station where every game is a planet, and rename the games to match.
- Build one game with a moving part: a cup hanging from a string that swings while you throw.
- Add a "boss game" that costs 3 tickets to play but pays 10 if you win.

### Stuck?
- Balls bounce right back out: tape a cup or a small box behind the hole as a catcher.
- The box keeps sliding: put a can or a book inside it for weight.
- Nobody wins: make one hole huge. Every arcade needs one easy game.

### Show it off
Hold a grand opening when the family gets home. Your child hands each player 3 balls, keeps score, pays tickets and runs the prize counter.

### Clean up
Stack the games inside the biggest box so the arcade can reopen tomorrow. Your child gathers every ball and ticket.

### For the grown-up
This is real engineering: plan, build, test and fix. Keeping score and making change with tickets is adding practice, and the signs are short, useful writing. Do all cutting with the box cutter yourself, and keep throws to soft balls indoors. **Ask at dinner:** "Which game did you have to fix the most, and what did you change?"

## 2. Paper Bridge for Toy Cars

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** printer paper (about 15 sheets), tape, two stacks of books the same height, a toy car, coins or small blocks for weight, a ruler, a pencil

**Nice to have:** a paper cup to hold the weights, index cards, string

### The big idea
Read aloud: "A river has cut Toy Town in half, and the cars can't get across! The town needs an engineer. You have only paper and tape. Can you build a bridge strong enough to hold the cars?"

### Goals
- A paper bridge that spans a gap of 8 inches and holds a toy car.
- A record of how many coins each bridge design held before it sagged.

### Objectives
- Your child can try a design, see where it fails, and change it.
- Your child can count and compare weights to tell which design is strongest.
- Your child can use the words fold, strong, weak and span.

### Get ready
Make two book stacks about 4 inches tall and set them 8 inches apart (measure with the ruler). That gap is the river. Put the paper, tape, car and coins nearby. Write "Bridge Test" at the top of a sheet of paper with three lines under it: Flat, Folded, My Idea.

### Steps
1. **Test a flat bridge.** Lay one plain sheet across the gap. Set the car on it. Splash! Ask: "Why do you think it fell?"
2. **Fold it.** Show your child how to fold a sheet like a fan: a fold about the width of a finger, flip, fold again, all the way down. Lay it across the gap. Now add coins one at a time and count until it sags. Your child writes the number on the Bridge Test sheet.
3. **Try other shapes** (10 minutes). Roll a sheet into a tube and tape it. Fold one into a U-shaped channel. Test each with coins.
4. **Design your own** (20 minutes). Your child picks the best ideas and combines them: two tubes side by side with a flat deck on top, a fan folded under a flat sheet, sides folded up like walls. Use no more than 5 sheets.
5. **The big test.** Drive the car across slowly. Then load coins in the middle until it bends. Write down the number.
6. **Make it wider.** Move the books to 12 inches apart. Does the winning bridge still work? What has to change?
7. **Name it.** Every real bridge has a name. Your child writes one on a paper flag and tapes it to the end.

### Make it yours
- Build a drawbridge: tape a string to one end so it can lift for paper boats.
- Make it a two-lane bridge so two cars can pass at once.
- Add a toll booth made from a small box, and your child writes the price sign.

### Stuck?
- Folds keep flattening out: tape the ends of the fan folds so they hold their shape.
- The bridge slides off the books: tape each end to the top book.
- Your child gets frustrated: remind them that real engineers test and break lots of bridges. A broken bridge is information.

### Show it off
Hold a ribbon-cutting. Cut a strip of paper across the bridge and let the family drive the first car over while your child tells how many coins it held.

### Clean up
Collect the coins in a cup, recycle the scrap paper, and put the books back on the shelf.

### For the grown-up
This builds early engineering and measurement: same paper, different shapes, very different strength. Counting and comparing coins is real math. Keep coins away from younger siblings. **Ask at dinner:** "Why do you think a folded sheet is so much stronger than a flat one?"

## 3. Two-Ending Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 6 sheets of paper, a stapler or tape, pencils, crayons or markers

**Nice to have:** colored paper for the two endings, a ribbon or yarn

### The big idea
Read aloud: "What if a story could go two ways? Today you are writing a book where the reader gets to choose. At the big moment, they pick a door, and each door leads to a different ending. You decide what is behind both doors."

### Goals
- A small book with a beginning, a big choice, and two different endings.
- A reader at home picks a path and finds out what happens.

### Objectives
- Your child can tell a story with a beginning, a middle and an end.
- Your child can imagine two different results from one choice.
- Your child can write short sentences under their pictures, with help spelling bigger words.

### Get ready
Fold 3 sheets in half and staple along the fold for the main book (12 small pages). Fold 3 more sheets the same way and staple them into two thin "ending" books, or use colored paper so the endings look special.

### Steps
1. **Pick a hero and a place** (5 minutes). Ask: "Who is your story about, and where are they?" A hamster in a castle, a girl on a cloud, a robot at the beach. Anything works.
2. **Find the big choice.** Something happens that needs a decision. Examples: two doors, one red and one blue. A path that splits left and right. A wizard who offers a cookie or a key.
3. **Draw the beginning** (20 minutes). On 4 or 5 pages, your child draws the hero getting to the big moment. Under each picture they write one sentence, like "Pip found a door in the tree." Help spell, but let them sound words out first.
4. **Write the choice page.** The last page of the main book says: "If Pip opens the red door, go to the RED book. If Pip opens the blue door, go to the BLUE book."
5. **Write ending one** (15 minutes). Ask: "What's the surprising thing behind the first door?" Your child draws and writes 3 or 4 pages.
6. **Write ending two.** Ask: "Should this one be funny, scary or happy? Make it different from the first." Another 3 or 4 pages.
7. **Make the covers.** Title the main book, then label each ending book with its color and "THE END?" on its last page.

### Make it yours
- Make one ending lead to a third choice, so the reader has to pick again.
- Hide a clue in the beginning that tells the careful reader which door is best.
- Tell the story from the point of view of the door, or the wizard, instead of the hero.

### Stuck?
- Your child can't think of a choice: offer three and let them pick one.
- Writing is getting tiring: you write what they say, and they copy just the first word of each sentence.
- Both endings come out the same: ask, "What is the opposite of what happened?"

### Show it off
Read it to someone at home after work. Let them pick the door, read that ending, then go back and read the other.

### Clean up
Gather the three books together with a ribbon or a paper band so they don't get separated.

### For the grown-up
This builds story structure (beginning, problem, end), cause and effect, and early writing. Writing two endings stretches imagination and helps kids see that choices change outcomes. **Ask at dinner:** "Which ending do you like better, and which one would your hero choose?"

## 4. Trip to Anywhere Travel Agency

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper, crayons or markers, scissors, tape, a table and two chairs, a small bag or backpack, a partner to be the customer (you, a sibling, or a grown-up after work)

**Nice to have:** a globe or world map, old magazines or catalogs to cut up, a toy phone, a stamp and ink pad

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Welcome to Trip to Anywhere, the only travel agency that can send you anywhere at all. The moon? Sure. The bottom of the ocean? Easy. A land where everything is made of pancakes? Of course. You are the travel agent. Where will you send your customers?"

### Goals
- A travel agency desk with posters, tickets and passports your child made.
- At least two customers "sent" on trips, with tickets, packing lists and a pretend journey.

### Objectives
- Your child can describe a real or imaginary place using details about weather, food and things to do.
- Your child can write a short packing list that fits a place.
- Your child can use polite talk to help a customer.

### Get ready
Set up a table as the agency desk with two chairs facing each other. Fold a few sheets of paper in half to be blank passports. Cut 6 strips of paper about 3 inches by 6 inches for tickets.

### Steps
1. **Choose three destinations** (5 minutes). Your child picks three: one real place (the beach, a city, a jungle), one far-out place (the moon, a volcano island), and one totally made up (Pancake Land).
2. **Make posters** (20 minutes). One poster per place, with a big picture and a short line: "Visit the Moon! Jump 6 times higher!" Tape them up behind the desk.
3. **Make the tickets.** On each ticket, your child writes FROM, TO, and a time, like "Leaves at 3:00."
4. **Make a passport.** Your child draws your face inside and writes your name. Each trip gets a stamp (a drawn star works).
5. **Open for business.** You walk in as the customer. Your child greets you, asks where you want to go, and describes each place. Try customer questions: "Is it cold there? What do people eat?"
6. **Pack the bag.** Your child writes a 4-item packing list for your trip (for the moon: helmet, snacks, moon boots, camera) and finds pretend items around the house to put in the backpack.
7. **Take the trip** (10 minutes). Everyone "travels": count down for a rocket, sway for a boat. Your child is the tour guide and points out the sights.
8. **Switch.** Your child becomes the customer and you are the agent.

### Make it yours
- Add a weather report for each place, with a drawn sun, cloud or snowflake and a temperature.
- Make a souvenir shop where travelers buy one tiny item from each place.
- Plan a trip with three stops and draw the route on a map.

### Stuck?
- Your child runs out of things to say about a place: ask, "What does it sound like? What does it smell like?"
- No partner right now: stuffed animals can be customers, and your child writes their tickets.

### Show it off
Invite a family member for a "booking" after dinner. Your child sells them a trip and gives them a ticket to keep.

### Clean up
Take down the posters and keep them, the tickets and the passports in a folder for the next trip.

### For the grown-up
Pretend play like this builds speaking, describing, early geography and practical writing (tickets, lists, labels). It is also great practice in asking questions and listening. **Ask at dinner:** "If you could really go to one of your three places, which would it be, and what would you pack first?"

## 5. Fizzing Volcano

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | High | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a small plastic bottle (about 16 ounces), a baking pan or tray with sides, foil or newspaper, tape, baking soda, white vinegar, dish soap, a spoon, measuring spoons, a measuring cup, paper and a pencil

**Nice to have:** red and yellow food coloring, play dough or salt dough to build the mountain, small toy figures for the village, safety glasses or sunglasses

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Deep under Fizz Mountain, something is bubbling. The people of the tiny village below need a scientist to find out what makes the volcano erupt, and how to make it bigger or smaller. That scientist is you."

### Goals
- A volcano mountain your child built around a bottle.
- At least three eruptions, with a guess and a result for each.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a guess, test it and tell what happened.
- Your child can change one thing at a time (more soap, more vinegar) and compare.
- Your child can say that mixing baking soda and vinegar makes a gas that makes bubbles.

### Get ready
Work outside on the ground, or inside in a bathtub or a big pan on a towel. Set the bottle in the middle of the pan. Put on old clothes. Pour a cup of vinegar into a measuring cup you can refill.

### Steps
1. **Build the mountain** (15 minutes). Your child scrunches foil or newspaper around the bottle and tapes it into a cone shape, keeping the bottle top open. Add a village of toys around the bottom.
2. **First eruption.** Put 2 spoonfuls of baking soda in the bottle. Your child pours in a half cup of vinegar. Step back and watch!
3. **Talk about it.** Ask: "What did you see? What did you hear?" Explain the bubbles are a gas (carbon dioxide) made when the two mix. Your child writes "Test 1: big fizz" or whatever they saw.
4. **Guess and test.** Now add a squirt of dish soap with the baking soda. Ask: "Will it be bigger or smaller?" Your child writes the guess, then tries it.
5. **Change one thing.** Your child picks the next test: more baking soda, warm vinegar, or a drop of red and yellow food coloring for lava. Write the guess first, then test.
6. **Make the biggest eruption** (15 minutes). Your child uses what they learned to plan the biggest one. Count down from 10 together.
7. **Rescue the village.** Wash off the toys and report to the villagers what makes the volcano erupt.

### Make it yours
- Make a "slow" volcano that oozes instead of shoots: try less vinegar and more soap.
- Write a short news report: "Fizz Mountain erupts! Village safe!" and read it out loud like a TV reporter.
- Build a second volcano and hold a race to see which one fizzes over first.

### Stuck?
- The fizz is small: add more baking soda (it gets used up) and rinse the bottle between tests.
- The mountain gets soggy: rebuild it with fresh foil, which keeps its shape.

### Show it off
Save one big eruption for when the family gets home and let your child explain each test before the countdown.

### Clean up
Pour the foam down the drain with plenty of water, rinse the bottle, and wipe the pan. Your child washes the toy villagers.

### For the grown-up
This is a first chemistry experiment with a fair test: change one thing, keep the rest the same, write down the result. Baking soda and vinegar are safe kitchen items, but vinegar stings eyes, so keep faces back and use glasses or sunglasses if you have them. Never cap the bottle. Food coloring can stain, so work on a washable surface. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you change to make the biggest eruption?"

## 6. Tiny Twig House

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-90 min | Outdoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** sticks and twigs, leaves, small stones, bark, moss or grass, string or yarn, a paper bag or basket for collecting, a sheet of cardboard about the size of a placemat

**Nice to have:** school glue, scissors, a small dish of water for a "pond", a magnifying glass

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Somewhere outside lives a family of tiny forest people, smaller than your thumb. Tonight a storm is coming, and they need a house. Only you can build it, using what you find on the ground."

### Goals
- A tiny house built only from nature, standing up on its own.
- A name and a story for the tiny family that lives there.

### Objectives
- Your child can collect and sort natural materials by size and use.
- Your child can solve building problems, like making walls stand or a roof stay on.
- Your child can describe the house using words like roof, wall, door and floor.

### Get ready
Head to a yard, a park, or a patch of trees with your child. Take only things already on the ground: no pulling live plants. If you have no outdoor space, collect on a walk, then build on the cardboard indoors or on a balcony.

### Steps
1. **Collect** (15 minutes). Your child gathers: 10 sticks about as long as a pencil, a handful of short twigs, big flat leaves, bark pieces, and a few pebbles. Ask them to sort the pile into "walls," "roof" and "fancy stuff."
2. **Pick a spot.** At the base of a tree, by a rock, or on the cardboard. Ask: "Where would a tiny person feel safe?"
3. **Build the walls** (15 minutes). Two easy ways: push sticks into the dirt in a square like a fence, or lean sticks against each other in a tent shape. Tie corners with string if they wobble.
4. **Add the roof.** Lay big leaves or bark across the top. Ask: "Will rain get in? How could you fix it?"
5. **Furnish it** (15 minutes). Your child decides what the family needs: a pebble path, a moss bed, an acorn-cap bowl, a bark table. Let them invent.
6. **Rain test.** Sprinkle a few drops of water over the roof. Is anyone inside getting wet? Your child fixes leaks.
7. **Name the family.** Your child writes their names on a leaf-shaped piece of paper or tells you their story while you write it down.

### Make it yours
- Build a whole village with a bridge, a well and a garden.
- Make a fairy door from flat bark and a pebble doorknob.
- Leave a tiny "welcome" note in the house and check tomorrow to see if the weather changed anything.

### Stuck?
- The walls keep falling: build in soft dirt, or press sticks into a ball of mud or play dough.
- The ground is too hard: build on the cardboard and glue pieces in place.

### Show it off
Give a guided tour to someone at home, using a stick as a pointer. If it's in a park, take a photo to show later.

### Clean up
Wash hands well. Leave the house to return to nature, or bring the cardboard version inside.

### For the grown-up
This builds fine motor skills, problem solving, early engineering and care for nature. Check the area for broken glass, sharp things and animal droppings first, and teach your child not to touch mushrooms, berries or anything they don't know. Wash hands after. **Ask at dinner:** "Who lives in your twig house, and what do they do all day?"

## 7. Flip Book Animation

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1 |

**Materials:** a small notepad or 20 index cards (or paper cut into 20 rectangles about 3 by 5 inches), a pencil, an eraser, a stapler or binder clip, colored pencils

**Nice to have:** sticky notes (they make a ready flip book), a light window to trace through

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Did you know cartoons are just lots of drawings shown really fast? Today you are an animator. You will draw a tiny movie on paper, and when you flip the pages, your drawings will come alive and move!"

### Goals
- A flip book of at least 15 pages with a drawing that moves.
- A title page with your child's name as the animator.

### Objectives
- Your child can draw the same thing again and again with a small change each time.
- Your child can plan a short action with a start, a middle and an end.
- Your child can explain that many still pictures shown fast look like movement.

### Get ready
If you're using loose paper, stack 20 rectangles and staple or clip along the left edge. Sit at a table near a window. Sharpen the pencil.

### Steps
1. **Try a quick one** (10 minutes). On the last 5 pages of the pad, draw a ball rising a little higher on each page. Flip from back to front. It bounces! Explain that animators start at the back.
2. **Pick a movie.** Your child chooses something simple: a ball bouncing, a seed growing into a flower, a fish swimming across, a stick person jumping, a sun rising. Simple shapes work best.
3. **Draw the first page.** On the last page of the pad, draw the starting picture. Press firmly so it shows through the next page.
4. **Trace and change** (30 minutes). On the next page up, trace the picture through the paper, but move or change one thing a tiny bit (about a fingernail's width). Keep going, page by page. Hold the pages up to a window if it's hard to see through.
5. **Flip check.** After every 5 pages, flip to see how it moves. Too jumpy? Make smaller changes.
6. **Finish the action.** Your child decides how it ends: the flower blooms, the fish eats a worm, the stick person lands a flip.
7. **Color it.** Color the main thing the same color on every page.
8. **Make a title page.** On top, your child writes the movie name and "by" with their name.

### Make it yours
- Add a surprise ending, like the bouncing ball turning into a bird and flying away.
- Make a two-character movie where one chases the other.
- Draw a background (grass, a sky) on every page so the movie has a setting.

### Stuck?
- The picture jumps instead of moves: make each change smaller, like a tiny step.
- Tracing is hard: use thinner paper or tape the pages to a sunny window.

### Show it off
Hold a "movie premiere" after dinner. Dim the lights, have everyone sit down, and let your child flip the movie for each person.

### Clean up
Put pencils away and keep the flip book in a safe spot where it won't get bent.

### For the grown-up
Flip books build drawing control, patience, planning and the science of how animation works (our eyes blend quick pictures together). It's also a gentle lesson in small, steady changes. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest part of making the drawing move?"

## 8. Water Glass Xylophone

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 45-75 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 6 to 8 glasses or glass jars the same size, water, a measuring cup, a metal spoon and a wooden spoon, a towel, paper and crayons

**Nice to have:** food coloring, masking tape for labels

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Did you know water can sing? If you tap a glass, it rings. Add water and the sound changes. Today you are building your own musical instrument from glasses and water, and then you'll write a song for it."

### Goals
- A row of glasses that play different notes, from low to high.
- A short song your child made up and wrote down in colors.

### Objectives
- Your child can tell which sound is higher and which is lower.
- Your child can change the amount of water to change the sound.
- Your child can play a simple pattern and repeat it.

### Get ready
Set the glasses in a row on a towel on a sturdy table. Have water in a pitcher nearby. Your child stays sitting while playing, so nothing gets knocked over.

### Steps
1. **Tap an empty glass.** Use the metal spoon, gently. Listen. Now pour water halfway and tap again. Ask: "Higher or lower?"
2. **Fill the row** (15 minutes). Leave the first glass almost empty and add a little more water to each glass down the row, about a quarter cup more each time. Tap them in order. Is each one a little lower than the last?
3. **Tune it.** Your child listens and adjusts: add a splash or pour a little out until the notes step down evenly, like stairs.
4. **Color the notes.** Add a drop of a different food coloring to each glass, or put a colored tape label on each. Your child writes the color names in order on a paper.
5. **Play a pattern.** Try "Up the stairs" (play glasses 1 to 6), then "Down the stairs." Then try a pattern like red, red, blue, red.
6. **Write a song** (15 minutes). Your child makes up a short song and writes it with colored dots in the order to play. Five to 10 notes is plenty.
7. **Name it and practice.** Give the song a title. Play it three times until it's smooth.
8. **Try the wooden spoon.** How does the sound change? Your child picks the sound they like best.

### Make it yours
- Make a "rain song" with soft, quick taps and a "thunder" note on the emptiest glass.
- Play along to a song you both know, finding the notes by ear.
- Make a "secret song" and see if a grown-up can guess what it is.

### Stuck?
- All the glasses sound the same: make the water amounts more different, from nearly empty to nearly full.
- The sound is dull: tap near the rim, not the side, and don't touch the glass with your hand while it rings.

### Show it off
Hold a concert. Your child announces the song title, plays it, then teaches someone in the family to play it.

### Clean up
Pour out the water, dry the glasses, and wipe the table. Your child is in charge of the towel.

### For the grown-up
This builds listening, early music reading and real physics: less water means faster vibration and a higher note when you tap. Use sturdy glasses or jars, tap gently, and keep the setup low and on a towel. If a glass chips, you clean it up. **Ask at dinner:** "Why do you think the glass with the most water sounded the lowest?"

## 9. Clue Writer Scavenger Hunt

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 60-90 min | Either | Low | With you | 2+ |

**Materials:** index cards or paper cut into 8 pieces, a pencil, a small "treasure" (a snack, a sticker, a note, a small toy), a bag or box for the treasure, tape

**Nice to have:** an envelope for the first clue, a paper crown for the winner, a flashlight for a nighttime hunt

### The big idea
Read aloud: "This time YOU are the treasure master. You will hide a prize and write the clues that lead to it. Will your hunter figure them out? Make the clues tricky, but not too tricky!"

### Goals
- A treasure hunt with 6 clues your child wrote and hid.
- A hunter (you, a sibling or a friend over) follows every clue and finds the prize.

### Objectives
- Your child can write a short clue that describes a place without naming it.
- Your child can put clues in order so each one leads to the next.
- Your child can test a clue by reading it out loud and checking that it makes sense.

### Get ready
Decide on the hunt area: two or three rooms, or a yard if you have one. Pick the treasure. Number the cards 1 to 6 in the corner.

### Steps
1. **Learn the trick.** Show a sample clue: "I'm cold inside and keep the milk. Look on my door." (The fridge.) Ask your child to guess. A clue tells what a place does or looks like.
2. **Pick 6 hiding spots** (5 minutes). Walk around together. Your child chooses six spots: the bathtub, a shoe, under a pillow, the mailbox, a plant pot, the bookshelf.
3. **Write the clues** (25 minutes). For each spot, your child writes a clue. Help with spelling, but let them come up with the ideas. Examples: "I have lots of stories but I can't talk." "You put your feet in me every day." Rhymes are a bonus: "Look where you get clean, in the tub so white and green."
4. **Check the order.** Clue 1 leads to spot 1, where clue 2 waits. The last spot holds the treasure.
5. **Hide them.** Your child tapes each clue in its spot, out of sight but reachable. You hold clue 1.
6. **Run the hunt.** The hunter reads clue 1 and goes. Your child follows along and may give one hint per clue.
7. **Treasure!** The hunter finds the prize. Then swap: the hunter writes a hunt for your child.

### Make it yours
- Make a theme: a pirate hunt with "ye" and "arr" in every clue, or a space hunt.
- Use picture clues for a younger sibling and word clues for a grown-up.
- Add one secret-code clue, where every letter is written backward.

### Stuck?
- A clue is too hard: add one more detail, like the color or the room.
- Your child gets stuck writing: ask, "What does this thing do? What does it look like?"
- No partner right now: hide the clues and save the hunt for when someone gets home.

### Show it off
Run the hunt for each person who comes home. Your child times each hunter and announces the fastest.

### Clean up
Collect every card, even the ones nobody found. Keep the clues for a future hunt.

### For the grown-up
Writing clues builds describing words, reading for meaning, and sequence (first, next, last). It also teaches kids to think about the reader. Keep hiding spots safe: nothing high, near the stove, or inside appliances. **Ask at dinner:** "Which clue were you proudest of, and why?"

Read aloud: "Today you invent a brand-new character that no one has ever seen. Maybe a cloud knight, a forgetful wizard, or a dog who runs a bakery. Then you make the costume and BECOME that character for the rest of the day."

### Goals
- A full costume with at least three parts your child designed.
- A character card that tells the character's name, power and favorite food.

### Objectives
- Your child can invent a character with clear traits.
- Your child can plan a design on paper and then build it.
- Your child can speak and move like their character.

### Get ready
Pull out a pile of old clothes, scarves and recycling. Clear the floor and set out tape, scissors and markers.

### Steps
1. **Invent the character** (10 minutes). Ask three questions: "What kind of creature or person are they? What can they do that's special? What's one silly thing about them?" Write the answers down.

### Show it off
Hold a "character entrance" for the family: your child walks in, says the catchphrase, and answers questions in character.

### Clean up
Put clothes back, recycle scraps, and keep the costume in a bag for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds creativity, planning, labeling and confidence in speaking. Inventing an original character, instead of copying one from a show, stretches imagination further. You do all cutting of eye holes and any tight spots. Never put plastic bags over a child's head. **Ask at dinner:** "If your character came to dinner, what would they say about the food?"

## 12. Bird Watcher Notebook

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-90 min | Either | Low | With you | 1 |

**Materials:** 4 sheets of paper folded and stapled into a notebook, a pencil, colored pencils, a window or an outdoor spot, a clipboard or a hardcover book to write on

**Nice to have:** binoculars, a bird guide book from the library, bread crumbs or seeds, a blanket to sit on

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Birds are all around us, but most people never really look at them. Today you become a bird watcher, a scientist who watches quietly and writes down what they see. What birds live near you, and what are they doing?"

### Goals
- A bird notebook with at least 3 pages of sketches and notes.
- A tally of how many birds your child spotted.

### Objectives
- Your child can sit still and observe carefully for 5 minutes at a time.
- Your child can sketch a bird and label its beak, wings, tail and feet.
- Your child can write short notes about color, size and what the bird did.

### Get ready
Pick a spot: a window with a view of trees, a balcony, a yard, or a park bench. Make the notebook and write "BIRD NOTEBOOK" on the cover. On the first page, draw a simple bird and label the beak, wings, tail and feet together.

### Steps
1. **Be invisible.** Talk about how to watch birds: sit still, use a quiet voice, and don't make sudden moves.
2. **First watch** (10 minutes). Set a timer or count slowly. Every bird seen gets a tally mark on the back page.
3. **Pick one bird to study.** When a bird stays put for a bit, your child looks closely. Ask: "What color is it? Is it bigger than your hand? What's it doing?"
4. **Sketch it** (10 minutes). Quick drawing: an oval for the body, a circle for the head, a triangle for the beak. Then add color.
5. **Write notes.** Under the sketch, your child writes 2 or 3 short notes: "Brown. Hops. Eats on the ground." If you don't know the name, make one up: "Hopping Brown Bird."
6. **Listen.** Close eyes for one minute and count how many different bird sounds you hear. Your child writes the sound like a word: "chip chip," "coo-coo."
7. **Repeat** for two more birds. Then count up all the tally marks.

### Make it yours
- Make a "bird map" of the yard or view, showing where each bird was seen.
- Invent a bird that doesn't exist yet and draw it with labels for its special features.
- Do it again at a different time of day and compare.

### Stuck?
- No birds show up: sprinkle a few seeds or crumbs in view and wait quietly for 10 minutes.
- Birds fly away too fast: sketch from memory, or draw the shape of the bird in flight.

### Show it off
Share the notebook at dinner and challenge the family to guess which bird was "Hopping Brown Bird."

### Clean up
Put the notebook somewhere safe for the next watch, and bring in any blankets or crumbs.

### For the grown-up
This is real field science: observing, sketching, and recording. It builds patience, attention to detail, and early writing. If you go to a park, stay together. Wash hands after handling seeds. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most interesting thing a bird did today?"

## 15. Magic Milk Colors

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 45-75 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a shallow dish or plate with a rim, milk (whole milk works best), food coloring (at least 3 colors), dish soap, cotton swabs, a small cup, paper towels, paper and crayons

**Nice to have:** a pipette or eyedropper, a magnifying glass, a second dish to compare skim milk

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Today you are a color wizard. You will pour milk, drop in colors, and with one magic touch, the colors will swirl and dance like fireworks. Can you figure out the secret behind the magic?"

### Goals
- At least three color shows made with milk, food coloring and soap.
- A drawing of your child's favorite swirl pattern.

### Objectives
- Your child can follow steps in order to repeat an experiment.
- Your child can describe what they see using words like swirl, spread and mix.
- Your child can compare two tests and say which one moved more.

### Get ready
Set the dish on a tray or a towel. Pour milk until it just covers the bottom (about half an inch). Pour a little dish soap into a small cup.

### Steps
1. **Add colors.** Your child puts 4 drops of food coloring in the middle of the milk, each a different color, close together but not touching.
2. **Test a plain swab.** Touch a clean, dry cotton swab to the middle. Ask: "What happened?" (Not much.)
3. **The magic touch.** Dip a new swab in soap and touch it to the middle of the colors. Hold it still for 10 seconds. Watch them burst away!
4. **Try again.** Touch the soapy swab to different spots: near the edge, between colors. Your child decides where.
5. **Explain the trick.** Milk has tiny bits of fat. Soap pulls on the fat and makes it move, and the colors ride along.
6. **Test a new milk** (10 minutes). If you have skim milk, try it in a second dish. Ask: "Which one moves more? Why do you think?"
7. **Draw it.** Your child draws their favorite swirl and writes one sentence: "The colors ran away from the soap."

### Make it yours
- Drop colors in a pattern, like a rainbow ring or a smiley face, and see what the soap does to it.
- Try warm milk and cold milk side by side.
- Lay a paper towel on top for a second to make a colorful print to keep.

### Stuck?
- Colors don't move: use more soap on the swab, or switch to whole milk.
- The show stops: add a new drop of soap in a different spot.

### Show it off
Save a fresh dish of milk for when the family gets home, and let your child perform the "magic" and explain the secret.

### Clean up
Pour the milk down the sink, rinse the dish, and throw away the swabs. Your child wipes up drips.

### For the grown-up
This shows how soap breaks up fats, the same reason it cleans greasy dishes. It builds observation, describing words and repeated testing. Food coloring can stain, so use old clothes and a towel. The milk is for science, not drinking. **Ask at dinner:** "Why did the colors run away from the soap?"

## 16. The Monster Under My Bed Picture Book

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |

**Materials:** 5 sheets of paper, a stapler, pencils, crayons or markers, a ruler

**Nice to have:** cardboard for a hard cover, yarn to tie it, stickers

### The big idea
Read aloud: "What if there really WAS a monster under your bed, but it wasn't scary at all? Maybe it's afraid of the dark. Maybe it wants to borrow your socks. Today you will write and draw a picture book all about it."

### Goals
- A picture book of about 8 pages with a cover, pictures and words on every page.
- A monster with a name, a look and a problem to solve.

### Objectives
- Your child can plan a story with a beginning, a problem and an ending.
- Your child can write one or two sentences on each page to match the picture.
- Your child can describe a character using details.

### Get ready
Fold 5 sheets of paper in half and staple them along the fold to make a 20-page book (you'll use about 10). Put pencils and crayons nearby.

### Steps
1. **Meet the monster** (10 minutes). Ask: "What does your monster look like? How many eyes? What color? What's its name?" Your child draws it on scrap paper first.
2. **Find the problem.** What's wrong? The monster is afraid of the dark. It's lonely. It lost its favorite toy. Its snoring keeps everyone awake.
3. **Plan the pages.** Talk it through: Page 1, meet the monster. Page 2 and 3, the problem. Pages 4 to 6, trying to fix it (first try fails, second try fails, third try works!). Page 7, the happy ending.
4. **Write and draw** (40 minutes). One page at a time. Your child draws the picture first, then writes a sentence under it. Example: "Grumble was scared of the dark." Help spell big words, but let them try first.
5. **Make the cover.** Title, a big picture of the monster, and "By" with your child's name.
6. **Add a last page.** "About the Author": your child draws themselves and writes one fact, like "She is 7 and likes pancakes."
7. **Read it aloud.** Your child reads the whole book to you, or reads with you.

### Make it yours
- Add a lift-the-flap page: tape a paper flap over a picture so readers lift it to see what's under the bed.
- Write it from the monster's point of view: "I'm the monster, and there's a KID on top of my bed!"
- Make a sequel cover for the next adventure.

### Stuck?
- Your child gets tired of writing: they say the words and you write, then they trace or copy one word per page.
- Ideas run out in the middle: ask, "What's the silliest way the monster could try to fix it?"

### Show it off
Hold a bedtime reading: your child reads the book to the family before bed.

### Clean up
Put crayons back in their box and keep the book on a shelf like a real library book.

### For the grown-up
Writing a full picture book builds story structure, sentence writing, spelling and drawing. A friendly monster also gives kids a safe way to talk about fears. **Ask at dinner:** "What was your monster afraid of, and how did it get brave?"

## 17. Sponge Boat Race

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 or 3 kitchen sponges, drinking straws or craft sticks, paper, tape, scissors, a bathtub, kiddie pool or big plastic bin filled with a few inches of water, a towel

**Nice to have:** rubber bands, foil, a small toy to be the captain, a timer

### The big idea
Read aloud: "The Great Sponge Boat Race is about to begin, and every boat must be built by its own captain. Your boat must float, carry a passenger and sail to the finish line using only wind. Will your boat be the fastest?"

### Goals
- A sponge boat with a sail that floats and moves with wind.
- At least three races where your child changes the sail to go faster.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a guess about which sail will be fastest and test it.
- Your child can change one part of a design and compare results.
- Your child can use the words float, sink, sail and wind.

### Get ready
Fill a tub, pool or bin with about 3 inches of water, inside or outside. Put a towel down. Mark a start line and a finish line with tape on each end.

### Steps
1. **Build the boat.** Your child pushes a straw or craft stick into the middle of a sponge for the mast.
2. **Make the sail** (10 minutes). Cut a paper triangle or square about 3 inches tall. Your child decorates it, then tapes it to the mast.
3. **First race.** Set the boat at the start. Your child blows on the sail. Count how many seconds to the finish.
4. **Change the sail** (20 minutes). Try a bigger sail, a smaller one, a square instead of a triangle, or a foil sail. Ask: "Which do you think will be fastest?" Test each one and count.
5. **Add a passenger.** Put a small toy on the boat. Does it still float? Does it go slower?
6. **Big race.** Build two boats and race them side by side. You and your child each blow on one.
7. **Write the winner.** Your child writes on paper: "Fastest sail: big square. 8 seconds."

### Make it yours
- Build a raft from two sponges tied together with a rubber band.
- Make a rescue mission: the boat must carry a toy from one side to an island (a plastic lid) and back.
- Add a rudder from a craft stick taped to the back and see if the boat goes straighter.

### Stuck?
- The boat tips over: use a smaller sail, or tape a coin to the bottom of the sponge.
- The boat won't move: blow at the sail, not the sponge, and make the sail bigger.

### Show it off
Hold a race day for the family. Your child explains which sail is fastest and lets everyone pick a boat.

### Clean up
Squeeze out the sponges, dry them, and pour the water on a plant. Your child mops up spills with the towel.

### For the grown-up
This builds testing and comparing, and it's a first look at wind power and floating. Stay right beside your child near any water, even a few inches, and empty the tub when you're done. **Ask at dinner:** "What made your boat go the fastest?"

## 18. Animal Yoga Adventure

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 45-75 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a soft floor, a rug, mat, or towel, paper, crayons or markers, tape

**Nice to have:** calm music, a bell, stuffed animals to "join in"

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Today you will go on a jungle adventure without leaving the room. Every animal you meet teaches you a new way to move your body. Can you stretch like a cat, balance like a flamingo, and roar like a lion?"

### Goals
- A set of at least 6 animal poses your child learned or invented.
- A pose-card "story path" taped on the floor that your child can lead.

### Objectives
- Your child can hold a balance pose for 5 slow breaths.
- Your child can follow and give clear movement directions.
- Your child can invent a new pose and describe it with a name.

### Get ready
Clear a space about two arm-lengths wide. Lay out a mat or towel. Take off socks for better grip.

### Steps
1. **Warm up.** Stand tall, reach up high, then fold over and dangle like a monkey. Breathe in slowly through your nose, out through your mouth.
2. **Learn 4 poses** (15 minutes). Try them together:
   **Cat:** hands and knees, round your back and say "meow."
   **Cow:** hands and knees, drop your belly and look up, say "moo."
   **Flamingo:** stand on one foot, other foot on your ankle, arms out like wings.
   **Lion:** kneel, hands on knees, open your mouth wide and roar!
3. **Invent 2 more** (10 minutes). Your child makes up two new animal poses, like a sleepy sloth or a jumping frog. Give each a name and a sound.
4. **Make pose cards.** Your child draws each animal on a half sheet and writes its name. Tape them on the floor in a path.

### Show it off

### Clean up
Wash hands with soap, throw away used tape, and wipe the table.

### For the grown-up
This builds careful observation, comparing and contrasting, and writing a conclusion. Detectives and scientists both use evidence, and this is a fun first taste of it. Keep powder away from eyes and noses. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you know whose fingerprint was on the glass?"

### Goals
- A ramp your child built and changed to test different heights.
- A chart that shows how far each car rolled.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a fair test by changing only one thing at a time.
- Your child can measure distance with a ruler or by counting steps.
- Your child can say which ramp height made cars go farthest and guess why.

### Clean up
Pull up the tape, return the books, and flatten the ramp to save.

### For the grown-up

- Add a "danger" in the habitat, like a hawk or a flood, and how the animal stays safe.

### Stuck?
- Things won't stick: use tape on the back, or glue and hold for 10 seconds.
- Paint takes too long to dry: color with crayons and markers instead.

### Show it off
Set the habitat on a table and give a "nature show" with your child narrating like an explorer.

### Clean up
Put lids on paint and glue, wash brushes, and roll up the newspaper. Your child wipes the table.

### For the grown-up
This builds life science (habitats and what animals need), planning and fact writing, which match second-grade science. You help with any cutting of cardboard. **Ask at dinner:** "What does your animal need to live, and where does it find it?"

## 29. Sound Effects Story Show

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 2+ |

**Materials:** a short story (a picture book or one you make up), household things that make noise (pots, spoons, paper, a bowl of water, rice in a closed container, a box, a comb), paper and a pencil, a partner to read (you, a sibling, or a grown-up after work)

**Nice to have:** a flashlight, a blanket for a "stage," a recorder or phone to play back the show (nice to have, not needed)

### The big idea
Read aloud: "In movies and radio shows, sound effects are made by people with everyday things. Squeezing a bag sounds like footsteps in snow. Shaking a box of rice sounds like rain. Today you are the sound effects artist for a story show!"

### Goals
- A sound effects kit with at least 6 sounds made from household things.
- A story performed live with your child making every sound.

### Objectives
- Your child can listen carefully and match a sound to a real-world event.
- Your child can play sounds at the right time, following cues in a story.
- Your child can change how loud, soft, fast or slow a sound is to match the story.

### Get ready
Gather noise-makers on a towel or table. Pick a short picture book or plan to make up a story together.

### Steps
1. **Explore the sounds** (15 minutes). Try everything: crinkle paper (fire crackling), tap two spoons (horse feet), shake rice (rain), blow across a bottle (wind), stomp on a box (thunder), splash fingers in a bowl (water). Your child names each sound.
2. **Make a sound list.** Your child writes each sound and its tool: "Rain: rice box. Thunder: pot."
3. **Pick a story.** Choose a book, or make up a short one: a storm at sea, a knight's journey, a lost dog finding home.
4. **Mark the cues** (10 minutes). Read the story slowly. Every time something makes a noise, your child shouts "Sound!" and picks a tool. Write it on a sticky note or the sound list.
5. **Rehearse** (15 minutes). You read the story; your child plays the sounds at each cue. Practice loud and soft. Ask: "Is it light rain or a big storm?"
6. **Invent a new sound.** Your child finds a sound no one would expect, like a comb strummed for a creaky door.
7. **Perform!** Dim the lights, use a flashlight, and do the show from start to finish.

### Make it yours
- Make up an original story where your child chooses every event, so they can make every sound.
- Do a "sound-only" round: just sounds, no words, and the audience guesses the story.
- Add a song between scenes, played on pots and pans.

### Stuck?
- Your child misses cues: you pause and point to them when it's time.
- A sound doesn't sound right: try two or three tools and let your child pick the best one.
- No partner right now: your child makes the sounds while a stuffed animal "listens," and the full show waits for later.

### Show it off
Perform the story for the family as a "radio show" with everyone's eyes closed.

### Clean up
Put the pots, spoons and rice away, and wipe up any water.

### For the grown-up
This builds listening, timing, rhythm and story sense, and it's a lot like how real radio and film sound is made. Keep rice in a closed container and pots low and steady. **Ask at dinner:** "What was your favorite sound to make, and what did you make it with?"

## 30. Box Lid Marble Maze

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1 |

**Materials:** a shallow box lid (a shoebox lid or the bottom of a cereal box tray), drinking straws or strips of thin cardboard, tape, glue, scissors, a marble or a small ball, a pencil, markers

**Nice to have:** craft sticks, small paper cups for "traps," a timer

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Can you guide a tiny marble from start to finish without it falling into a trap? Today you are a maze designer. You will build walls, tunnels and traps inside a box lid. Then you'll tilt it to play!"

### Goals
- A working marble maze with a start, a finish and at least 3 turns.
- A maze that someone else can try to solve.

### Objectives
- Your child can plan a path on paper before building.
- Your child can measure and cut walls to fit.
- Your child can test and fix a design so the marble gets through.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Cut straws or cardboard into strips about the length of a finger and a pencil. Have tape ready.

### Steps
1. **Draw the maze** (10 minutes). Inside the lid, your child draws a path with a pencil: START in one corner, FINISH in another, with turns along the way.
2. **Build the walls** (20 minutes). Tape or glue straw or cardboard strips along the lines. Leave enough space for the marble to roll between them. Test with the marble as you go.
3. **Add traps.** Cut a hole (you cut) a little bigger than the marble in one or two spots. If the marble falls through, you start over! Or tape on a paper cup "pit."
4. **Add tricky parts.** Your child chooses: a dead end, a narrow bridge, a zig-zag.
5. **Test** (10 minutes). Put the marble at START and tilt the lid. Does it get to the FINISH? If it gets stuck, fix the walls.
6. **Label and decorate.** Your child writes START and FINISH, names the maze ("The Snake Pit"), and adds drawings: lava, water, a monster near the traps.
7. **Time it.** How fast can your child finish? Can they beat it?

### Make it yours
- Make a two-marble race maze with two paths side by side.
- Add a "secret door" flap that opens a shortcut.
- Make a maze with a story: the marble is a mouse trying to reach the cheese.

### Stuck?
- The marble gets stuck: make the gaps between walls wider.
- Walls fall off: use more tape, or glue and let it dry for 10 minutes.
- The maze is too easy: add more turns and a trap near the finish.

### Show it off
Challenge each family member to solve the maze. Your child keeps a list of everyone's times.

### Clean up
Keep the maze flat on a shelf, put the marble in a cup, and pick up scraps.

### For the grown-up
This builds planning, spatial thinking and the test-and-fix steps of engineering. It also builds hand control while tilting. Marbles are a choking risk for babies and toddlers, so keep them away from younger siblings. You cut any holes in the lid. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest part of the maze to get through?"
