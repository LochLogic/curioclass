## 1. Model Wind Turbine

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 75-120 min | Either | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a cardboard box, a paper cup, a wooden skewer or pencil, a straw that fits loosely over the skewer, scissors, tape, a ruler, a protractor or a folded-paper angle guide, string, a few paper clips, a fan or a windy spot

**Nice to have:** a hot glue gun, a small hobby motor and a little bulb (from an old toy), a craft knife and cutting mat

### The big idea
A town on a windswept island has hired you to keep its lights on. Every real turbine is a fight between three
things: blade count, blade angle and drag. You will build a test rig, run actual trials, and find the blade design
that lifts the most weight. Engineers call it optimizing; you can call it winning.

### Goals
- A working turbine on a stand that spins in a fan or real wind.
- A winning blade design, chosen by data, that winds up the heaviest load of paper clips.

### Objectives
- I can change one variable at a time and keep the rest fixed.
- I can measure output by timing how fast the turbine lifts a load.
- I can explain why more blades or steeper angles do not always mean more power.

### Get ready
Clear a table near an outlet for the fan, or find a doorway or balcony with a steady breeze. Cut a 30 cm tall tower
from the box and tape it into a triangle so it stands on its own. Gather four or five spare cardboard pieces for
blades.

### Steps
1. Build the axle. Push the skewer through the straw, then tape the straw across the top of the tower so the skewer
   spins freely. It should turn with one breath.
2. Make the hub. Push the paper cup's base onto the front of the skewer and tape it tight. Blades will tape to the cup.
3. Cut your first set of blades (15 minutes): three blades, each 12 cm by 4 cm. Tape them to the hub at about 20
   degrees to the wind. Use the protractor, or fold a paper guide.
4. Build the load. Tie 50 cm of string to the back end of the skewer and hang a paper clip from it. As the skewer
   spins, it winds the string up like a tiny crane.
5. Run your trials (30 minutes). Pick one variable to test: blade count (2, 3, 4, 6), blade angle (10, 20, 35, 50
   degrees) or blade shape (rectangle, tapered, curved). Time how long it takes to lift the load to the top. Run each
   design three times and average.
6. Add paper clips until each design stalls. Record the max load in a simple table.
7. Build your champion: combine the best count, angle and shape. Does it beat every single-change design?

### Make it yours
- Design for a storm: make blades that feather (bend back) in strong wind so the turbine does not tear itself apart.
- Build a vertical-axis turbine from two halves of a cup sliced lengthwise, and race it against your best horizontal one.
- Add the hobby motor and bulb from an old toy and see whether you can make a light flicker.

### Stuck?
- It will not spin: the axle has too much friction. Widen the straw hole or use a smoother skewer.
- Results jump around wildly: keep the fan at the same distance (mark the spot with tape) and on the same setting.

### Show it off
Stage a demo: run your worst design and then your champion side by side, and show the table that proves the winner.

### Clean up
Unplug the fan, recycle cardboard scraps, and keep the champion on a shelf.

### For the grown-up
This is a full engineering design cycle: controlled variables, repeated trials, averages and an evidence-based final
design, all physics and math skills from high school science. A craft knife is fine with your permission; a cutting
mat or thick cardboard underneath saves the table. Keep fingers clear of fan blades. **Ask at dinner:** "What did
the data tell you that you did not expect?"

## 2. Unreliable Narrator Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper or a notebook, two pens in different colors, sticky notes or scrap paper

**Nice to have:** a timer, a highlighter

### The big idea
Some of the best stories are told by someone who is lying, wrong, or hiding something, and the reader slowly figures
it out. You are going to write a short story where the narrator says one thing and the clues say another. The trick
is to play fair: every clue is on the page, and the reader still does not see it coming.

### Goals
- A finished short story, 800 to 1,500 words, told by a narrator who cannot be trusted.
- A second reader who catches the truth only at the end, or on a reread.

### Objectives
- I can plant clues that mean one thing on a first read and another on a second.
- I can give a narrator a reason to mislead, whether on purpose or by self-deception.
- I can control what the reader knows and when they learn it.

### Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Draw a line down the middle of a page. Label the left side "What the narrator says" and the right
side "What really happened."

### Steps
1. Pick your narrator type. A **liar** (knows the truth, hides it), a **self-deceiver** (believes their own version),
   or a **limited** narrator (a child, a pet, a stranger who misreads everything).
2. Write the real events first (10 minutes): five or six plain sentences on the right side. Example: "Dev broke the
   window. He blamed his brother. His brother got grounded."
3. Write the narrator's version on the left. Example: "Some things just happen around me. Windows, for example."
4. Plant three clues. Ideas: a detail the narrator mentions and quickly drops, other characters reacting oddly, or a
   time gap ("Anyway, by Tuesday it was sorted").
5. Draft the story (35 minutes). First person, past tense is easiest. Stay in the narrator's head the whole time.
6. Write the reveal. It can be one line, a final action, or a quiet image. Do not explain it.
7. Reread in the second color pen and underline every clue. If you can spot them in under a minute, bury one deeper.

### Make it yours
- Tell it as a diary with dates, and let the gaps between entries do the lying.
- Make the narrator an object, like a house, a ring or a ship's log, that misunderstands the humans around it.
- Write a second short version from another character's view that exposes the first.

### Stuck?
- The twist is too obvious: cut your two most direct clues and keep only the subtle one.
- The story feels flat: give the narrator a strong want, like being liked, being innocent or being right.

### Show it off
Hand it to someone at home with no setup. When they finish, ask when they first got suspicious, and show them your
underlined clues.

### Clean up
Keep your clue map with the draft. It is a useful planning tool for later stories.

### For the grown-up
This is literary craft at the level of high school English: point of view, dramatic irony and foreshadowing, built
by writing instead of analyzing. It is also good practice at revision. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the clue you are
proudest of hiding?"

## 3. Family Recipe and Its Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a dish that someone in your life makes (or made), its ingredients, normal cooking tools, paper and a
pen, an index card or a sheet of heavy paper

**Nice to have:** a phone to call a relative, an old photo, colored pencils

### The big idea
Every family has a dish that is never written down quite right: "a handful of this, cook it till it smells done."
Your mission is to capture one before it disappears. You will cook it, turn the vague bits into a real recipe, and
write the story that goes with it, so someone fifty years from now can make it too.

### Goals
- The dish, cooked by you, on the table.
- A finished recipe card with exact amounts plus a short story of where the dish came from.

### Objectives
- I can turn vague directions into measured amounts by testing and noting.
- I can interview someone with open questions and pull out a good story.
- I can write a recipe clear enough for a stranger to follow.

### Get ready
Choose a dish someone in your life knows by heart: a sauce, a bread, a soup, a holiday plate, or a weeknight
standby. Check the kitchen has the ingredients. Ask a grown-up which pans and burners are fine to use.

### Steps
1. Interview the keeper of the recipe (15 minutes), in person or by phone. Ask: "Who taught you this? When did you
   first make it? What goes wrong if you rush it?" Write down their exact words.
2. Ask them to walk you through it while you write. Every time they say "some" or "a little," ask them to show you or
   guess in spoons or cups.
3. Measure as you cook. Before each ingredient goes in, measure it and write it down. If you add more later, write that too.
4. Taste at three points and note what changes. These notes become the "how you know it's ready" lines.
5. Write the card. Ingredients in order of use, numbered steps, oven or burner settings, and a "you'll know it's
   done when..." line.
6. Write the story (15 minutes): 150 to 250 words on the back. Lead with a moment, not a date. Example: "My
   grandmother never used a timer. She listened to the onions."

### Make it yours
- Make a "remix" version: same soul, one new twist, and write a card for that too.
- Draw the dish or the person who taught it on the card's front.
- Start a family cookbook: a folder with this card as page one and blank pages for the next.

### Stuck?
- Nobody is around to interview: pick the dish you make most often and write your own story with it.
- The amounts still feel like guesses: make a half batch first, then adjust.

### Show it off
Serve the dish at dinner and read the story aloud before anyone eats.

### Clean up
Wash every pan, wipe the stove and counters, and put the card somewhere it will survive (a page protector helps).

### For the grown-up
This builds practical cooking, measurement and fractions, interviewing and personal writing, and it often starts
family conversations that would not otherwise happen. Agree beforehand on which appliances are okay, and check
allergies for anyone eating. **Ask at dinner:** "What surprised you about how this recipe is really made?"

## 4. Legacy Card Game

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 40 to 60 index cards or cut paper rectangles, pens, an envelope or two, stickers or tape, a pencil

**Nice to have:** a sealed "do not open until..." envelope, colored markers, a few dice

### The big idea
In a legacy game, every play changes the game forever. Cards get written on, torn up or unlocked, and the box
remembers what happened last time. You are going to design one: a small card game with secret envelopes that open
after certain events, so the fifth game is nothing like the first.

### Goals
- A playable card game with a core loop that works in 15 minutes.
- At least three sealed envelopes that permanently change the rules when triggered.

### Objectives
- I can design a simple core rule and balance it through playtesting.
- I can write triggers and unlocks that build tension over many plays.
- I can explain how a game's history can tell a story.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Pick a theme in one minute: a rival bakery war, two space colonies, a haunted lighthouse, a dragon
heist. The theme decides your card names.

### Steps
1. Design the core loop (20 minutes). Keep it simple: each turn, draw one card and play one card. Cards score points,
   steal points, or block. First to 10 wins.
2. Make 30 starter cards: about 15 scorers (1 to 3 points), 8 stealers, 7 blockers. Name them for your theme.
3. Playtest twice, alone (playing both hands) or with anyone home. Fix anything that wins too often.
4. Write your envelopes (25 minutes). Each one has a trigger on the outside, like "Open when someone wins with
   exactly 10" or "Open after the third game." Inside: new cards, a rule change, or a sticker that permanently
   alters a card.
5. Add permanence. Give the winner of each game one action: write their name on a card to give it +1 forever, or
   tear one card in half. Write down the rule.
6. Make a "history card" where every winner and every opened envelope is logged.
7. Play a round or two and open your first envelope for real.

### Make it yours
- Make a final envelope that ends the campaign with a winner of the whole series.
- Add a story card for each envelope that reveals one chapter of a mystery.
- Make it cooperative: both players fight the deck, and the deck gets meaner with every loss.

### Stuck?
- One card is too strong: raise its cost or add a rule that it must be discarded after use.
- Unlocks feel random: tie each one to something players care about, like a comeback or a perfect game.

### Show it off
Teach it to someone at home after dinner and play game one. Promise them the envelopes will make game two very different.

### Clean up
Rubber-band the deck, keep envelopes sealed in a box, and store the history card on top.

### For the grown-up
This is systems design: probability, balance, testing and fixing, plus storytelling across sessions. It is a great
game to play together over a whole break. **Ask at dinner:** "What's inside the envelope you hope we open first?"

## 5. Still Life Shading Study

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** three objects (an egg, a mug, a crumpled paper bag or a piece of fruit), a desk lamp or a flashlight,
plain paper, a regular pencil, an eraser, a tissue or cotton swab

**Nice to have:** drawing pencils (2H, 2B, 6B), a kneaded eraser, a sheet of toned or brown paper and a white pencil

### The big idea
A pencil can make a plain egg look like a museum piece. The secret is light: once you can see where it hits, where
it rolls away and where it bounces back, flat drawings turn into solid objects. Old painters called this
**chiaroscuro**, the drama of light and dark. Set up one lamp, turn off the rest, and make something look real.

### Goals
- A finished shaded drawing of a three-object still life lit from one side.
- A value scale you made yourself, showing seven steps from white to black.

### Objectives
- I can find the highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow on an object.
- I can make smooth value changes with layers instead of pressing hard.
- I can arrange objects for a strong composition.

### Get ready
Darken the room. Place the lamp low and to one side, about 45 degrees from your objects. Sit so the light falls
across, not straight on. Squint: everything should split into clear light and dark shapes.

### Steps
1. Make a value scale (10 minutes): seven boxes in a row, from paper white to as dark as your pencil goes. Build
   darks by layering light strokes, not by pressing hard.
2. Arrange your still life. Overlap at least two objects. Put the tallest one off-center.
3. Block in shapes lightly (10 minutes). Draw the big outlines and the shape of each cast shadow. Shadows are
   shapes, so draw them like objects.
4. Map the values. On each object, find these five: highlight, light side, core shadow (the darkest band on the
   object), reflected light (a faint glow near the shadowed edge), cast shadow.
5. Shade in layers (30 minutes). Start with mid tones over everything in shadow, then go darker, then darkest. Blend
   gently with a tissue only where you want softness.
6. Lift highlights with the eraser. The brightest spot is often just clean paper.
7. Step back three meters and squint. Darken anything that looks too timid.

### Make it yours
- Use toned paper: shade with pencil, then add highlights with a white pencil for a dramatic result.
- Make it strange: a lit egg next to a toy dinosaur and a teacup, and give the drawing a story title.
- Do a 5-minute, 2-minute and 30-second version and compare which captures the light best.

### Stuck?
- It looks flat: your darks are not dark enough. Push the core shadow and cast shadow harder.
- Everything is smudgy: rest your hand on a scrap sheet and work from top left to bottom right (top right if left-handed).

### Show it off
Prop the drawing next to the real setup with the lamp still on and let your family compare.

### Clean up
Turn the lights back on, return the objects, and put the drawing between two clean sheets so it does not smear.

### For the grown-up
This is the core of observational drawing taught in high school and early college art: value, form and
composition. It trains patient looking that carries into any visual work. **Ask at dinner:** "Where is the
reflected light on this plate right now?"

## 6. Kitchen Chemistry: Butter and Fresh Cheese

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 75-105 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 1 cup heavy cream, a jar with a tight lid, 4 cups whole milk (not ultra-pasteurized), 3 tablespoons
lemon juice or white vinegar, salt, a pot, a spoon, a thermometer if you have one, a strainer, a clean thin cloth or
paper towels, two bowls

**Nice to have:** herbs, garlic, pepper, crackers or bread

### The big idea
Milk and cream are full of hidden structure: fat globules wrapped in membranes, and proteins floating in balance.
With nothing but shaking, heat and acid you will break that structure two different ways and end up with real
butter and real fresh cheese. It is chemistry you can spread on toast.

### Goals
- A small pat of homemade butter and a bowl of fresh cheese.
- A clear explanation of what broke apart in each and why.

### Objectives
- I can describe an emulsion and what happens when it breaks.
- I can use heat and acid to make proteins clump (curdle) on purpose.
- I can record observations at each stage like a lab notebook.

### Get ready
Take the cream out of the fridge 20 minutes early so it is cool, not cold. Wash the jar, pot and strainer. Line the
strainer with the cloth and set it over a bowl. Draw a two-column page: "What I see" and "What is happening."

### Steps
1. **Butter.** Fill the jar no more than halfway with cream, close tight, and shake hard (10 to 15 minutes). Note
   each stage: liquid, whipped cream, a sudden slosh. That slosh is the moment.
2. The emulsion broke. Fat globules have smashed together and the leftover liquid is real buttermilk. Pour it off
   and save it for pancakes.
3. Rinse the butter under cold water, pressing with a spoon, until the water runs clear. Leftover buttermilk spoils
   butter fast. Salt it to taste.
4. **Cheese.** Heat the milk in the pot over medium, stirring so it does not scorch, until it steams and tiny bubbles
   form at the edge (about 185 F or 85 C).
5. Turn off the heat and stir in the acid one tablespoon at a time. Watch: white curds separate from yellow-green whey.
6. Wait 10 minutes, then pour it through the cloth. Let it drain 15 to 30 minutes. Shorter is creamy, longer is firm.
7. Salt and season your cheese. Taste butter and cheese side by side on bread.

### Make it yours
- Run a variable test: split the milk and curdle half with lemon, half with vinegar. Which tastes and feels better?
- Make compound butter with herbs, garlic, honey or citrus zest and roll it in plastic wrap into a log.
- Press the cheese under a plate and a can for an hour for a firm slice you can pan-fry.

### Stuck?
- The cream will not turn: it is too cold or the jar is too full. Warm it slightly and pour some out.
- No curds form: the milk was not hot enough or it is ultra-pasteurized. Reheat and add one more spoon of acid.

### Show it off
Set out a tasting board with your butter, cheese and bread, and explain the chemistry in one minute before anyone eats.

### Clean up
Rinse milk out of the pot right away (it sticks), wash the cloth, and refrigerate both products. Use within 4 days.

### For the grown-up
This is food chemistry at the level of high school chemistry and biology: emulsions, protein denaturation and pH.
It uses the stove, so agree on that first. Check allergies (dairy) before anyone tastes. **Ask at dinner:** "Why did
the cream turn into butter all of a sudden instead of slowly?"

## 7. Cover Song in a New Genre

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a song you know by heart, paper and a pen, your voice, anything to make sound (a table to drum on,
pots, a comb and paper, a shaker of rice)

**Nice to have:** any instrument in the house, a phone to record a voice memo, a friend or sibling to harmonize

### The big idea
Take a song everyone knows and drag it into a completely different world: a breakup ballad turned into a sea shanty,
a birthday song as a slow blues, a pop hit as a march. Great covers prove a song's bones are strong. Your job is to
find those bones and build something new on them.

### Goals
- A full performance of a familiar song transformed into a different genre.
- A one-page "arrangement chart" showing your choices for tempo, feel and instruments.

### Objectives
- I can name the features that define a genre: tempo, rhythm feel, instruments and vocal style.
- I can change a song's arrangement while keeping its melody recognizable.
- I can perform a full arrangement from start to finish.

### Get ready
Pick a song you can sing all the way through without notes. Choose a genre that feels like the opposite: fast to
slow, happy to haunting, electric to acoustic. Find a room where you can be loud for an hour.

### Steps
1. Map the song (10 minutes). Write its sections in order: verse, chorus, verse, bridge, chorus. Mark where it builds.
2. Study the target genre. List four features. A sea shanty, for example: call and response, stomping beat, group
   chorus, no instruments needed.
3. Change the **tempo**. Clap the old beat, then the new one. Slower songs can feel sadder; faster can turn sad words funny.
4. Change the **feel**. Try straight beats, a swing (long-short), or a waltz (count in three). Pick the one that
   surprises you most.
5. Build the sound (20 minutes). Pick two homemade instruments: a pot for kick drum, rice for shaker, a table for
   snare. Sing over your own beat.
6. Rewrite one small thing: an intro, a key line spoken instead of sung, a new ending. Write it on the chart.
7. Rehearse three full runs. On the last, do not stop for mistakes.

### Make it yours
- Do three versions of the same chorus in three genres and let the family vote.
- Write a new verse that fits the new genre's world, like a shanty verse about a ship.
- Make it a duet: one person sings the original style, the other answers in the new one.

### Stuck?
- It just sounds like the original: change two things at once, like tempo and feel.
- You lose the melody: hum the original chorus once, then sing the new version right after.

### Show it off
Announce the song with a deadpan intro ("Tonight, a traditional lullaby"), then perform. Record a voice memo if you want to keep it.

### Clean up
Put the pots and shakers back and keep your arrangement chart with your other music.

### For the grown-up
This is arranging, a real music skill: form, tempo, meter and genre. It also builds stage confidence. Expect some
noise. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you have to change most to make it fit the new genre?"

## 8. Radio Drama with Foley Sound

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper and a pen, household sound makers (celery or a cabbage, a pair of shoes, a box of cornstarch or
flour in a bag, cooking pots, a sheet of paper, an umbrella, gloves, keys, a bowl of water)

**Nice to have:** a phone to record, family members or friends as extra voices, a blanket to build a sound booth

### The big idea
Before television, millions of people crowded around radios for stories told only with voices and sound effects.
The sound artists, called **Foley artists**, made horse hooves with coconut shells and broken bones with celery.
You are going to write and perform a ten-minute audio drama where every sound comes from things in your house.

### Goals
- A finished script of about four pages with sound cues marked.
- A live or recorded performance with at least eight Foley effects.

### Objectives
- I can tell a story using only sound, dialogue and narration.
- I can create convincing sound effects from everyday objects.
- I can write a script with clear cues so performers know when to act.

### Get ready
Gather possible sound makers on a table. Spend 10 minutes playing: crunch, shake, drip, slap. Write down every sound
that surprised you. Those are your toolkit.

### Steps
1. Pick a genre that loves sound: a storm at a lighthouse, a creaky haunted hotel, a heist, a space station alarm.
2. Plan three scenes, each with a clear place the listener can hear (rain on a roof, footsteps on gravel).
3. Write the script (30 minutes). Use this format: character name in capitals, then their line. Sound cues in
   brackets: [SFX: Door creaks open]. Example: "MARA: Did you hear that? [SFX: Three slow footsteps above.]"
4. Write for the ear. Characters should say names and places out loud ("Run, Theo, to the boathouse!") because the
   audience cannot see.
5. Build your Foley (20 minutes). Ideas: snapping celery for bones, cornstarch squeezed in a bag for footsteps in
   snow, a flapping umbrella for wings, gloves flapped for birds, keys for jail cells.
6. Rehearse with sound and voices together. If you are solo, play every part, using a different voice for each.
7. Perform it live behind a sheet or blanket, or record it in one take.

### Make it yours
- Make it an old-time radio show with a fake sponsor jingle for an imaginary product.
- Write a cliffhanger ending and promise episode two tomorrow.
- Perform it in total darkness for the audience, with only a flashlight on your script.

### Stuck?
- A sound does not work: listen with your eyes closed. If it is not clear, add a line of dialogue that names it.
- Too many parts for one person: cut to two characters plus a narrator.

### Show it off
Turn off the lights after dinner and play or perform it. The audience must keep their eyes closed.

### Clean up
Return the pots and shoes, compost the celery, and sweep any cornstarch.

### For the grown-up
This is drama, script writing and sound design, and it builds voice work, timing and creative problem solving.
Recording is optional; it works as a live show. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest sound to fake?"

## 9. Bike or Walk Route Scout

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 75-120 min | Outdoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a bike and helmet or good walking shoes, paper, a pencil, a clipboard or a book to press on, water, a watch or phone for time

**Nice to have:** a paper street map of your area, colored pencils, a friend to scout with

### The big idea
Cities pay planners to figure out the best routes for bikes and walkers: the quiet streets, the steep hills, the
spot where everyone gets stuck at a crossing. Today you are the scout. Ride or walk a loop near home, rate every
stretch, and turn it into a hand-drawn route map someone else could follow.

### Goals
- A completed loop of 3 to 10 km (bike) or 2 to 5 km (walking) that starts and ends at home.
- A hand-drawn map rating each stretch for safety, effort and scenery.

## 11. Grid Coordinate Treasure Hunt

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 2+ |

**Materials:** graph paper or paper you rule into a grid, a pencil, a ruler, a tape measure or a long string marked
every 50 cm, a small "treasure" (a snack, a note, a coin), scrap paper for clues, a partner to hunt (a sibling, a
friend over, or a grown-up after work)

**Nice to have:** a compass, chalk or sticks to mark the axes, envelopes

### The big idea
You are setting a hunt only a mathematician could crack. You will turn your room, yard or hallway into a coordinate
plane, hide the treasure at a secret point, and leave clues that use real math: distances, reflections, slopes. Your
hunter has to solve their way there.

### Goals
- A measured coordinate grid of a real space, with an origin and labeled axes.
- A chain of five to seven math clues that lead to one exact point.

### Objectives
- I can map a real space onto a coordinate plane with a consistent scale.
- I can write clues using distance, midpoint, reflection and slope.
- I can check that every clue has exactly one answer.

### Get ready
Pick a space at least 3 m by 3 m. Choose a corner as the origin (0, 0) and mark it. Decide your scale: 1 unit = 50 cm
works well. Mark a few points along the x-axis and y-axis with tape, sticks or chalk.

### Steps
1. Draw your space on paper to scale (10 minutes): walls, furniture, trees. Label the axes.
2. Pick a secret treasure point, like (6, 4). Hide the treasure there.
3. Work backward to write the clue chain (25 minutes). Each clue gives the next point. Examples: "Start at the
   midpoint of (0, 2) and (4, 6)." "Reflect that point across the line y = x." "Walk a slope of 1/2 for 4 units right."
4. Mix in real-world clues: "Find the point exactly 5 units from the origin on the line y = 3."
5. Solve your own hunt from start to finish on paper. Fix any clue with two answers.
6. Hide the clues in envelopes along the path, or hand them over one at a time when the hunter reaches each point.
7. Run the hunt. Hunter gets graph paper and a pencil. Time them.

### Make it yours
- Use a polar grid instead: clues give an angle and distance from the origin, like a ship's navigator.
- Add a decoy point that looks right if someone makes a common mistake.
- Make the final clue a system of two equations whose solution is the treasure point.

### Stuck?
- The scale is off: measure one long side again and recalculate units.
- Your hunter is completely stuck: offer one hint card per clue, each costing them a minute on the clock.

### Show it off
Post the times on the fridge and challenge the family to beat the record.

### Clean up
Pull up tape, collect markers and envelopes, and keep your map for a rematch.

### For the grown-up
This uses geometry and algebra (distance, midpoint, reflections, slopes, systems) in a hands-on way. If outdoors, set
clear limits on the space. **Ask at dinner:** "Which clue was hardest to write so it had only one answer?"

## 12. All-Dialogue Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper or a notebook, a pen, a highlighter or second pen

**Nice to have:** a timer, a friend or family member to read it aloud with you

### The big idea
No narration. No "she said." No description of the room. Just two people talking, and the reader still knows where
they are, who they are, what they want and what just went wrong. It is one of the hardest constraints in writing, and
when it works it feels like eavesdropping on something you should not hear.

### Goals
- A finished short story, 600 to 1,200 words, told only in dialogue.
- A reader who can tell you the setting and the secret without being told.

### Objectives
- I can reveal setting, character and conflict only through what people say.
- I can use subtext, where characters mean more than they say.
- I can give two characters voices so different that tags are not needed.

### Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Write at the top of the page: Who are they? Where are they? What does each one want? What are they
not saying?

### Steps
1. Choose your two characters (10 minutes). Give each a want that clashes with the other's. Example: two astronauts
   on a station, one wants to go home, the other knows they cannot.
2. Write a voice sheet: three habits for each speaker. One talks in short bursts and changes the subject; the other
   asks a lot of questions and uses formal words.
3. Plan the hidden thing. One character knows something the other does not.
4. Draft (30 minutes). Start in the middle of a conversation. Plant the setting through detail: "Stop tapping the
   glass. Nothing's out there but stars."
5. Build tension through dodges, interruptions and silence ("..." or a short line that does not answer).
6. End on a line that lands the reveal or changes everything. Do not explain it.
7. Highlight any line that is there only to explain. Cut it or rewrite it so it sounds like a person talking.

### Make it yours
- Make it a phone call, so one side is missing and the reader guesses the other half.
- Add exactly one line of narration at the very end and make it count.
- Write it as an interrogation where the suspect is cleverer than the detective.

### Stuck?
- You cannot tell who is talking: make one character use a nickname for the other or speak in a distinctive rhythm.
- It sounds stiff: read it aloud. Cut anything people would not actually say.

### Show it off
Do a table read with someone at home, each of you taking a part, after dinner.

### Clean up
Keep your voice sheet and draft together in your notebook.

### For the grown-up
This is advanced writing craft: subtext, characterization and economy, the same skills used in plays and screenplays.
**Ask at dinner:** "What was the secret in your story, and how did you hide it?"

## 13. Tensegrity Floating Table

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** stiff cardboard (a box), strong string or thread, scissors, a ruler, a pencil, tape, a hole punch or
a sharp pencil to poke holes

**Nice to have:** thin wood scraps or craft sticks, fishing line, a craft knife and cutting mat, a glue gun

### The big idea
A tensegrity table looks impossible: the top floats above the base, held up by what looks like a few loose strings.
It works because of **tension** and **compression** in balance. One string pulls up, others pull down, and the
whole thing locks. Build one small enough to fit in your hand, then try to make it hold a mug.

### Goals
- A working tensegrity table where the top seems to float.
- A load test showing how much weight it can hold before tipping.

### Objectives
- I can explain how tension and compression can balance in a structure.
- I can measure and adjust string lengths to make a stable structure.
- I can test a design and improve it based on how it fails.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Cut two cardboard squares, each 12 cm by 12 cm (stack three layers and tape them for stiffness). Cut
two L-shaped arms, each about 10 cm tall and 6 cm long at the foot.

### Steps
1. Glue or tape one L-arm to the base so it rises from one edge and hooks over the center. Tape the other L-arm
   upside down to the top square, so it hangs down toward the center.
2. Poke a small hole near each corner of both squares and at the tip of each arm.
3. Tie the **main tension string** (15 minutes): from the tip of the base arm to the tip of the top arm, about 3 cm
   long. The top should now hang from the base arm with the two arms overlapping.
4. Tie four corner strings from each corner of the base to the matching corner of the top. Pull them just tight
   enough that the top stops tilting.
5. Balance it. Shorten or lengthen strings a few millimeters at a time until the top sits level.
6. Load test (15 minutes). Add coins or small objects to the center. Note where it fails: does a string slip, does a
   corner sag, or does it twist?
7. Fix the weak point and test again.

### Make it yours
- Scale it up with wood scraps or sturdy cardboard tubes and try to support a book.
- Build a three-level tower where each level floats on the one below.
- Hide the strings with fishing line so it looks like magic.

### Stuck?
- The top flips over: the main string is too long. Shorten it so the arms overlap more.
- It wobbles: the corner strings are uneven. Measure them all with a ruler and match their lengths.

### Show it off
Set a cup of water on it at dinner and ask everyone how they think it stays up before you explain.

### Clean up
Trim loose ends, recycle cardboard scraps, and put the scissors away.

### For the grown-up
This builds physics (forces, tension, compression) and engineering problem solving through testing and fixing. If
they use a craft knife or glue gun, a cutting mat and a clear table help. **Ask at dinner:** "Which string is doing
the most work?"

## 14. Model Lung

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a clear plastic bottle (about 1 to 2 liters), two balloons, a straw, tape, scissors, a rubber band,
play dough or tape to seal, a ruler

**Nice to have:** a Y-shaped connector or two straws, a marker, a second bottle for a comparison model

### The big idea
You never think about breathing, but it is a pressure trick. Your diaphragm pulls down, the space in your chest gets
bigger, the pressure drops, and air rushes in. You will build a working model of a lung and then use it to show
what happens in asthma, a collapsed lung and a deep breath.

### Goals
- A working model where a balloon "lung" inflates when you pull the diaphragm.
- At least two modified models that show how a lung problem changes breathing.

### Objectives
- I can explain how the diaphragm changes pressure in the chest.
- I can build a model that shows cause and effect.
- I can describe the limits of a model compared with a real lung.

### Get ready
Cut the bottom 5 cm off the bottle. Clear a counter. Lay out the balloons, straw and tape.

### Steps
1. Make the lung. Slide a balloon over one end of the straw and tape it airtight.
2. Push the straw through the bottle neck so the balloon hangs inside. Seal the neck around the straw with play dough
   or tape so no air leaks.
3. Make the diaphragm. Tie a knot in the second balloon and cut off the top (wide) half. Stretch it over the open
   bottom of the bottle and hold it with a rubber band.
4. Pull the knot down gently. The lung balloon inflates. Push up and it deflates. Write down why in one sentence.
5. Measure (15 minutes). Pull the diaphragm 1, 2 and 3 cm and estimate how much the lung grows each time.
6. Model a problem. **Asthma:** squeeze the straw slightly to narrow it. **Collapsed lung:** poke a small hole in
   the bottle and seal it, then unseal it. What happens to the lung each time?
7. Build two lungs. Add a second straw and balloon for a two-lung model.

### Make it yours
- Model a cough: pull hard, then push up fast and feel the air rush out of the straw.
- Compare bottle sizes: does a bigger chest make a bigger breath?
- Invent a "lung of the future" with a fix for one of the problems you modeled.

### Stuck?
- The lung does not inflate: there is a leak. Press play dough firmly around the neck and check the rubber band.
- The diaphragm balloon tears: use a piece of a plastic bag and tape it on tightly.

### Show it off
Give a two-minute demo to the family: show a healthy breath, then show asthma, and explain the difference.

### Clean up
Recycle the bottle or keep the model, and toss balloon scraps in the trash (not where pets can get them).

### For the grown-up
This is biology and physics together: anatomy, air pressure and how models help and mislead. Watch for latex
allergies with balloons. **Ask at dinner:** "What happens to your breathing when you get a stitch in your side?"

## 16. Fermi Problem Showdown

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Nice to have:** a bag of rice or dry beans, a measuring cup, a friend or family member to compete

### The big idea
How many grains of rice would fill your bedroom? How many heartbeats have you had? The physicist Enrico Fermi could
estimate wild things like this in minutes by breaking them into smaller guesses he could reason through. Today you
will tackle five outrageous questions and see how close a clever estimate can get.

### Goals
- Five finished estimates, each with a clear chain of reasoning.
- One estimate checked with a real measurement.

### Objectives
- I can break a big question into smaller ones I can estimate.
- I can use powers of ten to keep numbers manageable.
- I can check whether an answer is within a factor of ten.

### Get ready

   in one cup, and convert.

### Stuck?
- You have no idea where to start: ask "what would I need to know to answer this?" and write those things down.
- Numbers are huge: write them in powers of ten and add exponents when you multiply.

### Show it off
Read one question at dinner and let everyone guess before you reveal your reasoning and answer.

### Clean up
Put the rice back and keep your estimate sheets.

### For the grown-up
This is estimation and scientific reasoning used by physicists and engineers, plus scientific notation and unit

### Objectives
- I can explain a thought experiment and the question it tests.
- I can argue a position I do not agree with.
- I can ask follow-up questions that push an argument further.

### Get ready
Set up a "stage": two chairs facing the audience. Write each thought experiment on a card in 3 or 4 sentences.

### Steps
1. Write your deck (20 minutes). Classics: **The Ship of Theseus**, **The Experience Machine** (a perfect
   simulation), **The Trolley Problem** (steer a runaway trolley), **The Teleporter** (if a machine copies you and
   destroys the original, did you travel?). Invent at least two of your own.
2. Set rules: each round, draw a card, read it, then two players argue opposite sides for two minutes each.
3. Add a twist card after each round: "The ship's old planks were used to build a second ship." Players must respond.
4. Vote. The audience moves to one side of the room or the other. Then swap sides and argue again.
5. Host it (40 minutes). Play the part of a dramatic host. Keep time strictly.
6. End with the "mind changer" award: who switched their opinion the most?

### Make it yours
- Make it a courtroom: one person is the judge, and evidence is anything said earlier in the night.
- Run a "future dinner party" where each person plays a famous thinker from history.
- Write thought experiments about your own house: "If you swapped every item in your room, is it still your room?"

### Stuck?
- Nobody wants to argue: give points for the most creative argument, not the right one.
- The debate gets heated: call a 30-second "agree on one thing" pause.

### Show it off
Invite a grown-up to judge one round and give a ruling.

### Clean up
Stack the cards, put the chairs back, and keep the deck for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds philosophy, logic and debate skills: building and testing arguments, listening and changing your mind.
**Ask at dinner:** "Which thought experiment do you still disagree with yourself about?"

## 20. Design Your Own Typeface

### The big idea
Every letter you have ever read was designed by someone. Type designers decide the height, weight, curves and
personality of every letter. You are going to invent a complete alphabet with its own voice, maybe sharp and
futuristic, maybe soft and handmade, and then use it to set a phrase that could only look right in your type.

### Goals
- A full 26-letter alphabet (capitals or lowercase) in your own consistent style.
- A sample phrase set in your typeface, like a title for a book or poster.

### Objectives
- I can use guidelines (baseline, x-height, cap height) to keep letters consistent.
- I can design repeated parts so the alphabet looks like a family.
- I can space letters by eye so words read smoothly.

### Get ready

- Spacing looks off: flip the page upside down so you see shapes, not words.

### Show it off

### Clean up
Keep your alphabet sheet flat in a folder.

### For the grown-up
This builds design, precision and visual consistency, the same thinking used in graphic design and typography.

## 21. Steady-Hand Circuit Game

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a flashlight that takes 2 AA or D batteries (or 2 loose AA batteries and the bulb from an old
flashlight), aluminum foil, tape, a wire coat hanger or a long piece of stiff wire, cardboard, scissors, paper clips

**Nice to have:** insulated wire with bare ends, a small buzzer or LED from an old toy, electrical tape

### The big idea
You know the game: guide a loop along a twisty wire without touching it, or the buzzer screams. You are going to
build it from scratch, which means building a real circuit with a switch that you trigger by mistake. Along the way
you will learn why a circuit needs a complete loop and what an open or closed switch really is.

### Goals
- A working game where touching the wire lights the bulb (or sounds a buzzer).
- A circuit diagram on paper using real symbols.

### Objectives
- I can build a complete circuit with a power source, a load and a switch.
- I can draw the circuit with standard symbols.
- I can troubleshoot an open circuit by testing each connection.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Cut a cardboard base about 30 cm by 15 cm. Straighten the coat hanger and bend a 2 cm loop at one end.
Cut the rest into a 40 cm twisty track (or use foil rolled into a stiff rope).

### Steps
1. Test the power. Make a quick circuit: foil strip from the top of a battery stack to the bulb's base tip, bulb's
   metal side back to the bottom battery. It should light. That is a **closed circuit**.
2. Make wires from foil (10 minutes): fold strips 2 cm wide into thin ropes and tape them along the cardboard.
3. Build the track. Poke the ends of the twisty wire into the cardboard and tape one end to a foil wire leading to
   the battery.
4. Make the wand. Bend a loop around the track, then connect the wand's handle end to the bulb with a long foil wire
   so it can move freely.
5. Finish the loop: bulb back to the other end of the batteries. Now touching wand to track closes the circuit and
   lights the bulb.
6. Draw the circuit (10 minutes): battery, wire, switch (wand and track), bulb. Use real symbols.
7. Play. Time each run. Make the track harder with tighter curves if it is too easy.

### Make it yours
- Add a "safe zone" at the start and end made of tape so the wand can rest.
- Build two tracks in parallel and race a friend; whoever lights up first loses.
- Make the bulb a story: the track is a laser corridor, and the light means the alarm.

### Stuck?
- Nothing lights: check each joint. Press foil firmly onto metal and tape it.
- Bulb stays on: the wand is touching the track at rest. Bend the loop wider.

### Show it off
Challenge the family to beat your time after dinner, with a "laser corridor" story.

### Clean up
Take the batteries out so they do not drain, and recycle foil scraps.

### For the grown-up
This is basic electricity from physics class: circuits, switches and troubleshooting. Use only AA, C or D batteries,
never button or coin cells, and never anything from a wall outlet. If foil touches both battery ends directly with no
bulb, it gets warm fast, so stop and disconnect. **Ask at dinner:** "What makes a circuit open or closed?"

### The big idea
Picture books look simple, but every word and picture is chosen with care. You are going to make one for a specific

## 23. Musical Theater Number

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper and a pen, a room with space to move, your voice, a table or pots for rhythm

**Nice to have:** a piano or keyboard, a hat or scarf as a prop, a sibling or friend for a duet

### The big idea
In a musical, characters sing when talking is not enough. The **"I want" song** is where the hero tells the audience
what they want more than anything. Write one for an original character in an original show: words, tune and staging,
all ready for a living room premiere.

### Goals
- A finished song of about two minutes with verses and a chorus.
- A staged performance with movement and at least one prop.

### Objectives
- I can write lyrics that reveal a character's goal and feelings.
- I can build a melody that rises toward the most important line.
- I can stage a song with movement that tells the story.

### Get ready
Invent your show in two minutes: a title, a setting and a hero. Example: "Late Shift," set in a 24-hour laundromat,
where a night worker dreams of being an astronaut.

### Steps
1. Write the hero's want in one sentence: "I want to leave this town and see the ocean."
2. Write the song's arc: verse one (where they are now), chorus (the want), verse two (what stands in the way), final
   chorus (bigger and more desperate).
3. Write lyrics (25 minutes). Use concrete images from their world: "Folding someone else's shirts at 3 a.m." Rhyme
   at line ends but avoid forced rhymes.
4. Find the tune. Speak the chorus with feeling, then exaggerate the rise and fall of your voice until it becomes a
   melody. Put the highest note on the most important word.
5. Add a rhythm: tap the table or a pot. Musical theater loves a strong, simple beat.
6. Stage it (20 minutes). Plan three moments: where you start, a big gesture on the chorus, where you end. Use one
   prop (a laundry basket, a hat).
7. Rehearse until you can do it without looking at the lyrics.

### Make it yours
- Write the villain's song instead and make them likable.
- Make it a duet where two characters want opposite things and sing over each other in the last chorus.
- Write a reprise: the same chorus at the end of the show, sad or triumphant.

### Stuck?
- The melody is boring: change direction on the important word, jump up instead of stepping.
- Lyrics feel cheesy: swap feelings for specific details. Instead of "I'm sad," write what the character sees.

### Show it off
Set up chairs, announce the show, set the scene in one sentence, and perform. Bow.

### Clean up
Put the chairs and props back and keep your lyric sheet.

### For the grown-up
This builds songwriting, character work and performance confidence, combining music, writing and drama. **Ask at
dinner:** "What does your character want, and do they get it?"

### The big idea

## 25. Hand-Sewn Pillow

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** an old T-shirt, pillowcase, or fabric scraps, a needle, thread, scissors, pins or clips, a ruler,
chalk or a pencil, stuffing (old socks, fabric scraps, cotton balls, or an old pillow's filling)

**Nice to have:** embroidery thread, buttons, felt for appliqué, a thimble

### The big idea
Sewing by hand is how every piece of clothing was made for thousands of years, and it is still the skill that saves
a ripped hoodie at midnight. You will design and sew a pillow from an old shirt and give it a design that is all
yours: a monster face, a map, a giant donut, a band logo you invent.

### Goals
- A finished, stuffed pillow sewn by hand.
- A design added with appliqué, embroidery or fabric paint.

### Objectives
- I can thread a needle, tie a knot and sew a backstitch.
- I can measure and cut fabric with seam allowance.
- I can add a design that changes the look and story of the object.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Cut two squares from the shirt, each 30 cm by 30 cm (or any shape, plus 1.5 cm extra on each side for
the **seam allowance**). Thread a needle with about 60 cm of thread, doubled, and knot the end.

### Steps
1. Practice a **backstitch** on a scrap (10 minutes): come up, go back one stitch, come up one stitch ahead. It is
   strong and looks like a machine seam.
2. Design the front. Sketch it, then cut felt or fabric shapes. Sew them on with small stitches around the edges
   (**appliqué**), or stitch a design with thread.
3. Place the two squares face to face, design inside. Pin or clip the edges.
4. Sew around three sides and part of the fourth, 1.5 cm from the edge, with a backstitch (30 minutes). Leave a 10 cm gap.
5. Turn the pillow right side out through the gap. Push out the corners with a pencil.
6. Stuff it firmly, pushing filling into the corners first.
7. Close the gap with a **ladder stitch**, which hides inside the seam.

### Make it yours
- Make it shaped: a star, a fish, a cloud, or a tooth.
- Add a hidden pocket on the back for a note or a secret.
- Make a matching tiny version as a gift.

### Stuck?
- Thread keeps tangling: use shorter thread and let the needle dangle to untwist.
- The seam is puckered: your stitches are too tight. Smooth the fabric after every few stitches.

### Show it off
Give the pillow a name and a backstory, and introduce it at dinner.

### Clean up
Put needles back in a pincushion or box so no one steps on one, and collect thread scraps.

### For the grown-up
This builds hand skills, measuring and design, and a practical life skill. Count needles in and out to avoid any on
the floor. **Ask at dinner:** "What would you fix or make next with these stitches?"

### The big idea
For thousands of years, people learned the sky by drawing it. Astronomers before photography kept careful sketches of
the Moon, planets and comets. Tonight you will sit outside, let your eyes adjust, and sketch what you see like an

### Goals
- Two sky sketches: one wide view of a section of sky and one close-up of the Moon or a bright star cluster.

Take a grown-up outside and point out your invented constellation and tell its story.

### Clean up
Bring everything inside, fold the blanket, and keep your sketches in order by date.

### For the grown-up

## 28. Artifact from a Lost Civilization

Wipe the table, wash bowls and brushes, and turn the oven off.

### For the grown-up

## 29. Trading Economy Game

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper or index cards, pens, small items for goods (dried beans, buttons, paper clips, coins, pasta),
a timer, two to five players (siblings, friends over, or the family after dinner)

**Nice to have:** a die, envelopes for secret goals, colored paper for different currencies

### The big idea
Why does a bag of rice cost less than a diamond? Why do prices jump when something is scarce? You will design a
trading game where players run small island nations, each rich in some goods and short on others. Prices are not
fixed; they move with supply and demand. Can you get rich, or will a sudden storm wreck your plans?

### Goals
- A playable trading game with resources, secret goals and events.
- At least one full game played with real negotiation.

### Objectives
- I can explain supply and demand using examples from the game.
- I can design rules that make trading necessary.
- I can negotiate and adjust my strategy as conditions change.

### Get ready
Pick four goods and assign items: beans are grain, buttons are cloth, paper clips are iron, pasta is spice. Make one
"island card" per player.

### Steps

   A has 12 grain, 2 cloth, 1 iron, 3 spice.
2. Write secret goal cards. Each player needs a mix to "build" things: a ship needs 4 iron, 3 cloth, 2 spice.
3. Write event cards: "Storm: lose half your grain." "Spice festival: spice is worth double." "Trade ban for one round."
4. Set rules: each round, draw one event card, then trade freely for 3 minutes, then each player builds what they can.
5. Play (40 minutes). Watch prices: how much grain does one iron cost? Write it down each round.
6. After the game, look at your price notes. What happened when something became scarce?

### Make it yours
- Add a bank and loans with interest, and see who goes bankrupt.
- Add a currency players can print, then an event that makes it worthless.
- Make it cooperative: all islands must build one lighthouse together before the storm season.

### Stuck?
- Nobody trades: make goals impossible without trading.
- One player gets too rich too fast: add an event that taxes the richest island.

### Show it off
Host a game night and post the final rich list on the fridge.

### Clean up
Sort goods back into containers and put cards in an envelope.

### For the grown-up
This builds economics, negotiation, math and game design. Players get a real feel for supply and demand. If little kids join,
skip buttons and paper clips and use pasta. **Ask at dinner:** "What made prices change in your game?"

## 30. Pollinator Patch

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 75-120 min | Outdoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a notebook, a pencil, a trowel or big spoon, a few pots or containers with drainage holes (or a patch
of ground), soil, seeds from the spice cabinet (coriander, dill, mustard or fennel seeds often sprout), a shallow dish,
pebbles, water

**Nice to have:** native wildflower seeds, a magnifying glass, a field guide to insects

### The big idea
Bees, butterflies and hoverflies keep a third of our food growing, and many of them are in trouble. You are going
to scout your area for pollinators, find out what they visit, and plant a small patch built just for them. Even a pot
on a balcony can become a pollinator rest stop.

### Goals
- A 20-minute pollinator survey with notes on every visitor you see.
- A planted patch or pot and a "puddling station" where insects can drink.

### Objectives
- I can observe and record insects and the flowers they visit.
- I can choose plants based on what pollinators need: food, water and shelter.
- I can design a habitat for a specific purpose.

### Get ready
Tell a grown-up where you will be. Pick a sunny spot: a corner of a yard, a balcony, or a sunny windowsill box. Check
the spice cabinet for whole seeds and ask if you can use a few.

### Steps
1. Do the survey (20 minutes). Sit near flowers or a weedy area. For each visitor, note type (bee, wasp, fly,
   butterfly), color and what flower it chose. Keep your distance; most are calm if left alone.
2. Make a quick tally. Which flowers got the most visitors? What colors?
3. Plan the patch on paper: sun, soil and what to plant. Mix flower shapes and colors for different insects.
4. Plant (25 minutes). Fill pots or loosen soil. Sow seeds about twice as deep as they are wide. Water gently.
5. Build a puddling station: a shallow dish of pebbles and water, so insects can land and drink without drowning.
6. Add shelter: a small pile of sticks or a bundle of hollow stems in a sunny corner.
7. Make a care plan: water every day or two and check for sprouts in a week.

### Make it yours
- Make a pollinator field guide for your own block with drawings of each visitor.
- Design a "pollinator highway" map showing where patches could connect across your neighborhood.
- Paint a sign for the patch with the names of the visitors you hope to attract.

### Stuck?
- No pollinators around: it may be too cold or cloudy. Plant first and survey on a warm sunny day.
- Seeds do not sprout: spice seeds can be old. Try a different spice or ask a neighbor for a few cuttings.

### Show it off
Give the family a tour of the patch and your survey results, and set a date to check on it together.

### Clean up
Rinse the trowel, sweep spilled soil, and wash your hands.

### For the grown-up
This builds ecology, observation and planning skills. If anyone has a bee or wasp allergy, keep them away during the
survey. Without a yard, the windowsill version works. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most common pollinator you
saw?"
