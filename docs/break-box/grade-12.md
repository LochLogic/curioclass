## 1. Fix Something Broken

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** one broken, non-electric thing (a wobbly chair, a drawer that sticks, ripped jeans, a cracked mug, a
toy with a snapped part, a backpack with a dead zipper), a screwdriver set, glue suitable for the material, tape,
needle and thread, sandpaper, a pencil and paper

**Nice to have:** wood glue and clamps, a bar of soap for sticky drawers, pliers, a sewing patch

### The big idea
Every house has a pile of things waiting for "someday." Today is someday. You are the repair shop: you will
diagnose what actually failed, plan a fix, and bring one thing back to life. Repair is a quiet superpower, and the
first fix is always the hardest.

### Goals
- One broken object, working again or better than before.
- A short repair log: what failed, why, and what you did.

### Objectives
- I can diagnose a failure by looking for the root cause, not just the symptom.
- I can choose the right fix and the right material for the job.
- I can test a repair and judge whether it will last.

### Get ready
Ask the household which broken thing they would most like back. Clear a work surface and lay down newspaper or an
old towel. Lay out tools so you can see them all.

### Steps
1. Inspect it (10 minutes). Take photos or sketch it if it comes apart. Ask: what moves, what should not, and where
   is the wear?
2. Find the root cause. A wobbly chair is often loose screws or a joint where old glue failed. A sticky drawer is
   usually swollen wood or a bent runner. A zipper that splits is usually a worn slider, not the teeth.
3. Plan the fix and write it down in three steps before touching anything.
4. Repair it (30 to 60 minutes). Chairs: remove the loose joint, scrape off old glue, apply wood glue, clamp or tie
   tight with string overnight. Drawers: sand the rubbing edge and rub soap on the runners. Jeans: patch from the inside
   and stitch around with a tight running stitch. Zippers: squeeze the slider gently with pliers.
5. Test it. Sit in the chair, slide the drawer 20 times, tug the seam.
6. Fill in your repair log: problem, cause, fix, test result, what you would do differently.

### Make it yours
- Add an upgrade: a visible mend in a bright color (the Japanese idea of making repairs beautiful), or a new handle.
- Open a family "repair desk" for the week and take two more jobs.
- Turn something unfixable into something new: a broken mug into a pen holder or a planter.

### Stuck?
- You cannot figure out the cause: move it slowly and watch where it gives.
- Glue will not hold: the surfaces were dirty or smooth. Scrape and sand them, then glue again.

### Show it off
Hand it back to its owner at dinner with your repair log, like a shop ticket.

### Clean up
Put every tool back where it lives, sweep sanding dust, and cap the glue.

### For the grown-up
This builds problem solving, practical engineering and independence. Keep it non-electrical; nothing that plugs in.
Tools are fine with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "What was really wrong with it, under the obvious problem?"

## 2. Story Told Backward

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper or a notebook, a pen, index cards or scrap paper, tape

**Nice to have:** a highlighter, a second reader

### The big idea
What if a story started with the ending, a broken vase, a stranger at the door, two people who will not speak, and
every scene after that moved backward in time until the reader finally saw the moment it all began? Reverse order
turns "what happens next?" into "how did it come to this?" and that question can be even more gripping.

### Goals
- A short story of five to seven scenes, told in reverse chronological order.
- A first scene that hooks and a final scene (the beginning) that lands like a reveal.

### Objectives
- I can structure a story out of time order and keep it clear.
- I can plant details early that gain meaning later.
- I can end on a beginning that recasts everything before it.

### Get ready
Get five to seven index cards. You will plan one scene per card, then shuffle their order.

### Steps
1. Brainstorm the end (10 minutes): a vivid, odd moment. Example: "Two old friends sit at opposite ends of a train
   car, both pretending not to see each other."
2. Plan the story forward first, one scene per card, from the start to that moment. What happened between them?
3. Flip the order. The story now opens with the last card.
4. Choose a time marker for each scene ("Three years earlier," a season, an age) so readers stay oriented.
5. Draft each scene (40 minutes), 150 to 250 words each. Every scene should answer one question and raise another.
6. Plant echoes: a line, an object, a gesture that appears in the opening and gets its meaning only in the last scene.
7. Make the last scene, the beginning, small and hopeful or small and ominous. Let readers connect it themselves.

### Make it yours
- Tell it through objects: each scene is the history of one item going back in time.
- Run two timelines at once: one forward, one backward, meeting in the middle.
- Write the whole thing in second person, as if the reader is living it in reverse.

### Stuck?
- It is confusing: make your time markers bigger and add one concrete detail in each scene that changes over time.
- The ending is flat: ask "what is the moment they could have chosen differently?" and end there.

### Show it off
Have someone read it, then ask them to tell you the story in forward order. See what they noticed.

### Clean up
Tape your cards into your notebook as a structure map.

### For the grown-up
This builds structure, foreshadowing and revision skills from high school and college writing courses, with a fun
challenge built in. **Ask at dinner:** "What happens at the very beginning of your story?"

## 3. Plan a Week of Meals, Cook One

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper and a pen, the fridge and pantry, normal cooking tools, ingredients for one meal

**Nice to have:** a cookbook, store flyers, a calculator

### The big idea
Pretend you have your own place next year and one rule: you cook five dinners a week without wasting food or going
broke. Chefs and home cooks beat this with a trick called **cook once, eat three times**: one batch of something that
becomes three different meals. Plan the week, then cook the anchor meal for the family tonight.

### Goals
- A five-dinner plan with a shopping list and cost estimate.
- One meal from the plan, cooked and served.

### Objectives
- I can plan meals that share ingredients to cut waste and cost.
- I can write a shopping list organized by store section.
- I can cook a full meal and time it so everything is ready together.

### Get ready
Take inventory: write down what is already in the fridge, freezer and pantry. Ask a grown-up about allergies,
tonight's budget and which appliances are okay.

### Steps
1. Choose an **anchor** (10 minutes): a big batch of one thing, like roasted chicken, a pot of beans, a tray of
   roasted vegetables, or seasoned rice.
2. Plan five dinners. Three use the anchor in different ways (example: roasted chicken night one, chicken tacos
   night two, chicken soup night four). Two are quick standbys (pasta, eggs, a grain bowl).
3. Write the shopping list by section: produce, dairy, protein, pantry. Cross off what you already have.
4. Estimate the cost. Guess prices from memory or ask a grown-up, then divide by the number of servings.
5. Cook the anchor meal (50 minutes). Read the full recipe first, prep everything, and start whatever takes longest.
6. Taste before serving. Salt, acid, a little fat, or something fresh usually fixes flat food.
7. Pack and label leftovers for night two.

### Make it yours
- Add a challenge: no more than 15 ingredients for the whole week.
- Plan a vegetarian week, or a week built around one cuisine.
- Make a "fridge rescue" night that uses whatever is left on Friday.

### Stuck?
- You cannot think of meals: list five things you like to eat, then find the one ingredient they share.
- The anchor takes too long: pick a faster one, like seasoned ground meat, beans or a big pot of grains.

### Show it off
Serve dinner and hand around the week's menu. Ask if anyone wants to be your "tenant" next week.

### Clean up
Wash everything, wipe the stove and counters, and label leftovers with the date.

### For the grown-up
This builds cooking, budgeting and planning, which are the skills for living on their own. Agree on the stove and
oven and check allergies before cooking. **Ask at dinner:** "What will this turn into on night two?"

## 4. Nature Documentary About Your Family

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper and a pen, a clipboard, comfortable shoes, a quiet voice

**Nice to have:** a pair of binoculars or two paper tubes taped together, a hat with a brim, a phone to record audio

### The big idea
"Here, in the warm glow of the refrigerator, the adult male searches for the last yogurt. He has not yet noticed it
is gone." Wildlife documentaries take ordinary animal behavior and make it epic with a hushed voice and sweeping
music. You are going to observe the humans in your home like a field biologist, then narrate a live documentary about them.

### Goals
- A field notebook with observations of at least three "species" in your home (family, pets, neighbors at a distance).
- A live, narrated five-minute documentary performance.

### Objectives
- I can observe and record behavior with specific, accurate details.
- I can write narration that uses a documentary voice: calm, dramatic, scientific.
- I can perform with timing and comic restraint.

### Get ready
Make a field notebook page for each species: name, habitat, diet, behavior. Tell your family you are observing them
for a project, so they act natural (or not).

### Steps
1. Observe (20 minutes). Pick a time when people are home: after work, cooking, in the evening. Note small,
   specific behaviors: the way someone hunts for their keys, the evening migration to the couch.
2. Name the species with a Latin-style name: "Homo sapiens snackus."
3. Write your narration (20 minutes). Use documentary language: "remarkable," "territory," "the season of homework."
   Example: "The young one is defending her territory, the bathroom, from all challengers."
4. Plan a **story arc**: a hunt, a rivalry, a triumph. Every good documentary has drama.
5. Rehearse your voice: slow, low and quiet, like you do not want to scare the animals.
6. Perform it live, narrating from behind the couch or doorway as someone does the thing. Or narrate from your notes
   while the family watches you act it out.

### Make it yours
- Narrate the backyard, a balcony or your street: birds, dogs, the mail carrier's route.
- Add a sound track: hum dramatic music during the chase scene.
- Write it as a nature special about your pet, from the pet's point of view.

### Stuck?
- Nothing interesting is happening: zoom in. The way someone makes tea is full of ritual.
- It feels mean: aim the jokes at yourself first, and make every species a little heroic.

### Show it off
Perform it at dinner and give each family member a "species card" with their observed traits.

### Clean up
Put the notebook away and return any borrowed props.

### For the grown-up
This builds observation, scientific vocabulary, comic writing and performance. Your teen will be watching closely, so
play along. **Ask at dinner:** "What behavior did you observe that surprised you most?"

## 5. Window Landscape Painting

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper (heavy paper or cardboard if using paint), any paint you have (watercolors, poster paint, or
acrylic), brushes, a cup of water, a palette or plate, paper towels, a pencil

**Nice to have:** an easel or a box to prop the paper, masking tape, colored pencils for detail

### The big idea
The view from one window changes every hour: morning light, clouds rolling in, streetlights at dusk. Painters like
the Impressionists painted the same view again and again just to catch light. You will paint the view from your
window with real landscape techniques, then decide how true or how strange to make it.

### Goals
- A finished landscape painting of the view from a window.
- A small "color study" of the same view done quickly in a different light.

### Objectives
- I can use atmospheric perspective: far things are lighter, cooler and less detailed.
- I can paint in layers, from big shapes to small details.
- I can mix colors to match what I see.

### Get ready
Choose your window and set up so the light falls on your paper, not in your eyes. Tape your paper to a board.
Squint at the view: what are the three or four big shapes?

### Steps
1. Sketch lightly (10 minutes): the horizon line, the big shapes (sky, buildings, trees, ground). Do not draw details.
2. Paint the sky first, from top to bottom, getting lighter near the horizon.
3. Paint the far background in pale, cool colors (blues, lavenders). This is **atmospheric perspective**.
4. Paint the middle ground with stronger colors and some detail.
5. Paint the foreground last (30 minutes) with the darkest darks and the most detail: leaves, window frames, fences.
6. Add light: a few bright touches where the sun hits.
7. Do a 10-minute color study of the same view on a scrap. Paint fast and loose.

### Make it yours
- Paint the view as it might look 500 years ago or 500 years from now.
- Paint it as if it is underwater, or after a snowstorm in the middle of summer.
- Include the window frame and part of your room, so the painting shows inside and outside.

### Stuck?
- Colors look muddy: wait for each layer to dry before painting on top, and rinse your brush more often.
- The view is boring: crop it. Paint one tree, one rooftop or one corner.

### Show it off
Hang the painting next to the window and let the family compare.

### Clean up
Rinse brushes until the water runs clear, dry them flat, and wipe the table.

### For the grown-up
This builds painting technique, observation and color mixing. Use acrylics on a covered surface; they do not wash out
of clothes. **Ask at dinner:** "What changed in the view while you were painting?"

## 6. Yogurt and Sourdough Starter

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 4 cups milk, 2 tablespoons plain yogurt with live cultures, a pot, a thermometer if you have one,
clean jars, a towel, flour (whole wheat or rye is best, but any works), water, a spoon, tape and a marker

**Nice to have:** an oven with a light, a cooler, a rubber band

### The big idea
You are about to farm trillions of invisible creatures. Yogurt is milk transformed by bacteria; sourdough starter is
flour and water colonized by wild yeast and bacteria from the air and the grain. Today you set up both experiments,
and over the next week you will watch two tiny ecosystems come alive.

### Goals
- A jar of homemade yogurt incubating and a sourdough starter begun.
- A lab log you will update every day for a week.

### Objectives
- I can explain how bacteria and yeast change food through fermentation.
- I can control temperature to help the right microbes win.
- I can keep a lab log with observations over time.

### Get ready
Wash the pot, spoon and jars in very hot, soapy water and rinse well. Take the yogurt out of the fridge to warm a little.

### Steps
1. **Yogurt.** Heat the milk to about 180 F (82 C), just steaming with tiny bubbles. This changes milk proteins so
   yogurt sets thick.
2. Cool it to about 110 F (43 C): warm, like a hot bath. Test with a clean finger if no thermometer. Too hot kills
   the bacteria.
3. Stir in the starter yogurt, pour into jars, and keep warm for 6 to 12 hours: in an oven with only the light on,
   or wrapped in towels in a cooler.
4. **Sourdough starter.** In a clean jar, mix 50 g flour and 50 g water (about 1/3 cup flour and 3 1/2 tablespoons
   water). Stir hard, cover loosely, and mark the level with tape.
5. Start the lab log. Write the time, temperature, smell and look of each jar.
6. Every day for a week: discard half the starter, add 50 g flour and 50 g water, and mark the level. Bubbles and
   a rise mean wild yeast is winning.
7. Check the yogurt after 6 hours. Taste it. Longer means tangier.

### Make it yours
- Run a temperature test: one yogurt jar warm, one at room temperature. Compare.
- Name your starter and give it a backstory as a pet.
- Make a flavored yogurt with fruit, honey or jam.

### Stuck?
- The yogurt is runny: it needed more time or more warmth. Leave it longer.
- The starter is not bubbling: move it somewhere warmer and keep feeding it. It can take a week.

### Show it off
Serve a yogurt breakfast and show the family your bubbling starter and your lab log.

### Clean up
Wash the pot right away (milk sticks), refrigerate the yogurt, and keep the starter on the counter.

### For the grown-up
This builds microbiology and chemistry: fermentation, temperature control and lab records. Stove use is involved;
agree first. If the starter grows fuzzy mold or pink streaks, throw it out. Check dairy allergies. **Ask at dinner:**
"Which microbes are living in your starter?"

## 7. Senior Year Soundtrack Liner Notes

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, pens, a ruler, scissors, glue, a piece of cardboard about 12 cm by 12 cm

**Nice to have:** a phone or radio to listen to songs, old photos, colored pens

### The big idea
Albums once came with **liner notes**: a little booklet with the songs, the story behind them and the people who made
them. You are going to make the liner notes for the soundtrack of your senior year. Pick twelve songs, write why each
belongs, and design the case and booklet like a real album release.

### Goals
- A 12-track list in a deliberate order, with a short note for each song.
- A handmade album case and fold-out booklet.

### Objectives
- I can choose and order songs to create an emotional arc.
- I can write short, vivid notes that connect music to memory.
- I can design an album cover and layout.

### Get ready
Brainstorm 20 songs that meant something this year: songs from car rides, parties, sad days, studying. Do not judge
yet.

### Steps
1. Cut it to 12 (10 minutes). Keep the songs tied to a moment, not just your favorites.
2. Order them. Albums have arcs: open strong, dip into a quiet middle, build to a big finish. Example arc: September
   nerves, winter grind, spring freedom.
3. Write a liner note for each (25 minutes): 40 to 80 words. Start with a moment, not an opinion. Example: "Track 4.
   The bus broke down outside town and someone played this on a phone speaker. Twenty people sang along."
4. Name the album, like "Last Year Before Everything."
5. Design the cover on the cardboard square. Use one strong image: a drawing, a collage, a photo, or bold type.
6. Make the booklet: fold paper accordion-style to fit the case, and write the notes inside.
7. Add credits: thank the people who were part of the year.

### Make it yours
- Make a "director's cut" with three hidden tracks and secret notes.
- Make it for a friend's senior year instead, and give it to them.
- Make it for a fictional character, a band or a movie that does not exist.

### Stuck?
- Too many songs: pick one per month of the school year.
- Notes feel generic: write what you saw, heard and smelled in that moment, not what the song is "about."

### Show it off
Play a few tracks at dinner while you read the liner notes aloud.

### Clean up
Recycle scraps and keep the case somewhere safe.

### For the grown-up
This builds writing, design and curation, and helps your teen make sense of a big year. **Ask at dinner:** "Which
song is the most 'this year'?"

## 8. Comedy Sketch for Friends

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper and pens, simple props from around the house, a space to perform, at least one partner (a
friend over, a sibling, or a grown-up after work)

**Nice to have:** hats, wigs, glasses and costumes, a timer

### The big idea
Sketch comedy runs on one rule: find the **game** of the scene, the one funny pattern, and heighten it until it
explodes. A waiter who keeps bringing the wrong food is a premise. A waiter who brings things that get more and more
impossible (a live goose, a canoe) is a game. Write and perform a three-minute sketch with friends.

### Goals
- A written sketch of about three minutes with a clear game.
- A live performance with at least one partner.

### Objectives
- I can find and heighten the game of a comedy scene.
- I can write a sketch with a setup, escalation and a button ending.
- I can perform with timing and commitment.

### Get ready
Gather props and clear a "stage." Agree on how many people are performing.

### Steps
1. Brainstorm premises (10 minutes). Normal place plus one weird thing: a job interview where the interviewer is a
   toddler, a support group for retired villains, a tour guide who has never been outside.
2. Find the game: what is the funny pattern? Write it in one sentence.
3. Write the setup (first 30 seconds): who, where, what is normal.
4. Heighten three times. Each beat bigger than the last.
5. Write a **button**: a final line or action that ends with a laugh. Example: the toddler interviewer offers the
   job, then bursts into tears because it is naptime.
6. Rehearse twice for lines, then twice with full commitment. Play it straight; characters do not know they are funny.

### Make it yours
- Make it a parody commercial for a ridiculous product.
- Do it as a silent sketch with only actions.
- Write a series of three short sketches that connect at the end.

### Stuck?
- It is not funny: you probably have a premise, not a game. Find the pattern and repeat it bigger.
- The ending fizzles: end on the biggest heightening or a surprise twist.

### Show it off
Perform it for family or friends as "a special presentation," with a host introducing the sketch.

### Clean up
Return costumes and props, and keep the script.

### For the grown-up
This builds writing, timing, collaboration and stage confidence. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the game of your sketch?"

## 9. First Run Plan

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 60-90 min | Outdoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** running or athletic shoes, comfortable clothes, water, paper and a pen, a watch or phone for timing

**Nice to have:** a friend or family member to run with, a map of your neighborhood

### The big idea
You do not have to be a runner to start running. Most beginners quit because they go too fast and too far. You will
learn the walk-run method and build a four-week plan, then head out for your first mission run: a loop with a
secret goal you set yourself, like spotting five red doors or running to the far side of the park.

### Goals
- One completed walk-run session of about 25 minutes.
- A four-week plan, written out and stuck on your wall.

### Objectives
- I can use run-walk intervals to build fitness safely.
- I can find an easy pace using the talk test.
- I can plan a gradual increase over four weeks.

### Get ready
Tell a grown-up your route and when you will be back. Pick a safe loop with sidewalks or a track. Drink water.

### Steps
1. Warm up with 5 minutes of brisk walking and a few leg swings.
2. Run-walk (20 minutes): run 1 minute, walk 2 minutes, repeat 6 or 7 times.
3. Use the **talk test**: you should be able to speak in short sentences while running. If not, slow down.
4. Pick a mission during the run: count dogs, find a mural, or reach a landmark.
5. Cool down with 5 minutes of walking, then stretch calves, quads and hamstrings.
6. Write your plan (15 minutes). Week 1: three sessions of run 1, walk 2. Week 2: run 2, walk 2. Week 3: run 3, walk
   1. Week 4: run 5, walk 1. Rest a day between sessions.
7. Write a "why" at the top of the plan, in one sentence.

### Make it yours
- Plan a story run: each session you are a courier carrying an urgent message across a fantasy city.
- Make a playlist of songs at running tempo (about 160 to 180 beats per minute) for later.
- Plan a fun finish line in week four, with family cheering at the end.

### Stuck?
- Your shins hurt: slow down, land softly and shorten your stride. Take an extra rest day.
- You are bored: change the route or invite a friend.

### Show it off
Post your plan on the fridge and tick off each run. Invite the family to the week-four finish.

### Clean up
Stretch, drink water and put your shoes by the door for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds fitness, pacing and planning. Agree on the route and check-in time. Running in daylight and in visible
clothes is safest. **Ask at dinner:** "How did the run feel by the end?"

## 10. Volunteer Project for Your Town

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kindness | 90-120 min | Outdoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** gloves (garden or kitchen), trash bags, a bucket, paper and pen, water

**Nice to have:** a grabber tool, a friend or family member, a sign you make yourself

### The big idea
Real change in a town starts small: one person cleans one corner, and others notice. You are going to find a spot
near home that needs care, a littered path, a messy bus stop, a neglected planter, and fix it in one afternoon. Then
you will make it easy for others to keep it that way.

### Goals
- One spot near home cleaned up and noticeably better.
- A small sign or plan that helps keep it that way.

### Objectives
- I can identify a real need in my community.
- I can plan and carry out a small project from start to finish.
- I can measure the impact of what I did.

### Get ready
Tell a grown-up exactly where you are going and when you will be back. Pick one spot close to home. Gather gloves,
bags and water.

### Steps
1. Scout the spot (10 minutes). Take a "before" sketch or photo. Note what the problems are.
2. Plan the work: what can you fix in an hour? Picking up trash, sweeping, pulling weeds, clearing a drain.
3. Do the work (45 minutes). Wear gloves. Skip anything sharp, like broken glass or needles, and tell an adult instead.
4. Sort what you collected: trash, recycling and things to report.
5. Measure the impact: count the bags, guess the weight, compare before and after.
6. Make a sign or note for the spot: "This corner was cleaned on [date]. Please help keep it clean."

## 11. Write a Cryptic Crossword

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** graph paper (or paper ruled into a grid), a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a pen

**Nice to have:** a dictionary or thesaurus, a friend who likes puzzles to test it

### The big idea
In a cryptic crossword, every clue is a tiny riddle with two halves: a plain definition and some wordplay that
leads to the same answer. "Hear silent ramble (6)" means LISTEN: "hear" is the definition, and "silent" rambles
(rearranges) into LISTEN. Once you see the trick, they are addictive. Learn the main clue types and build a small
cryptic of your own that will stump your family.

### Goals
- A finished 9 by 9 crossword grid, filled with real words.
- Eight or more cryptic clues that a solver can crack.

### Objectives
- I can write clues using anagrams, hidden words, charades and double definitions.
- I can build a grid where every word crosses others.
- I can test a clue for fairness: one definition, one piece of wordplay, one answer.

### Get ready
Draw a 9 by 9 grid with 1 cm squares. Shade a pattern of black squares that is symmetrical: if you shade one, shade
its mirror across the center too. Aim for words of 3 to 9 letters.

### Steps
1. Learn four clue types (15 minutes). Solve each example before reading the answer.
   - **Anagram:** "Hear silent ramble (6)" is LISTEN. Words like "ramble," "broken" or "wild" signal a rearrangement.
   - **Hidden word:** "Small house found in some cab in town (5)" is CABIN, hiding in "cab in."
   - **Charade:** pieces in a row. "Vehicle with a pet makes a rug (6)" is CAR plus PET, CARPET.
   - **Double definition:** two meanings, one word. "Tree's hand (4)" is PALM.
2. Fill the grid with words first (20 minutes). Place the longest words, then work outward. Pencil and erase freely.
3. Write a clue for each answer (40 minutes). The definition always goes at the very start or very end of the clue.
4. Polish the **surface**: each clue should read like a normal, slightly odd sentence that hides how it works.
5. Number the grid and write Across and Down lists with letter counts in brackets.
6. Solve-test every clue cold the next hour. Is there only one possible answer?

### Make it yours
- Hide a theme: every answer relates to your town, your friends or a book you love.
- Hide a secret message in the first letters of the Across answers.
- Make a mini 5 by 5 cryptic for the family to solve at breakfast.

### Stuck?
- The grid will not fill: swap a tricky long word for two shorter ones, or move a black square (and its mirror).
- A clue is too hard: make the wordplay indicator more obvious and the definition more direct.

### Show it off
Give the puzzle to someone at dinner, with one free hint card per person.

### Clean up
Keep your clue notes and a clean inked copy of the puzzle.

### For the grown-up
This builds vocabulary, wordplay, logic and precise writing. Cryptics are a classic word-lover's hobby, and solving
your teen's puzzle together is half the fun. **Ask at dinner:** "What's your sneakiest clue?"

## 14. Pendulum Wave Machine

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a broom handle or a long stick, two chairs to rest it on, string or thread, 10 to 12 identical small
weights (metal nuts, washers, keys or same-size beads), a ruler or tape measure, scissors, tape

**Nice to have:** a calculator, a second person to release the pendulums, a dark background

### The big idea
Line up a row of pendulums with carefully chosen lengths, release them together, and something hypnotic happens:
they form waves, snakes and spirals, fall into chaos, and then snap back into a perfect line. It is pure physics: each
pendulum's swing time depends only on its length. You will calculate the lengths, build it, and run the show.

### Goals
- A working pendulum wave with 10 to 12 pendulums.
- A calculation table showing how you chose each length.

### Objectives
- I can use the pendulum formula to calculate string lengths.
- I can measure and build precisely so a pattern works.
- I can explain why the pattern repeats.

### Get ready
Rest the broom handle across two chair backs, about 1 m off the floor. Clear the space below.

### Steps
1. Learn the rule. A pendulum's period (time for one full swing) is T = 2 pi times the square root of (L / g), where
   g is 9.8 m/s squared. Longer string, slower swing.
2. Pick a cycle time: 60 seconds works well. In 60 seconds, the longest pendulum does 50 swings, the next 51, the
   next 52, and so on.
3. Calculate lengths (20 minutes). For each pendulum, T = 60 / N, then L = g x (T / 2 pi) squared. For N = 50, T is
   1.2 seconds and L is about 35.7 cm. Do the same for N = 51 to 61.
4. Cut and tie strings (25 minutes). Measure each length from the stick to the center of the weight. Precision
   matters: within 2 or 3 mm.
5. Tie each string in a V shape (two points on the stick, 5 cm apart) so the pendulums swing in a single plane.
6. Line them up, lift all of them together with a ruler, and release.
7. Watch for 60 seconds. Note when patterns appear and when they all line up again.

### Make it yours
- Try a 30-second cycle and compare the patterns.
- Add a light behind them in a dark room and watch the shadows on the wall.
- Mess up one length on purpose and see how it breaks the pattern.

### Stuck?
- The pattern is messy: check each length again. Small errors grow quickly.
- Pendulums swing in circles: use the V-shaped tie so they stay in one plane.

### Show it off
Turn down the lights and give a two-minute show, explaining the math before you let go.

### Clean up
Coil and label the strings so you can rebuild it, and put the chairs back.

### For the grown-up
This builds physics, measurement and algebra: the pendulum equation from high school physics, put to work. **Ask at
dinner:** "What happens when they all line up again?"

Engineers call it **reverse engineering**: taking something apart to understand how it was designed. Inside every
gadget is a story of choices, like where to save money, how to make it easy to build, and how to make it last (or not).
Open one up, map what every part does, and figure out the designer's mind.

### Goals
- A gadget fully disassembled, with its parts sorted and labeled.
- A labeled diagram showing how the parts work together.

### Objectives
- I can disassemble a device carefully and track the order of parts.
- I can identify common components like circuit boards, motors, switches, springs and gears.
- I can explain how a device works from input to output.

### Get ready
Check with a grown-up that the gadget is truly unwanted and that it is not a wall-plug device with a big power
supply, a microwave, an old tube TV or anything with a swollen or rechargeable battery. Remove any AA or AAA batteries.
Lay a towel down so screws do not roll away.

### Steps
1. Sketch the outside (5 minutes). Note every button, light and port.
2. Find the screws. Many hide under rubber feet or stickers. Put screws from each step into a separate muffin cup.
3. Open it slowly. Pry gently at seams. Take a quick sketch before you remove each layer.
4. Identify the parts (25 minutes). Common finds: a **circuit board** (green or brown, with tiny parts), a small
   **motor**, **gears**, a **speaker** (has a magnet), **switches** under buttons, and rubber pads that complete a
   circuit when pressed.
5. Trace the path: what happens from a button press to a light or sound? Draw arrows.
6. Look for the designer's choices: what is glued vs screwed? Was it made to be repaired?
7. Make your final diagram, labeling each part and its job.

### Make it yours
- Make a "Frankenstein" sculpture or creature from the parts.
- Rebuild it, and see if it works again (bonus if it was broken).
- Write a "design review": what you would change to make it last longer or easier to repair.

### Stuck?
- It will not open: check under labels and feet again, or look for clips you need to press while pulling.
- You have extra screws: check your sketches and muffin cups to see where they belong.

### Show it off
Lay the parts out like an exploded diagram on the table and walk the family through how it worked.

### Clean up
Bag the parts, put tools away, and take electronic scraps to e-waste recycling when you can (not the trash).

### For the grown-up
This builds engineering thinking, mechanical understanding and curiosity about how things work. Only small,
unplugged, battery-free devices are safe. Avoid microwaves, old TVs, large power supplies and lithium or swollen
batteries; never puncture a battery. **Ask at dinner:** "What surprised you most inside the gadget?"

## 24. Learn a Partner Dance

**Nice to have:** a speaker, a mirror, a library book with dance steps

### The big idea
Partner dances like swing, salsa and the waltz are a conversation without words: one person leads, the other
follows, and both listen through their hands. You will learn the basic step of one dance, a turn, and a simple
lead-and-follow pattern, then teach it to someone and dance a full song together.

### Goals
- A basic step, a turn and one fancy move learned with a partner.
- A full song danced without stopping.

### Objectives
- I can perform a basic step in time with music.
- I can lead or follow using clear, gentle signals through the hands and frame.
- I can teach a sequence to someone else.

### Steps

### Show it off

### Clean up

### For the grown-up

## 28. Hand-Bound Sketchbook

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 20 to 30 sheets of plain paper, two pieces of cardboard (from a cereal box or shipping box), a
needle, strong thread or dental floss, a thumbtack or pushpin, scissors, a ruler, a pencil, glue, scrap fabric or
paper for covers

**Nice to have:** a bone folder or butter knife for crisp folds, waxed thread, binder clips, an awl

### The big idea
Before machines, every book was sewn by hand. Bookbinders folded pages into bundles called **signatures** and sewed
them together with a stitch that leaves the spine open and flexible. You will bind a real sketchbook with an exposed
stitch spine that lies flat, then give it a cover nobody else has.

### Goals
- A finished hand-bound sketchbook with at least four signatures.
- A custom cover that you designed.

### Objectives
- I can fold and arrange paper into signatures.
- I can sew a binding that holds pages together firmly.
- I can measure and cut covers to fit.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Fold each sheet in half. Nest five or six folded sheets inside each other to make one signature. Make
four or five signatures.

### Steps
1. Make a punching guide (10 minutes): a folded strip of paper the height of your pages, with four or six evenly
   spaced marks, starting 2 cm from each end.
2. Punch holes along the fold of every signature using the guide and the thumbtack. Open the signature on a folded
   towel so the tack goes through cleanly.
3. Cut two covers from cardboard, 5 mm larger than the pages on three sides. Punch matching holes along one edge.
4. Sew (35 minutes). Use about 1.5 m of thread. Start at the back cover and the first signature. Go in and out
   through the holes. At each hole, loop around the thread of the signature below before going into the next
   signature. This **kettle stitch** locks them together.
5. Add the front cover last and tie off with a double knot inside.
6. Cover the boards with fabric, paper or collage, wrapping and gluing edges inside.
7. Press it under a stack of heavy books for 15 minutes.

### Make it yours
- Make a tiny pocket-sized notebook as a gift.
- Use mixed paper: lined, colored, and grid paper in different signatures.
- Design the cover around a theme: a field journal, a spell book, a ship's logbook.

### Stuck?
- Thread tangles: use shorter thread and rub it over a bar of soap or a crayon to wax it.
- Signatures are loose: pull each stitch snug before moving to the next hole.

### Show it off
Show the family how the spine works, and draw the first page in front of them.

### Clean up
Put the needle and tack away safely and recycle paper scraps.

### For the grown-up
This builds fine motor skills, measuring, patience and an understanding of how books were made for centuries. **Ask
at dinner:** "What will you put on the first page?"
