## 1. Rubber Band Car

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a piece of stiff cardboard about 10 by 25 cm, 2 drinking straws, 2 wooden skewers or thin dowels (or 2 long pencils), 4 bottle caps or cardboard circles for wheels, several rubber bands of different sizes, masking tape, scissors, a ruler, a pushpin or nail to poke holes, a tape measure

**Nice to have:** old CDs or jar lids for big back wheels, a hot glue gun (with permission), balloons cut into rings for tire grip

### The big idea
A stretched rubber band is stored energy waiting for a job. Your job is to give it one: build a car that winds up, lets go, and drives itself across the floor. Then tune it until it goes farther than you thought cardboard could.

### Goals
- A car that drives at least 2 meters on rubber band power alone.
- A test log showing what you changed and how far each version went.

### Objectives
- I can explain how stored (elastic) energy turns into motion.
- I can change one variable at a time and measure the result.
- I can reduce friction and improve traction in a design.

### Get ready
Clear a smooth, straight test track at least 4 meters long: a hallway, a hard floor, or a driveway. Put a strip of tape at the start line.

### Steps
1. **Make the chassis.** Cut the straws to the width of the cardboard and tape one across the front and one across the back on the underside. They're your axle holders (bearings).
2. **Make the wheels.** Poke a hole in the exact center of each cap or circle. An off-center hole makes the car wobble, so measure.
3. **Add axles.** Slide a skewer through each straw and push a wheel on each end. Tape or glue the wheels so they turn with the axle. Give it a push: it should roll straight and easily.
4. **Rig the motor.** Cut a notch in the front of the chassis. Tape one end of a rubber band to the middle of the rear axle (or hook it on a small piece of tape stuck to the axle). Stretch the other end to the front notch.
5. **Wind and launch.** Roll the car backward so the band wraps around the rear axle. Set it down and let go. Measure the distance.
6. **Tune it (40 minutes).** Now experiment, changing one thing per round and running three tests each time: two bands instead of one, a longer or shorter band, bigger rear wheels, rubber "tires" for grip, a lighter chassis. Record the best distance for each change.
7. **Final build.** Combine your best changes into one ultimate car and do three official runs.

### Make it yours
- Go for speed instead of distance: time it over 2 meters.
- Build a car that carries a "passenger" (an egg-sized ball of foil or play dough) without dropping it.
- Design a body for it: a race car, a beetle, a moon rover, cut and folded from cardboard.

### Stuck?
- The wheels spin but the car doesn't move: add grip. Wrap a rubber band or ring of balloon around the back wheels.
- It veers to one side: the axles aren't parallel. Re-tape the straws using a ruler.
- It barely rolls: friction. Make sure the axles turn freely inside the straws and the wheels don't rub the chassis.

### Show it off
Hold a "grand prix" for your family: three official runs, a measured finish line, and a trophy you make for yourself (or for whoever wins with your car).

### Clean up
Sweep up scraps, put the skewers away carefully, and park your car on a shelf.

### For the grown-up
This is physics and engineering: energy transfer, friction and traction, and fair testing, all in eighth grade science. Skewers are sharp, so trimming the points with scissors is a good idea. A glue gun is fine with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the one change that made the biggest difference?"

## 2. First Chapter of a Novel

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a notebook or paper, a pen or pencil, index cards or sticky notes

**Nice to have:** a favorite novel to look at for how first chapters work, a quiet spot with a door

### The big idea
Every novel you've ever loved started with one person writing one chapter. That's the hardest part, and also the best: you get to invent the world, meet the main character, and set off the trouble. Today you write chapter one of your own book.

### Goals
- A finished first chapter, about 1,000 to 2,000 words.
- A one-page plan for where the book goes next.

### Objectives
- I can write an opening line that hooks the reader.
- I can introduce a character with a clear want and a problem.
- I can end a chapter so the reader has to keep reading.

### Get ready
Find a quiet place and set a "do not disturb" sign. Make three cards: Character, Want, Trouble.

### Steps
1. **Build your main character (10 minutes).** On the Character card, write their name, age, one thing they're great at, one flaw, and a secret. Specific beats general: not "she's brave" but "she once climbed the school roof to get a lost shoe."
2. **The want and the trouble.** What does your character want more than anything? What's in the way? Example: "Jae wants to win the town's scavenger hunt. His rival is his own older sister."
3. **Pick the opening moment.** Don't start with the alarm clock. Start when something is already happening or about to change. The rule: enter late.
4. **Write a hook first line.** Try a strange fact ("The day I was supposed to become invisible, I overslept."), a question, or a line of dialogue mid-argument. Write five options and pick the best.
5. **Draft the chapter (45 minutes).** Show the character doing something, not you explaining them. Bring in the world through details, not a lecture. Put the trouble in by the middle of the chapter.
6. **End on a hook.** The last line should leave a question hanging: a door opens, a message arrives, a lie is discovered.
7. **Plan ahead.** On one page, list five big things that will happen in the rest of the book, ending with how it might end.

### Make it yours
- Write it in first person from someone unexpected: the villain, the sidekick, the house the story happens in.
- Start in the middle of the action and skip backward in chapter two.
- Write a prologue first: a short, mysterious scene from 100 years earlier.

### Stuck?
- The blank page is scary: write the worst possible first sentence on purpose, then keep going. You can change it later.
- The chapter feels slow: cut the first paragraph. Most first drafts start a little too early.
- Not sure what happens: give your character a choice and pick the one that makes things worse.

### Show it off
Read your first page out loud to someone at home and stop at a cliffhanger. Make them beg for chapter two.

### Clean up
Number your pages, clip them together with your plan, and put them in a folder labeled with the title.

### For the grown-up
This builds the craft of fiction (character, conflict, pacing, hooks) and the stamina of sustained writing, which is huge for high school English. **Ask at dinner:** "Who is your main character, and what do they want?"

## 3. Run a Role-Playing Adventure

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper, pencils, index cards, one or more six-sided dice (from a board game, or make one from a paper cube), small objects as tokens

**Nice to have:** a big sheet of paper for a map, a box to use as a game master screen

### The big idea
In a tabletop role-playing game, players imagine their heroes and say what they do, and the game master (you) describes the world and decides what happens. You're writing and running an adventure for your family or friends. You're the storyteller, the referee and every villain at once.

### Goals
- A one-session adventure with three scenes and a final challenge.
- Simple rules and character sheets you made, and a played game with at least one other person.

### Objectives
- I can design a story with choices that change what happens.
- I can create fair rules that use dice and chance.
- I can improvise when players do something unexpected.

### Get ready
Decide on your players: a sibling, a friend over, or a grown-up after work. You need at least one player. Set aside the first hour to prepare and the second to play.

### Steps
1. **Make the rules.** Every hero has three stats: Strong, Quick, and Clever. Players split 6 points between them (each stat 1 to 3). To try something risky, roll one die and add the matching stat. Beat 4 for easy, 6 for hard, 8 for heroic.
2. **Make character sheets.** On index cards: name, the three stats, one special item, and 5 health points.
3. **Write the hook.** One sentence that starts the quest: "The town's clock tower has started running backward, and every time it chimes, someone forgets their name."
4. **Plan three scenes (30 minutes).** A talking scene (convince a grumpy bridge keeper), a puzzle or exploring scene (a locked room with a riddle), and an action scene (a chase across rooftops). For each, write what the players see and two or three ways it could go.
5. **Plan the finale.** A boss, a big puzzle, or a big choice. Give the villain a reason, not just evil.
6. **Run the game (45 minutes).** Describe the scene, then always ask: "What do you do?" Say "yes, and" or "yes, but" more than "no." If they do something you didn't plan for, roll with it.
7. **Wrap up.** Narrate how the town changed because of what they did, and give each hero one reward.

### Make it yours
- Pick a different setting: a space station, a haunted school, a city of talking animals.
- Make it a mystery: the players have to figure out who did it from clues you hide in each scene.
- Add a map or drawn tokens so players can see where everyone is.

### Stuck?
- Players go somewhere you didn't plan: invent quickly. Describe three things there and ask what they do.
- The game is dragging: have something happen. The floor shakes, a messenger arrives, the lights go out.
- Dice rolls keep failing: let a failure still move the story forward, just with a cost.

### Show it off
Run it for your family after dinner. At the end, ask each player for their favorite moment.

### Clean up
Keep the rules, sheets and notes in a folder so the heroes can come back for part two.

### For the grown-up
This builds storytelling, rule design, probability and real-time thinking, plus leadership: running a table is hard. It works with any number of players and no special materials. **Ask at dinner:** "What did your players do that you didn't expect?"

## 4. Cook a Dish from Another Culture

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** for shakshuka: 2 tablespoons olive or vegetable oil, 1 onion, 1 bell pepper if you have it, 2 garlic cloves, 1 teaspoon ground cumin, 1 teaspoon paprika, 1 large can (about 800 g) of chopped tomatoes, half a teaspoon of salt, 4 to 6 eggs, a wide pan with a lid, a cutting board, a knife, a wooden spoon, bread to serve

**Nice to have:** fresh parsley or cilantro, a pinch of chili flakes, crumbled cheese

**Get ready the night before:** Ask a grown-up for permission to use the stove and knife, and decide on the dish together.

### The big idea
Food is one of the best ways to travel without leaving home. Tonight you'll cook shakshuka, a dish of eggs simmered in spiced tomato sauce eaten across North Africa and the Middle East, or another dish your family has a recipe for. You'll cook it, learn its story, and serve it with a menu.

### Goals
- A dish from a culture that's new to you, cooked and served to your family.
- A menu card that tells where the dish comes from and how it's eaten.

### Objectives
- I can follow a recipe and adjust it by tasting.
- I can use a knife and stove safely.
- I can share something I learned about the food and the people who make it.

### Get ready
Wash your hands and clear the counter. Check allergies with your grown-up (eggs are the big one here). Ask anyone at home if they know about the dish or have a family dish from another country they'd like to share instead.

### Steps
1. **Learn about it.** Ask a grown-up, look in a cookbook, or use what you know. Shakshuka is often eaten for breakfast or dinner, straight from the pan, with bread to scoop it up.
2. **Prep (10 minutes).** Dice the onion and pepper using the claw grip, fingertips tucked under. Finely chop the garlic.
3. **Cook the base (10 minutes).** Heat the oil in the pan on medium. Add the onion and pepper and cook until soft. Add the garlic, cumin and paprika and stir for 1 minute until it smells amazing.
4. **Make the sauce (10 minutes).** Pour in the tomatoes and salt. Simmer on low until it thickens a little. Taste it and adjust: more salt, more cumin, or a pinch of chili for heat. This part is up to you.
5. **Add the eggs.** Use the back of the spoon to make 4 to 6 little wells in the sauce. Crack an egg into each one.
6. **Cover and cook (6 to 8 minutes).** Put the lid on. It's done when the whites are firm and the yolks are still a little jiggly, or cook longer if your family likes them firm.
7. **Write the menu** while it cooks: the name of the dish, where it's from, how it's eaten, and one fact you learned.

### Make it yours
- Cook a dish from your own family's heritage instead, and interview the person who knows the recipe.
- Make a full menu: add a simple side, like cucumber and tomato salad or warm bread.
- Hold a "tasting passport": each person gets a paper passport stamped after they try the dish.

### Stuck?
- Sauce is too watery: let it simmer longer with the lid off before adding eggs.
- Eggs cook unevenly: turn the heat lower and keep the lid on.
- No eggs: make the sauce and serve it on bread or rice, or add a can of chickpeas.

### Show it off
Serve it straight from the pan in the middle of the table and give a two-minute talk about the dish.

### Clean up
Soak the pan right away, wash everything, wipe the stove once cool, and leave the kitchen clean.

### For the grown-up
This builds cooking skills, tasting and adjusting, and cultural curiosity. Middle schoolers can use the stove and knife with your permission and a check-in or two. Please check allergies (eggs) for everyone eating. If your family has a dish from its own background, that's a wonderful swap. **Ask at dinner:** "Where does this dish come from, and what's one thing you learned about it?"

## 5. Twenty Things in Twenty Minutes Sketch Sprint

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a stack of printer paper or a sketchbook, a pencil, a pen, a timer or clock, a clipboard if outside

**Nice to have:** a marker, colored pencils, a friend or sibling to sprint with you

### The big idea
Artists don't get good by drawing one perfect thing. They get good by drawing a hundred quick, imperfect things. This is a sprint: fast drawing with no time to worry. You'll be shocked at what your hand can do when your brain gets out of the way.

### Goals
- At least 20 one-minute sketches, plus longer ones.
- One finished drawing you're proud of, made at the end.

### Objectives
- I can draw quickly and loosely without stopping to erase.
- I can use different techniques: gesture, contour and blind contour.
- I can notice shapes, proportions and shadows in real objects.

### Get ready
Gather paper and set a timer. No eraser allowed for the sprints. Pick a space with lots of objects: the kitchen, your room, a yard, a park with permission.

### Steps
1. **Warm up.** Fill a page with circles, lines and spirals for 2 minutes. Loosen your wrist.
2. **Sprint 1: Twenty things, twenty minutes.** Set the timer for one minute and draw one object. When it beeps, start the next one. A mug, a shoe, a plant, a hand, a fork, a chair. Don't stop. Don't erase.
3. **Look back.** Spread them out. Circle your three favorites. Notice what they have in common: maybe the faster ones have more energy.
4. **Sprint 2: Blind contour (10 minutes).** Draw five objects without looking at the paper at all, only at the object. Go slow, like your pencil is touching the edge. They'll look weird. That's the point.
5. **Sprint 3: Five things in fifteen minutes.** Three minutes each. Add shading this time: find the darkest part and the lightest part.
6. **The long one (20 minutes).** Choose your favorite object from the day and draw it once more, carefully, using everything you just practiced.
7. **Make a gallery.** Pick your best 10 sketches and lay them out in a grid.

### Make it yours
- Theme it: twenty things that are red, twenty kitchen tools, twenty things smaller than your thumb.
- Draw people: family members doing ordinary things, in 30-second poses.
- Make it a competition: you and a sibling draw the same twenty things, then vote on each round.

### Stuck?
- Can't finish in a minute: that's fine. Draw the outline only and move on.
- Hate your drawings: everyone's first sprint is rough. Look for one line you like on each page.
- Run out of things to draw: draw the same object from five different angles.

### Show it off
Tape up your best 10 like a gallery wall and let your family pick their favorite.

### Clean up
Stack the drawings, date them, and keep them. Compare in a month.

### For the grown-up
This builds observational drawing, speed and the confidence to try without perfectionism, which is a skill well beyond art. **Ask at dinner:** "Which of your sketches surprised you the most?"

## 6. Silly Debate Club

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper, pencils, index cards, a timer or clock, a spoon or object as a "speaking stick"

**Nice to have:** a podium (a stack of books or a box), more family members as judges

### The big idea
Is cereal soup? Should socks be sold in threes? Real debaters can argue either side of any question, and the sillier the topic, the better the practice. Start your own debate club and argue the most ridiculous topics like they're the most important things in the world.

### Goals
- At least three debates, each with opening statements, rebuttals and a winner.
- A scorecard and a set of debate topics for the future.

### Objectives
- I can make a claim and back it up with evidence and reasons.
- I can listen to the other side and respond to their arguments.
- I can speak clearly and persuasively under time pressure.

### Get ready
Find a partner: a sibling, a friend, or a grown-up after work. Make a "podium" from books or a box. Write ten silly topics on cards.

### Steps
1. **Write topics.** Some starters: "Cereal is soup." "Pineapple belongs on pizza." "Cats are better roommates than dogs." "Homework should be done in pajamas." Make your own.
2. **Learn the structure.** Each argument has a claim (what you think), evidence (facts, examples), and reasoning (why the evidence proves it). Example: "Cereal is soup. Soup is a food in liquid, eaten with a spoon. Cereal is food in milk, eaten with a spoon. Therefore, cereal is soup."
3. **Flip for sides.** Draw a topic and flip a coin: heads argues for, tails argues against. You don't get to choose.
4. **Prep (5 minutes).** Write three arguments on an index card, plus one thing your opponent might say and how you'll answer it.
5. **Debate round.** Opening statements: 2 minutes each. Rebuttals: 1 minute each, answering the other side's points. Closing: 30 seconds each.
6. **Judge it.** A third person or a scorecard gives points for: strong reasons (1 to 5), answering the other side (1 to 5), and style (1 to 5).
7. **Run two more rounds** with new topics. Switch sides so you have to argue the opposite.

### Make it yours
- Make it "speed debate": 30 seconds per speech, no prep time.
- Argue as characters: a pirate, a judge, a grumpy grandparent.
- Hold a "family town hall" on a real house question, like what should be for dinner Friday.

### Stuck?
- Run out of arguments: use an example from real life or an "imagine if" story.
- Partner talks over you: use the speaking stick. Only the person holding it can talk.
- Getting heated: remember it's about cereal. Laugh and shake hands.

### Show it off
Put on a championship debate at dinner with the whole family as the judges.

### Clean up
Keep your topic cards and scorecards in an envelope for the next club meeting.

### For the grown-up
This builds argument writing (claim, evidence, reasoning), listening and public speaking, which are core skills in English and social studies, while keeping the stakes low and silly. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the funniest argument you made, and did it win?"

## 7. Reaction Time Ruler Drop

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 2+ |

**Materials:** a 30 cm ruler, paper, a pencil, a chair and table, a partner (a sibling, a friend, or a grown-up after work)

**Nice to have:** a calculator, graph paper, colored pencils for a chart

### The big idea
How fast are you, really? A falling ruler can measure your reaction time to the hundredth of a second, because gravity always pulls things down at the same rate. Measure yourself, test what makes you faster or slower, and find out who in your family has the quickest reflexes.

### Goals
- Your reaction time measured in seconds, based on at least 10 trials.
- An experiment that tests one thing that could change reaction time.

### Objectives
- I can collect data and find the average of several trials.
- I can convert distance to time using a chart.
- I can design a fair experiment and explain the results.

### Get ready
Sit in a chair with your arm on a table and your hand hanging just off the edge. Make a data table with columns for trial number, distance (cm) and time (s).

### Steps
1. **Learn the setup.** Your partner holds the ruler at the 30 cm end so the 0 mark hangs between your open thumb and finger, not touching. They drop it without warning. You catch it as fast as you can.
2. **Read the distance.** Look where your thumb caught it. That's your distance in cm.
3. **Convert to time.** Use this chart: 5 cm = 0.10 s, 10 cm = 0.14 s, 15 cm = 0.17 s, 20 cm = 0.20 s, 25 cm = 0.23 s, 30 cm = 0.25 s. For distances in between, estimate. (The formula is time = the square root of (2 times distance in meters divided by 9.8).)
4. **Run 10 trials.** Write each one down. Find your average distance and time. Throw out any trial where you guessed early.
5. **Design an experiment (20 minutes).** Pick one question: Is your other hand slower? Are you faster with your eyes closed and your partner saying "now" as they drop it? Are you slower while counting backward from 100 by 7s? Do 10 trials each way.
6. **Compare.** Make a bar chart of your results. What made the biggest difference?
7. **Test the family.** Measure everyone and make a leaderboard.

### Make it yours
- Test before and after exercise: do 20 jumping jacks, then test right away.
- Make a "reaction time ruler": mark a strip of paper with times instead of centimeters so you can read times directly.
- Test whether practice helps: 30 trials in a row. Do you get faster?

### Stuck?
- Results jump all over: make sure your fingers start at the same spot every time, and your partner doesn't give hints.
- You catch it every time at 0: you're guessing. Your partner should wait a random time before dropping.
- Ruler falls all the way through: count it as 30 cm or more, and try again.

### Show it off
Host a "reflex challenge" after dinner with the family leaderboard.

### Clean up
Put the ruler away, and keep your data and chart together.

### For the grown-up
This is a real experiment with data, averages and graphing (eighth grade math and science), plus a bit of physics: gravity accelerates every falling object the same way. **Ask at dinner:** "What slowed down your reaction time the most?"

## 8. Skate Park Model

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** cardboard boxes and sheets, cereal-box cardboard for curves, masking tape, scissors, glue, a ruler, a pencil, straws or paper clips for rails, a marble, toy car, or a skateboard made from a strip of cardboard and 4 bottle caps

**Nice to have:** a hot glue gun (with permission), paint or markers, a craft knife (with permission)

### The big idea
Skate park designers think about flow: how a skater moves from one ramp to the next without stopping. You're the designer. Build a scale model of a skate park where everything connects, then test your design with a "skater" that rolls.

### Goals
- A scale model skate park with at least four features: a ramp, a rail, a quarter pipe and a ledge.
- A test run where your skater rolls through at least two features in a row.

### Objectives
- I can use a scale to turn real sizes into model sizes.
- I can design curved and flat surfaces from cardboard.
- I can test a design and improve the flow.

### Get ready
Pick a scale and write it down. A good one is 1 to 20: 1 cm in the model is 20 cm in real life. A real quarter pipe about 1.2 m tall becomes 6 cm. Use a cardboard sheet about 40 by 60 cm as the base.

### Steps
1. **Sketch the plan.** Draw the park from above. Plan a path: the skater starts on a high spot, drops in, rides through features, and ends somewhere.
2. **Build a quarter pipe.** Cut two curved side pieces from cardboard (trace a plate edge for the curve). Tape a strip of thin cereal-box cardboard along the curve. Make it 6 cm tall.
3. **Build a ramp and ledge.** A ramp is a wedge: fold a triangle of cardboard. A ledge is a box about 2 cm tall.
4. **Add rails.** Tape a straw or straightened paper clip on two small supports.
5. **Design your own features (30 minutes).** Add at least two: a bowl, a funbox, stairs, a half pipe, a spine. Place them so they flow from one to the next.
6. **Test runs.** Release a marble or toy car from the top. Where does it stop or crash? Adjust angles, smooth the joints with tape, and move features.
7. **Finish it.** Paint or color the concrete gray, add little benches, trees, and a sign with your park's name.

### Make it yours
- Design it for a specific place: a rooftop, an abandoned train station, the moon.
- Add a "street" section with stairs, rails and planters, like a real city plaza.
- Build a skate park for a different sport: a BMX track or a scooter park, with different features.

### Stuck?
- Curves keep bending wrong: tape strips from the back, and make the side pieces tight.
- The marble flies off: add low walls along the edges or reduce the ramp angle.
- Joints catch the skater: tape smooth across every seam.

### Show it off
Give your family a guided "park tour" and show your best run.

### Clean up
Recycle scraps, put away the tools, and keep the park somewhere safe.

### For the grown-up
This builds scale and ratio skills (grade 7 and 8 math), design thinking and physics of slopes and momentum. A glue gun or craft knife is fine with your permission and a check-in. **Ask at dinner:** "What feature was the hardest to build, and how did you make it work?"

## 9. Screenplay Scene and Table Read

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen or pencil, a ruler, highlighters or colored pencils

**Nice to have:** family members or friends to read the parts, a desk lamp for "set lighting"

### The big idea
Movies start as screenplays: a special format that tells actors what to say and directors what to film. You're writing a short scene, formatted like the real thing, then holding a "table read" where actors read it aloud for the first time, just like in a real studio.

### Goals
- A screenplay scene, about three to five pages, in real screenplay format.
- A table read with at least one other person.

### Objectives
- I can use screenplay format: scene heading, action, character, dialogue.
- I can write dialogue that shows character and moves the story.
- I can revise after hearing a scene read aloud.

### Get ready
Decide on two or three characters and a place. Simple is best: one location, one problem, a few minutes of time.

### Steps
1. **Learn the format.** A scene starts with a heading in capitals: INT. KITCHEN - NIGHT (INT means inside, EXT means outside). Under it, action lines in normal sentences describe what we see. Character names are in capitals in the middle of the line, with their dialogue underneath.
2. **Example:**
   INT. KITCHEN - NIGHT
   A dark kitchen. The fridge hums. MAYA (13) tiptoes in, holding a flashlight.
   MAYA: (whispering) If anyone finds out about the cake, we're done.
3. **Plan the scene.** Who wants what? What's in the way? How does it end? A good scene has a turn: something changes between the beginning and the end.
4. **Write it (40 minutes).** Keep action lines short: describe only what the camera sees and hears. Give each character a different way of talking. Cut "hi, how are you" small talk. Start late and end early.
5. **Add one surprise.** A secret is revealed, someone walks in, or the plan goes wrong.
6. **Prep the table read.** Make a copy for each reader or share one. Highlight each character's lines in a different color.
7. **Hold the table read.** Sit around a table. One person reads the action lines; others read their characters. Listen: which lines got a laugh? Which felt clunky? Mark them, then rewrite.

### Make it yours
- Write the same scene in two genres: as a comedy and as a thriller.
- Write a scene with almost no dialogue, told mostly through action.
- Write a scene that's a "lost" moment between two characters from a story you already love, but make the characters your own.

### Stuck?
- Dialogue sounds fake: read it out loud. If you wouldn't say it, change it.
- The scene feels flat: raise the stakes. What happens if the character fails?
- No readers yet: read every part yourself in different voices, or wait for family after dinner.

### Show it off
Hold a table read at dinner and give everyone a part. Take notes like a real director.

### Clean up
Keep your script and notes in a folder, marked "Draft 1."

### For the grown-up
This builds dialogue, structure and revision skills, and introduces a professional writing format. Hearing writing read aloud is one of the best ways to improve it. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the best line in your scene, and who said it?"

## 10. Beatbox Loop Composition

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, a ruler, a mirror, a glass of water

**Nice to have:** family members to be a "human loop machine," a phone to record for the family (just for the family)

### The big idea
Beatboxers make whole songs using only their mouths: drums, bass, sound effects, all at once. You're going to learn the three basic sounds, write your beats down on a grid, and build a composition by layering loops on top of each other.

### Goals
- Three basic beatbox sounds you can make clearly.
- A written beat composition with at least three different loops.

### Objectives
- I can make a kick drum, hi-hat and snare sound with my mouth.
- I can write a beat on a 16-step grid and play it back.
- I can build a piece by layering and changing loops.

### Get ready
Get a glass of water: beatboxing dries your mouth out. Draw a grid with 16 boxes in a row and three rows labeled Kick, Hat and Snare. Each box is one step. Count it as "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a."

### Steps
1. **Learn the kick.** Say "b" but without your voice: just a burst of air with your lips pressed. Make it punchy.
2. **Learn the hi-hat.** Say "t" lightly, with your tongue tapping behind your teeth: "ts."
3. **Learn the snare.** Say "pf" with a sharp burst through your lips, or "k" for a softer version. Practice each one 20 times in the mirror.
4. **Write your first beat.** Fill the grid: Kick on steps 1 and 9, Snare on 5 and 13, Hat on every odd step. Play it: "b ts pf ts b ts pf ts." That's a basic beat.
5. **Write three loops (20 minutes).** Loop A: your basic beat. Loop B: move the kicks around for something bouncier. Loop C: drop the kick and add sound effects (a click, a "wub," a siren). Write each on its own grid.
6. **Arrange it.** Plan the song: intro (Loop A, quiet), verse (Loop B), break (Loop C), drop (everything, loud). Write the order out.
7. **Perform it.** Play the whole composition from start to end. Practice until you can play it twice in a row without stopping.

### Make it yours
- Be a human loop machine: teach each family member one loop and layer them, then add yours on top.
- Add a melody: hum a bass line while you beatbox (it's hard!).
- Compose a beat for a specific scene: a heist, a race, a rainy day.

### Stuck?
- Sounds are too quiet: get the mouth closer to a corner of the room or cupped hands. Push more air.
- Can't keep the beat: tap your foot on 1, 2, 3, 4 while you play.
- Mouth is dry: water break. Seriously.

### Show it off
Perform your composition at dinner, then teach everyone their loop and do a family jam.

### Clean up
Keep your grids together in a folder, labeled with your song name.

### For the grown-up
This builds rhythm, music notation (the grid is real music theory) and composition, using only the voice. It can get loud, so a closed door helps. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you teach me one of your beats?"

## 11. Park Orienteering Course

**Nice to have:** a compass, colored pencils, a friend or family member to run the course

### The big idea
Orienteering is a sport where you race through unfamiliar ground with only a map and your brain. No phone, no GPS. Design a real orienteering course in a park or yard: map it, set checkpoints, and challenge someone to find them all.

### Goals
- A hand-drawn map with a scale and at least six checkpoints.
- A course that someone can run from start to finish using only the map.

### Objectives
- I can measure distances by pacing.
- I can draw a map with a scale, a north arrow and a key.
- I can design a course that is challenging but fair.

### Get ready
Get permission to go to a park, or use a yard, a school field, or even your whole home for an indoor version. If you're going to a park, go with a grown-up, or with their permission and a time to be back.

### Steps
1. **Measure your pace.** Mark 10 meters (about 10 big steps for most adults; measure with a tape measure if you have one). Walk it normally and count every time your right foot lands. That's your pace count for 10 meters.
2. **Find north.** Use a compass, or the sun: in the morning, it's in the east; in the afternoon, it's in the west.

6. **Test it.** Run it yourself using only the map. Fix any checkpoint that was too hard to find.
7. **Race.** Have someone run your course with a timer, or run it again yourself and try to beat your time.

### Make it yours
- Make it a story: each checkpoint letter spells a secret word.
- Make a "score" course: checkpoints are worth different points, and runners get 20 minutes to collect as many as they can.
- Make the indoor version: map every room of your home and hide checkpoints in drawers and shelves.

### Stuck?
- The map is confusing: add more landmarks and fewer small details.
- Checkpoints blow away: clip them to something or weigh them down with a rock.
- No park nearby: use the yard, the building's courtyard, or go indoor.

### Show it off
Invite your family to run it after dinner (or on the weekend) and keep a leaderboard.

### Clean up
Collect every clothespin and flag. Leave nothing behind.

### For the grown-up
This builds map skills, measuring, scale, and real navigation without a screen. If your child is going to a park, decide if you'll go along, or set clear rules: where, with whom, and when they'll be back. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest checkpoint to find?"

### Goals
- A finished crossword with at least 15 answers about your life.

### Objectives
- I can fit words together in a grid so they cross correctly.
- I can write clues that are fair but clever.

- Make a cryptic clue or two: a clue with a hidden word or wordplay.

### Stuck?
- Words won't cross: swap in a shorter word, or pick a different crossing letter.
- Too many black squares: try to add short words (2 or 3 letters) in the gaps.
- Clues are too hard: test them on yourself the next day. If you can't get it, nobody can.

### Show it off
Give a copy to each person at home and see who solves it first.

### Clean up
Keep the answer key safe from cheaters.

### For the grown-up
This builds vocabulary, spelling, spatial reasoning and writing clever clues, which is a real writing skill. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the hardest clue in your crossword?"

### The big idea

### For the grown-up
This is geometry (angles, circles, rotational symmetry) and design, and many kids find it calming. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you make every slice match?"

## 15. Milk Plastic

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1 |

**Materials:** 1 cup (250 ml) of milk, white vinegar, a microwave-safe mug or small pot, a spoon, a strainer or a paper towel, a bowl, a measuring cup and teaspoon, cookie cutters or a butter knife

**Nice to have:** food coloring, lemon juice to compare, a magnifying glass

### The big idea
Before modern plastics, people made buttons and jewelry from milk. Milk holds a protein called casein, and with heat and a little acid, you can pull it out and shape it. Turn milk into plastic, then mold something you'll keep.

### Goals
- A batch of milk plastic shaped into something useful or decorative.
- A comparison of at least two recipes.

### Objectives
- I can follow a lab procedure carefully and safely.
- I can explain what a polymer is and how this one formed.
- I can change one variable and compare the results.

### Get ready
Ask your grown-up for permission to heat the milk. Clear the counter and set out everything.

### Steps
1. **Heat the milk.** Heat 1 cup of milk until it's hot and steaming but not boiling: about 1 to 2 minutes in the microwave or 3 minutes on the stove. Use an oven mitt.
2. **Add the acid.** Stir in 4 teaspoons of vinegar slowly. Watch: the milk separates into lumpy curds and a yellowish liquid. Keep stirring for a minute.
3. **Strain it.** Pour the mix through a strainer or a paper towel over a bowl. Let it cool a few minutes, then squeeze out as much liquid as you can.
4. **Knead it.** The curds are your plastic. Knead them together like dough for 2 minutes. Add food coloring now if you want.
5. **Shape it (15 minutes).** Press into cookie cutters, roll into beads, or shape a small figure, coaster or button. Poke holes for beads with a toothpick or a skewer.
6. **Try a second batch.** Change one thing: lemon juice instead of vinegar, half the vinegar, or skim milk instead of whole. Compare: how much plastic did you get? Is it smoother or crumblier?
7. **Dry it.** Set your shapes on a paper towel to dry and harden for 2 days. Write down the date.

### Make it yours
- Make a set of buttons or a necklace of beads.
- Mold a tiny sculpture of an animal or character.
- Make a "chemistry kit" display with your two batches side by side and labels.

### Stuck?
- No curds: the milk wasn't hot enough. Heat it a bit more.
- Plastic is too crumbly: you used too much vinegar or not enough squeezing.
- Shapes crack while drying: press them firmly together while they're still wet, and dry them slowly.

### Show it off
Show your family your plastic creations and explain what a polymer is in one sentence.

### Clean up
Rinse everything quickly, because the curds stick as they dry. Pour the liquid down the drain with lots of water.

### For the grown-up
This is chemistry: an acid changes the protein in milk into a natural polymer, like early plastics. The milk gets hot, so please be nearby and check that they're using an oven mitt. If anyone at home has a milk allergy, pick a different project. **Ask at dinner:** "What's a polymer, and how did you make one from milk?"

## 16. Invent a Dice Game with Odds

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** two six-sided dice (or make them from paper), paper, a pencil, a ruler, coins or beans as points

**Nice to have:** a third die, a sibling or grown-up to play against

### The big idea
Why is 7 the most common roll with two dice? The secret is probability. Map out every possible roll, then invent a dice game where the scoring matches the odds, so rare rolls feel amazing and the game is fair.

### Goals
- A chart of all 36 possible rolls of two dice.
- An original dice game with rules based on probability, tested at least twice.

### Objectives
- I can find the probability of each sum using a table.
- I can compare expected results with real results.
- I can use probability to balance a game.

### Get ready
Draw a 6 by 6 table. Label the top row 1 to 6 (die 1) and the left column 1 to 6 (die 2).

### Steps
1. **Fill the table.** In each box, add the two numbers. You'll have 36 sums.
2. **Count.** How many times does each sum appear? Sum 7 appears 6 times, so its probability is 6 out of 36, or 1 in 6. Sum 2 appears only once: 1 in 36.
3. **Test it (15 minutes).** Roll two dice 50 times and tally each sum. Does it match the table? Make a bar chart of your results.
4. **Design the scoring.** Rare rolls should be worth more. Example: sum 2 or 12 earns 10 points, sum 3 or 11 earns 5, and a 7 earns nothing.
5. **Add push-your-luck (20 minutes).** Make a rule where you can keep rolling to add points, but if you roll a certain sum, you lose everything you earned that turn. Which sum should be the danger number? (Hint: the more common it is, the riskier the game.)
6. **Name the game and write the rules.** Give it a theme: gold miners, potion makers, deep-sea divers.
7. **Play-test.** Play twice. Is it too easy to win? Too hard? Change one number and play again.

### Make it yours
- Add a third die. How does the probability change? (Make a new count.)
- Design a two-player game where you can steal points from each other on doubles.
- Make it a board game: dice sums move you along a track with risky shortcuts.

### Stuck?
- The table is confusing: color all the 7s first. They form a diagonal line.
- Real rolls don't match the table: roll more. The more you roll, the closer it gets.
- The game takes forever: lower the winning score.

### Show it off
Teach your game to your family and play a championship round.

### Clean up
Keep the dice, rules and score sheets in an envelope or small box.

### For the grown-up
This is probability, a big topic in seventh and eighth grade math, with real data, charts and game design. Comparing "what should happen" to "what did happen" is the heart of statistics. **Ask at dinner:** "Why is 7 the most common roll with two dice?"

**Nice to have:** a big sheet of paper for a family tree of gods, a stapler for a booklet

### The big idea
Every ancient culture told stories to explain the world: why the sun rises, where rivers come from, why cats hate water. Invent a people who never existed, and then write the myths they would tell: their gods, their heroes, and the stories they tell by the fire.

5. **Write a hero legend.** A human who did something great, or foolish. What did they want? Which gods helped or hurt them? What lesson does the story teach?
6. **Design a festival.** Your people celebrate one of these myths every year. What do they eat, wear and do?
7. **Illustrate.** Draw one scene from a myth, in the style of an ancient carving, a painted vase, or a woven tapestry.

### Make it yours
- Write a myth from the point of view of a god who thinks they're the hero, but clearly isn't.
- Make two peoples with competing myths about the same event.
- Write a modern myth for your town: why the traffic light on Main Street is always red.

### Stuck?
- Gods feel flat: give each one a want and a weakness.
- Myths feel random: connect them to the people's life. Fishing people care about the sea. Desert people care about water.

### Show it off

### Clean up
Staple your pages into a booklet with a title and keep it with your art.

### For the grown-up
This builds creative writing, world-building, and an understanding of myths and why cultures tell them, which comes up in English and social studies. **Ask at dinner:** "Which god or spirit in your mythology would you most want to meet?"

### Goals
- A cardboard chair that holds you sitting down for at least 30 seconds.

- I can test a design with weight before trusting it.

## 21. Scale-a-Recipe Cookie Bake

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** for 24 cookies: 1 cup (225 g) softened butter, three quarters of a cup of brown sugar, half a cup of white sugar, 2 eggs, 1 teaspoon vanilla, 2 and a quarter cups of flour, 1 teaspoon baking soda, half a teaspoon of salt, 1 and a half cups of chocolate chips or raisins or oats; measuring cups and spoons, a large bowl, a spoon or mixer, baking sheets, baking paper, an oven mitt, paper and a pencil

**Nice to have:** a kitchen scale, a cooling rack

**Get ready the night before:** Ask your grown-up for permission to use the oven and check that you have the ingredients.

### The big idea
Recipes are math problems you can eat. What if you only want 12 cookies, or you need 36 for a party? Scale a real recipe up or down using fractions, bake it, and see if your math is right.

### Goals
- A recipe rewritten at a new size, with all the math worked out.
- A batch of cookies baked from your scaled recipe.

### Objectives
- I can multiply fractions to scale a recipe.
- I can convert between measurements (cups, tablespoons, teaspoons).
- I can use the oven safely with permission.

### Get ready
Check allergies for everyone who'll eat the cookies (eggs, dairy, wheat, and no nuts unless everyone's okay). Wash your hands and clear the counter.

### Steps
1. **Pick your scale.** Half batch (12 cookies) = times one half. One and a half batches (36 cookies) = times one and a half. Write it at the top of a page.
2. **Do the math (15 minutes).** Rewrite every ingredient. Example for one and a half: three quarters of a cup of brown sugar times 1.5 is one and an eighth cups. One eighth of a cup is 2 tablespoons, so that's 1 cup plus 2 tablespoons. 2 eggs times 1.5 = 3 eggs. For a half batch, 2 eggs becomes 1 egg.
3. **Check it.** Have a grown-up or older sibling check two of your numbers, or double-check by working backward.
4. **Preheat.** Set the oven to 350 F (175 C). Line baking sheets with baking paper.
5. **Mix.** Beat the butter and both sugars until fluffy. Add the eggs and vanilla. In another bowl, mix the flour, baking soda and salt, then stir it into the butter mix. Fold in your add-ins.
6. **Shape and bake.** Roll tablespoon-sized balls and set them 5 cm apart. Bake 9 to 11 minutes, until the edges are golden. Use the oven mitt every time.
7. **Taste test.** Let them cool 10 minutes. Did your math work? Are they the same as the original recipe?

### Make it yours
- Invent your own add-in combo: oats and raisins, chocolate and dried cherries, cinnamon and white chocolate.
- Make one giant cookie from the whole dough in a pan. How does baking time change?
- Scale it for a crowd of 100. Write the recipe but don't bake it.

### Stuck?
- Cookies spread flat: the butter was too soft or melted. Chill the dough for 15 minutes.
- Math gets messy: turn every fraction into tablespoons first. 1 cup = 16 tablespoons.
- Cookies are hard: bake 1 or 2 minutes less next time.

### Show it off
Serve them with your math page next to the plate, so everyone can see the cookies were also a math test.

### Clean up
Turn off the oven, wash every bowl and spoon, wipe the counter and put ingredients away.

### For the grown-up
This is fraction multiplication and measurement conversion (grades 6 to 8 math), plus real baking. Middle schoolers can use the oven with your permission and a check-in or two; please make sure they use mitts. Check allergies for everyone eating. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you figure out how many eggs to use?"

## 22. Mystery Powder Forensics

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1 |

**Materials:** about 2 teaspoons each of five white kitchen powders (baking soda, cornstarch, flour, table salt, sugar), white vinegar, water, a muffin tin or 15 small cups, spoons, a dropper or a straw, a sheet of dark paper, masking tape, a marker, paper and a pencil, a tray

**Nice to have:** a magnifying glass, a grown-up or sibling to make the mystery samples

**Get ready the night before:** Ask a grown-up to secretly mix two "mystery" samples in cups labeled X and Y: one single powder, and one mix of two powders. They write the answers on a folded note.

### The big idea
White powder was found next to the empty cookie jar. Flour? Sugar? Baking soda? Forensic chemists identify unknown substances by testing their properties. Build a reference chart of five known powders, then crack the case.

### Goals
- A reference chart showing how five powders behave in four tests.
- The two mystery samples identified, with your evidence.

### Objectives
- I can test physical and chemical properties in a careful, repeatable way.
- I can record observations in a data table.
- I can use evidence to reach a conclusion and explain it.

### Get ready
Work on a tray. Label strips of tape for each powder and stick them on the muffin tin. Draw a data table: five rows (one per powder) and four columns (Look, Feel, Water, Vinegar). Lab rule: you never taste anything in a lab, even kitchen powders.

### Steps
1. **Test 1: Look (10 minutes).** Sprinkle a pinch of each powder on the dark paper. Use the magnifying glass if you have one. Salt and sugar are clear cube-like crystals. Flour, cornstarch and baking soda are fine dust. Write what you see.
2. **Test 2: Feel.** Rub a pinch between two fingers. Cornstarch squeaks and feels silky. Salt and sugar feel gritty. Note the differences, then wash your hands.
3. **Test 3: Water.** Put half a teaspoon of each powder in its own cup and add 2 teaspoons of water. Stir 20 times. Does it disappear (dissolve), turn cloudy, or sink and get gooey? Record it.
4. **Test 4: Vinegar.** Put half a teaspoon of each in a fresh cup and add 10 drops of vinegar. Look and listen. Which fizzes? That fizz is a chemical reaction making a gas.
5. **Study your chart.** Circle the test that tells each powder apart. Salt and sugar look alike: which test could split them? (Hint: dry a drop of each solution on dark paper overnight and compare the crystals.)
6. **Crack the case (20 minutes).** Run all four tests on mystery samples X and Y. Compare them to your chart. For the mix, look for clues from two powders at once: maybe it fizzes and turns cloudy.
7. **Write the forensic report.** For each sample: your answer, the evidence from each test, and how sure you are. Then open the note.

### Make it yours
- Write the crime story: three suspects, each with a different powder in their kitchen. Which one matches the evidence?
- Add a sixth powder, like powdered milk or baking powder (ask first). Can your tests still tell it apart?
- Make a blind challenge for someone else: mix samples for them and see if they can use your chart.

### Stuck?
- Two powders act the same: add a test. Try warm water, or wait 10 minutes and look again.
- The mix is confusing: test a tiny amount on its own with each powder you suspect, side by side.
- Nothing fizzes: you may have the baking soda cup mixed up. Check your labels.

### Show it off
Present your case to the family like a forensic expert in court: show the evidence, then name the powder.

### Clean up
Pour the cups down the sink with plenty of water, wash the tin, and wipe the tray. Used powders go in the trash, not back in the jars.

### For the grown-up
This is real chemistry: physical properties (look, feel, dissolving) versus a chemical change (the vinegar fizz), and drawing conclusions from evidence, all part of eighth grade physical science. Making the two mystery samples takes two minutes. **Ask at dinner:** "What was in mystery sample Y, and which test gave it away?"

## 24. Magic Squares Puzzle Pack

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper (graph paper if you have it), a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a pen, index cards

**Nice to have:** colored pencils, a calculator for checking

### The big idea
A magic square is a grid of numbers where every row, every column and both diagonals add up to the same total. People have been amazed by them for over 2,000 years. Crack the secret pattern, then make a pack of puzzles to stump your family.

### Goals
- Solved 3 by 3 and 4 by 4 magic squares.
- A pack of at least six puzzle cards with some numbers missing.

### Objectives
- I can find patterns in numbers and use them to solve a puzzle.
- I can use a method (an algorithm) to build a magic square.
- I can design puzzles with exactly one answer.

### Get ready
Draw several 3 by 3, 4 by 4 and 5 by 5 grids with squares about 2 cm wide.

### Steps
1. **Try the 3 by 3.** Use the numbers 1 to 9, once each. Every row, column and diagonal must add up to 15. Spend 10 minutes trying before you look at the hint. Hint: 5 goes in the middle, and the even numbers go in the corners.
2. **Figure out why 15.** The numbers 1 to 9 add up to 45. There are 3 rows, so each row is 45 divided by 3. Use the same idea to find the magic total for a 4 by 4 using 1 to 16 (hint: it's 34).
3. **Learn the stair-step method for odd squares.** For a 5 by 5 using 1 to 25: put 1 in the middle of the top row. Then move up one and right one for each next number. If you go off the top, wrap around to the bottom. If you go off the right, wrap to the left. If the square is already full, drop down one instead. Every line adds up to 65.
4. **Check it.** Add every row, column and diagonal. Circle any that don't match.
5. **Try the 4 by 4 (15 minutes).** Write 1 to 16 in order, left to right, row by row. Now swap each number on the two diagonals with its "opposite" (1 with 16, 6 with 11, and so on). Check that every line adds to 34.
6. **Make puzzle cards (20 minutes).** Copy your solved squares onto cards, but leave some numbers blank. Easy: 3 blanks. Hard: 6 blanks. Put the answer on the back. Make sure there's only one way to fill each card.
7. **Rate and order them** from easiest to hardest.

### Make it yours
- Make a magic square with a different set of numbers, like 2, 4, 6 and so on. What's the magic total now?
- Make an "Alphabet square" where the answer spells a word using A = 1, B = 2.
- Design a "magic star" or "magic triangle" with numbers on each point.

### Stuck?
- Lines don't add up: you probably repeated a number. Cross off each number as you use it.
- The stair-step gets confusing: draw the grid twice as big and number the moves with arrows.
- Puzzle has two answers: fill in one more number to lock it down.

### Show it off
Give each family member a card and see who can solve theirs first.

### Clean up
Keep your cards in an envelope with "Magic Squares" written on it.

### For the grown-up
This is number sense, patterns and algorithms (step-by-step methods), a fun connection to algebra, where the magic total comes from a formula. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you show us the trick for building a 5 by 5 magic square?"

## 25. Container Garden

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-90 min | Either | High | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 4 to 6 containers (yogurt cups, tin cans, milk cartons cut down, old pots), soil from the yard or potting soil, seeds from the kitchen (dried beans, lentils, whole dried peas, seeds scooped from a pepper, tomato or squash), a nail or scissors for drainage holes, a tray, water, paper, a pencil, sticks or craft sticks for labels

**Nice to have:** a spray bottle, pebbles for drainage, a ruler

### The big idea
Farmers and plant scientists don't just plant seeds and hope. They plan, run experiments, and keep records. Plant a container garden from seeds already in your kitchen on a windowsill, balcony, step or yard, and set it up as a real experiment you'll track for two weeks.

### Goals
- At least four planted containers, including a fair test of one growing condition.
- A growth log with predictions and a two-week plan for measuring.

### Objectives
- I can plant seeds and cuttings at the right depth with drainage.
- I can set up a fair test with one variable and a control.
- I can explain what plants need to grow.

### Get ready
Get permission for the containers and the spot. Find a place with at least 4 hours of sun a day: a sunny window, a balcony, or the yard. Spread newspaper or work outside: this gets messy.

### Steps
1. **Make drainage.** Poke 3 or 4 holes in the bottom of every container with a nail or scissors (have a grown-up help with cans). Without holes, roots rot.
2. **Pick your plants.** Mix fast and slow. Fast: dried beans and peas (sprout in 3 to 5 days), lentils. Slow: tomato, pepper or squash seeds (1 to 2 weeks). Rinse and dry seeds from fresh vegetables first.
3. **Fill and plant (20 minutes).** Fill containers with soil, leaving 2 cm at the top. Plant seeds about twice as deep as they are wide: a bean goes about 2 cm deep, a tomato seed barely half a centimeter.
4. **Label everything.** A stick in each container with the plant's name and today's date.
5. **Water.** Soak the soil until water drips from the bottom. Set the containers on a tray.
6. **Set up the experiment.** Plant two extra bean pots exactly the same way. Change one thing for one of them: a dark closet, half the water, or seeds planted 6 cm deep. The other is your control. In your log, make a table: pot, date planted, predicted sprout date, actual sprout date, and height every two days.
7. **Design a garden layout.** Draw where each container goes and why. Taller plants in back, sun lovers in front.

### Make it yours
- Run a second experiment: soak half your beans overnight before planting. Do they sprout faster?
- Make a "salad garden" plan: what would you need to grow a full salad?
- Make a garden for pollinators: plant flower seeds if you have any, and make a "bee hotel" sign.

### Stuck?
- Nothing sprouts after a week: some seeds from the store are treated to not grow. Try dried beans, which almost always work.
- The soil dries out fast: water when the top feels dry, and keep containers out of hot afternoon sun.
- Mold on the soil: water less and give it more air.

### Show it off
Give your family a "garden tour" and show your predictions. Update them when the first sprouts appear.

### Clean up
Sweep up soil, wash the tray and tools, and wash your hands well.

### For the grown-up
This builds life science (what plants need, germination), data collection over time, and patience. Help with poking holes in tin cans (sharp edges), and help them find a sunny spot that works for your home. **Ask at dinner:** "Which plant do you think will sprout first, and why?"

**Nice to have:** a craft knife (with permission), glue, a small wooden block, colored paper

### The big idea
A ukulele is just strings, a box and a neck. With a shoebox and rubber bands, you can build one that actually plays notes, and use real math to put the frets in the right places. Build it, tune it, and write a song.

### Goals
- A playable four-string shoebox ukulele with marked frets.
- A short tune you can play on it.

### Objectives
- I can explain how string length, tension and thickness change pitch.
- I can use measurements and a formula to place frets.
- I can tune an instrument and play a simple melody.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Trace a cup on the lid, about 5 cm from one end, for a sound hole.

### Steps
1. **Cut the sound hole.** Cut out the circle (start a hole with the pencil, then cut with scissors). This lets the sound out.
2. **Attach the neck.** Tape or glue the stick to the box so it sticks out about 30 cm from one end, along the center of the lid.
3. **Add the bridge and nut.** Tape a pencil across the lid on the far side of the sound hole: that's the bridge. Tape the second pencil across the top end of the neck: that's the nut. Measure the distance between them. That's your string length. Try to make it 30 cm.
4. **String it.** Stretch the 4 rubber bands the long way around the box and neck, so they pass over the bridge and the nut. Space them about 1 cm apart. Thickest band on top.
5. **Tune it (15 minutes).** Pluck each string. Tighter or thinner = higher pitch. Adjust the bands or the bridge until each string sounds different, from low to high.
6. **Mark the frets.** For a 30 cm string, mark these distances from the nut: 1.7 cm, 3.3 cm, 4.8 cm, 6.2 cm, 7.5 cm, 10 cm, 15 cm. (Each fret shortens the string a little less than the one before. The 15 cm fret is exactly half the string: one octave up.) Tape thin strips of paper there.
7. **Play.** Press a string down at each fret and pluck. Make up a 6 to 8 note tune and practice it.

### Make it yours
- Decorate the box: paint it or wrap it in colored paper.
- Build a bass version with thicker bands and a longer neck, and compare the sound.
- Write a short song with lyrics and play it on your ukulele.

### Stuck?
- Strings sound buzzy: lift them higher at the bridge with a taller pencil or a block.
- The neck bends: make it stronger with a second stick or more tape.
- The notes are too quiet: make the sound hole a bit bigger, or use a stronger box.

### Show it off
Give a short concert for your family with your tune, and explain how the frets work.

### Clean up
Put away the tools and store the ukulele where it won't get crushed.

### For the grown-up
This is the physics of sound (vibration, frequency, string length) and real math: fret spacing comes from a ratio based on the number 12. A craft knife is fine with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you figure out where to put the frets?"

## 28. Paper Football League

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, tape, a table, a pencil, a ruler, markers, index cards

**Nice to have:** a sibling, friend or grown-up to play against, a cup or small box for a trophy

### The big idea
A folded paper triangle, a table, and a flick of your finger: that's tabletop paper football. But you're not just playing. You're the league commissioner. Make the teams, set the rules, run a season, keep the stats, and crown a champion.

### Goals
- At least four teams and a schedule for a short season.
- A completed season with a standings table and a champion.

### Objectives
- I can design rules that are fair and clear.
- I can keep stats and calculate averages and percentages.
- I can run a tournament from start to finish.

### Get ready
Fold the football: cut a strip of paper about 4 cm wide and 30 cm long. Fold one corner over to make a triangle, then keep folding the triangle down the strip. Tuck the end in and tape it.

### Steps
1. **Learn the basics.** Players sit at opposite ends of a table. Take turns flicking the football. If it stops hanging over the edge without falling, that's a touchdown (6 points). Then you get a field goal kick: the other player makes a goal post with their thumbs and index fingers, and you flick it through (1 point).
2. **Write the official rules.** How many flicks per turn? What happens if it goes off the side? How long is a game? Write them on a rules card.
3. **Create teams (15 minutes).** Make at least four teams with names, colors and logos on index cards. Invent mascots: the Harbor Owls, the Desert Tacos.
4. **Make a schedule.** Every team plays every other team once. With four teams, that's six games.
5. **Play the season (30 minutes).** Play each game. If you're playing alone, flick for both teams and play fair. With a partner, take turns being different teams.
6. **Keep stats.** For each team: wins, losses, points scored, touchdowns, field goals made and attempted. Calculate field goal percentage: made divided by attempted.
7. **Playoffs.** Top two teams play in the championship. Make a trophy for the winner.

### Make it yours
- Add special plays: a "trick shot" off a book worth bonus points.
- Make player cards for star flickers with stats.
- Write a short newspaper story about the championship game.

### Stuck?
- The football is too floppy: fold it tighter and tape it more.
- Every flick goes off the table: flick softer, with the middle of your finger.
- It gets boring alone: give each team a personality and do play-by-play commentary out loud.

### Show it off
Host the championship game at dinner and have someone at home be the guest commentator.

### Clean up
Keep the football, teams and stats in an envelope labeled "League Office."

### For the grown-up
This builds rule design, fairness, and real stats: averages and percentages, both in grades 6 to 8 math. It's also fine-motor practice and a great game with a sibling or a grown-up. **Ask at dinner:** "Who won your league, and who had the best field goal percentage?"

## 29. Day Trip Itinerary

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-120 min | Either | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, a ruler, colored pencils, a calendar

**Nice to have:** a paper map of your town or area, brochures, a bus or train timetable, a compass

### The big idea
Planning a trip is like solving a puzzle: how do you fit the most fun into one day with a limited budget, travel time, and a family that gets hungry at noon? You're the trip planner. Plan an outdoor day trip for your family, then go scout part of it.

### Goals
- A full itinerary for an outdoor day trip, hour by hour, with a budget.
- A scouting report from a short walk to check one part of the plan.

### Objectives
- I can plan a schedule that fits travel time, activities and breaks.
- I can make a budget and stay within it.
- I can plan for problems like bad weather.

### Get ready
Talk to your grown-up about a possible trip: a park, a beach, a lake, a hiking trail, a free outdoor festival, or a long walk around your own town. Ask what the budget is (zero dollars is a fun challenge). Ask what's realistic for travel.

### Steps
1. **Pick the destination.** Write down three options and why. Choose the best one with your grown-up.
2. **Plan the schedule (20 minutes).** Break the day into chunks: travel there, morning activity, lunch, afternoon activity, travel home. Add times. Example: "9:00 leave home. 9:30 arrive at the trail. 9:45 to 11:30 hike the 4 km loop. 11:45 picnic lunch by the pond."
3. **Make a budget.** List costs: travel (gas, bus fare), food, entry fees. If the budget is zero, pack everything from home and pick free places.
4. **Draw the map.** Sketch the route from home and the main stops. Mark where to find bathrooms, water, and shade.
5. **Make the packing list.** Water, snacks, sunscreen, layers, a first aid kit, a bag for trash.
6. **Plan B.** What if it rains? What if someone's tired? Write a backup plan for each.
7. **Scout it (30 minutes).** With your grown-up's permission, scout a nearby part of the trip on foot, like the walk to the bus stop or a local park. Note how long it took and anything that might change your plan.

### Make it yours
- Plan a "mystery trip": give your family clues about where they're going instead of telling them.
- Plan a day trip for a stuffed animal, with a postcard from each stop.
- Plan an adventure in your own neighborhood: a walking tour of five spots you love.

### Stuck?
- The schedule's too tight: add 15 minutes of buffer for every hour. Things always take longer.
- No idea where to go: ask a grown-up where they went as a kid, or pick the closest green space.
- The budget won't work: cut one paid thing, or swap it for something free.

### Show it off
Present your itinerary to your family like a travel agent, with the map and budget, and ask them to "book" the trip.

### Clean up
Put your plan in a folder so it's ready for the day you go.

### For the grown-up
This builds planning, time math, budgeting and map skills. Please set the rules for scouting outside the yard: where they can go, with whom, and when they'll be back. **Ask at dinner:** "If we take your trip, what's the first thing we'll do when we get there?"

## 30. Peace Treaty Summit

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, markers or colored pencils, index cards, a table and two chairs, a ruler, string or tape for a border

**Nice to have:** a sibling, friend or grown-up after work as the rival ambassador, a fancy pen for the signing, a scarf or jacket for a "diplomat" look

### The big idea
Two tiny nations are on the edge of a ridiculous war: the Republic of the Top Bunk and the Kingdom of the Bottom Bunk, or the Couch Federation and the Armchair Empire. You're the ambassador sent to stop it. Build your nation, plan your demands, and negotiate a peace treaty both sides can sign.

### Goals
- A nation with a flag, a motto and a list of what it wants.
- A signed treaty with at least five articles that both sides agreed to.

### Objectives
- I can figure out what each side really needs, not just what it says it wants.
- I can make a trade where both sides get something.
- I can write clear, exact rules that leave no loopholes.

### Get ready
Pick the two nations. They can be two parts of your home, two invented countries, or your nation and a rival played by stuffed animals. Use string or tape to mark the "border" on the floor or table. Set up the summit table with a chair on each side.

### Steps
1. **Build your nation (15 minutes).** Name it, draw a flag, and write a motto ("Comfort Before All"). List its resources: what does it have plenty of? (Pillows, snacks, the good window.)
2. **Build the rival.** Do the same for the other side, or have your partner do it. Every nation needs something the other one has.
3. **Write the dispute.** One paragraph explaining the conflict. Example: "The Couch Federation claims the blanket. The Armchair Empire says it has been draped over the armchair for three generations."
4. **Plan your strategy.** On index cards, write your three demands, ranked from "must have" to "nice to have." Then write two things you're willing to give up. Diplomats call the line you won't cross a "red line."
5. **Hold the summit (25 minutes).** Each side gives a two-minute opening statement. Then negotiate one issue at a time. Try "if you..., then we...": "If you share the blanket on weekends, we'll give you control of the footstool." If you're playing alone, argue both sides honestly, switching chairs each time.
6. **Write the treaty.** Number each article and make it exact. Not "share the blanket" but "Article 3: The blanket belongs to the Couch Federation from Monday to Thursday and to the Armchair Empire from Friday to Sunday."
7. **The signing.** Read it aloud, both sides sign with the fancy pen, and shake hands for the history books.

### Make it yours
- Add a third nation that changes the balance of power.
- Hit the summit with a crisis halfway through: a "natural disaster" (someone sits on the remote) that both sides have to solve together.
- Make it real: negotiate an actual house issue with a sibling, like who picks the music in the car.

### Stuck?
- Both sides refuse to budge: ask "Why do you want that?" The real reason often has a different fix.
- The treaty has a loophole: read every article and ask "How could someone cheat this?" Then rewrite it.
- It gets too heated: call a 5-minute recess. Real diplomats do it all the time.

### Show it off
Hold the official signing ceremony at dinner and have your family act as the world press.

### Clean up
Take up the border tape and keep the signed treaty somewhere official, like the fridge.

### For the grown-up
This builds negotiation, perspective-taking and precise writing, plus a playful look at how countries resolve conflicts (civics and history). It can also be a low-stakes way to work out a real sibling dispute. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you give up to get the treaty signed, and was it worth it?"
