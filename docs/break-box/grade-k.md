## 1. Box Rocket to the Moon

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a cardboard box your child can sit in, a sharp knife or scissors (grown-up only), tape, markers or crayons, two paper towel tubes, paper plates, aluminum foil, a stuffed animal "co-pilot"

**Nice to have:** a flashlight, bottle caps for buttons (glued on by a grown-up), a gray blanket or pillows for the moon

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Mission control is calling! We need a brave astronaut to fly to the Moon. But first we have to build the rocket. Are you ready to blast off?"

### Goals
- Your child builds a rocket big enough to sit in and flies it to the Moon.
- Your child plans a mission and tells what they found on the Moon.

### Objectives
- Your child can choose where the parts of the rocket go (window, buttons, engines) and say why.
- Your child can count backward from 10 to 1 for the launch.
- Your child can use space words: astronaut, rocket, Moon, launch.

### Get ready
Clear a spot on the floor big enough for the box and a little room around it. Tape the bottom flaps of the box shut so it is sturdy. Cut a round window about the size of a dinner plate in one side (grown-up only). Set out the markers, foil, tubes and plates.

### Steps
1. Show your child the box and ask: "Where should the window go? Where do the engines go?" Let them point, and mark the spots with a marker.
2. **Make the engines.** Help your child wrap the two paper towel tubes in foil. Tape them to the back of the box as rocket boosters.
3. **Make the control panel** (15 minutes). Your child draws buttons, dials and a big red LAUNCH button on a paper plate. Tape it inside the box. Ask: "What does this button do?" and write their answer next to it.
4. Your child names the rocket and you write the name in big letters on the side. Let them color the letters in.
5. Pick a co-pilot. Buckle the stuffed animal in with a strip of tape "seat belt."
6. **Blast off!** Your child climbs in. Count down together from 10 to 1, then shake the box gently and make the loudest rocket noise you can.
7. **Land on the Moon** (20 minutes). Turn the room into the Moon: pillows are craters, a gray blanket is moon dust. Your child takes big, slow, floaty moon steps and collects "moon rocks" (balled-up foil).
8. Fly home and splash down on the couch.

### Make it yours
- Fly somewhere else: to a planet made of candy, a star with a dragon on it, or Grandma's house in space.
- Make it a two-seater and take a sibling or a grown-up as passenger, with tickets drawn on paper.
- Add an alien your child meets on the Moon, made from a sock or a drawing, and have them decide what it says.

### Stuck?
- The box is too small to sit in: make a rocket backpack instead from a cereal box and two tubes, and your child "flies" around the room.
- Your child wants to fly before building: fly first in the plain box, then come back to "fix the rocket" after a "bumpy trip."

### Show it off
When the family gets home, hold a launch. Your child gives the countdown and tells everyone what they saw on the Moon.

### Clean up
Collect the moon rocks in a bowl for next time. Your child puts markers back in the box and stacks the pillows.

### For the grown-up
This builds early science (the Moon, space words), counting backward, planning and storytelling. Do all cutting yourself and keep any bottle caps or small buttons glued down well, since loose small parts are a choking risk at this age. Check the box for staples before your child climbs in. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most surprising thing you found on the Moon?"

## 2. Sock Puppet Family

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 3 or 4 old socks (odd socks are perfect), paper, markers, glue stick or school glue, tape, scissors (grown-up only), scraps of yarn, fabric or paper for hair

**Nice to have:** felt, a few big buttons sewn or hot-glued on by a grown-up, a cardboard box for a stage

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Somewhere in the laundry basket, a whole family of sock people is waiting to wake up! They need faces, hair and names. You get to make them and give them voices."

### Goals
- Your child makes a family of at least three sock puppets, each with a name.
- Your child puts on a short puppet show where the puppets talk to each other.

### Objectives
- Your child can make choices about color and shape to show a character (a grumpy face, a happy face).
- Your child can give each puppet a different voice.
- Your child can tell a tiny story with a beginning, middle and end.

### Get ready
Gather the odd socks. Cut out a pile of paper shapes ahead of time: circles for eyes (about the size of a quarter, two per puppet), ovals for noses, curved strips for mouths, and short strips of yarn or paper for hair. Spread newspaper on the table.

### Steps
1. Put a sock on your hand and make it "wake up" and yawn. Ask your child: "Who should be in your sock family? A grandma? A baby? A pet?" Write the list.
2. **Faces** (20 minutes). For each sock, your child chooses eyes, a nose and a mouth from the pile and glues them on while the sock is on your hand (it keeps the shape). Ask: "Is this one happy, sleepy or silly?"
3. **Hair and extras.** Your child picks hair for each puppet. Ideas: yarn curls, paper spikes, a fabric bow, a paper hat.
4. Name every puppet. Write the names on tape and stick it inside each sock cuff.
5. **Find the voices.** Your child tries each puppet: a deep voice, a squeaky voice, a sing-song voice. Let them pick.
6. **Practice the show** (15 minutes). Pick one problem for the family: the baby lost a shoe, or the dog ate the birthday cake. The puppets talk about it and fix it.
7. Turn a table on its side or drape a blanket over two chairs for a stage, and perform.

### Make it yours
- Make a monster family instead, with three eyes, paper teeth and wild hair.
- Give one puppet a secret (it can talk to animals), and build the show around it.
- Make the puppets sing a song together instead of talking.

### Stuck?
- Glue won't stick to the sock: use tape loops, or stuff a paper ball inside so you can press harder.
- Your child is shy to do voices: you do one puppet, they do the other, and take turns.

### Show it off
Give a puppet show after dinner. Your child can make paper tickets and hand them out.

### Clean up
Lay the puppets flat to dry. Your child collects scraps into a bag to save for the next project.

### For the grown-up
This builds fine motor skills, early storytelling and expressive language. Kids this age often say more through a puppet than in their own voice. Do all cutting, and skip small loose buttons or googly eyes unless they are sewn or glued down very well. **Ask at dinner:** "Which puppet is the funniest, and what does it like to do?"

## 3. Blanket Fort Kingdom

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 to 4 blankets or sheets, kitchen chairs, couch cushions, pillows, clothespins or chip clips, paper, crayons, tape, stuffed animals

**Nice to have:** a flashlight, string lights (plugged in by a grown-up), a cardboard crown

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Today you are the ruler of a brand-new kingdom! Every kingdom needs a castle, a flag and some loyal subjects. Let's build your castle out of blankets and decide the rules of your land."

### Goals
- Your child builds a blanket fort castle big enough to sit inside.
- Your child creates a kingdom with a name, a flag and a royal rule.

### Objectives
- Your child can solve a building problem (the roof sags, the wall falls) by trying a new idea.
- Your child can draw a flag with a symbol that means something to them.
- Your child can make up a rule and explain why it is fair.

### Get ready
Pick a room where the fort can stay up for a while. Move fragile things away. Pull out 4 chairs, the blankets, the cushions and the clips. Set paper and crayons nearby.

### Steps
1. Ask your child: "What should your castle look like? Tall? Long? One room or two?" Let them pick a spot.
2. **Build the walls** (20 minutes). Set chairs back to back about one blanket-width apart. Your child drapes a blanket over the tops and you clip the corners. Cushions stand up as side walls.
3. Test it. Can the ruler sit inside? If the roof sags in the middle, ask your child how to fix it. (Ideas: another chair, a broom across the top, a tighter pull.)
4. **Name the kingdom.** Help your child pick a name, like "The Kingdom of Pancakes." Write it on a sign and tape it to the door.
5. **Make the flag** (15 minutes). Your child draws a symbol for the kingdom: a favorite animal, a star, a color. Tape it to a wooden spoon or rolled-up paper and stick it in the roof.
6. **Royal subjects.** Bring in the stuffed animals. Your child gives each one a job: guard, cook, royal dragon.
7. **The royal rule.** The ruler makes one rule for the kingdom ("Everyone must hop on Tuesdays"). Write it on paper and read it out in a royal voice.
8. Hold a royal feast inside the fort with a snack.

### Make it yours
- Make it an underwater kingdom: blue blankets, fish drawings and a mermaid ruler.
- Add a secret tunnel made from a long cushion row or a big box.
- Your child becomes a dragon guarding treasure instead of a king or queen.

### Stuck?
- The blankets keep sliding off: put heavy books on the ends of the blanket on the chair seats.
- Your child is frustrated with building: you build the frame, they do the inside, the sign and the flag.

### Show it off
Give the family a royal tour of the kingdom. The ruler reads the royal rule and everyone has to follow it for one minute.

### Clean up
Fold blankets together (one person holds each end, walk to the middle). Your child returns the stuffed animals to their "homes."

### For the grown-up
This builds problem solving, early engineering (balance, support), drawing and fairness talk. If you use string lights, keep them away from the blankets' folds and turn them off when no one is inside. Keep heavy items off the top of the fort. **Ask at dinner:** "If you were really in charge for one day, what rule would you make?"

## 4. Stuffed Animal Doctor Clinic

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 4 to 6 stuffed animals, a towel for an exam bed, an old shirt for a doctor coat, cotton balls, toilet paper or strips of cloth for bandages, tape, paper, crayons, a spoon, an empty clean box for a doctor's bag

**Nice to have:** a toy doctor kit, a kitchen scale, a measuring tape, adhesive bandages

### The big idea
Read aloud: "The animals are not feeling well! Bunny has a tummy ache and Bear hurt his paw. Dr. You is needed at the clinic right away. Grab your doctor coat!"

### Goals
- Your child runs a clinic, checks every patient and helps each one feel better.
- Your child makes a get-well card or chart for at least one patient.

### Objectives
- Your child can listen to a problem and choose a way to help.
- Your child can measure and compare (who is taller, who is heavier).
- Your child can use body words: heart, tummy, paw, knee, temperature.

### Get ready
Set up a "waiting room" (a row of stuffed animals on the couch) and an "exam room" (a towel on the floor or a table). Put the bandages, cotton balls, spoon and crayons in the doctor's bag. Roll up the shirt sleeves so it fits as a coat.

### Steps
1. Help your child put on the doctor coat. Ask: "What is the name of your clinic?" Write it on a sign.
2. You play the parent of each patient. Bring the first animal over and say what is wrong: "Doctor, Bear fell off his bike and his paw hurts."
3. **The checkup** (5 minutes per patient). Your child listens to the heart (ear on the chest, or a toilet paper tube as a stethoscope), checks the temperature (a spoon under the arm), and looks in the ears.
4. **The fix.** Your child decides what each patient needs: a bandage, a rest, "medicine" from a spoon, a hug. Let them choose. There is no wrong cure.
5. **Measure the patients.** Lay each one next to a measuring tape, or line them up by height. Ask: "Who is the tallest? Who is the smallest?" Weigh them on a kitchen scale if you have one.
6. **Doctor's notes** (10 minutes). Your child draws a picture of each patient with their bandage. Write what they say: "Bear: bad paw. Needs 2 hugs."
7. Send patients home with a "get well" sticker drawn on paper.

### Make it yours
- Make it a vet clinic for wild animals: a dragon with a sore throat, a dinosaur with a wobbly tooth.
- Add an ambulance (a laundry basket pulled with a scarf) to rush patients in.
- Your child becomes the patient, and a stuffed animal is the doctor.

### Stuck?
- Your child runs out of ideas: hand them a patient with a silly problem ("Giraffe has hiccups!").
- The bandages unroll: tape the ends, or use a sock pulled over the paw as a cast.

### Show it off
Make a "patient wall": tape the doctor's notes up. When the family gets home, your child tells each patient's story.

### Clean up
Unwrap the bandages and put them in the doctor's bag. Patients go back to bed (the shelf).

### For the grown-up
Pretend doctor play helps kids feel less nervous about real checkups. It builds care, listening, measurement and body vocabulary. If your child has a checkup coming, act one out. Keep real medicine out of the play. **Ask at dinner:** "Which patient was the hardest to fix, and what did you do?"

## 5. Shape Monster Hunt

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 45-75 min | Either | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, crayons or markers, scissors (grown-up only), tape, a paper bag or box, a paper towel tube

**Nice to have:** a clipboard, a flashlight, stickers

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Shape Monsters are hiding all over the house! They look like circles, squares, triangles and rectangles. They are sneaky, but if you know your shapes, you can find them. Ready to hunt?"

### Goals
- Your child finds at least 12 shapes hiding in the house (or yard).
- Your child invents their own Shape Monster.

### Objectives
- Your child can name circle, square, triangle and rectangle.
- Your child can find each shape in real things (a plate is a circle, a door is a rectangle).
- Your child can sort what they find into groups.

### Get ready
On one sheet of paper, draw four big boxes and draw a shape in each: circle, square, triangle, rectangle. Give each shape monster eyes and teeth. This is the "Monster Map." Make a "monster scope" from the paper towel tube.

### Steps
1. Show the Monster Map. Introduce each monster in a funny voice: "I am Circle! I am round with no corners!" Trace each one with your child's finger and count the corners.
2. **Practice spotting.** Look around the room together. Ask: "Where is Circle hiding?" (A clock? A plate?)
3. **The hunt** (20 minutes). Your child walks through the house with the monster scope. Each time they find a shape, they say "Gotcha!" and you make a tally mark in that monster's box.
4. Let your child lead the route and choose which room to search next.
5. **Count the catch.** Which monster had the most hiding spots? Which was hardest to find? (Triangles are usually tricky!)
6. **Invent a monster** (15 minutes). Your child draws their own Shape Monster built from shapes: a square body, triangle ears, circle eyes. Help name it ("Trianglo the Crunchy").
7. Cut it out and tape it somewhere in the house for someone else to find.

### Make it yours
- Hunt outside: round wheels, rectangle windows, triangle roofs.
- Hunt for colors or letters instead of shapes.
- Make it a race: how many circles can your child find before you count to 60?

### Stuck?
- Triangles are hard to find: look at a folded napkin, a roof in a picture, a slice of bread cut corner to corner.
- Square or rectangle confusion: count the sides. If all four sides look the same length, it is a square.

### Show it off
Your child hides their own Shape Monster drawing for a family member to find after dinner, then tells where the other monsters were hiding.

### Clean up
Your child collects the monster drawings and puts them in an envelope for next time.

### For the grown-up
This is early geometry: naming shapes, counting sides and corners, and seeing shapes in the real world, all kindergarten math goals. Tally marks are a first step to counting in groups of five. **Ask at dinner:** "Where was the sneakiest place a Shape Monster was hiding?"

## 6. Ice Rescue

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** small plastic toys (bigger than a ping-pong ball), a muffin tin or small plastic containers, water, a freezer, table salt, warm water in a cup, a spoon, a towel, a baking pan or tray to work in

**Nice to have:** food coloring, a turkey baster or squeeze bottle, a magnifying glass

**Get ready the night before:** Put one toy in each muffin cup, fill with water, add a drop of food coloring if you have it, and freeze overnight.

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Oh no! A freezing storm trapped your toys inside blocks of ice! You are the rescue team. Can you figure out the fastest way to set them free?"

### Goals
- Every toy is rescued from the ice.
- Your child finds out which tool melts ice the fastest.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a guess before testing ("I think warm water will be fastest").
- Your child can compare two ways of melting and say which worked better.
- Your child can use new words: freeze, melt, solid, liquid.

### Get ready
Pop the ice blocks out of the tin (run warm water on the bottom for a few seconds) and set them in the baking pan. Put the salt, warm water, spoon and towel next to the pan. Lay the towel under the pan.

### Steps
1. Let your child hold an ice block for a moment. Ask: "How does it feel? Is it hard or soft?" Use the word **solid**.
2. Show the rescue tools: salt, warm water, the spoon. Ask your child to guess which will free a toy fastest. Draw a smiley next to their pick on a scrap of paper.
3. **Rescue one: salt.** Sprinkle a spoonful of salt on one block. Wait and watch for cracks and little tunnels (5 minutes). Look with the magnifying glass if you have it.
4. **Rescue two: warm water.** Pour or squeeze warm water slowly over a second block. Count out loud to see how long until the toy's head pops out.
5. **Rescue three: your child's own idea.** Let them try something: breathing on it, holding it in their hands, tapping with the spoon. Every idea is worth testing.
6. Free the rest of the toys any way your child likes (20 minutes). Ask: "Where did the ice go?" The answer is that it became water, a **liquid**.
7. Line up the rescued toys and give each one a "warm-up blanket" (a paper towel).

### Make it yours
- Make it a story: the toys are explorers stuck at the North Pole, and your child is the rescue pilot.
- Freeze toys in layers (fill halfway, freeze, add more) for a "two-level" ice block that is harder to crack.
- Freeze two toys in yogurt cups and race two rescue tools side by side.

### Stuck?
- Melting is too slow: switch to warmer water (warm, not hot) or break the block with a gentle tap of the spoon on the pan.
- Your child loses interest: hide a "secret" toy inside one block and let them guess what it is as it melts.

### Show it off
Line up the rescued toys for a "welcome home" parade when the rest of the family gets home, and let your child tell how each one was saved.

### Clean up
Pour the water down the sink, dry the toys, and wipe the pan. Your child can be in charge of drying.

### For the grown-up
This is a first science experiment: guess, test, compare, explain. It builds early ideas about solids and liquids that come back in kindergarten and grade 1 science. Use warm tap water, never hot. Keep toys bigger than a ping-pong ball so nothing small ends up in a mouth. Salt stings eyes and cuts, so watch hands. **Ask at dinner:** "Which rescue tool worked best, and why do you think so?"

## 7. Pattern Necklaces

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 45-75 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** yarn or string, tape, uncooked tube pasta (like rigatoni or penne), drinking straws cut into 1-inch pieces (grown-up cuts), paper, markers, scissors (grown-up only), a muffin tin or bowls for sorting

**Nice to have:** food coloring and a zip bag to dye pasta, a hole punch for paper shapes

**Get ready the night before:** If you want colored pasta, put a handful of pasta in a zip bag with a few drops of food coloring and a splash of vinegar, shake, and spread it on foil to dry.

### The big idea
Read aloud: "The Queen of Patterns has lost her necklace! She needs a new one made with a secret pattern. Can you make a necklace that goes red, blue, red, blue all the way around?"

### Goals
- Your child makes at least one pattern necklace to wear.
- Your child invents their own pattern and makes a second necklace for someone else.

### Objectives
- Your child can copy an AB pattern (pasta, straw, pasta, straw).
- Your child can say what comes next in a pattern.
- Your child can make a new pattern of their own, like ABB or ABC.

### Get ready
Cut yarn about 24 inches long (long enough to slip over your child's head when tied). Wrap 2 inches of tape tightly around one end to make a stiff "needle." Tie a piece of pasta to the other end so nothing slides off. Sort the pasta and straw pieces into the muffin tin cups.

### Steps
1. Make a pattern on the table with pasta and straws: pasta, straw, pasta, straw. Ask your child: "What comes next?" Play this a few times, with you making mistakes for them to catch.
2. **First necklace** (15 minutes). Your child threads pasta, straw, pasta, straw onto the yarn. Say the pattern out loud together like a chant.
3. Stop halfway and check: "Is the pattern still going?" Let your child find any oops.
4. Tie it off (grown-up) and try it on.
5. **The secret pattern** (15 minutes). Your child invents a new pattern. Ideas: two pasta, one straw. Or pasta, straw, paper heart. Draw the pattern on paper first as a "recipe."
6. Your child makes the second necklace for someone special and decides who gets it.
7. Make a matching bracelet for a stuffed animal using a shorter piece of yarn.

### Make it yours
- Make paper shape beads: draw small shapes, you cut them out and punch a hole, and your child adds them to the pattern.
- Make a sound pattern to go with it: clap for pasta, stomp for straw, then "play" the necklace.
- Make a giant pattern snake across the floor with socks, spoons and blocks.

### Stuck?
- Threading is hard: make the tape needle longer and stiffer, or use a pipe cleaner as the string.
- The pattern breaks: lay the pieces out in a line on the table first, check it, then thread them in order.

### Show it off
Give the secret-pattern necklace as a gift. The receiver has to guess the pattern out loud.

### Clean up
Sweep up loose pasta and straw bits. Your child puts leftover pieces in a zip bag for next time.

### For the grown-up
Patterns are a big kindergarten math idea and a first step to algebra: noticing what repeats and predicting what comes next. Threading also builds the hand muscles kids use for writing. Pasta and straw pieces are small, so stay close and keep them away from younger siblings. Do all cutting yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you tell me the secret pattern in your necklace?"

## 8. Paper Plate Animal Masks

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper plates (one per mask), crayons or markers, scissors (grown-up only), tape or glue, yarn or string, scrap paper, cotton balls, a pencil

**Nice to have:** paint and brushes, a hole punch, construction paper, feathers

### The big idea
Read aloud: "The animals are having a parade, and you are invited! But you can only march if you come as an animal. Which animal will you be? A lion? A frog? A creature no one has ever seen?"

### Goals
- Your child makes one or two animal masks to wear.
- Your child leads an animal parade, moving and sounding like their animal.

### Objectives
- Your child can name features of an animal (whiskers, mane, beak) and add them to the mask.
- Your child can use shapes and colors to make a face.
- Your child can move and make sounds like their animal.

### Get ready
For each mask, hold a plate up to your child's face and mark where the eyes go with a pencil. Cut two eye holes about the size of a quarter (grown-up only). Punch or poke a small hole on each side and tie on yarn long enough to go around the head. Spread newspaper on the table.

### Steps
1. Ask your child: "What animal do you want to be?" Talk about what makes that animal special. A lion has a big mane. A bird has a beak. A cat has whiskers.
2. **Color the face** (15 minutes). Your child colors the plate the main color of the animal.
3. **Add the features** (20 minutes). Your child tells you what shapes they need: triangle ears, a long beak, a curly mane. You cut, they glue or tape. Cotton balls make a fluffy sheep. Paper strips curled around a pencil make a lion's mane.
4. Try the mask on in front of a mirror. Ask: "Do you look like your animal? What else does it need?"
5. **Find the moves.** Your child practices: How does the animal walk? What sound does it make? Does it hop, stomp, slither or fly?
6. Make a second mask for you or a stuffed animal, so the parade has more than one marcher.
7. **The parade** (10 minutes). March through the house in a line. Your child is the leader and calls out moves: "Everybody stomp like an elephant!"

### Make it yours
- Make a mixed-up animal: half tiger, half owl. Give it a new name.
- Make masks for a whole jungle and act out a story where they meet at the river.
- Make a quiet animal parade at bedtime, all tiptoeing like cats.

### Stuck?
- The mask is uncomfortable: hold it up on a craft stick or a paper roll taped to the bottom instead of tying it.
- Glue won't hold heavy parts: use tape on the back of the plate.

### Show it off
Hold the parade for the family when they get home. Your child can hand out masks so everyone marches together.

### Clean up
Lay masks flat to dry. Your child picks up scraps and puts the crayons back.

### For the grown-up
This builds fine motor skills, shape knowledge, animal facts and gross motor play. Kids learn a lot about animals by being them for a while. Do all cutting yourself and make sure the eye holes are big enough to see well while walking. **Ask at dinner:** "If you could be your animal for a whole day, what would you do first?"

## 9. Sink or Float Boat Party

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a bathtub, sink or big plastic bin of water, a towel, aluminum foil, a plastic lid, a sponge, a spoon, a crayon, a cork or bottle cap, an apple or orange, a toy car, a big rock, a plastic bottle, pennies or small washers (grown-up handles), paper and a marker

**Nice to have:** a muffin tin to sort, small toy animals as passengers

### The big idea
Read aloud: "The toys are having a party on the water, but some of them keep sinking to the bottom! Let's test what floats and what sinks, then build a boat that can carry passengers to the party."

### Goals
- Your child tests at least 10 things to see if they sink or float.
- Your child builds a foil boat that carries passengers.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a guess (sink or float) before each test.
- Your child can sort things into two groups: sink and float.
- Your child can change the shape of a boat to help it carry more.

### Get ready
Fill the tub or bin about 6 inches deep with water. Lay a towel under it. Make a chart on paper with two columns: "Sink" (draw a down arrow) and "Float" (draw a wave). Gather the test objects on a tray.

### Steps
1. Hold up each object. Ask your child: "Sink or float?" Have them show thumbs up for float and thumbs down for sink.
2. **Test them** (15 minutes). Your child drops each one in gently. Was the guess right? Draw the object in the right column, or set it in the right pile.
3. Surprise test: drop in an orange. Then peel half of it and test again. (The peel helps it float!)
4. **Boat building** (15 minutes). Give your child a square of foil about the size of a sheet of paper. Ask: "How can we make this float and carry passengers?" Help them fold up the edges to make walls.
5. **Passenger test.** Your child adds small toys, or you add pennies one at a time while your child counts. How many before it sinks?
6. Make a new boat with a different shape: tall walls, wide and flat, or long and skinny. Which carries the most? Let your child choose the design.
7. **The party.** Float all the boats, add passengers, and make waves with your hands. Which boat survives the storm?

### Make it yours
- Make a pirate boat with a paper sail on a toothpick (grown-up adds the toothpick) and a flag your child designs.
- Test fruit: lemon, grape, banana, potato. Which ones surprise you?
- Make a submarine: can your child make a floating bottle sink by adding water?

### Stuck?
- The boat leaks: pinch the corners tighter, or start with a fresh piece of foil.
- Your child is upset a guess was wrong: cheer for the surprise. "Scientists love surprises!"

### Show it off
Show the family the winning boat and the sink-or-float chart. Let them guess before your child tells the answer.

### Clean up
Dry all the objects on the towel. Your child can drain the water with a cup into the sink or water a plant with it.

### For the grown-up
This is early physics: predicting, testing and sorting, plus the idea that shape matters (a flat sheet of foil sinks when balled up but floats as a boat). Stay right next to your child the whole time they are near water, even shallow water. Handle pennies and washers yourself and keep them away from mouths. **Ask at dinner:** "What was something that surprised you by floating?"

## 27. Bubble Lab

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Outdoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 1 cup water, 2 tablespoons dish soap, 1 teaspoon sugar, a bowl or shallow pan, pipe cleaners, a slotted spoon, a plastic cup with the bottom cut out (grown-up cuts), string and two straws, a towel

**Nice to have:** glycerin or corn syrup for stronger bubbles, a flyswatter, a cookie cutter, a dark shirt for seeing colors

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Welcome to the Bubble Lab, scientist! Your job is to find out what makes the biggest bubble, the longest-lasting bubble and the most bubbles. Can bubbles be square? Let's find out!"

### Goals
- Your child mixes bubble solution and makes at least 4 bubble wands.
- Your child finds out which wand makes the biggest bubbles.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a guess and test it.
- Your child can compare sizes: big, bigger, biggest.
- Your child can describe bubbles: round, shiny, rainbow, pop.

### Get ready

6. **Look closely.** Catch a bubble on a wet wand and look at the colors. Ask: "What colors do you see?"

### Make it yours
- Make a bubble snake: cover the cup end with a sock, dip it, and blow gently through the other end.
- Have a bubble race: whose bubble stays up the longest?
- Make bubble art: add a few drops of food coloring and pop bubbles onto paper (outside).

### Stuck?
- Bubbles keep popping: let the mix sit longer, add a little sugar, and keep wands wet. Dry fingers pop bubbles.
- It's windy: stand with your back to the wind and let the wind blow the bubbles for you.

### Show it off
Have a bubble show on the doorstep or balcony when the family gets home, with the winning wand.

### Clean up
Rinse the wands and bowl. Wipe soapy floors right away, since they get slippery.

### For the grown-up
This is early science: guessing, testing and comparing, and seeing that bubbles always pull into round shapes. Blowing also builds mouth muscles used in speech. Soap stings eyes, so rinse with water if it splashes. Soapy floors are slippery, so do this outside or on a towel. Do the cup cutting yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "Why do you think all the bubbles came out round?"

## 28. Corner Grocery Store

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** empty clean food boxes and cans (no sharp edges), fruits and vegetables from the kitchen, paper, crayons, tape, a table, a bag or basket, scissors (grown-up only), a box for a cash register

**Nice to have:** a toy cash register, a kitchen scale, play money

### The big idea
Read aloud: "There's a brand-new store opening on the corner, and you are the store owner! You need shelves full of food, price tags, a cash register and shopping bags. Let's get the store ready for customers."

### Goals
- Your child sets up a grocery store with at least 10 things to sell.
- Your child plays both store owner and shopper.

### Objectives
- Your child can sort food into groups (fruits, vegetables, boxes, cans).
- Your child can count coins up to 10 and match a number to a price.
- Your child can make a short shopping list using pictures.

### Get ready
Collect empty boxes, clean cans, and real fruits and vegetables (put them back later). Set up a table or a couch as the counter. Cut out 10 paper coins and 10 small squares for price tags.

### Steps
1. **Sort the food.** Your child sorts everything into sections: fruits, vegetables, boxes, cans. Ask: "Where should the apples go?"
2. **Stock the shelves** (15 minutes). Your child arranges each section on the table, the couch or a low shelf. Make a sign for each section.
3. **Price tags.** Your child chooses a price for each thing: 1, 2 or 3 coins. You write the number and they draw the right number of dots next to it.
4. **The cash register.** Decorate a box with buttons drawn on it. Keep the coins inside.
5. **Shopping list** (10 minutes). You draw a picture shopping list with 4 things. Your child is the cashier: you shop, then pay. Your child counts your coins.
6. **Switch.** Your child draws their own shopping list, takes a bag and goes shopping. You are the cashier. Your child has to count out the right number of coins.
7. **Store problem!** A customer forgot their money, or the store ran out of bananas. Your child figures out what to do.

### Make it yours
- Make it a pet store, a toy store or a magic shop selling potions and wands.
- Run a bakery counter inside the store with play dough or paper treats.
- Add a weekly sale: everything with a red sticker costs only 1 coin today.

### Stuck?
- Counting coins is hard: keep prices to 1 or 2 coins, and count out loud together.
- Your child wants only to be the cashier: let them, and bring in stuffed animal customers.

### Show it off
Open the store for the family when they get home. Everyone gets 5 coins to shop with.

### Clean up
Real food goes back to the kitchen. Your child puts the boxes in a bin labeled "Store."

### For the grown-up
This builds counting, number recognition, sorting, early money ideas and real-life vocabulary. Check cans for sharp edges before play (tape over any rim) and wash hands after handling real produce. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most popular thing in your store?"

## 29. Dinosaur Dig

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | High | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a plastic bin or baking pan, dirt, sand, or 4 cups flour mixed with 1/2 cup vegetable oil (homemade "dig sand"), toy dinosaurs or animals (bigger than a ping-pong ball), clean craft sticks or old paintbrushes, spoons, a ruler or measuring tape, paper, crayons, an old towel

**Nice to have:** a magnifying glass, a soft pastry brush, a bucket and spade for a real outdoor dig spot

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Long, long ago, dinosaurs walked right here. Now their bones are buried deep in the ground. You are a paleontologist, a scientist who digs up dinosaur bones. Grab your tools, there's a dig to do!"

### Goals
- Your child digs up at least 5 buried dinosaurs.
- Your child measures, sorts and names each find in a dig report.

### Objectives
- Your child can dig carefully with tools, brushing gently.
- Your child can measure and compare finds (longest, shortest).
- Your child can use the word paleontologist and tell what one does.

### Get ready
Fill the bin halfway with dirt, sand or dig sand. Bury the dinosaurs at different depths when your child isn't looking. Set it on a towel outside, or on a floor you can sweep. Put the tools next to it.

### Steps
1. Teach the big word: **paleontologist** (pay-lee-on-TALL-oh-jist). Say it in a deep voice. It means "dinosaur scientist."
2. **Dig rules.** Real scientists dig slowly so they don't break the bones. Use the spoon to scoop and the brush to sweep.
3. **The dig** (25 minutes). Your child digs carefully. When they find something, they shout "Fossil!" and brush it off.
4. **Measure the finds.** Lay each dinosaur next to the ruler. Which is longest? Shortest? Line them up from smallest to biggest.
5. **Dig report** (15 minutes). For each find, your child draws it and names it. Real names or made-up ones: "Spiky-Back-a-Saurus." You write how long it is.
6. **Museum display.** Your child sets up a display on a towel with signs: "Found by Dr. ___."
7. Bury them again for a second dig, this time with your child hiding the dinosaurs for you.

### Make it yours
- Make dinosaur footprints: press dinos into a flat layer of dig sand and guess whose track is whose.
- Your child becomes a dinosaur and acts out what each one ate and how it moved.
- Make it an egg dig: wrap toys in a ball of dig sand or play dough for "dinosaur eggs" to crack open.

### Stuck?
- No toy dinosaurs: bury other toys, or draw dinosaur bones on cardboard pieces for your child to dig up and put together.
- Your child digs too fast and gets frustrated: set a "gentle dig" timer and cheer when a find comes out whole.

### Show it off
Open the Dinosaur Museum when the family gets home. Your child explains each find and where it was buried.

### Clean up
Dig sand keeps in a sealed bag for weeks. Your child brushes off the toys and you sweep or shake the towel outside.

### For the grown-up
This builds early science (fossils, careful observation), measuring and comparing, and new vocabulary. Kids this age love big words like paleontologist. Use toys bigger than a ping-pong ball. Flour dig sand contains wheat, so check allergies; skip the flour if anyone is allergic and use dirt or sand. **Ask at dinner:** "What does a paleontologist do, and what did you find today?"

## 30. A Book About Me

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1 |

**Materials:** 5 sheets of paper, a stapler or yarn (grown-up), crayons or markers, a pencil, a mirror, a ruler or measuring tape

**Nice to have:** family photos to glue in, stickers, a glue stick, a hole punch

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Every library has books about dinosaurs, trucks and princesses. But there is no book about YOU yet! Today you are the author. Let's make a book all about you."

### Goals
- Your child makes a 5-page book about themselves with a cover.
- Your child "reads" the book aloud to someone.

### Objectives
- Your child can draw a self-portrait looking in a mirror.
- Your child can tell facts about themselves: name, age, family, favorites.
- Your child can write their name and try writing a few letters or words.

### Get ready
Fold the 5 sheets in half and staple along the fold to make a 10-page book (grown-up). Write a short starter at the top of each page: "This is me." "My family." "I love to eat..." "When I grow up..." "My favorite thing to do is..."

### Steps
1. **The cover** (10 minutes). Your child looks in the mirror and draws their face: what color are their eyes and hair? Write "A Book About" and your child writes their name (or traces yours).
2. **This is me.** Measure your child with the tape. Write how tall they are. Your child draws themselves standing tall.
3. **My family.** Your child draws the people (and pets) they live with or love. You write the names they say.
4. **I love to eat...** Your child draws a favorite food. Ask: "What does it taste like?" and write their words.
5. **When I grow up...** Ask: "What do you want to be?" Let them dream big: astronaut, baker, dragon trainer. They draw it.
6. **My favorite thing to do** (10 minutes). Your child draws it and tells you a sentence to write.
7. **The last page.** Your child chooses what goes on the last page. A pet, a best friend, a place, a wish.

### Make it yours
- Make a book about a stuffed animal's life instead, written by your child.
- Add a handprint page: trace your child's hand and write their age in it.
- Make it a "superhero me" book, where your child has a superpower on every page.

### Stuck?
- Your child doesn't want to draw people: use stick figures, or let them glue in a photo.
- Your child gets tired: do the cover and two pages today, and finish tomorrow.

### Show it off
Your child "reads" the book to the family at dinner (they tell it from the pictures). Keep it on the bookshelf next to the real books.

### Clean up
Your child puts the crayons back and puts the book on the shelf.

### For the grown-up
This builds early writing (name, letters), self-awareness, and the idea that books are made by people, including them. It is lovely to save, and to make again next year to see how they've grown. Do the stapling yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "What is your favorite page in your book, and why?"
