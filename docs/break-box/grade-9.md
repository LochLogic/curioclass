## 1. Pulley Elevator Model

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 3 or 4 empty thread spools or plastic bottle caps, pencils or chopsticks for axles, a cardboard box (a shipping or cereal box), string (about 3 m), a paper cup, coins or metal washers, a rubber band, a ruler, tape, scissors, a hole punch or sharp pencil

**Nice to have:** a kitchen scale, a hot glue gun, a bookshelf or doorframe to hang from

### The big idea
A five-story tower (your bookshelf) needs a freight elevator, and the client wants the motor as small as possible. That means you need **mechanical advantage**: trading a longer pull for a lighter one. You'll build three pulley systems, measure the force each one needs, and pick the winner.

### Goals
- A working elevator car that rides up and down a cardboard shaft.
- A test sheet comparing a fixed pulley, a movable pulley and a block and tackle.

### Objectives
- I can build fixed and movable pulleys and explain the difference.
- I can measure effort force with a homemade spring scale.
- I can calculate mechanical advantage as load divided by effort.

### Get ready
Clear a table. Make your spring scale: tape a rubber band to the top of a ruler so it hangs down, and hook a paper clip on the bottom. More stretch means more force. Punch two holes near the rim of the paper cup and tie on a string handle: that's your elevator car.

### Steps
1. Build the shaft. Stand the box on end and cut away one side so you can see in. Push a pencil axle through the top, about 3 cm below the rim, and slide a spool on it (15 minutes).
2. Load the car with 20 coins. Lift it straight up with the spring scale and note the stretch in millimeters. This is your baseline load.
3. **Test one: fixed pulley.** Run the string from the car over the top spool and pull down with the scale. Record the stretch.
4. **Test two: movable pulley.** Tie one end of the string to the top of the shaft, run it down under a spool attached to the car's handle, then back up over the fixed spool. Pull and record. You should need about half the force.
5. **Test three: block and tackle.** Add a second fixed spool on a lower axle and a second movable one. Thread it so four strands hold the car. Predict the force before you pull (20 minutes).
6. Measure distance too. For each system, pull the car up 10 cm and measure how much string you pulled. Less force always costs more string.
7. Choose your design and finish it: add a counterweight, a brake (a clothespin that pinches the string), or floor markers on the shaft.

### Make it yours
- Add a counterweight on the other side of the fixed pulley, like real elevators, and find the weight that makes the car almost float.
- Build a crank: a pencil through the box with the string wound around it, so you turn instead of pull.
- Make it a rescue story: the car must carry 50 coins to the top floor using a scale reading no bigger than your baseline for 20.

### Stuck?
- The string slips off the spools: wrap a strip of tape around each spool's rim to make a raised edge.
- The axle sags: put a second piece of cardboard inside the box wall to double its thickness.
- Readings jump around: pull slowly and read the scale while the car moves at a steady speed.

### Show it off
Give a two-minute "client pitch" at dinner: lift the same load three ways and let someone feel the difference.

### Clean up
Coins back in the jar, scraps in recycling.

### For the grown-up
This is physics in the hands: simple machines, force, work and mechanical advantage, the same ideas in high school physical science. It also builds careful measuring and data comparison. The only sharp tool is scissors or a craft knife for the box. **Ask at dinner:** "Why does the easier pulley make you pull more string?"

## 2. Story in a Genre You Never Read

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper or a notebook, a pen, a die or a slip-of-paper draw

**Nice to have:** an index card for each trope, a timer

### The big idea
Every reader has a comfort genre. This is the day you leave it. You'll roll for a genre you'd never pick, study how it works from the inside, and then write a scene so good a fan of that genre would think you were one of them. Bonus points for breaking one of its rules on purpose.

### Goals
- A finished scene of 800 to 1,500 words in a genre new to you.
- A trope list you built yourself, with one trope you flipped.

### Objectives
- I can name the conventions (the expected moves) of a genre.
- I can use a convention on purpose and subvert another one.
- I can control tone through word choice and sentence length.

### Get ready
Roll a die or draw a slip: 1 western, 2 cozy mystery, 3 space opera, 4 gothic horror, 5 heist, 6 sports underdog. If you already love the one you rolled, roll again. Get paper and something to drink.

### Steps
1. Brainstorm the genre's **tropes**: what always shows up? For a western: a dusty town, a stranger who rides in, a showdown at noon, a code of honor. Write at least 8 (10 minutes).
2. Note the **tone** tools. Gothic horror uses long, heavy sentences and decaying houses. A heist uses short, fast sentences and a ticking clock. Write three style rules for yourself.
3. Pick the trope you'll **subvert**: the heist crew's genius is a grandmother, the space opera's chosen one refuses the job.
4. Build a character in five lines: name, want, fear, one weird habit, one secret.
5. Outline one scene: where it opens, what goes wrong, and the last line. One scene, not a whole book.
6. Draft fast without stopping to fix (35 minutes). If you get stuck, add a genre trope and keep going.
7. Revise one pass. Cut your weakest sentence on each page, and make your first line pull harder.
8. Title it the way the genre would: a western wants something like "Dust at Kettle Creek."

### Make it yours
- Mash two genres: roll twice and write a gothic horror heist.
- Write the same scene twice, once straight and once as a parody, and compare what changed.
- Tell it from the point of view of the side character the genre usually ignores.

### Stuck?
- Blank page: start in the middle of action, with a line of dialogue.
- It sounds fake: read one paragraph aloud and replace any phrase you've heard a thousand times.

### Show it off
Read it aloud to someone at home without naming the genre first. Can they guess it from the first page?

### Clean up
Keep your trope list in your notebook. It works for the next genre too.

### For the grown-up
Writing in an unfamiliar genre teaches the craft ideas English class names: genre, convention, tone and point of view. It stretches a reader as much as a writer. **Ask at dinner:** "Which rule of that genre did you break, and did it work?"

## 3. Three-Course Meal from the Pantry

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** whatever is in your kitchen (with a grown-up's OK on what you can use), pots, a pan, a cutting board, a knife, measuring cups, plates, paper and a pen for the menu

**Nice to have:** fresh herbs, a lemon, a squeeze bottle for sauces

### The big idea
You're the head chef, and the delivery truck never came. The challenge: a starter, a main and a dessert using only what's already in the house, including three "wildcard" ingredients somebody else picks for you. Great cooks aren't the ones with perfect groceries. They're the ones who can see a meal in a half-empty cupboard.

### Goals
- A three-course meal for at least two people, plated with care.
- A handwritten menu card that names each dish.

### Objectives
- I can plan a menu that balances flavors, textures and timing.
- I can use basic knife and stove skills safely on my own.
- I can work backward from serving time so everything is ready.

### Get ready
Ask a grown-up which foods are off limits and check for allergies. Take inventory: write down every protein, grain, vegetable, fruit, sauce and spice. Ask someone to choose three wildcards you must use (say, canned chickpeas, an apple, peanut-free crackers).

### Steps
1. Sketch three dish ideas per course, then pick one each. Aim for contrast: if the main is rich, make the starter fresh and crunchy (10 minutes).
2. Write a **back-timed schedule**. If dinner is at 6:00 and pasta takes 12 minutes, water goes on at 5:35. Do dessert first if it needs to chill.
3. **Mise en place**: wash, chop and measure everything into bowls before you turn on the heat. Use the claw grip, fingertips tucked.
4. Cook the main (30 to 40 minutes). Taste as you go. Most dishes want salt, acid (lemon or vinegar) or heat, in that order of likely fixes.
5. Make the starter: a soup, a salad, or toast with a topping. Keep it small, three or four bites.
6. Plate like a restaurant: odd numbers of items, wipe the plate rim, add one bright color on top.
7. Write the menu card in your best handwriting, with a name for each dish ("Crispy Chickpea Crunch Salad").

### Make it yours
- Give the meal a theme: a dinner from a made-up country, or a meal for a starship crew.
- Cook a "one-ingredient three ways" menu, like potato soup, potato hash and a sweet potato pudding.
- Serve it blind: guests guess the wildcards from the taste.

### Stuck?
- Something is bland: add salt a pinch at a time, then a squeeze of lemon.
- Everything is ready at different times: keep finished food warm in a low oven or covered pot.

### Show it off
Serve the courses one at a time, and introduce each one like a server would.

### Clean up
Wash as you go, so the sink is half done before you eat. Wipe the stove when it's cool.

### For the grown-up
This builds real independence: planning, timing, knife and stove safety, and food chemistry. Give clear permission for the stove, oven and knives, and name any foods that are off limits or set aside for other meals. Check allergies before the wildcards are chosen. **Ask at dinner:** "Which wildcard was hardest to use, and what did you do with it?"

## 4. Cooperative Board Game

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a cereal box or cardboard for the board, paper, index cards or cut paper for cards, markers, a die, coins or bottle caps for pieces, scissors

**Nice to have:** a second die, sticky notes for quick changes

### The big idea
In most games, the players fight each other. In this one, everyone is on the same side, and the board is the enemy. A flood is rising, a dragon is waking, a spaceship is falling apart. You'll design a game where the table wins or loses together, and where the game is hard enough that winning feels like a miracle.

### Goals
- A playable cooperative board game with a board, cards and a rules page.
- At least two playtests, with a rule you changed after each one.

### Objectives
- I can design a **threat engine**, the rules that make the game fight back.
- I can balance difficulty by changing numbers and testing again.
- I can write clear rules someone else can learn without me.

### Get ready
Pick your threat. Ideas: a city during a rising flood, a lighthouse crew in a storm, healers during a strange fever, a ship's crew patching leaks. Lay out cardboard, cards and markers.

### Steps
1. Write the win and loss conditions in one sentence each. Win: "Rescue all 6 villagers." Lose: "The water reaches the top row, or the deck runs out."
2. Design the threat engine (15 minutes). After each player's turn, flip a threat card: the water rises in one area, a storm blocks a road. Make 20 cards; about a third should be nasty.
3. Give each player a **role** with one special power: the Engineer can build a wall, the Medic can move two spaces. Different powers force teamwork.
4. Draw the board: 12 to 20 spaces or zones, with paths between them.
5. Write the turn: "Take 3 actions: move, rescue, repair or share a card. Then flip a threat card."
6. **Playtest solo** by playing two roles yourself (20 minutes). Track how close the game came. A good co-op game is lost about half the time.
7. Tune it. Too easy? Add a card or remove an action. Too hard? Give one extra action when players share a space.
8. Write a one-page rulebook with a setup diagram.

### Make it yours
- Add a traitor card: one player might secretly be on the threat's side.
- Make the threat smarter, so it gets faster each time a certain card shows up.
- Give the game a story campaign: three short scenarios where each one changes the next.

### Stuck?
- Players just do the same thing every turn: give each role one thing only they can do.
- It's boring: add a decision with a cost, like "spend an action to peek at the next threat card."

### Show it off
Teach it to your family after dinner and play one round. Watch who argues about strategy: that means it works.

### Clean up
Put cards, pieces and rules in an envelope taped to the back of the board.

### For the grown-up
Game design is systems thinking: probability, balancing numbers, and clear technical writing. Co-op games also make a great family night because nobody loses to anybody. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the most dangerous card in your game, and why did you put it in?"

## 5. Concept Vehicle Design

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** plain paper, a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a black pen or marker, colored pencils or markers

**Nice to have:** tracing paper or thin printer paper, a gray marker for shading

### The big idea
Car companies hire designers to dream up vehicles that don't exist yet, and they always start with a client and a problem. Your client is strange: a mail carrier on Mars, a floating-market seller in a flooded city, a mountain rescue team on a planet with twice Earth's gravity. Design the machine they need, from rough sketch to a presentation sheet.

### Goals
- A finished presentation sheet: a hero drawing in perspective, plus side and front views.
- A spec card with your vehicle's name, size, power source and three key features.

### Objectives
- I can draw a box in two-point perspective and build a vehicle on it.
- I can draw **orthographic views** (front, side, top) that match each other.
- I can make design choices based on a user's needs.

### Get ready
Pick or invent your client. Write their problem in one line: "She delivers 200 packages a day across dusty, rocky ground at minus 60 degrees." Get paper and a sharp pencil.

### Steps
1. **Thumbnails**: draw 8 tiny vehicle shapes, each about 5 cm long, in 10 minutes. Wheels, legs, tracks, hover pads, anything. Speed beats polish here.
2. Circle the two best and ask: does it solve the client's problem? Write one reason next to each.
3. Draw a **side view** of your pick on a ruled baseline. Use the client as scale: sketch a person next to it.
4. Draw a front view lined up beside it, using light guidelines from the side view so the heights match.
5. **Hero shot** (25 minutes): draw a horizon line, place two vanishing points near the paper's edges, and draw a box in perspective. Carve your vehicle out of that box. Wheels in perspective are ellipses, not circles.
6. Ink your best lines with pen, vary the line weight (thicker lines near the bottom and closer to you), then erase the pencil.
7. Add shading from one light source, and one accent color.
8. Write the spec card: name, length, top speed, power source, three features with one-line reasons.

### Make it yours
- Design a family of three vehicles that share the same look: a scout, a hauler and a rescue model.
- Add a cutaway view showing what's inside: the cab, cargo bay, engine.
- Redesign it for a different client and show how the same base changes.

### Stuck?
- The perspective looks warped: move your vanishing points farther apart, even off the paper with tape and extra sheets.
- It looks like a regular car: change one big thing, like the number of wheels or where the driver sits.

### Show it off
Pin the sheet up and give a 60-second "design review" to someone at home. Let them ask one tough question.

### Clean up
Keep the thumbnails. Designers save every sketch, because old rejects become new ideas.

### For the grown-up
This mixes drawing technique (perspective, orthographic views, line weight) with industrial design thinking: who it's for, what problem it solves. Both show up in art, engineering and drafting classes. **Ask at dinner:** "What problem did your vehicle solve that a normal car couldn't?"

## 6. Cartesian Diver Submarine

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a clear plastic bottle with a cap (1 or 2 liter), water, a plastic pen cap or a bendy straw, paper clips, modeling clay or a small piece of tape, a tall glass of water for testing, a towel

**Nice to have:** a condiment packet, a permanent marker, a dropper

### The big idea
Squeeze a sealed bottle and a tiny submarine inside sinks. Let go and it rises. No wires, no motor, just air, water and pressure. Your mission: build a diver that you can park at any depth on command, then use it to "salvage" a paper clip treasure from the bottom.

### Goals
- A diver that sinks when you squeeze and rises when you let go.
- A diver that can hover in the middle of the bottle, and one salvage mission completed.

### Objectives
- I can explain **buoyancy**: an object floats when it pushes aside more water than its own weight.
- I can use the idea that squeezing air makes it take up less space (Boyle's law) to explain my diver.
- I can adjust ballast to tune my diver's density.

### Get ready
Fill the bottle to the very top with water. Fold the towel under your work area. Fill a tall glass with water for test runs.

### Steps
1. Build the diver. Fold a bendy straw at the bend, trim each side to about 4 cm, and slide a paper clip over the open ends to hold it. Or use a pen cap with a small ball of clay stuck on the clip.
2. **Tune it in the glass** (15 minutes). The diver should *barely* float, with just its tip above the water. Too floaty? Add clay or another clip. Sinks? Remove some.
3. Drop it in the bottle, top off the water and screw the cap on tight.
4. Squeeze. Watch the air bubble inside the diver shrink as water pushes in. Less air means the diver is denser than water, so it sinks.
5. Practice control: get it to stop at the halfway mark and hold it there for five seconds.
6. **Salvage mission.** Bend a paper clip into a hook on the bottom of your diver. Drop a second paper clip bent into a loop into the bottle. Can you hook it and lift it up? (20 minutes.)
7. Write down the forces in one paragraph: weight down, buoyant force up, and what squeezing changes.

### Make it yours
- Build a squad: two divers with slightly different ballast that sink at different squeezes.
- Make the diver from a condiment packet and compare how it behaves.
- Design a "deep sea trench": a heavier object glued to the bottom that your diver must touch without landing.

### Stuck?
- The diver won't sink no matter how hard you squeeze: it floats too high. Add a little ballast and top off the bottle's water.
- It sinks and won't come up: it's too heavy, or the air bubble is too small. Take it out and remove weight.

### Show it off
Challenge someone at home to make it hover. Then explain how it works without saying "magic."

### Clean up
Pour the water on a plant, dry the parts, and keep the bottle sealed as a desk toy.

### For the grown-up
This is a classic physics demo covering density, buoyancy and gas pressure, all part of high school physical science and chemistry. Nothing here is hazardous beyond water on the floor. **Ask at dinner:** "When you squeeze the bottle, what actually happens inside the diver?"

## 7. Bucket Drum Composition

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** an upside-down bucket, a cardboard box or a big plastic tub, two wooden spoons or thick pencils, a pot lid or baking sheet, paper and a pen

**Nice to have:** a timer or metronome, an old towel to dampen sound, a second drummer

### The big idea
Street drummers can make an overturned bucket sound like a full drum kit. The secret isn't the bucket: it's **rudiments**, the basic sticking patterns every drummer drills. You'll learn three, build grooves out of them, and compose a two-minute piece you can play start to finish.

### Goals
- A 2-minute bucket drum piece with an intro, a main groove, a fill and an ending.
- Your piece written down in grid notation so someone else could play it.

### Objectives
- I can play singles, doubles and a paradiddle at a steady tempo.
- I can write rhythms on a 16-step grid.
- I can structure a piece with contrast and repetition.

### Get ready
Set up your kit: the bucket center is your bass sound, the bucket rim is your sharp "snare," the box is a low tom, and the pot lid is a cymbal. Outside or in a garage is best. Ask before you start drumming if anyone at home is working or sleeping.

### Steps
1. **Rudiments** (15 minutes). Count "1-e-and-a, 2-e-and-a" out loud. Play singles: R L R L. Then doubles: R R L L. Then the **paradiddle**: R L R R, L R L L. Start slow and speed up only when it's even.
2. Draw a 16-box grid on paper, one box per count. Write B for bass, S for snare, T for tom, C for cymbal.
3. Build **Groove A**: bass on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, then add your own ghost notes in between. Play it 8 times without stopping.
4. Build **Groove B**, which feels different: faster, more toms, or a paradiddle moving between surfaces.
5. Write a **fill**: one bar of something busy that signals "change is coming." Fills often end with a cymbal crash on the next 1.
6. Arrange it: Intro (4 bars, sparse), A (8 bars), Fill, B (8 bars), Fill, A (8 bars), Ending (one big hit, then silence). Tap tempo around 90 to 100 beats per minute.
7. Rehearse the whole piece three times. Fix the transition that falls apart most.

### Make it yours
- Add a call and response section: you play a bar, then answer yourself on different surfaces.
- Score a scene: write the piece as the soundtrack for a chase through a market.
- Use only one surface for a whole section and see how much variety you can get from dynamics alone.

### Stuck?
- You keep speeding up: count out loud, or tap your foot on every beat.
- The grooves sound the same: change which surface gets the downbeat.

### Show it off
Perform it for your family. If you have a second drummer, teach them Groove A and play it as a duet.

### Clean up
Stack the kit back where it came from and keep your grid sheet for the next session.

### For the grown-up
This builds rhythm reading, steady tempo and song form, the same skills taught in band and music theory. It's loud: you may want to pick the time of day. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you clap me the paradiddle?"

## 8. Spoken Word Poem

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, a quiet spot

**Nice to have:** a phone timer, a mirror

### The big idea
A spoken word poem is written for the ear, not the page. It's meant to be performed, with rhythm, repetition and silence doing as much work as the words. Today you'll write one about something you actually care about and deliver it like you mean it.

### Goals
- A finished spoken word poem, 1 to 3 minutes long.
- One full performance, from memory or close to it.

### Objectives
- I can use **anaphora** (repeating a phrase at the start of lines) to build power.
- I can write concrete images instead of abstract statements.
- I can use pauses, volume and pace on purpose when I perform.

### Get ready
Find a spot where you can talk out loud without feeling watched. Get paper and a pen.

### Steps
1. **Topic dump** (5 minutes). List 15 things you have strong feelings about: your grandmother's kitchen, being the oldest, a song, a place, a thing people get wrong about you.
2. Circle one. Write the phrase "I come from" or "They don't tell you" at the top of a page.
3. **Freewrite** for 10 minutes using that phrase as the starting point of every line. Don't stop. Go for specifics: not "my neighborhood is loud" but "the bus brakes sigh at 6:14 every morning."
4. Find your **turn**: the line where the poem changes direction, from anger to hope, or from a memory to right now. Spoken word poems almost always have one.
5. Shape it. Keep the best 20 to 30 lines. Repeat your strongest line three times in the poem, once near the start, once in the middle, and once to end it.
6. Mark it for performance: a slash / for a short pause, // for a long one, and underline words to hit hard.
7. Practice it 5 times out loud. On the last two, try not to look at the page.

### Make it yours
- Write it as a letter to someone: a friend, your town, your younger self.
- Write a call and response, with a line your audience says back.
- Build it around a single object, such as a pair of shoes, and let everything come back to it.

### Stuck?
- It sounds like an essay: cut every sentence that starts with "I think" or "I feel" and replace it with an image.
- You lose your place: tie each section to one memorable image, so you remember the poem as a series of pictures.

### Show it off
Host a one-poem "open mic" in the living room. Turn off the main light and use a single lamp.

### Clean up
Copy the final version into your notebook. Toss the drafts or keep them for later poems.

### For the grown-up
This builds the English skills of figurative language, revision and public speaking, and it gives teens a real outlet for what's on their mind. Listen without critiquing the content. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the line in your poem you're proudest of?"

## 9. Personal Record Training Plan

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** comfortable clothes, a water bottle, paper, a pen, a clock or timer

**Nice to have:** a jump rope, a yoga mat, a training partner

### The big idea
Athletes don't get better by trying hard once. They get better by testing, planning and adding a little at a time. Today you'll pick one challenge, test your starting number, and design a two-week plan built on **progressive overload**, the same idea coaches use. Then you run day one.

### Goals
- A baseline score for one challenge.
- A written 14-day plan with work days, rest days and a final test day.

### Objectives
- I can measure a baseline and set a realistic target.
- I can use progressive overload: increase reps, time or sets by small steps.
- I can plan rest and warm-ups to train safely.

### Get ready
Pick your challenge: longest plank, most push-ups in a row (knees or toes), longest wall sit, jump rope skips in 60 seconds, or the time to run a set loop around your block. Clear some floor space. Drink water.

### Steps
1. **Warm-up** (8 minutes): march or jog in place for 2 minutes, then 10 arm circles each way, 10 leg swings each leg, 10 bodyweight squats, 10 slow lunges.
2. **Baseline test.** Do your challenge once, all out but with good form. Write down the number. Stop if anything hurts, not just burns.
3. Set a target: about 15 to 25 percent better in two weeks. If your plank is 60 seconds, aim for 72.
4. Plan the work (20 minutes). Draw 14 boxes. Work days use sets below your max: for a 60-second plank, try 3 sets of 40 seconds with 60 seconds of rest. Each work day, add a little: 5 seconds, or 1 rep.
5. Put in rest days: at least one after every two work days. Muscles grow during rest, not during the workout.
6. Add one helper exercise that trains a related muscle: side planks for planks, squats for wall sits.
7. Mark day 14 as test day and run day one right now. Log it in the first box.
8. **Cool-down**: 5 minutes of slow stretching. Hold each stretch 20 to 30 seconds and breathe.

### Make it yours
- Turn your plan into a training camp story: you're preparing for a fictional championship, with a rival named on the plan.
- Train with someone: each person picks a different challenge and you check each other's logs.
- Add a skill goal instead of a number, like a smooth crow pose or a 10-skip double-under combo.

### Stuck?
- The first test is discouraging: that's why it's a baseline. Everyone starts somewhere.
- You can't do the session: cut the sets in half. Showing up counts more than the amount.

### Show it off
Post the plan on your door or fridge and invite someone home to watch test day.

### Clean up
Put away the mat and rope, and keep the log where you'll see it tomorrow.

### For the grown-up
This builds goal setting, data tracking and the basics of safe training from health and PE class. Encourage form over speed and a stop at any sharp pain. If your teen runs outside, they tell someone their route first. **Ask at dinner:** "What's your target for day 14, and how did you pick it?"

## 10. Teach a Younger Kid a Skill

**Nice to have:** a small "certificate" or prize, a timer

### The big idea
You know how to do things a younger kid would love to learn: tie a shoe, throw a spiral, fold a paper crane, shuffle cards, draw a cartoon face. Teaching is harder than it looks and more fun than it sounds. Today you're the coach. Your learner can be a younger sibling, a cousin, a neighbor kid (with their grown-up's OK) or even a grown-up who's never learned.

### Goals
- Your learner can do the new skill on their own by the end.
- You have a one-page lesson plan you could use again.

### Objectives
- I can break a skill into small steps in the right order.
- I can use "I do, we do, you do" to teach.
- I can give specific praise and calm corrections.

### Get ready
Pick a skill you can do without thinking, that takes 5 to 20 minutes to learn. Make sure your learner wants to learn it. Gather materials for both of you.

### Steps
1. **Break it down** (10 minutes). Do the skill slowly yourself and write each tiny step. Folding a paper airplane might be 8 steps. Tying a shoe might be 6.

- Teach a skill that's secretly science or math, like a card trick that works by counting.
- Make a step-by-step picture guide they can keep and use later.
- Switch at the end: let them teach you something they're good at.

### Stuck?
- They get frustrated: back up to the last step they could do, and make the next step smaller.
- They lose interest: shorten it, and make the next try a contest against you.

### Show it off
Your learner shows off their new skill to the family, and you introduce them like a proud coach.

### Clean up
Put supplies away together and keep your lesson plan in a folder.

### For the grown-up
Teaching a skill builds patience, clear communication and leadership, and it shows teens what they already know. A grown-up should be home, and for a neighbor's child, their family's OK comes first. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest step to teach, and how did you get around it?"

## 11. Riddle Trail for Friends

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 60-120 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, envelopes or folded paper, tape, a small prize or a sealed "treasure" note

**Nice to have:** a padlock with a number code, a flashlight, colored paper for each clue

### The big idea
You're the puzzle master. Your friends or family will follow a trail of 6 to 8 clues, each one hidden where the last one points. Every clue uses a different kind of puzzle, and the last one unlocks the treasure. Make it hard enough to feel clever and fair enough that nobody gives up.

### Goals
- A trail of 6 to 8 linked clues hidden around your home or yard.
- A group that solves it start to finish.

### Objectives
- I can write riddles, ciphers and logic clues with exactly one answer.
- I can plan a sequence so each answer leads to the next location.
- I can test puzzles for difficulty and fairness.

### Get ready
Walk your home and list 10 possible hiding spots: under the doormat, inside the freezer door, behind a picture frame, in a shoe. Ask which spots are off limits.

### Steps
1. **Map it backward** (10 minutes). Pick the treasure spot first, then the clue before it, then the one before that. Write the order: Start, Clue 1 at the mailbox, Clue 2 in the bathtub, and so on.
2. Write each clue using a different style:
   - Riddle: "I have hands but no fingers, a face but no eyes." (A clock.)
   - Cipher: shift every letter by 3, so "OVEN" becomes "RYHQ." Give the key on the clue.
   - Rebus: pictures and letters, like a drawing of a bee + "D" for "bed."
   - Logic: "It's not in a room with a window. It's cold. It's not where food lives." (The garage freezer? Make sure only one answer fits.)
3. Add a **meta-puzzle**: each clue has one circled letter. All the letters together spell the final hiding place or the code to the lock (20 minutes).
4. **Fairness test.** Read each clue as if you've never seen it. Could there be two answers? Rewrite until there's one.
5. Hide the clues in reverse order, so you don't get lost.
6. Write a hint card for each clue, sealed, so stuck players can take a hint for a 2-minute penalty.
7. Run it. Hand over Clue 1, start a timer, and stay quiet.

### Make it yours
- Give it a story: a museum heist in reverse, where the players return a stolen treasure before the guard wakes up.
- Make it a race: two teams with different paths that meet at the end.
- Add a physical challenge clue: the answer only appears when you shine a flashlight through the paper from behind.

### Stuck?
- It's too easy: remove the word that gives the answer away, or add a step.
- Players are stuck for over 5 minutes: hand them the hint card, then note that clue to fix for next time.

### Show it off
Run it when friends visit or after dinner. Time them and keep a record board on the fridge.

### Clean up
Collect every clue (count them) so nobody finds a riddle in the freezer next month.

### For the grown-up
Writing puzzles takes logic, wordplay and planning, plus thinking from another person's point of view. Keep the trail indoors and in the yard unless your teen has permission to go farther. **Ask at dinner:** "Which clue fooled people the longest?"

## 12. Urban Sketching Walk

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-120 min | Outdoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a small sketchbook or paper clipped to cardboard, a pencil, a black pen, an eraser, a bag, water, a jacket

**Nice to have:** a small folding stool, colored pencils or a small set of watercolors, a friend to sketch with

### The big idea
Urban sketchers draw cities on the spot, in all weather, fast. They don't wait for the perfect view. They draw what's in front of them: a corner shop, a bus stop, someone walking a dog. Today you head out with a sketchbook and come home with a page that captures a real place at a real moment.

### Goals
- Three or more on-location sketches, at least one fully finished.
- A page with the place, date and time written on it, like a field journal.

### Objectives
- I can use a thumbnail to plan composition before drawing.
- I can find the **horizon line** and use it for perspective.
- I can draw quick people and use three values (light, middle, dark).

### Get ready
Tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. Pick a spot within your allowed area: a street corner, a park bench, a front step, a balcony, or a window if you can't go out. Dress for the weather.

### Steps
1. **Look first** (5 minutes). Don't draw. Find the most interesting thing: a strange shadow, a tangle of wires, a busy doorway.
2. Draw two **thumbnails**, each the size of a playing card. Try a different crop in each. Pick the better one.
3. Find your eye level and draw a light horizon line across the page. Lines above eye level slope down toward it, lines below slope up.
4. **Block in big shapes** in light pencil (10 minutes): buildings as boxes, trees as clouds, the street as a big triangle.
5. Add people fast. Heads of people at your eye level all sit on the horizon line, near or far. Draw them as a bean-shaped body and stick legs. A person should take 10 seconds.
6. Go over your lines in pen. Don't erase mistakes: urban sketchers keep their wobbly lines.
7. Add **three values**: leave the brightest parts white, hatch the middle tones, and fill the darkest shadows solid (15 minutes).
8. Write the place, date, time and one detail you heard ("a dog barked at the mail truck").

### Make it yours
- Do a "one street, four directions" page, turning to face north, east, south and west.
- Sketch the same spot three times, 20 minutes apart, and show how the light moved.
- Draw as if you're a visitor from another planet, labeling everything with your best guess about its purpose.

### Stuck?
- The scene feels too complicated: draw only what's inside a frame made by your fingers.
- People keep moving: draw a pose half from one person and half from another. Everyone does it.

### Show it off
Lay your sketches out and give your family a tour of where you went, page by page.

### Clean up
Pack up your trash and leave the spot as you found it. Store the sketchbook flat so pages don't smudge.

### For the grown-up
This builds observational drawing, perspective and the habit of noticing, all useful in art and design classes. Your teen should tell you the route and return time, stay in agreed areas and carry water. A window or balcony version works fine. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you notice that you'd never seen before?"

## 13. Furniture Makeover

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Either | High | Nearby | 1 |

**Materials:** one piece of furniture a grown-up approves (a wobbly chair, a scuffed stool, a plain shelf, a side table), a screwdriver set, wood glue or white glue, sandpaper, old fabric or a large old shirt, scissors, clothespins or clamps, rags

**Nice to have:** a staple gun, leftover house paint and a brush, felt pads, a tape measure

### The big idea
Somewhere in your house is a chair that wobbles or a stool that looks tired. Instead of tossing it, you'll diagnose what's wrong, fix it so it's solid, and give it a new look. Restorers call this "giving it a second life." It's real repair work with real tools.

### Goals
- A piece of furniture that's sturdier than before.
- A visible upgrade: new seat fabric, a fresh finish, or a new purpose.

### Objectives
- I can diagnose a structural problem like a loose joint or uneven leg.
- I can use a screwdriver, glue and clamps correctly.
- I can plan a design change and carry it out.

### Get ready
Get a grown-up's OK on which piece you're working on and what you're allowed to change. Lay down newspaper or work outside. Take a "before" sketch or photo.

### Steps
1. **Inspect** (10 minutes). Turn it upside down. Rock it. Which joint moves? Are any screws loose or missing? Is one leg shorter?
2. Tighten every screw you find, turning clockwise until snug, not until it strips.
3. Fix loose joints. If a leg wiggles where it meets the seat, squeeze wood glue into the gap, press it together, and clamp it with clothespins or by wrapping string tight. Wipe extra glue right away with a damp rag.
4. Fix a short leg: stack felt pads or a folded piece of cardboard glued under it until it sits flat.
5. **Design the new look** (10 minutes). Sketch two options. A chair seat can be re-covered. A shelf can get painted edges. A stool can become a plant stand.
6. **Re-cover a seat**: unscrew the seat, lay it face down on your fabric, cut the fabric 5 cm bigger on all sides, pull it tight, and staple or tape it underneath. Do opposite sides first, then the corners.
7. **Refresh a surface**: sand scuffs lightly along the grain, wipe the dust, and paint if you're allowed.
8. Reassemble and test. Sit on it, lean on it, rock it.

### Make it yours
- Turn it into something new: a chair with a broken seat becomes a bedside shelf.
- Add a hidden feature, like a small pocket under the seat or a pencil holder on the side.
- Theme the design for a person: a reading stool for a grandparent, in their favorite color.

### Stuck?
- A screw spins and won't tighten: the hole is stripped. Push a toothpick with glue into the hole, snap it off, let it dry, and screw back in.
- The fabric wrinkles at corners: fold the corner like wrapping a present before you staple.

### Show it off
Show "before" and "after" side by side and walk your family through the repairs.

### Clean up
Put the tools back where they belong, sweep up, and wash the brush in water right away if you used water-based paint.

### For the grown-up
This is practical engineering and design: diagnosing a problem, using hand tools and following through. Approve the piece and the tools first. Water-based glue and paint only, used in a ventilated space, and a staple gun only if they've been shown how. **Ask at dinner:** "What was actually wrong with it?"

**Materials:** paper, pens, index cards or scraps, a large sheet of paper or the back of a poster

**Nice to have:** a gavel (a wooden spoon), costumes or name tags, people at home to play citizens

### The big idea
A city of 5,000 people has just been built on a ring of platforms in the middle of the ocean. No country owns it. You're one of the founders, and there are no rules yet. Who decides things? What happens when two people disagree? What if a storm hits? You'll write the city's founding charter and then test it in a town meeting full of trouble.

### Goals
- A one-page charter with a government structure and at least 5 rights.
- A town meeting role-play where the charter faces three crisis cards.

### Objectives
- I can compare forms of government, like direct democracy and representative democracy.
- I can write rules that are clear enough to settle a real argument.
- I can play a role and argue a point of view that isn't mine.

### Get ready
Draw a quick map of the city: 6 platforms, each with a purpose (fishing docks, school, farms, power station, homes, harbor). Gather paper and cards.

- Write the charter's opening statement, the way founding documents begin with a big promise.
- Run it as two rival founder groups, each with its own charter, and see which one handles crises better.
- Jump ahead 50 years and write a newspaper front page from the city's future.

- Every crisis ends in a tie: add a tie-breaking rule, like the council president's vote.
- It feels like a textbook: play the citizens as big, specific characters with names and grudges.

### Show it off
Read the charter aloud at dinner, then let the family draw one crisis card and vote on it.

### Clean up
Fold the map and charter into a folder. You may want to add amendments later.

### For the grown-up
This is civics through play: forms of government, rights, checks on power and compromise, all topics in high school government and history. It also builds argument and empathy. **Ask at dinner:** "Which crisis broke your charter, and how did you fix it?"

## 15. Home Energy Detective

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a clipboard or cardboard, a pen, a strip of tissue paper about 3 cm wide, a sheet of printer paper, a flashlight

**Nice to have:** a thermometer, a calculator, an old utility bill (ask first)

### The big idea
Your home is leaking energy right now, through drafts, gadgets that never really turn off, and old bulbs. You're the detective hired to find every leak, measure it and report back with a plan. Real energy auditors do this for a living, and they start with the same simple tools you have.

### Goals
- A room-by-room energy audit of your home.
- A one-page report with your top three fixes and an estimate of what they'd save.

### Objectives
- I can find air leaks using a tissue-paper draft test.
- I can read wattage on labels and calculate kilowatt-hours.
- I can rank fixes by how much they save and how easy they are.

### Get ready
Make an audit sheet with columns: Room, What I found, Watts or problem, Hours a day, Fix. Ask a grown-up if any rooms are off limits.

### Steps
1. **Draft hunt** (15 minutes). Hold the tissue strip near window edges, door frames, outlets on outside walls and the bottom of doors. If it flutters, air is leaking. Mark each one.
2. **Phantom loads.** Count every glowing standby light, every charger left in a wall, every device that's "off" but warm. Each one draws power all day.
3. **Fridge seal test.** Close the fridge door on a sheet of paper. If it slides out easily, the seal is leaking cold air. Test four spots around the door.
4. **Lighting inventory.** Check bulbs (when cool) for their wattage. An old 60-watt bulb and a newer 9-watt bulb make similar light.
5. **Do the math** (15 minutes). Energy in kilowatt-hours = watts times hours, divided by 1,000. A 60-watt bulb on 5 hours a day uses 0.3 kWh a day, about 110 kWh a year. Multiply by your local price if you can find it on a bill, or use 15 cents per kWh as a rough guess.
6. Rank your findings by savings and effort. Weatherstripping a door may be cheap. Unplugging a game console is free.
7. Write the report: what you found, three fixes, and the yearly savings estimate.

### Make it yours
- Present it as a detective case file: "The Case of the Vampire Toaster."
- Track one fix for a week: unplug chargers every night and compare a meter reading, if a grown-up can show you where the meter is.
- Design a "power off" checklist sign for the front door.

### Stuck?
- No wattage on a label: look for volts and amps instead. Watts = volts times amps.
- No drafts found: try on a windy day, or hold a flashlight on one side of a door while someone looks for light on the other.

### Show it off
Present your report to the family like a consultant, with the biggest leak first.

### Clean up
Plug back in anything you unplugged that needs to stay on, like the fridge or a clock.

### For the grown-up
This is a hands-on energy audit: electricity, units and estimation from physical science, plus real-world math. Don't let your teen open electrical panels or outlet covers; looking and labels only. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the biggest energy vampire in our house?"

## 16. Portrait Proportions

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a mirror, good light

**Nice to have:** a softer pencil (marked 2B or 4B), a person willing to sit still for 20 minutes

### The big idea
Most people draw eyes way too high on the head. It's not your fault: your brain thinks eyes are important, so it exaggerates them. Artists fix this with a few proportion rules that work on almost every human face. Learn them, then use them to draw a portrait that actually looks like someone.

### Goals
- A proportion guide drawing of a face, with every guideline labeled.
- A finished portrait from life: yourself in a mirror or someone at home.

### Objectives
- I can place the eyes, nose, mouth and ears using proportion guidelines.
- I can measure with my pencil held at arm's length (sight-sizing).
- I can shade with light and shadow to show form.

### Get ready
Set up near a window or lamp so light hits one side of the face. Prop a mirror at eye level, or seat your model where you can see them easily.

### Steps
1. **Proportion guide** (15 minutes). Draw an egg shape, wider at the top. Draw a vertical line down the middle. Then:
   - Eyes go on a line **halfway** down the head, not a third.
   - The face is about **five eyes wide**, with one eye's width between the eyes.
   - The bottom of the nose is halfway between the eye line and the chin.
   - The mouth is one-third of the way from the nose to the chin.
   - The ears sit between the eyebrow line and the nose line.
2. Label each line. This is your cheat sheet.
3. **Sight-size.** Hold your pencil at arm's length, close one eye, and measure your model's head height with your thumb on the pencil. Compare it to the width. Most heads are about one and a half widths tall. Check yours.
4. Lightly draw the head shape and guidelines on a fresh page. Then start placing features where *your* model's are, adjusting from the guide (30 minutes).
5. Look more than you draw. Glance at the face, then the paper, every few seconds.
6. **Shade.** Squint to see the big shadow shapes. Shade the dark side of the face, under the nose, under the lower lip, and the eye sockets.
7. Erase the guidelines and add the hair as big shapes, not single strands.

### Make it yours
- Push it into a caricature: find the one feature that's most "them" and exaggerate it by 50 percent.
- Draw the same face three times: looking straight, at three-quarter view and in profile.
- Draw a portrait of a character from a story you're writing, using a family member as the model.

### Stuck?
- It looks like a cartoon: you're drawing symbols for eyes. Draw the shapes of light and shadow you actually see.
- The model keeps moving: mark three key points (eye corner, nose tip, chin) early so they can return to the pose.

### Show it off
Hang the proportion guide and portrait side by side. Let your model see it first.

### Clean up
Brush eraser crumbs into the trash. Keep it in a folder to avoid smudges.

### For the grown-up
This is the foundation of figure drawing taught in art classes: proportion, measuring and value. If your teen asks you to model, it's a chance to sit still together for 20 minutes. **Ask at dinner:** "Where do the eyes really go on a face?"

## 17. Bread from Scratch

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 500 g (about 4 cups) all-purpose flour, 7 g (one packet, about 2 teaspoons) instant or active dry yeast, 10 g (2 teaspoons) salt, 400 ml (about 1 and 2/3 cups) warm water, olive oil or other cooking oil, a big bowl, a baking pan about 23 x 33 cm (9 x 13 in), a clean towel, oven mitts

**Nice to have:** rosemary, garlic, cherry tomatoes or olives for the top, coarse salt, a kitchen scale

### The big idea
Four ingredients: flour, water, salt and yeast. That's it. And yet the result is one of the most loved foods on Earth. Today you'll make a dimpled, golden flatbread (an olive-oil pan bread) and watch a living organism do half the work for you. Bread isn't a recipe. It's a science experiment you eat.

### Goals
- A baked pan of crisp-bottomed, airy bread.
- Your own topping design pressed into the dough.

### Objectives
- I can explain what yeast does: it eats sugar and makes carbon dioxide gas that puffs up the dough.
- I can measure ingredients accurately by weight or volume.
- I can use the oven safely on my own.

### Get ready
Get a grown-up's OK to use the oven and check for wheat or other allergies. Wash your hands. Measure everything before you start.

### Steps
1. Mix the flour, yeast and salt in a big bowl. Add the warm water (it should feel like bath water, not hot; too hot kills yeast). Stir until there are no dry spots. It will be very sticky. That's right.
2. Drizzle 2 tablespoons of oil over it, cover with a towel and let it rise in a warm spot for 45 to 60 minutes, until doubled.
3. While you wait, plan your topping. Sketch a pattern on paper: a field of olives, a garden of herbs, a face made of tomatoes.
4. Oil the pan generously, about 3 tablespoons. Tip the dough in. With oily fingers, gently stretch it toward the corners. If it springs back, wait 10 minutes and try again.
5. Preheat the oven to 220°C (425°F). Let the dough rise in the pan for 20 more minutes.
6. **Dimple it.** Press all your fingertips straight down to the bottom of the pan, all over. Drizzle with more oil, sprinkle salt, and press your toppings in.
7. Bake 20 to 25 minutes until golden on top. Lift a corner with a spatula: the bottom should be crisp and brown.
8. Let it cool 10 minutes before cutting. Steam inside is still cooking it.

### Make it yours
- Make it sweet: skip the salt on top and press in apple slices with cinnamon sugar.
- Split the dough into two pans and test one variable: one rises longer, or one uses cold water.
- Bake a "landscape": use toppings to build a picture, with rosemary trees and tomato suns.

### Stuck?
- The dough didn't rise: the yeast may be old, or the water was too hot. Try again with lukewarm water, or turn this batch into crackers by rolling it thin.
- No yeast at home: make soda bread instead with flour, baking soda, salt and yogurt or milk soured with lemon juice.

### Show it off
Serve it warm, cut into squares, as the bread course for dinner.

### Clean up
Scrape the bowl with a spatula before washing, since dough clogs sinks. Soak sticky tools in cold water first.

### For the grown-up
Baking bread teaches biology (yeast fermentation), chemistry (gluten and browning), careful measuring and real oven independence. Give clear permission for the oven, and check for wheat allergies. **Ask at dinner:** "What were the bubbles in the bread, and where did they come from?"

## 18. Ten-Minute Play

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, a stapler or paper clips, chairs for a "stage"

**Nice to have:** a few props, a lamp for stage lighting, two or three people to read parts

### The big idea
A ten-minute play is a complete story on stage: one place, a few people, one big change. Theaters hold whole festivals of them. You'll write one, then gather whoever's home for a **table read**, where actors read the script aloud for the first time. Hearing your words in someone else's voice is a strange and great feeling.

### Goals
- A finished script of about 8 to 10 pages in play format.
- A table read with at least one other person, or a solo read where you voice everyone.

### Objectives
- I can write in correct play format with character names, dialogue and stage directions.
- I can give each character a clear want and an obstacle.
- I can build a scene to a turning point, then a change.

### Get ready
Set up a writing spot. Write the format at the top of the page so you remember it: character name in capitals, dialogue below, stage directions in parentheses.

### Steps
1. **Premise** (10 minutes). Pick two characters and one place they can't easily leave: two strangers stuck in an elevator, a knight and a dragon at a job interview, siblings cleaning out a grandparent's attic.
2. Give each character a **want** that clashes with the other's. The knight needs the job; the dragon is the interviewer and hates knights.
3. Plan in three beats: the setup (page 1 to 3), the rising fight (page 4 to 7) and the **turn**, where something is revealed or someone changes (page 8 to 10).
4. Write (45 minutes). One page of script is about one minute on stage. Keep stage directions short: "(She slams the door. It doesn't close.)"
5. Read it aloud to yourself. Cut any line that explains feelings the actors could show.
6. **Table read.** Hand out parts. Read it straight through without stopping. Take notes on where people laughed and where they lost the thread.
7. Make one round of changes based on what you heard.

### Make it yours
- Add a third character who enters halfway through and flips everything.
- Write it with no dialogue at all, only stage directions, as a silent play.
- Write it so the last line is the same as the first line, but it means something totally different.

### Stuck?
- The characters just talk: give one of them a secret and a reason to hide it.
- It ends too soon: add an obstacle right when the problem seems solved.

### Show it off
Stage it in the living room with a lamp as a spotlight. Even scripts in hand count.

### Clean up
Staple the final draft and file it. Put any props back.

### For the grown-up
Playwriting teaches structure, character and dialogue, all central to English and drama classes, and a table read builds confidence. If they ask you to read a part, say yes. **Ask at dinner:** "What does each character want, and who gets it?"

## 19. New Game with a Standard Deck

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |

| Game | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a standard deck of 52 playing cards, paper, a pen, coins or buttons for points

**Nice to have:** a second deck, a few people to playtest

### The big idea
Thousands of games have been played with the same 52 cards. You're about to invent one more. The trick is choosing a **core mechanic** (what players mainly do) and twisting it into something nobody's tried. Make it a game your family asks to play again.

### Goals
- A new card game with a name and a written rule sheet.
- At least two test games, with one rule improved each time.

### Objectives
- I can name common card game mechanics: trick-taking, set collection, shedding and bluffing.
- I can use probability to balance a game (a deck has 4 suits of 13).
- I can write rules that a new player can follow.

### Get ready
Shuffle the deck. Get paper. Think of a card game you know well, and write down what you like and don't like about it.

### Steps
1. **Pick a mechanic** (5 minutes):
   - Trick-taking: everyone plays one card, the highest wins the "trick."
   - Set collection: gather matching cards, like three of a kind.
   - Shedding: be the first to get rid of all your cards.
   - Bluffing: play cards face down and claim what they are.

3. Write the setup: how many cards per player, what's in the middle, who goes first.

6. **Check the math.** If your special power is on Aces, there are only 4 in the deck. Is that too rare? Too common?
7. Revise, give it a name, and write a clean rule sheet with an example turn.

### Make it yours
- Give the game a theme: suits become four kingdoms at war, and face cards are their rulers.
- Make it a two-player duel or a 6-player chaos game.
- Make some rules secret: each player draws a hidden "goal card" from a set you write.

### Stuck?
- The game ends too fast: add a rule that you draw a card at the end of each turn.
- Luck decides everything: give players a choice every turn, like keeping or swapping a card.

### Show it off
Teach it at the table after dinner. You must explain the rules in under two minutes.

### Clean up
Count the deck back to 52. Fold the rule sheet into the box.

### For the grown-up
Designing a card game uses probability, logic, and clear technical writing. Playing it together is a no-screen family night with a real creator in the room. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the trick in your game that makes it different from the ones we know?"

## 20. Truss Bridge Model

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** old newspaper or printer paper, tape, white glue, scissors, a ruler, two chairs or two stacks of books, a plastic bag, cans or full water bottles for weights

**Nice to have:** a kitchen scale, craft sticks or wooden skewers, a pencil to roll tubes around

### The big idea
A flat sheet of paper can't hold a pencil across a gap. Roll it into tubes and connect them in triangles, and it can hold a stack of cans. That's the magic of the **truss**. Your challenge: build a paper bridge that spans 40 cm and holds the most weight for the least paper.

### Goals
- A truss bridge that spans 40 cm between two supports.
- A load test with your bridge's strength-to-weight score.

### Objectives
- I can explain why triangles are rigid and squares aren't.
- I can identify parts under **tension** (being pulled) and **compression** (being pushed).
- I can compare designs using a strength-to-weight ratio.

### Get ready
Set two chairs or two book stacks 40 cm apart. Make a stack of 20 paper tubes: roll each sheet tightly around a pencil, tape the edge, and slide out the pencil.

### Steps
1. **Test a triangle.** Tape three tubes into a triangle and four into a square. Press on each. The square folds flat. The triangle doesn't. That's the whole idea.
2. Pick a truss design and sketch it:
   - **Warren**: triangles that alternate up and down.
   - **Pratt**: vertical posts with diagonals slanting toward the center.
   - **Howe**: vertical posts with diagonals slanting toward the ends.
3. Build two identical side trusses, about 50 cm long so they rest 5 cm on each support (30 minutes). Glue and tape every joint.
4. Connect the sides with cross pieces along the top and bottom to make a box. Add a paper deck.
5. Weigh the bridge if you have a scale, or count the sheets of paper you used.
6. **Load test.** Hang a plastic bag from the center and add cans or bottles one at a time. Note which part fails first: a top piece buckling (compression) or a bottom joint pulling apart (tension).
7. Calculate the score: weight held divided by bridge weight (or sheets used).
8. Rebuild the part that failed, stronger, and test again.

### Make it yours
- Design for a story: a bridge for a mountain village that only has paper, and the whole village must cross with their goats.
- Build two designs (Warren and Pratt) with the same paper count and race them.
- Make an arch bridge and compare it to your truss.

### Stuck?
- Tubes bend in the middle: roll them tighter, or use two sheets per tube for the top chords.
- Joints pull apart: wrap each joint in a figure-eight of tape and add glue.

### Show it off
Run a live load test after dinner. Let the family guess the number of cans before it breaks.

### Clean up
Recycle the paper (after the crash) and put the cans back in the cupboard.

### For the grown-up
This is structural engineering: forces, geometry and testing, all part of high school physics and engineering classes. It also teaches that failure is data. Keep feet clear of the falling cans. **Ask at dinner:** "Which part of your bridge failed first, and why?"

## 21. Start a Compost Bin

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-90 min | Outdoors | High | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a plastic storage tub with a lid or a large bucket (or a corner of the yard), a screwdriver or nail to make holes, dry "browns" (dead leaves, shredded newspaper, plain cardboard), fresh "greens" (fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, grass clippings), a trowel or old spoon, water, gloves

**Nice to have:** a thermometer, a clear plastic bottle for a mini see-through compost, a sign marker

### The big idea
Banana peels, apple cores and dead leaves don't have to go to a landfill. With the right mix, billions of tiny organisms will turn them into rich, dark soil. You're building a home for those decomposers and learning how to keep them happy. Think of it as running a very small, very hungry city.

### Goals
- A working compost bin with its first layers of browns and greens.
- A posted guide for your household: what goes in and what stays out.

### Objectives
- I can explain how **decomposers** (bacteria, fungi and worms) break down food waste.
- I can balance browns (carbon) and greens (nitrogen) at about 3 to 1 by volume.
- I can keep compost moist and aerated so it doesn't smell.

### Get ready
Ask a grown-up where the bin can live: a shady corner of a yard, a balcony, or near a back door. Put on gloves. Collect a bag of browns before you start.

### Steps
1. **Make the bin** (15 minutes). With a grown-up's OK, poke 15 to 20 small holes in the lid and sides of the tub with a screwdriver, and a few in the bottom for drainage. Decomposers need air.
2. Set the bin on bricks, a tray or bare ground.
3. **Layer it.** Start with 10 cm of browns on the bottom, loose and fluffy. Add a thin layer of greens. Add another layer of browns, about three times as thick as the greens.
4. Chop scraps into pieces the size of a coin. Smaller pieces break down faster.
5. Water it until it feels like a wrung-out sponge. Too wet smells. Too dry does nothing.
6. **Write the guide** (15 minutes). Yes: fruit and vegetable scraps, eggshells, coffee grounds, tea leaves, paper, leaves. No: meat, fish, dairy, oils, pet waste, anything greasy. Tape it inside a kitchen cupboard.
7. Set up a kitchen scrap container (a lidded jar or bowl) and plan who empties it.
8. Start a log: date, what went in, temperature if you have a thermometer, and smell.

### Make it yours
- Build a mini compost in a clear plastic bottle, cut in half and taped back, so you can watch the layers change for weeks.
- Run an experiment: two bins, one turned every three days and one never turned. Which breaks down faster?
- Give the bin a personality and a name, and make a "menu" sign for it.

### Stuck?
- It smells: too many greens or too wet. Add browns and mix.
- Nothing is happening after two weeks: too dry or too cold. Add water and more greens, and turn it with the trowel.
- No yard: do the bottle version on a windowsill, or keep the bin on a balcony.

### Show it off
Give your family a tour and teach them the yes and no list. In a month, show them the first dark soil.

### Clean up
Rinse the trowel and gloves, wash your hands well, and sweep up any spilled soil.

### For the grown-up
Composting teaches the biology of decomposition, the carbon and nitrogen cycles, and real stewardship. Help with location and hole-poking if needed. Gloves and hand washing matter here. **Ask at dinner:** "What can and can't go in the compost, and why no meat?"

## 23. Invent a Chess Variant

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a chess set, or an 8 x 8 grid drawn on cardboard with 32 pieces made from bottle caps or paper squares labeled with letters, paper, a pen

**Nice to have:** a second set of pieces, a partner to play

### The big idea
Chess has hundreds of official variants: bigger boards, new pieces, pieces that wrap around the edges. Most started with one person asking "what if?" Today you're that person. You'll invent a new piece or rule, test whether it breaks the game, and play a full match of your version.

### Goals
- A chess variant with a name and a one-page rule sheet.
- At least one full game (or solo test game) played with the new rules.

### Objectives
- I can describe how each standard chess piece moves.
- I can design a new piece and estimate its power compared to the others.
- I can test for balance and fix rules that break the game.

### Get ready
Set up a board. If you don't have one, draw 8 rows of 8 squares, each 4 cm, and shade every other square. Label pieces: K, Q, R, B, N (knight) and P. If you don't know the rules, ask someone who does or write down the moves on a card first.

### Steps
1. **Know the values.** Chess players count pawn = 1, knight = 3, bishop = 3, rook = 5, queen = 9. You'll use this to balance your idea.
2. **Pick one change** (10 minutes):
   - A new piece, like a "Leaper" that jumps exactly 3 squares in a straight line.
   - A new board, like 10 x 10 or with two "lake" squares nobody can enter.
   - A new rule, like a captured piece switching sides, or every piece moving twice on your first turn.
3. Write your rule as clearly as an official rule. Can it jump? Can it capture backward? What happens at the edge?
4. **Estimate value.** Count how many squares your new piece can reach from the center of an empty board. A knight reaches 8, a rook 14, a queen 27. More reach usually means more value.
5. Replace one standard piece with yours, or add it, and set up the board.
6. **Playtest** (30 minutes). Play a partner, or play both sides yourself making the best move for each. Watch for a broken strategy, like one piece that wins every game.
7. Fix it, name your variant and write the rule sheet.

### Make it yours
- Add hidden information: each player secretly picks which piece is their "spy."
- Build a story: the board is a city at night, and your new piece is a thief who can only move on dark squares.
- Design a variant for three players on a triangle-shaped board.

### Stuck?
- Your piece is way too strong: limit it, like "can't capture on the move it jumps."
- Games last forever: add a win condition, like reaching the far row with your king.

### Show it off
Challenge someone at home to a match. Explain your new rule in under one minute first.

### Clean up
Keep all pieces in a bag, and tape the rule sheet inside the box lid.

### For the grown-up
Designing a chess variant uses logic, strategy and estimation, plus clear rule writing. If you play chess, play your teen's version; if you don't, it's a fun way to learn. **Ask at dinner:** "What does your new piece do, and is it worth more than a rook?"

## 24. Write a Verse About What You Love

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pen, a table or box to tap a beat on

**Nice to have:** a phone timer or metronome, a second person to keep the beat

### The big idea
A verse is 16 bars of rhythm, rhyme and voice, and the best ones sound like nobody else could have written them. Yours will be about something you love: a person, a place, a game, a food, a feeling. You'll build your own beat, write to it, and perform it with real flow.

### Goals
- A 16-bar verse (or song verse) with a hook.
- A performance over a beat you made yourself.

### Objectives
- I can count bars and fit syllables to a beat.
- I can use **multisyllabic rhymes** and internal rhyme.
- I can vary flow by changing speed, pauses and where words land.

### Get ready
Make a beat on a table: knock (low) on 1 and 3, slap (high) on 2 and 4. Keep it at a medium speed, about 90 beats per minute. That's about the speed of a calm walk.

### Steps
1. **Brainstorm** (10 minutes). Write your topic in the middle of a page. Around it, write 20 specific details: smells, sounds, names, places, inside jokes. "Grandma's kitchen" becomes "steam on the window, cumin, the radio in Spanish, the chair with the wobble."
2. **Rhyme bank.** Take your best details and list rhymes for them. Go past one-syllable rhymes. "Kitchen" rhymes with "missing," "listen," "glistenin'." "Window" with "slow flow" and "info."
3. **Write the hook**: 4 bars you'll repeat. Make it simple and catchy.
4. **Write 16 bars** (25 minutes). One bar is four beats, which is about 8 to 12 syllables at this speed. Read each line over your beat as you go.
5. Use **internal rhyme**: rhymes inside lines, not just at the end. "The cumin in the room is like a perfume blooming."
6. Change the flow in the middle. Slow down for four bars, or put your rhymes on the off-beats.
7. Mark breath spots with a slash. Practice the whole verse with the beat 5 times.

### Make it yours
- Write it from the point of view of the thing you love, like your old sneakers talking about you.
- Write two verses: one about what you love, and one about something that got in its way.
- Change the genre: turn it into a slow ballad or a chant for a stadium.

### Stuck?
- Lines are too long for the beat: cut filler words like "really," "just" and "so."
- It sounds forced: never use a rhyme you don't mean. Rewrite the line around a better word.

### Show it off
Perform it for someone at home with a second person keeping the beat. Then teach them the hook.

### Clean up
Copy the final verse into a notebook. Keep the rhyme bank for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds poetry skills (rhyme, rhythm, imagery and voice) and music skills like tempo and meter, the same skills in English and music classes. Let them perform without judging the genre. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you rap the hook for us?"

## 25. Solar Still

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-120 min | Outdoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a large bowl, a small cup or glass that fits inside it, plastic wrap, a small stone or coin, salt, water, a measuring cup, a teaspoon

**Nice to have:** a dark-colored bowl, a second setup for a comparison, food coloring, a thermometer

### The big idea
You're stranded on a desert island with seawater all around and nothing to drink. You can't drink salt water. But the sun can separate the water from the salt for you. Build a **solar still**, the same idea survival experts use, and see how much clean water you can make before "rescue."

### Goals
- A working solar still that collects drops of fresh water.
- A measured amount of collected water and proof that it isn't salty.

### Objectives
- I can explain **evaporation** and **condensation** in the water cycle.
- I can explain why salt stays behind when water evaporates.
- I can test a variable and measure the result.

### Get ready
Find a sunny spot outdoors: a step, a balcony, a patio. A very sunny windowsill works if you can't go out. Mix "seawater": 2 teaspoons of salt in 2 cups of warm tap water.

### Steps
1. Taste a drop of your seawater (just a drop) so you know what salty is.
2. Pour the seawater into the big bowl, 2 to 3 cm deep. Add food coloring if you have it: it'll prove that only water travels.
3. Set the empty cup in the center. It must be taller than the water but shorter than the bowl's rim.
4. Stretch plastic wrap tight over the bowl and seal the edges.
5. Put the stone in the middle of the wrap, right above the cup, so the wrap dips into a cone pointing down.
6. **Wait and watch** (45 to 90 minutes). Check every 15 minutes. Draw what you see: fog, then drops, then drips into the cup.
7. Carefully remove the wrap and measure what's in the cup with a teaspoon. Taste a drop. Is it salty? What color is it?
8. Calculate how many hours you'd need to make one cup of fresh water at this rate.

### Make it yours
- Build two stills to test a variable: a dark bowl vs. a light one, or full sun vs. shade.
- Write a short survival log from your island, with your water totals day by day.
- Collect water from leaves instead: tie a clear bag over a leafy branch outside and compare.

### Stuck?
- No drops after 30 minutes: it needs more heat. Use warmer water or a sunnier spot.
- Drops fall back into the bowl: make the cone steeper with a slightly heavier stone.

### Show it off
Give your family a blind taste test: a drop of seawater and a drop of your still water. Which is which?

### Clean up
Pour the salt water down the sink, not on plants (salt harms them), and wash the bowls.

### For the grown-up
This is earth science and chemistry: evaporation, condensation, solutions and the water cycle. Only drinking-water ingredients are used, so a taste is safe. **Ask at dinner:** "Why doesn't the salt end up in the cup?"

## 26. Graphic Memoir of One Day

### The big idea
Graphic memoir is a true story told in comics. It doesn't need explosions; some of the best are about one ordinary day that turned out to matter. You'll pick one real day from your life (the best, the worst, the weirdest) and tell it in a two-page comic.

## 27. Clothes Flip and Patch Art

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** an old piece of clothing you're allowed to change (jeans, a jacket, a plain shirt, a tote), fabric scraps from worn-out clothes, a needle, thread (contrast colors look best), scissors, pins or clips, chalk or a pencil

**Nice to have:** embroidery thread, a thimble, an iron (with a grown-up's OK)

### The big idea
A rip doesn't have to be the end. **Visible mending** turns holes into art with bold stitches and patches you show off instead of hide. You'll take a worn or boring piece of clothing and flip it into something one of a kind, with techniques tailors have used for centuries.

### Goals
- One piece of clothing repaired or redesigned with at least one patch and decorative stitching.
- A sample cloth with three stitches you practiced.

### Objectives
- I can thread a needle, knot thread and sew a running stitch and a whipstitch.
- I can plan a design that fits the shape of the clothing.
- I can attach a patch so it lasts through washing.

### Get ready
Get permission for the piece you're changing. Wash your hands. Cut a 15 cm square from a scrap for your sample cloth. Thread a needle with an arm's length of thread and tie a knot at the end.

### Steps
1. **Practice on the sample** (15 minutes):
   - Running stitch: up, down, up, down in a straight line, stitches about 5 mm long.
   - Whipstitch: loop over the edge of a fabric, good for attaching patches.
   - Backstitch: each stitch goes back to meet the last one, making a solid line.
2. **Plan the design.** Lay the clothing flat. Chalk where patches or stitching will go. Consider a patch on a knee, stitched lines across a pocket, or a stitched star on a back.
3. Cut a patch 2 cm bigger than the hole on all sides. Round the corners so they don't peel.
4. Pin the patch in place (on the outside for a bold look, or behind for a peek-through look).
5. Sew the patch with whipstitch around the edge (25 minutes). Keep your other hand inside the clothing so you don't sew the front to the back.
6. **Decorate** with running stitches in a grid, rays or rows across the patch. Contrast thread on dark denim looks great.
7. Knot off on the inside: make a tiny stitch, pass the needle through the loop twice, pull tight and trim.

### Make it yours
- Design a patch that tells a story: a map of your neighborhood, a symbol only your friends get.
- Turn the clothing into something else: a shirt into a tote bag, or jeans into shorts with a stitched hem.
- Make a matching pair: patch two items with the same design for you and a friend.

### Stuck?
- The thread keeps knotting: use a shorter piece, and let the needle dangle every few stitches to untwist.
- The patch puckers: you're pulling too tight. Stitch more loosely, and smooth the fabric as you go.

### Show it off
Wear it at dinner and give a "designer interview" about your choices.

### Clean up
Stick needles back in a pincushion or a scrap of fabric, count them, and sweep for dropped pins.

### For the grown-up
This is textile art and practical repair: hand sewing, design, and a habit of fixing instead of tossing. Needles are sharp; a pincushion and a count at the end help. If they use an iron, it's with your OK. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the story behind your patch?"

## 28. Stretch Flow Designer

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** comfortable clothes, a towel or mat, index cards or paper, a pen, a clock or timer

**Nice to have:** a belt or towel strap, calm music from a radio, a friend or family member to teach

### The big idea
Fitness coaches design routines for a reason, like waking up stiff legs or loosening tight shoulders after a long day. Today you're the coach. You'll design a 15-minute stretch and mobility flow for a real need, test it on your own body, and then teach it so clearly someone else can follow it without you.

### Goals
- A 15-minute flow of 8 to 12 moves on cue cards.
- A full run-through on your own and one taught session.

### Objectives
- I can tell the difference between **dynamic** stretches (moving) and **static** stretches (holding).
- I can order moves so the flow warms up the body before deep stretches.
- I can give clear, safe cues for posture and breathing.

### Get ready
Pick the purpose: "wake-up flow," "after a long day sitting," "before a run," or "wind down before bed." Clear a space about as big as a mat, with room to reach your arms out.

### Steps
1. **Warm-up first** (5 minutes). Choose 3 dynamic moves to raise your heart rate gently: arm circles, marching with high knees, torso twists.
2. **Pick the main stretches.** Choose 6 to 8 that fit your purpose. For sitting all day: cat-cow, hip flexor lunge, chest opener with hands clasped behind, hamstring stretch, figure-four, neck side stretch.
3. Order them so they **flow**: standing moves, then kneeling, then seated, then lying down. Less getting up and down feels calmer.
4. Set the timing: hold static stretches for 20 to 30 seconds per side. Breathe out as you go deeper. Never bounce.
5. **Write cue cards** (20 minutes): one per move with the name, a stick-figure drawing, and two cues ("Press your hips forward. Keep your back knee soft.").
6. Run the whole flow on yourself with a timer. Cut or swap anything that hurts or feels awkward.
7. **Teach it.** Lead someone through, reading only your cues. Watch them, and fix one cue that confused them.

### Make it yours
- Make it a story flow: each stretch is a part of an animal's journey, from a sleepy bear to a stretching cat.
- Design a 5-minute "desk break" version anyone could do between homework.
- Make a partner flow where two people help each other stretch.

### Stuck?
- A stretch hurts: ease off to where you feel a gentle pull, not pain. Sharp pain means stop.
- The flow feels choppy: add a breathing pause (3 slow breaths) between sections.

### Show it off
Lead the family through your flow after dinner. Hand out cue cards so everyone can follow.

### Clean up
Fold up the mat, and clip your cue cards together for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds understanding of anatomy, flexibility and safe exercise from health and PE, plus clear instruction-writing and leadership. Remind them that stretching should never be painful. **Ask at dinner:** "Which stretch from your flow should I do after a long day?"

## 29. Care Kits for Neighbors in Need

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kindness | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** zip bags or paper bags, spare items your family agrees to give (new toothbrushes, travel soaps, socks, wrapped snacks, tissues, lip balm, a pencil and notepad), paper, pens or markers

**Nice to have:** a tote or box for delivery, a list from a local shelter, food bank or community group

### The big idea
A care kit can make a hard day a little easier for someone. The difference between a nice kit and a truly useful one is planning: knowing who it's for, what they need and how it gets to them. You'll design, pack and plan the delivery of care kits, and write a note for each one that makes someone feel seen.

### Goals
- Two to six care kits, packed and labeled.
- A handwritten note in each kit, and a delivery plan your family agrees on.

### Objectives
- I can match items to the real needs of the people a kit is for.
- I can plan with a budget of what we already have.
- I can write a kind, respectful message to a stranger.

### Get ready
Talk with a grown-up first about who the kits are for and how they'll be delivered. Ideas: a community shelter or food bank (a grown-up calls ahead to ask what they accept), an older neighbor, or "rough night" kits for your own household. Gather spare items they say you can use.

### Steps
1. **Pick the recipient group** and write three of their likely needs. For people without housing: warm socks, hygiene items, snacks that don't need cooking. For an older neighbor: a puzzle book, a note, tea.
2. **Inventory** (10 minutes). Lay out everything you have. Sort into categories: hygiene, warmth, food, comfort.
3. Plan the kits so each one is balanced. If you have 3 pairs of socks, make 3 kits, not one huge kit.
4. Check the food rules: everything sealed, in date, and with no peanuts. Many groups can't accept open or homemade food.
5. Pack each bag in the same order so they're fair.
6. **Write the notes** (20 minutes). Keep them warm and simple, no pity: "Thinking of you today. Hope these help. Stay warm." Sign with a first name only, or just "a neighbor."
7. Label each bag with what's inside on the outside, so the people handing them out know.
8. Make the delivery plan with your grown-up: where, when and who goes.

### Make it yours
- Add a hand-drawn bookmark or small art card to each kit.
- Make a theme kit: a "winter warm-up" kit or a "rainy day" kit for kids at a shelter.
- Organize a household "kit drive": make a sign asking family members to give one item each.

### Stuck?
- You don't have much to give: notes and art cards in a simple bag still matter. Fewer, thoughtful kits beat many empty ones.
- You're not sure what's needed: write a question list for your grown-up to ask the group.

### Show it off
Lay the finished kits out for the family before delivery and read one note aloud.

### Clean up
Return unused items to where they belong and recycle packaging scraps.

### For the grown-up
This builds empathy, planning and community awareness. Please decide together who receives the kits and handle the call and delivery yourself or go along. Use only what your family can spare; no buying is needed. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you write in your notes?"

## 30. Mission Control Crisis

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 90-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** cardboard boxes for consoles, paper, markers, index cards or scraps, tape, scissors, a clock, and a "ship's supplies" box (tape, a sock, a cardboard tube, string, a plastic bag, rubber bands, a paper cup, a sheet of foil)

**Nice to have:** flashlights, walkie-talkies or two cups and string, headsets made from headbands, a crew of family or friends

### The big idea
"We have a problem." A spacecraft three days from Earth just had something go very wrong, and you're the **flight director** in Mission Control. The crew can only use what's on board. You'll build the control room, run the crisis, and solve a real hands-on engineering puzzle against the clock to bring them home.

### Goals
- A working Mission Control room with labeled consoles.
- A full mission run with at least three crises solved, including one built fix.

### Objectives
- I can stay calm and make decisions under time pressure.
- I can solve an engineering problem using only limited materials.
- I can write and follow a checklist, the way real flight teams do.

### Get ready
Set up 2 or 3 boxes as consoles. Label them: FLIGHT (that's you), LIFE SUPPORT, POWER, NAVIGATION. Draw dials, switches and screens on them. Put the "ship's supplies" box on a table: that's everything the crew has.

### Steps
1. **Write the mission** (10 minutes): ship name, crew of three, destination and the moment it goes wrong. "Day 3, a tank bursts. Power is dropping."
2. **Make 6 crisis cards**: oxygen leak, power drain, comms blackout, course drift, a crew member gets sick, a filter doesn't fit.
3. **Write checklists.** For each console, write a 4-step emergency checklist: "1. Confirm the reading. 2. Isolate the problem. 3. Report to Flight. 4. Propose a fix."
4. **Start the clock.** Draw a crisis card. Work through the right console's checklist (play each role yourself, or hand roles to family). Give each crisis 10 minutes.
5. **The build challenge** (20 minutes). Crisis: "The air filter is square. The slot is round." Using only the supplies box, build an adapter that lets a square filter (a folded paper square) attach to a round opening (the cardboard tube) with no gaps. Test it by blowing through.
6. Keep a **flight log**: time, problem, decision, result. Real flight directors log everything.
7. Bring them home: final crisis, the re-entry checklist. Count down, and call "splashdown."

### Make it yours
- Run it in real time with a family member as the astronaut in another room, talking by cup-and-string phone.
- Add a twist card that turns a fixed problem back on.
- Design your own mission patch and crew names.

### Stuck?
- The build challenge seems impossible: list every material and one way each could seal, bend or connect. Combine two.
- Solo play feels thin: talk out loud as each role, using a different voice for each console.

### Show it off
Run one crisis live for your family after dinner and let them vote on the flight director's decision.

### Clean up
Flatten the consoles or keep them for next time, and return the supplies.

### For the grown-up
This is a role-play built on real spaceflight problem-solving: engineering with limits, teamwork, checklists and calm thinking under pressure, all skills from science and career classes. Any crew size works, including one. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest crisis, and how did you solve it?"
