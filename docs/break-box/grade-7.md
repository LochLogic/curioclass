## 1. Trebuchet

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 9 unsharpened or used pencils, about 15 rubber bands, a 30 cm ruler, masking tape, a plastic spoon, string, a sandwich bag, about 20 coins or half a cup of dry rice or beans, mini marshmallows or balls of scrunched foil for ammo, a tape measure

**Nice to have:** a paper cup tower to knock down, a friend to compete against

### The big idea
In the Middle Ages, armies didn't need gunpowder to knock down a castle wall. They used a trebuchet: a giant arm swung by a falling weight. Today you build a desktop one and tune it until it can hit a target across the room.

### Goals
- A working trebuchet that launches a marshmallow at least 2 meters.
- A record of what you changed and how far each shot went.

### Objectives
- I can explain how a falling weight turns into a fast-moving arm.
- I can change one thing at a time and measure what happens.
- I can use triangles to make a frame strong.

### Get ready
Clear a launch lane at least 4 meters long: a hallway, a bare floor, or a patio. Put a strip of tape on the floor as your launch line. Fill the sandwich bag with the coins or rice and twist it closed with a rubber band.

### Steps
1. **Build two triangles.** Rubber-band three pencils into a triangle. Wrap each corner tight with two bands. Make a second one the same.
2. **Make the frame.** Stand the triangles up about 8 cm apart, points on top. Join their bottom corners with two more pencils, rubber-banded on. Lay one pencil across the two top points: that's your axle.
3. **Add the arm.** Rubber-band the ruler to the axle so about one quarter of it is on one side and three quarters on the other. Tape the spoon to the end of the long side, bowl facing up.
4. **Hang the weight.** Tie the bag to the short end with 5 cm of string, so it hangs free.
5. **First launch.** Hold the long end down to the floor, set a marshmallow in the spoon, and let go. Measure where it lands first, not where it rolls.
6. **Tune it (30 minutes).** Now you're the engineer. Change one thing per round: more or less weight, the axle spot on the ruler, a longer string on the weight. Fire three shots each round and write down the farthest. Which change made the biggest jump?
7. **Siege time.** Build a castle from paper cups 3 meters away and see how many shots it takes to knock it down.

### Make it yours
- Add a sling: a 20 cm string with a paper cup bottom on the end, hooked loosely on a bent paper clip at the arm tip. Real trebuchets used slings to throw much farther.
- Switch from distance to accuracy: draw a target with rings worth 10, 5 and 1 points.
- Build a second trebuchet with a different design and hold a duel.

### Stuck?
- The frame wobbles or falls over: tape the bottom pencils to a piece of cardboard as a base.
- The marshmallow flies backward or straight up: it's releasing too late. Tilt the spoon so it faces a little more forward.
- It barely moves: the weight is too light or the arm is rubbing. Add 5 more coins and loosen the band on the axle a little.

### Show it off
Run a "siege demo" for your family: explain how it works in one minute, then let each person take a shot at the cup castle.

### Clean up
Take off the rubber bands, return the pencils and ruler, and pick up every piece of ammo (pets love marshmallows).

### For the grown-up
This is physics and engineering: energy changing form, levers, and careful testing where only one thing changes at a time. Keep the ammo soft and the launch lane clear of faces, pets and anything breakable. **Ask at dinner:** "What one change made your trebuchet throw the farthest, and why do you think it worked?"

## 2. Twist Ending Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper or a notebook, a pencil or pen, sticky notes or scrap paper

**Nice to have:** a highlighter, a reader to test it on

### The big idea
The best twist endings make you gasp and then immediately want to reread the whole story, because the clues were there all along. Today you write one of those. The secret: you write the ending first.

### Goals
- A finished short story, about 600 to 1,000 words, with an ending nobody sees coming.
- At least three hidden clues that make the twist feel fair.

### Objectives
- I can plan a story backward from its ending.
- I can plant clues that mean one thing at first and another thing later.
- I can use a red herring to point the reader the wrong way.

### Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Fold a sheet of paper into three columns and label them: What the reader thinks, What's really true, Clues.

### Steps
1. **Pick your twist type.** Here are three that work: the narrator isn't who you think (the "kid" telling the story is a dog); the helper is the villain (the friendly neighbor took the missing trophy); the place isn't where you think (the "island" is a fish tank). Or invent your own.
2. **Write your last sentence first.** Example: "And that's when I remembered I'd never actually had a sister." Put it at the bottom of a page.
3. **Fill in your three columns (10 minutes).** What does the reader believe for most of the story? What's the truth? List five possible clues.
4. **Choose your best three clues** and decide where they go: one in the first paragraph, one in the middle, one just before the end. Each clue should have an innocent explanation the first time through.
5. **Add a red herring:** a fake suspect or false lead that pulls attention away from the truth.
6. **Draft (30 to 40 minutes).** Write fast. Don't stop to fix spelling. Keep your twist secret by never lying outright: you can hide things, but don't cheat.
7. **The reread test.** Read it again as if you know the ending. Does every clue click? Does any line give it away too early? Fix those.

### Make it yours
- Write it as diary entries, so the twist hides in what the writer doesn't say.
- Add a double twist: the reader figures out the first one and then gets hit by a second.
- Write it from the point of view of an object, like a house, a backpack, or a vending machine.

### Stuck?
- The twist feels random: you probably need more clues. Add one more, earlier.
- Everyone guesses it: your clues are too loud. Bury one in the middle of a sentence about something else.
- Can't think of a story: start with a normal day and ask "What if one thing about it was a lie?"

### Show it off
Read it aloud to someone at home. Watch their face at the end, then ask them which clue they missed.

### Clean up
Staple your pages together, title it, and put it somewhere safe so it doesn't end up as scrap paper.

### For the grown-up
This builds planning, revision, and the reading skill of noticing clues, which shows up in English class as "foreshadowing" and "unreliable narrator." **Ask at dinner:** "What was your twist, and did anyone guess it before the end?"

## 3. Dream House Floor Plan to Scale

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper (graph paper if you have it), a ruler, a pencil, an eraser, a tape measure, colored pencils or markers, scissors, scrap paper

**Nice to have:** tracing paper, a sheet of cardboard to mount the plan on

### The big idea
Architects don't just draw a pretty house. They draw a plan where every line is real, so a builder could actually make it. Design the house you'd live in if money and gravity were mostly optional, then draw it to scale like a pro.

### Goals
- A floor plan of your dream house where 1 square equals 1 foot (or 1 cm equals 50 cm).
- Paper furniture, cut to scale, that you can slide around the plan.

### Objectives
- I can use a scale to turn real sizes into drawing sizes.
- I can measure real objects and draw them correctly.
- I can plan a space so people can actually move through it.

### Get ready
If you don't have graph paper, rule your own: lines 1 cm apart across a full sheet, both ways. Pick your scale and write it in the corner: "1 square = 1 foot."

### Steps
1. **Measure real life (15 minutes).** Use the tape measure on your bed, a door, the couch, a hallway, the bathtub. Write each one down. A single bed is about 3 feet by 6.5 feet. A door is about 3 feet wide. A hallway needs at least 3 feet.
2. **List your rooms.** Include the normal ones (kitchen, bathroom, bedrooms) and at least three dream rooms: a climbing wall, a library with a secret door, an indoor pond, a slide down to the kitchen.
3. **Sketch a bubble plan.** Draw loose circles for each room and arrange them. What should be next to what? The kitchen near the dining area, the bedrooms away from the drum room.
4. **Draw the walls (30 minutes).** Turn bubbles into straight walls on your grid. Make outside walls thick (two lines) and inside walls thin. Leave gaps for doors and show which way each door swings with a quarter circle.
5. **Cut furniture to scale.** Use your measurements from step 1. A 3 by 6.5 foot bed is 3 by 6.5 squares. Make at least eight pieces.
6. **Test the flow.** Slide the furniture into place. Can you walk from the front door to every room? Can a door open without hitting the bed? Move walls if you need to.
7. **Finish it.** Color the floors (wood, tile, grass for the indoor garden), label rooms, and add a north arrow.

### Make it yours
- Design it for an unusual site: a cliff, a tree, underwater, or a city rooftop.
- Design a house for someone else: a grandparent who uses a wheelchair, a family of six, or a giant.
- Add a second floor on tracing paper so you can lay it over the first.

### Stuck?
- Rooms don't fit: shrink the hallway or stack rooms on a second floor.
- The math is confusing: stick to 1 square = 1 foot and round everything to the nearest half foot.
- It looks messy: go over your final lines in pen and erase the pencil after.

### Show it off
Give your family a "walkthrough": trace a path with your finger and describe each room as if they were buying the house.

### Clean up
Put the furniture pieces in an envelope taped to the back of the plan.

### For the grown-up
This is applied math (ratio, scale and measurement, all grade 7 topics) plus design thinking about how people use space. **Ask at dinner:** "Which room in your dream house would you spend the most time in, and what's in it?"

## 4. Pasta Night from Scratch Sauce

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 75-120 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** 1 large can (about 800 g or 28 oz) crushed or whole tomatoes, 1 onion, 2 garlic cloves, 2 tablespoons olive oil, 1 teaspoon salt, half a teaspoon sugar, 1 teaspoon dried oregano or basil, 450 g (1 pound) dry pasta, a big pot, a saucepan, a cutting board, a knife, a wooden spoon, a colander, a mug

**Nice to have:** grated cheese, chili flakes, fresh basil, a pat of butter

**Get ready the night before:** Ask a grown-up for permission to use the stove and knife tomorrow, and check that the ingredients are in the house.

### The big idea
Jarred sauce is fine. Sauce you made yourself, from a can of tomatoes and an onion, is the kind people talk about. Tonight you're the chef: you'll cook a real tomato sauce, tune its flavor by tasting, and serve the family a pasta night with a menu.

### Goals
- A pot of tomato sauce you made from scratch and dinner for the family.
- A menu card with the name of your sauce.

### Objectives
- I can dice an onion safely using the claw grip.
- I can taste a sauce and adjust salt, sweetness and acid.
- I can time two things on the stove so they finish together.

### Get ready
Wash your hands, clear the counter, and set out everything. Check with your grown-up about allergies (wheat, dairy) for anyone eating. Fill the big pot with water, add a big pinch of salt, and put a lid on it. Don't turn it on yet.

### Steps
1. **Chop.** Cut the onion in half through the root, peel it, and lay it flat. Curl your fingertips under (the claw) and dice it small. Peel and finely chop the garlic.
2. **Soften (10 minutes).** Heat the oil in the saucepan on medium-low. Add the onion and a pinch of salt. Stir now and then until it's soft and see-through, not brown. Add garlic for 1 minute.
3. **Simmer (20 to 25 minutes).** Pour in the tomatoes (squish whole ones with the spoon), add the sugar and herbs. Turn it to low so it bubbles gently. Stir every few minutes so it doesn't stick.
4. **Start the pasta.** About 15 minutes before the sauce is done, turn the water to high. When it's boiling hard, add the pasta and set a timer for the time on the box.
5. **Taste like a chef.** Taste the sauce on a spoon (let it cool). Flat? Add a pinch of salt. Sour? A pinch more sugar. Boring? Your choice: chili flakes, a pat of butter, more herbs. Taste again after each change.
6. **Save pasta water.** Before draining, scoop out a mug of the cooking water. Have your grown-up pour the pot into the colander if it's heavy.
7. **Bring it together.** Toss the pasta with the sauce and a splash of pasta water so it coats every piece.
8. **Write the menu** while it rests: the name of your sauce ("Nonna-Level Red" or "Volcano Sauce") and a one-line description.

### Make it yours
- Make it spicy (arrabbiata): add half a teaspoon of chili flakes with the garlic.
- Sneak in vegetables: grate a carrot into the onion, or stir in a handful of spinach at the end.
- Try the butter method: skip the oil and garlic, simmer the tomatoes with 3 tablespoons of butter and a halved onion, then remove the onion.

### Stuck?
- Sauce is too thin: simmer 10 more minutes with the lid off.
- Sauce is too thick or sticking: stir in a few spoons of pasta water.
- Onion makes you cry: chill it for 10 minutes first and use your sharpest knife.

### Show it off
Serve it restaurant style: menu on the table, you announce the dish, and every diner rates it out of five stars.

### Clean up
Wash the pots while they're warm (it's way easier), wipe the stovetop once it's cool, and leave the kitchen cleaner than you found it.

### For the grown-up
This builds real cooking skills, timing, and tasting with intent. Middle schoolers can use the stove and a knife with your permission and a check-in or two: be home, and stop by when the water goes on. Turn pot handles toward the back. Check allergies for everyone eating. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you add after tasting, and how did it change the sauce?"

## 5. Camera Obscura

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** a shoebox or similar box with a lid, aluminum foil, wax paper or baking parchment, black paper or a dark marker, tape, scissors, a pin or thumbtack, a towel or dark blanket

**Nice to have:** a magnifying glass, a long tube (like a cereal box) for a second model

### The big idea
Before photography, artists traced pictures made of pure light inside dark boxes and rooms. It works with a tiny hole and nothing else. Build one and watch the world appear upside down on a screen in your hands.

### Goals
- A working pinhole viewer that shows a live, upside-down image of the outside.
- Your answer to the question: what happens when the hole gets bigger?

### Objectives
- I can explain why light through a small hole makes an upside-down image.
- I can test a variable (hole size) and describe the trade-off.
- I can trace or draw an image from a projection.

### Get ready
Find a bright window with a view of something that's lit by the sun: trees, a street, a building. Your viewing spot should be a little darker.

### Steps
1. **Make the screen.** Cut the box in half the short way, so you have two open-ended halves. Tape a sheet of wax paper tightly across the open end of one half. That's your screen.
2. **Rejoin the box.** Slide or tape the two halves back together, screen in the middle. Now the screen sits about halfway inside.
3. **Make the pinhole.** Cut a 2 cm square window in the center of one end. Tape foil over it, smooth and tight, and poke one clean hole with the pin.
4. **Block the light.** Tape every seam and corner so light only gets in through the pinhole. Darken the inside with black paper or marker if you can.
5. **Look (10 minutes).** Point the pinhole at your bright scene, put your face to the open end, and drape the towel over your head and the box. Give your eyes 30 seconds. What's on the screen? Which way is up?
6. **Experiment.** Make the hole a little bigger with the pin. Brighter or dimmer? Sharper or blurrier? Try a second foil square with a hole the size of a pencil tip. Write down what you notice for each.
7. **Lens bonus.** If you have a magnifying glass, hold it over a bigger hole and slide the screen back and forth until the image snaps sharp.

### Make it yours
- Turn a whole room into a camera obscura: cover a window with cardboard and foil, poke a hole, and watch the outside appear on the far wall in the dark.
- Make a long version from a tube. Does a longer box make the image bigger?
- Use it as an art tool: tape thin paper over the screen and trace the scene.

### Stuck?
- You see nothing: it's not dark enough inside. Add more tape and a thicker blanket, and wait longer for your eyes.
- It's just a blur: the hole is too big or ragged. Make a new one in fresh foil.
- Image is too dim: point it at something brighter, like a sunny wall.

### Show it off
Give each person at home a turn under the blanket. Before they look, bet them they can't guess which way up the world will be.

### Clean up
Recycle scraps, put away the scissors, and keep your viewer for a sunny day.

### For the grown-up
This is optics: light travels in straight lines, so rays cross at the pinhole and flip the image. It's how eyes and cameras work, and it connects to seventh grade science on light and waves. Remind them never to point it at the sun or look at the sun directly. **Ask at dinner:** "Why do you think the image came out upside down?"

## 6. Invent a Language

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a notebook or paper, a pencil, index cards or scrap paper

**Nice to have:** a friend or sibling to learn it with you, colored pens for an alphabet

### The big idea
Some of the most famous fantasy worlds started with someone inventing a language for fun. A real language has its own sounds, its own rules and its own way of seeing the world. Build one for a people you make up, and by the end you'll be able to say a sentence nobody else on Earth understands.

### Goals
- A language with its own sound rules, at least 30 words, and three grammar rules.
- A tiny phrasebook with greetings, a proverb, and a sentence you can say aloud.

### Objectives
- I can describe how a language uses sounds, word order and word endings.
- I can create rules and apply them the same way every time.
- I can translate a sentence from English into my language.

### Get ready
Decide who speaks this language. Write two sentences about them: where they live and what matters most to them. Example: "The Velu live on floating rafts in a giant swamp. They care about rain, boats and family."

### Steps
1. **Pick the sounds.** Choose about 12 letters your people use and cross out the rest. Maybe they have no "s" because they're quiet hunters, or lots of "l" and "v" because it sounds like water. Make a rule like "every word ends in a vowel."
2. **Make 30 words (20 minutes).** Start with what your people talk about most. Example: rain = *lumi*, boat = *vaka*, I = *na*, you = *te*, see = *oro*. Put each one on a card or in a list.
3. **Choose word order.** English goes Subject, Verb, Object ("I see the boat"). Yours could go Verb first: *oro na vaka* (see I boat).
4. **Make three grammar rules.** Plurals: add *-ri* (*vakari* = boats). Past tense: put *ka* before the verb. Questions: end with *ho?*
5. **Words English doesn't have.** Invent two words for ideas your people care about, like *melisu*: the smell right before rain.
6. **Write a phrasebook page.** Hello, thank you, goodbye, "Where is the bathroom?", plus one proverb: "*Lumi ka oro, vaka nuva*" (The one who saw rain built a boat).
7. **Translate.** Turn a short sentence or two lines of a song into your language. Practice saying it smoothly.

### Make it yours
- Invent an alphabet: a symbol for each sound, then write your name in it.
- Make it a secret code for you and a sibling or friend, and pass notes only in your language.
- Write a short legend in your language with the English translation underneath.

### Stuck?
- Words all sound the same: give some words two syllables and some three, and use different starting sounds.
- Forgetting your own rules: write them on a "grammar card" and keep it next to you.
- Not sure what words to invent: make words for everything you can see from where you're sitting.

### Show it off
Teach your family three words and a greeting at dinner. Bonus: say grace, a toast, or "pass the salt" in your language.

### Clean up
Keep your cards in an envelope or clip them into your notebook. Languages grow, so you'll want them later.

### For the grown-up
This is linguistics: how sounds, word order and grammar work. It makes learning real languages, and English grammar, much easier to understand, and it's a great creative writing workout. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you say something in your language and teach us what it means?"

## 7. Creature Trading Card Set

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 20 index cards or cardboard cut into 6 by 9 cm cards, colored pencils or markers, a pencil, a ruler, scissors, a coin or a die

**Nice to have:** a sibling, friend or grown-up to play against, card sleeves or sandwich bags

### The big idea
You're the creator of a brand-new creature card game. Every card needs art, stats and a power, and the whole set needs to be balanced so no card is unbeatable. Design it, then battle with it.

### Goals
- A set of at least 16 original creature cards with art and stats.
- A rule sheet for a battle game you've play-tested at least twice.

### Objectives
- I can design a card template and use it the same way on every card.
- I can balance a set of numbers so the game is fair.
- I can test rules and change them based on what happens.

### Get ready
Cut your cards if you need to. Clear a table. Design one card layout on scrap paper first: name at top, art box, type symbol, three stats, a power at the bottom.

### Steps
1. **Invent four types** that beat each other in a circle: for example, Storm beats Tide, Tide beats Ember, Ember beats Moss, Moss beats Storm. Give each a symbol and a color.
2. **Pick three stats:** Power, Speed and Cunning, each from 1 to 10.
3. **Set the balance rule.** Every card's three stats must add up to exactly 15. A 9-Power creature has to be slow or not very clever. This is what keeps the game fair.
4. **Design creatures (45 minutes).** Four per type. Draw each one, name it, set its stats, and give it a short power, like "Once per game, swap Speed and Power." Mix it up: some tiny and sneaky, some huge and dumb.
5. **Write the battle rules.** Here's a starter: each player shuffles 8 cards. Both flip one. Roll a die: 1-2 compares Power, 3-4 Speed, 5-6 Cunning. If your type beats theirs, add 3. Higher number wins both cards.
6. **Play-test.** Play two rounds against someone, or play both sides yourself. Is one card winning everything? Lower a stat. Is a type useless? Give its creatures better powers.
7. **Finalize.** Make a rule card and a "set list" naming every creature in the set.

### Make it yours
- Add evolution: two weaker creatures of the same type can combine into a third, stronger card.
- Build a world: every creature lives in a region on a map, and each card has a one-line field note.
- Add rare cards with a gold border and a stat total of 17, but only one allowed per deck.

### Stuck?
- Running out of creature ideas: mash two animals together (an armadillo-moth) and give it one weird habit.
- One player always wins: check the stat totals. Somebody's card probably adds up to more than 15.
- Art is taking forever: use simple shapes and a bold outline. Detail can come later.

### Show it off
Teach the game to someone at home after dinner and give them first pick of the deck.

### Clean up
Rubber-band the set together with the rule card on top, and keep it in a box or bag.

### For the grown-up
This is game design: systems thinking, number balance (simple ratios and probability with the die), and art, plus testing and revising. It's also a great sibling or after-dinner game. **Ask at dinner:** "Which of your creatures is the strongest, and how did you keep it from being unbeatable?"

## 8. Write a Logic Puzzle Book

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** 4 sheets of paper folded in half and stapled into a booklet, a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a pen

**Nice to have:** colored pencils for the cover, a puzzle-loving person to test it

### The big idea
Making puzzles is harder, and more fun, than solving them. You have to think like the solver and make sure there's exactly one right answer. Build a little booklet of brain-benders that stumps your family.

### Goals
- A booklet with at least six original logic puzzles.
- An answer key at the back, and a tester who tried at least two.

### Objectives
- I can write clues that lead to exactly one answer.
- I can use a logic grid to check my own puzzle.
- I can explain how a puzzle is solved step by step.

### Get ready
Fold and staple your booklet. Write a title on the front, like "Brain Sprain, Volume 1." Leave the last page for answers.

### Steps
1. **Solve one first.** Try this: Ana, Bo and Cal each have a different pet: a cat, a fish and a lizard. Ana is allergic to fur. Bo's pet can't live out of water. Who has which? (Answer: Ana has the lizard, Bo the fish, Cal the cat.) Notice how each clue removes options.
2. **Write a grid puzzle.** Pick 3 people and two categories (pet and snack, or sport and color). Decide the answer first. Then write clues that rule things out until only one answer is left. Draw a grid on the page so the solver can mark X's.
3. **Test it with your own grid.** Solve it using only your clues. If more than one answer works, add a clue. If a clue isn't needed, cut it to make it harder.
4. **Try other puzzle types (30 minutes).** Pick at least three: a river crossing (a farmer, a fox, a goose and a sack of grain, but invent your own characters), a truth-teller and liar puzzle ("One guard always lies..."), a number riddle, or a "who sat where" seating puzzle.
5. **Level them.** Mark each puzzle easy, medium or hard with one, two or three stars, and put them in order from easiest to hardest.
6. **Write the answer key**, upside down on the last page, with one sentence explaining how to solve each.
7. **Test it.** Give it to someone at home and watch, without helping. Where did they get stuck? Fix any clue that confused them for the wrong reason.

### Make it yours

- Add a final "meta" puzzle that uses an answer from every earlier puzzle.

- It's way too easy: use "not" clues instead of direct ones ("Bo doesn't have the cat").

### Clean up

## 9. Bottle Lava Lamp

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 45-75 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1 |

**Materials:** a clear plastic bottle with a cap (500 ml to 1 liter), vegetable oil (enough to fill the bottle about three quarters), water, food coloring, table salt, a funnel or a paper cone, a measuring cup, a tray or baking pan, paper towels

**Nice to have:** a fizzy antacid or vitamin tablet (ask first), a flashlight, a second bottle to compare

### The big idea
Lava lamps look like magic: glowing blobs that rise and fall forever. The real trick is density, which is how much stuff is packed into a space. Build your own and figure out what makes the blobs move.

### Goals
- A working lava lamp with blobs that rise and sink.
- An explanation, in your own words, of why oil and water won't mix.

### Objectives
- I can predict which liquid floats using the idea of density.
- I can make blobs move by adding something to the bottle.
- I can change the setup and compare results.

### Get ready
Work on a tray: oil is slippery and hard to clean. Peel the label off the bottle so you can see clearly. Ask your grown-up how much oil you can use.

### Steps
1. **Predict.** If you pour water and oil in the same bottle, which ends up on top? Write your guess.
2. **Pour.** Fill the bottle about one quarter with water. Add 5 to 8 drops of food coloring and swirl. Then slowly pour in oil until the bottle is about 5 cm from the top. Wait one minute. Were you right?
3. **Look closely.** The food coloring went into the water, not the oil. Why might that be? (Hint: food coloring is water-based.)
4. **Make lava (15 minutes).** Sprinkle about a teaspoon of salt onto the oil. Watch: salt is denser than both, so it sinks and drags oil blobs down. As the salt dissolves in the water, the oil is freed and floats back up. Keep adding pinches.
5. **Test your own variable.** Pick one: more salt at once, less salt, a pinch dropped from higher up, or a bottle with more water. Write down what changes.
6. **Glow mode.** Turn off the lights and shine a flashlight up through the bottom of the bottle.
7. **Fizz mode (if you have a tablet).** Break it into four pieces and drop one in. The gas bubbles carry colored water up through the oil, then the water sinks again. Cap the bottle loosely only after the fizzing stops.

### Make it yours
- Make a two-color lamp: color the water with one color, add salt, then drip in a second color on top of the oil.
- Design a "lava race": two bottles side by side, different amounts of salt. Which runs longer?
- Invent a story for your lamp: it's a sample of alien ocean, and you're the scientist sending a report home.

### Stuck?
- The water and oil look cloudy and mixed: you shook it. Let it sit for 10 minutes and they'll separate again.
- Blobs stop moving: you're out of undissolved salt. Add another pinch.
- Oil spilled: soak it up with paper towels first, then wipe with soapy water.

### Show it off
Do a dark-room demo with the flashlight for your family and explain density in three sentences or less.

### Clean up
Cap the bottle tightly and keep it as a lamp, or ask a grown-up how to throw out the oil. Don't pour oil down the sink; it clogs pipes.

### For the grown-up
This is a hands-on look at density and why some liquids don't mix, topics in middle school physical science. The tablet version is fine with a normal fizzy antacid or vitamin tablet; keep the bottle uncapped while it fizzes. Oil should go in the trash in a sealed container, not down the drain. **Ask at dinner:** "Why did the salt make the oil sink and then come back up?"

## 12. Cartoon Expression Character Sheet

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, an eraser, a black pen or fine marker, colored pencils, a mirror

**Nice to have:** a sketchbook, a sibling willing to make faces

### The big idea
Animators make a "model sheet" for every character so the character looks the same in every scene, whether they're laughing or furious. Invent your own cartoon character, then show off every emotion it can feel.

### Goals
- An original character drawn from the front, side and back.
- A sheet of at least 12 different expressions.

### Objectives
- I can build a character from simple shapes.
- I can show emotion using eyebrows, eyes, mouth and posture.
- I can keep a character consistent across many drawings.

### Get ready
Set a mirror on the table. You'll be your own model. Draw a grid of 12 boxes, about 6 by 6 cm each, on one sheet of paper.

### Steps
1. **Build from shapes.** Start with one main shape: a bean, a triangle, a tall rectangle, a circle. Add a different shape for the head. Simple shapes are easier to draw over and over.
2. **Give it a personality.** Write three words about your character: maybe "nervous, loyal, snacky." Let the design match: a nervous character might have a long neck and big eyes.
3. **Draw a turnaround.** Draw it from the front, side and back, all the same height. Use light lines across the page at the top of the head, the shoulders and the feet so they match.
4. **Make faces in the mirror (10 minutes).** Do surprised, then angry, then embarrassed. Notice: eyebrows do most of the work. Up and apart is surprised. Down in the middle is angry. One up, one down is suspicious.

6. **Push it.** Cartoons exaggerate. Make the happy mouth bigger, the scared eyes tinier, the furious body tighter. Add little extras like sweat drops or steam.
7. **Ink and color.** Go over your best lines in pen and pick a simple color scheme of three or four colors.

### Make it yours
- Draw a three-panel comic where your character feels three very different emotions in a row.
- Design a sidekick that's the total opposite, and draw the two reacting to the same thing.
- Do full-body poses instead of faces: show "embarrassed" with no face at all.

### Stuck?
- Character looks different every time: trace over your front view as a starting guide for each box.
- Emotions look the same: cover the mouth with your finger. If you can't tell the emotion, push the eyebrows further.
- Hands are hard: give your character three fingers and a thumb, or mitten hands. Cartoonists do it all the time.

### Show it off
Play "guess the feeling": cover the labels and have your family guess each expression.

### Clean up
Erase the guidelines, cap your pens, and store the sheet flat.

### For the grown-up
This builds observational drawing, consistency and emotional vocabulary: naming and reading feelings is a real social skill, not just an art one. **Ask at dinner:** "Which emotion was hardest to draw, and what did you change to get it right?"

## 13. Earthquake Tower on a Shake Table

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** two pieces of stiff cardboard about 30 by 30 cm, 4 round pencils or markers, 2 big rubber bands, dry spaghetti (about 50 pieces) or 30 index cards, masking tape, a small book or a can, a ruler, a timer or clock

**Nice to have:** mini marshmallows or play dough for joints, a partner to shake the table

### The big idea
Engineers in earthquake zones design buildings that sway without falling down. Build a shake table that makes your own earthquakes, then design a tower that survives the biggest one you can make.

### Goals
- A shake table that makes a steady, repeatable quake.
- A tower at least 40 cm tall that holds a small book on top through a 10-second shake.

### Objectives
- I can use triangles and bracing to make a structure stiffer.
- I can run a fair test by shaking the same way each time.
- I can redesign after a failure and explain what I changed.

### Get ready
Build the shake table: lay the 4 pencils on one cardboard sheet, parallel and about 20 cm apart in pairs. Set the second sheet on top. Loop the two rubber bands around both sheets so the top slides and snaps back. Test it by pulling the top sideways and letting go.

### Steps
1. **Set a standard quake.** Pull the top board 3 cm to one side and let go, again and again, for 10 seconds. That's a "level 1." Pulling 5 cm is "level 2." Write this down so every test is fair.
2. **Sketch two designs.** One tall and skinny, one wide at the bottom. Which do you think will survive?
3. **Build tower one (20 minutes).** Tape spaghetti into a frame or fold index cards into columns and tape them together. Get to 40 cm. Tape the base to the top board.
4. **Test it.** Put the book on top and run a level 1 quake. Did it sway, crack, or collapse? Where did it break first?
5. **Upgrade (25 minutes).** Engineers use tricks. Pick at least two: X-shaped cross braces between columns, a wider base, a lower and heavier middle, or "base isolation" (setting the tower on a few marbles or bottle caps inside a lid so the ground moves under it).
6. **Retest at level 1, then level 2.** Keep notes: what changed, and what happened?
7. **Final quake.** Run the biggest quake your table can make. Does your best tower survive?

### Make it yours
- Build a city of three towers and see which survives the "big one."
- Add a damper: hang a heavy object like a bag of coins on a string inside the top of the tower. Some real skyscrapers do this.
- Make a "height challenge": what's the tallest tower that still survives a level 2?

### Stuck?
- The shake table jams: the pencils roll away. Tape a strip of cardboard at each end as a guide rail.
- The tower collapses before the test: the joints are weak. Wrap tape around every joint twice, or use marshmallows.
- The book slides off: tape a paper cup on top to hold it.

### Show it off
Run a "public safety demonstration": show your family a weak tower failing, then your best tower surviving.

### Clean up
Snap up spaghetti bits, put rubber bands away, and keep the shake table for another day.

### For the grown-up
This is structural engineering: triangles, center of gravity and fair testing. Earth science classes cover earthquakes too, and this makes "why buildings fall" concrete. **Ask at dinner:** "What engineering trick made the biggest difference to your tower?"

### Clean up
Wash and put back every pot and glass, and sweep up any spilled beans or rice.

### For the grown-up

### For the grown-up
This is kindness in action and real writing practice: audience, voice and the friendly letter. Please make the first contact with the care home and handle the return address. ## 18. Marker Chromatography Art

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** coffee filters or white paper towels, washable markers (black, brown and dark green are best), cups or glasses, water, a pencil, tape, scissors, a tray or plate

**Nice to have:** pipe cleaners for making flowers, a second brand of black marker

### The big idea
That black marker isn't really black. It's a mix of hidden colors, and water can pull them apart. Scientists call this chromatography, and forensic teams use it to tell inks apart. Use it to solve a mystery, then turn it into art.

### Goals
- Chromatography strips that reveal the hidden colors in at least four markers.
- A solved "ransom note" mystery and a set of chromatography art pieces.

### Objectives
- I can separate a mixture of inks using water.
- I can compare results and use them as evidence.
- I can explain why some colors travel farther than others.

### Get ready
Cut 6 strips of coffee filter, each about 2 by 12 cm. Draw a pencil line across each strip 2 cm from the bottom. Pencil won't run.

### Steps
1. **Predict.** Pick four markers. Write down what colors you think are hiding in each one.
2. **Dot the strips.** Make a thick dot of marker on the pencil line of each strip. Write the marker color at the top in pencil.
3. **Run the test (15 minutes).** Tape each strip to a pencil laid across a cup, so the bottom just touches 1 cm of water. The dot must stay above the water. Watch the water climb and carry the colors up.
4. **Read the results.** Take the strips out when the water is near the top. Which colors appeared? Which went farthest? Lighter, more soluble dyes travel farther.
5. **Solve the mystery.** Have someone (or yourself, eyes closed) write "GIVE ME THE COOKIES" with one of the markers on a strip. Test all your suspects' markers and match the pattern. Which marker wrote the note?
6. **Make art (25 minutes).** Draw a ring of colored dots on a whole coffee filter, about 3 cm from the center. Fold it into a cone and set the tip in a little water. Watch it bloom. Make at least four and choose your color combos.
7. **Shape them.** Once dry, pinch the middles to make flowers or butterflies, or layer them into a stained-glass window.

### Make it yours
- Test different liquids: plain water vs. salt water vs. white vinegar. Which separates the colors best?
- Make a "color family tree" poster showing every marker and the colors inside it.
- Write a detective case file for your ransom note, with suspects and evidence photos (drawings) of the strips.

### Stuck?
- Nothing happens: you're probably using permanent marker. It needs washable marker for water to work.
- The colors just blur: the dot was under the water. Use less water or move the dot higher.
- The strip falls in: tape it at the top to a pencil across the cup.

### Show it off
Present the "case of the cookie ransom note" to your family with your evidence strips taped in order.

### Clean up
Dump the water, rinse the cups, and lay wet filters flat on a plate to dry.

### For the grown-up
This is real lab science: separating a mixture and using evidence, part of middle school chemistry and forensic science. Everything here is washable and kitchen-safe; markers can stain clothes, so an old shirt helps. **Ask at dinner:** "What colors were hiding in the black marker?"

### For the grown-up

### The big idea
Naturalists have a trick: sit in the same spot, totally still, and the world starts happening around you. Birds come back, bugs crawl out, and you hear sounds you never noticed. Find your "sit spot" and map everything you hear.

### Goals
- Two or three sound maps from the same spot.
- A short piece of writing about the most interesting sound you heard.

- I can sort sounds as natural, animal or human-made.

- It's too noisy to hear anything natural: map the human sounds instead. Cities have their own wildlife.

### Clean up
Shake out the blanket, bring everything inside, and keep your maps together.

### For the grown-up
This builds focus, careful observation, and environmental awareness, the habits behind life science and nature writing. ## 21. Design a Planet and Its Life

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, colored pencils or markers, a ruler, a round object to trace (a plate or bowl)

**Nice to have:** a big sheet of paper or the back of a poster, a globe or map for reference

### The big idea
Scientists who search for life on other planets ask: what would life look like if gravity were stronger, or the sun were dimmer? Today you get to answer. Design a whole planet, then design the creatures that evolved to live there.

### Goals
- A planet fact sheet with its size, gravity, climate and day length.
- At least four creatures, each with features that match the planet.

### Objectives
- I can explain how an environment shapes the traits of living things.
- I can build a food web that connects producers, consumers and decomposers.
- I can justify my design choices with science reasons.

### Get ready
Fold a big sheet of paper into four sections: Planet, Life, Food Web, Field Notes.

### Steps
1. **Pick your planet's conditions.** Choose at least four: gravity (half of Earth's, or double), temperature (frozen, desert, swampy), day length (6 hours or 200 hours), sunlight (bright blue star or dim red star), land vs. water, and atmosphere (thick or thin).
2. **Name it and draw it.** Trace a plate for the planet and show its continents, oceans or ice caps. Write a fact card: "Planet Vell: 2 times Earth's gravity. Day lasts 60 hours. Mostly shallow seas."

5. **Design three consumers (30 minutes).** A plant-eater, a hunter, and one strange one (a decomposer, a parasite, or a creature that lives in the clouds). Draw each with labels: "Six legs to spread weight in high gravity."
6. **Build the food web.** Draw arrows from each living thing to whatever eats it.
7. **Field notes.** Write a short journal entry as an explorer who just landed. What do you see first? What surprises you?

### Make it yours
- Add a disaster: a giant volcano erupts or the planet's moon breaks apart. What survives?
- Design a smart species and invent one piece of their technology based on their body.
- Make it a twin planet: the same creatures, but on the cold side and the hot side, adapted differently.

### Stuck?
- Creatures look like Earth animals: start with the problem, not the animal. "How do you move in thick goo?"
- Too many choices: pick just three planet conditions and design around those.
- The food web is confusing: start with the producer and draw arrows up the chain.

### Show it off
Give a "space agency briefing": present your planet to your family and take questions from "the press."

### Clean up
Put your art supplies away and keep your planet sheet in a folder.

### For the grown-up
This is life science (adaptation, food webs, ecosystems) and earth and space science (gravity, light) with lots of room for imagination. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the weirdest creature on your planet, and why is it built that way?"

**Materials:** two straight, light sticks (thin dowels, garden canes, or straight branches), one about 60 cm and one about 50 cm; a big plastic trash bag or wrapping paper; strong string or kite line (at least 20 m); masking tape or packing tape; scissors; a ruler; a marker

### The big idea
People have flown kites for over 2,000 years: to measure weather, send signals, and just because it feels amazing. Build a real diamond kite from sticks and plastic, then get it in the air.

### Goals
- A finished diamond kite with a bridle and a tail.
- At least one flight that stays up for 30 seconds.

### Objectives
- I can build a frame and sail that are balanced side to side.
- I can adjust a kite (tail, bridle) to make it fly better.
- I can explain how wind creates lift on a kite.

### Get ready
Wait for a day with a steady breeze: leaves and small branches moving, but not whole trees bending. Find a wide open space: a big yard, field or park (with permission), far from trees, roads and power lines.

### Steps
1. **Make the frame.** Lay the long stick (the spine) down. Cross the short stick (the spar) over it about one quarter of the way down from the top, making a lowercase t. Tie and tape the cross tight.

- Design a sail: draw a creature or pattern with a marker before you attach it.

### Stuck?
- The kite spins or dives: add more tail.
- It won't climb: move the bridle loop up a little and check that the sticks are even.
- No wind: wait for a breezier day, or run with it for a quick launch (watch where you're going).

### Show it off
Invite the family out to watch a flight. See if they can keep it up too.

### Clean up
Wind your line neatly on a tube so it doesn't tangle, and pick up every scrap of plastic and tape.

### For the grown-up
This builds engineering and physics (lift, balance and drag) plus patience. Please make sure they fly in an open space away from roads, trees and power lines, never in rain or near storms, and that they have your permission for any spot beyond the yard. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you change to make your kite fly better?"

**Materials:** pages from old newspapers, magazines, junk mail or catalogs you're allowed to cut up (never a library book or anyone's book without permission), a black marker, a pencil, scissors, glue or tape, paper

**Nice to have:** colored markers or pencils, a stapler

### The big idea
Every page of a newspaper has a secret poem hiding inside it. You just have to black out everything else. Blackout poetry is part art, part treasure hunt: you find the words, and the page becomes something new.

### Goals
- At least four finished blackout poems.
- A mini booklet or wall display of your best ones.

### Objectives
- I can scan a page for strong, interesting words.
- I can build a poem by choosing and connecting words in order.
- I can design a page so the art supports the poem.

Gather at least ten pages. Pages with lots of text and interesting words work best: news articles, recipes, instruction manuals, old textbooks you can cut.

- Write a "found story" instead of a poem: a full tiny story using only words from one page.

### Stuck?
- Can't find a poem: start with one strong word, then look for one verb after it. Build from there.
- Words are too far apart: that's fine. Blackout fills the space between them.
- Ink bleeds through: put a scrap of cardboard under the page.

### Show it off
Tape them up in a hallway "gallery" and do a dramatic reading for your family.

### Clean up
Recycle used scraps, cap your markers, and keep your poems flat in a folder.

### For the grown-up
This builds word choice, close reading, and poetry skills, which are parts of English class, in a way that feels like play. Check that the pages are okay to cut. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you read me your favorite blackout poem and tell me what page it came from?"

## 25. Tangram Puzzle Pack

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a piece of stiff cardboard (a cereal box works), a ruler, a pencil, scissors, index cards or paper, a black marker, an envelope

**Nice to have:** colored paper to cover the pieces, a second set for a friend to race

### The big idea
Tangrams are an old puzzle from China: one square cut into seven pieces that can make hundreds of shapes, like a cat, a boat, or a person running. Cut your own perfect set, then make a pack of challenge cards to stump your family.

### Goals
- A precisely cut set of seven tangram pieces.
- A pack of at least 10 challenge cards, with answers on the back.

### Objectives
- I can measure and draw precise shapes using a grid.
- I can rotate and flip shapes in my head to solve a puzzle.
- I can design puzzles of different difficulty levels.

### Get ready
Cut a 20 by 20 cm square from the cardboard. Draw a grid of 5 cm squares on it: four across, four up. Label the grid points like a map, starting at the bottom-left corner as (0,0), so the top right is (4,4).

### Steps
1. **Draw the cut lines.** Connect these points with your ruler: (0,0) to (4,4), the big diagonal. (0,4) to (2,2). (2,0) to (4,2). (1,1) to (3,1). (2,2) to (3,1). (3,3) to (4,2).
2. **Check before cutting.** You should see two big triangles, one medium triangle, two small triangles, a square and a parallelogram (a slanted rectangle). If not, recheck your points.
3. **Cut carefully.** Cut on the lines slowly. Neat pieces make the puzzles fit. Color or cover them if you like.
4. **Learn the rules.** Every shape must use all seven pieces. They lie flat, touch, and never overlap.
5. **Try the classic.** Put them back into the square. Now try a triangle, a rectangle, and a house. Time yourself.
6. **Design challenge cards (30 minutes).** Make your own shapes: a cat, a fox, a running person, a rocket, a letter. When you make one you like, trace around the outside only onto a card and fill it in black. That's the puzzle. On the back, draw where each piece goes. That's the answer.
7. **Rate them.** Give each card one, two or three stars. Put them in an envelope with your set.

### Make it yours
- Make a story deck: the cards tell a story in order, like "a boat sails, hits a storm, becomes a bird."
- Design "impossible" pairs: two shapes that look almost the same but one has an extra bump. How can that be?
- Make a giant floor set from big cardboard, 1 meter across.

### Stuck?
- The pieces don't fit back into a square: one was cut unevenly. Trim it a tiny bit.
- Can't solve a shape: place the two big triangles first. They're the hardest to fit.
- Your shape needs fewer than seven pieces: add the missing ones as a tail, hat, or ears.

### Show it off
Hand someone at home a challenge card and the pieces, start a timer, and see how long it takes.

### Clean up
Put the pieces and cards in the envelope and recycle cardboard scraps.

### For the grown-up
This builds geometry (area, rotation and reflection), spatial reasoning, and careful measuring, all part of seventh grade math. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you show me the hardest shape you made and how fast you can solve it?"

## 26. Deck of Cards Workout

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Move | 45-75 min | Either | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** a deck of playing cards (or 52 slips of paper you make into one), paper, a pencil, water to drink, a towel or mat

**Nice to have:** a timer, a family member to work out with, music on a radio

### The big idea
A deck of cards can be a personal trainer. Flip a card, do the exercise for its suit, and do as many reps as its number. Nobody knows what's next, which makes it part game, part workout. Then design your own game modes.

### Goals
- A full workout from half a deck or more.
- Two of your own game modes, written up as rule cards.

### Objectives
- I can do exercises with good form and the right pace.
- I can design a workout with a warm-up and cool-down.
- I can track my effort and adjust to my level.

### Get ready
Clear a space about 2 meters by 2 meters, inside or out. Fill a glass of water. If you don't have cards, make a deck: write 2 to 10, J, Q, K, A on slips of paper in four colors.

### Steps
1. **Warm up (5 minutes).** March in place, roll your shoulders, do 10 slow arm circles each way, and 10 easy squats.
2. **Set the suits.** Hearts = squats. Diamonds = push-ups (from your knees is fine). Clubs = jumping jacks. Spades = plank, 2 seconds per card value. Write it on a card.
3. **Set the numbers.** Number cards are that many reps. Jack, Queen, King = 10. Ace = 11, or 1 if you're tired. Jokers = 30 seconds of dance party.
4. **Play round one (15 minutes).** Shuffle, flip one card at a time, do it, then flip the next. Get through at least half the deck. Rest 20 seconds whenever you need to.
5. **Design your own mode (15 minutes).** Ideas: "Pair Up" (if two cards in a row are the same suit, double the reps); "Red Means Rest" (red cards are a stretch); "Boss Battle" (every face card is a mini challenge like 20 seconds of high knees). Write the rules on a card.
6. **Play your mode.** Change it if it's way too easy or way too hard.
7. **Cool down (5 minutes).** Walk slowly, then stretch your legs, arms and back. Hold each stretch for 20 seconds.

### Make it yours
- Make it a story: each suit is a part of a ninja training mission, and the aces are the final test.
- Make a family challenge: everyone draws, everyone does it, and the youngest picks the exercise for each suit.
- Add a skill: clubs means 10 seconds of juggling a pair of balled-up socks.

### Stuck?
- Too tired too fast: lower the numbers (half reps) or swap push-ups for wall push-ups.
- Getting bored: add music, or race the clock to see how fast you finish half the deck.
- An exercise hurts: stop it. Swap it for something gentler, like marching in place.

### Show it off
Teach your family your best game mode and get them to play one round after dinner.

### Clean up
Put the deck back in its box with your rule cards, and put your towel and glass away.

### For the grown-up
This builds fitness, pacing and listening to your body, plus some game design. Encourage water breaks and good form over speed, and swapping any move that hurts. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest card you drew, and did you finish it?"

## 27. Family Talk Show

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 75-120 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 2+ |

**Materials:** paper and markers for cue cards, two chairs, a small table, a mug, a hairbrush or spoon as a microphone, a blanket or sheet as a backdrop

**Nice to have:** a desk lamp as a spotlight, a sibling as the band or co-host, a grown-up after work as the star guest

### The big idea
Tonight, live from your living room, it's your own late-night talk show. You're the host. You'll write the jokes, book the guests, run the segments, and keep the show moving. The audience is waiting.

### Goals
- A full talk show, about 15 minutes long, with at least three segments.
- A set, a show name, and cue cards for every segment.

### Objectives
- I can write and deliver a short comedy monologue.
- I can plan interview questions that get interesting answers.
- I can run a live show and keep it moving when things go off-script.

### Get ready
Set the stage: two chairs at an angle, a small table with a mug for the host, and a blanket on the wall as a backdrop. Pick your show name and write it big on paper.

### Steps
1. **Plan the rundown.** Write the order on one card. Example: Opening monologue, guest interview, "Kid Reviews" segment, a game, musical guest, goodbye.
2. **Write the monologue (15 minutes).** Three to five short jokes about real life at home. Example: "This week the fridge was opened 47 times. The milk has asked for privacy."
3. **Book your guest.** A sibling, a grown-up, or a stuffed animal "celebrity." Write five questions that aren't yes or no: "What's a skill you have that nobody knows about?" or "Tell us about the worst haircut you ever had."
4. **Plan a segment.** Your choice: a cooking demo (making a sandwich, dramatically), a "Kid Reviews" where you review a household object like a five-star critic, or a game with the guest.
5. **Make cue cards.** Write your lines in big letters. Use bullet points, not full sentences, so you can talk naturally.
6. **Rehearse (15 minutes).** Practice the monologue three times. Plan what you'll do if a joke flops ("Wow, tough crowd. My mom laughed at that one in rehearsal.").
7. **Go live!** Start with an announcer (someone saying "And now, here's your host!") and keep the energy up.

### Make it yours
- Make it a morning show instead, with a weather report and a pet of the day.
- Interview a fictional character, played by a family member who has to stay in character.
- Add a commercial break for products you invent, like a self-folding laundry basket.

### Stuck?
- Guest gives short answers: ask "What happened next?" or "How did that make you feel?"
- Jokes aren't working: exaggerate. Make the number bigger, the situation sillier.
- Nobody's home yet: prepare everything, then put on the show after dinner.

### Show it off
That's the whole point: put on the show when your family is home, with snacks for the "studio audience."

### Clean up
Return the chairs, fold the blanket, and keep your cue cards for the next episode.

### For the grown-up
This builds public speaking, writing for an audience, interview skills and thinking on your feet, all skills used in English and drama. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the best moment of your show, planned or unplanned?"

## 28. Periscope

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** two clean milk or juice cartons (the tall kind), or one long narrow box about 40 cm; two small flat mirrors (plastic is safest, or ask a grown-up for small compact mirrors); scissors; masking tape or packing tape; a ruler; a pencil; a square of paper

**Nice to have:** smooth aluminum foil glued flat on cardboard if you have no mirrors (blurrier, but it works), paint or markers

### The big idea
Submarines use periscopes to see above the waves without coming up. It's just two mirrors and a tube, set at the perfect angle. Build one, then use it to spy over the couch, around corners, and out of a hiding spot.

### Goals
- A working periscope that lets you see over something taller than you.
- A spy mission completed using only the periscope to look.

### Objectives
- I can set mirrors at a 45 degree angle.
- I can explain how light bounces off mirrors to reach my eye.
- I can test and fix a build until it works.

### Get ready
Rinse and dry the cartons and cut the tops off so they're open tubes. Tape them end to end into one long tube. Make a 45 degree guide: fold a square of paper corner to corner. The fold makes a perfect 45 degree angle.

### Steps
1. **Cut the top window.** Near the top of one side, cut a square window about 4 by 4 cm, 2 cm from the end.
2. **Cut the bottom window.** On the opposite side at the bottom, cut the same size window. If the top window faces forward, the bottom one faces back toward you.
3. **Place the top mirror.** Slide a mirror into the top so it faces down and outward, tilted at 45 degrees. Use the paper triangle to check. Tape it firmly.
4. **Place the bottom mirror.** Slide the second mirror into the bottom, facing up and toward the bottom window, at 45 degrees. It should be parallel to the top mirror.
5. **Test it.** Look into the bottom window. You should see what's in front of the top window. If you see the inside of the tube, adjust the angle.
6. **Close it up.** Tape the open ends shut so the mirrors can't fall out, and tape any light leaks.
7. **Spy mission (20 minutes).** Make a list of five things to observe without being seen: what's on the kitchen counter, who's walking past the window, what the cat is doing. Use only the periscope.

### Make it yours
- Make it longer: add a third carton. How does that change the view?
- Make it turnable: build the top section as a separate tube that rotates.
- Design it as spy gear: camouflage the outside and give your agent a code name.

### Stuck?
- All you see is the inside of the box: the mirrors are at the wrong angle. Recheck with the paper triangle.
- The view is upside down or sideways: the mirrors are facing the wrong way. They should be parallel.
- It's dark: make the windows a little bigger.

### Show it off
Hide behind the couch when your family comes home and report everything they did without them seeing you.

### Clean up
Pick up scraps and put away the scissors and tape. Be careful with mirror edges.

### For the grown-up
This is optics: light reflects off a mirror at the same angle it hits it. It's the same principle behind cameras and telescopes. If you use glass mirrors, tape the edges first. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you see through your periscope that nobody knew you were watching?"

## 29. Plan and Host a Picnic

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 90-120 min | Either | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a blanket or sheet, plates and cups, a basket, bag or box, bread, fillings for sandwiches from your kitchen, fruit, water or juice, a lemon or two and sugar for lemonade, a cutting board, a butter knife and a sharper knife (with permission), paper and markers for invitations

**Nice to have:** napkins, a pitcher, cards or a ball for picnic games

### The big idea
A picnic is just a meal, but on a blanket it feels like a holiday. You're the host and the chef. Plan the menu, make the food, set the scene, and serve your family (or your stuffed animals) a picnic to remember, outside or right on the living room floor.

### Goals
- A picnic you planned, cooked and served.
- At least three foods, a drink, and one picnic game.

### Objectives
- I can plan a menu using what's already in the kitchen.
- I can prepare food safely, including using a knife with permission.
- I can host guests and think about what they'll enjoy.

### Get ready
Check with your grown-up about which foods you can use, any allergies, and whether you can use a knife. Pick your spot: a yard, a balcony, a park with permission, or the living room floor.

### Steps
1. **Plan the menu.** Look through the fridge and pantry. Choose a main, a side, a sweet and a drink. Example: cheese and cucumber sandwiches cut into triangles, carrot sticks with a dip, apple slices with cinnamon, fresh lemonade.
2. **Make invitations.** Fold a card for each guest with the time, place and a theme ("Royal Picnic: crowns required").
3. **Make lemonade.** Squeeze 2 lemons into a pitcher, add 3 tablespoons of sugar and 4 cups of cold water. Stir and taste. Too sour? More sugar. Too sweet? More water.
4. **Prep the food (30 minutes).** Wash fruit and vegetables. Make sandwiches and cut them into shapes. Use the claw grip on anything you slice: fingertips tucked under.
5. **Pack it up.** Wrap food so it stays fresh. Keep cold things cold until you serve them.
6. **Set the scene.** Lay the blanket, set out plates and cups. Add a decoration: a jar of flowers, a paper sign with your picnic's name.
7. **Host it.** Welcome guests, serve the food, and lead a picnic game, like "two truths and a lie" or cloud spotting.

### Make it yours
- Theme it: a picnic from a different time or place, like a medieval feast (eat with your hands) or a space picnic (everything is "astronaut food").
- Make a menu card with funny names for each dish.
- Make it a mystery picnic: guests have to guess what's in each sandwich with their eyes closed.

### Stuck?
- Not much food in the house: a simple picnic is fine. Toast, fruit and a homemade drink count.
- Weather is bad: go indoors and set up the blanket by a window.
- Sandwiches get soggy: put wet fillings like tomato in the middle, between the cheese.

### Show it off
Host your guests and give a "chef's welcome" introducing every dish.

### Clean up
Shake out the blanket, wash every dish, wipe the counter, and put leftovers in the fridge.

### For the grown-up
This builds planning, real kitchen skills and hosting, which is thinking about other people's needs. Please check allergies for everyone eating, and agree on whether they can use a sharp knife. If they picnic beyond the yard, it's with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the best part of hosting, and what would you change next time?"

## 30. Chore Sea Shanty

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, a table or floor to stomp on, a chore that needs doing (dishes, laundry, sweeping, tidying)

**Nice to have:** a wooden spoon and pot for a drum, siblings or family as the crew

### The big idea
Sailors sang sea shanties to pull ropes and haul anchors together, all in rhythm, so the work went faster. The shantyman sang a line and the crew roared back. Your house has its own hard labor: dishes, laundry, the sock pile. Write a shanty for it, and make the work go faster.

### Goals
- An original sea shanty about a household chore, with at least four verses and a chorus.
- A performance of it while actually doing the chore.

### Objectives
- I can write lyrics that fit a steady beat.
- I can use call and response in a song.
- I can write rhymes that tell a funny story.

### Get ready
Pick your chore and get permission to do it. Find a spot where you can stomp: a hard floor or a table to bang on.

### Steps
1. **Find the beat.** Stomp your foot and clap: STOMP, clap, STOMP, clap. Keep it slow and steady. That's the pulse of a shanty. Every line you write needs to fit this beat.
2. **Write the chorus first (10 minutes).** It's the part everyone sings back, so keep it short and catchy, about four lines. Example: "Scrub away, me hearties, scrub away! / The plates are piled high as a mast today! / Rinse them down and stack them true, / There's a pot in the sink for me and you!"
3. **Write the story.** A good shanty tells a tale. Outline four verses: the start (the disaster, like a mountain of dishes), the struggle, the twist (a fork that won't come clean, a lost sock monster), and the victory.
4. **Write the verses (20 minutes).** Two to four lines each. Rhyme the ends of lines 2 and 4. Use sailor words: "ahoy," "heave," "mates," "the briny deep" (that's the sink).
5. **Add call and response.** In some lines, you sing a line and the crew answers with a short phrase: "Who left the pan in the sink?" "HEAVE HO!"
6. **Make a tune.** Shanties use simple tunes. Hum the chorus over your beat until it feels right, and repeat it the same way every time.
7. **Rehearse and perform.** Sing it while doing the chore, stomping on the beat. Teach the response lines to anyone nearby.

### Make it yours
- Write a shanty about something else: the morning rush, a long car trip, homework.
- Make it a battle shanty: two crews (you and a sibling) trade verses, each one bragging about their chore.
- Make it serious: a real sailor's song about missing home or a big storm.

### Stuck?
- Lines don't fit the beat: say them out loud with the stomp. Cut words until each line fits.
- Can't find a rhyme: change the last word of the first line to something easier to rhyme.
- Feels silly: that's the point. Sing it louder.

### Show it off
Perform it at dinner as the family does the dishes together. Everyone sings the chorus.

### Clean up
Finish the chore you sang about. That's the cleanup.

### For the grown-up
This builds rhythm, rhyme, songwriting and a little history (work songs and sea shanties were real tools for teamwork). It also, very sneakily, gets a chore done. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you teach us the chorus of your shanty?"
