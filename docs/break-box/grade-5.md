## 1. Paper Roller Coaster with a Loop

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a marble (or a small round bead or ball about 1.5 cm wide), cereal boxes or thin cardboard, paper, masking tape, scissors, a ruler, a pencil, a wall, a chair, or a stack of books to build against

**Nice to have:** card stock, painter's tape (it peels off walls cleanly), a cup at the end to catch the marble, a timer

### The big idea
A real roller coaster has no engine on the ride. One big climb gives it all the energy it needs. Then gravity does the rest, even upside down! Can you build a paper track that sends a marble through a loop without falling out?

### Goals
- You build a marble coaster with a hill, a turn, and a full loop.
- The marble runs from start to finish three times in a row.

### Objectives
- I can explain that a higher start gives the marble more energy.
- I can test my track and fix the spot where it fails.
- I can measure and fold parts so they fit together.

### Get ready
Pick a spot near a wall, a bookshelf, or a sturdy chair. Ask a grown-up which wall is okay for tape. Clear the floor so the marble can't roll under furniture.

### Steps
1. Make track pieces (15 minutes). Cut strips of cardboard 5 cm wide. Fold up 1.5 cm on each long side. Now you have a U-shaped channel.
2. Make about eight pieces. Tape them end to end, with the higher piece always sitting on top of the lower one. That way the marble never hits an edge.
3. Build the first drop. Tape the start high on the wall or books, at least 60 cm up. Slope it down to the floor.
4. Test a straight run. Does the marble stay in? Fix bumps at the joints with smooth tape.
5. Make the loop. Bend one long track piece into a circle about 15 cm across. The track goes in, around, and out a little to the side. Tape the bottom down well.
6. Connect the loop near the bottom of the drop. Here is the rule: start the drop at least twice as high as the loop is tall (20 minutes of testing).
7. Add your own parts: a curve, a tunnel, a jump, or a cup "station" at the end.

### Make it yours
- Name your coaster and make a sign with its height and a warning: "Only for brave marbles."
- Add a second loop. How much higher must the start be now?
- Build a split track where the marble picks a path at random.

### Stuck?
- The marble falls out at the top of the loop: it is too slow. Raise the start, or make the loop smaller.
- The marble flies off a turn: add taller side walls on the outside of the curve.
- The marble stops: a joint is bumpy. Re-tape it so the top piece overlaps the lower one.

### Show it off
Invite your family to opening day. Do a countdown, then release the marble. Ask riders to guess if it will make the loop.

### Clean up
Peel tape off the wall slowly. Keep the track pieces in a box for a new design.

### For the grown-up
This is hands-on physics: height gives energy (potential energy), and speed lets the marble stay on the loop. It also builds measuring and patient fixing. Painter's tape is gentlest on walls. Marbles are a choking risk for children under 3, so pick them up if little ones are around. **Ask at dinner:** "How high did the start have to be for the marble to make the loop?"

## 2. Balloon Powered Car

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Either | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a piece of cardboard about 10 cm by 20 cm, 4 bottle caps for wheels, 2 straws, 2 wooden skewers or thin straight sticks, a balloon, tape, scissors, a ruler or tape measure

**Nice to have:** a sharp pencil to poke holes, a third straw, a hallway or smooth floor for racing

### The big idea
A balloon is a tiny tank of pushed-in air. When the air rushes out the back, it shoves the balloon forward. Put that on wheels and you have a jet car. Can you build one that zooms across the whole kitchen?

### Goals
- You build a car that rolls forward on balloon power alone.
- You beat your own distance record at least once.

### Objectives
- I can explain that air rushing back pushes the car forward.
- I can find and fix what slows my car down, like friction (rubbing that slows things).
- I can measure and record each test run.

### Get ready
Find a smooth floor with about 4 meters of clear space. Ask a grown-up to poke a small hole in the center of each bottle cap. Lay out your parts on a table.

### Steps
1. Tape the two straws across the bottom of the cardboard, one near each end. These hold the axles (the sticks the wheels spin on).
2. Slide a skewer through each straw. Push a bottle cap onto each end of each skewer. Check that the wheels spin freely.
3. Tape the neck of the balloon around the end of a straw. Wrap it tight so air only comes out the straw.
4. Tape that straw on top of the car. The open end should stick off the back about 3 cm.
5. Blow up the balloon through the straw. Pinch the straw, set the car down, and let go!
6. Measure how far it went. Write it down. Now pick one thing to change: a bigger balloon breath, lighter body, or wheels that wobble less (20 minutes).
7. Run at least five tests. Circle your record.

### Make it yours
- Add a second balloon. Does twice the air mean twice the distance?
- Build a heavy "truck" and a light "racer." Which goes farther? Why?
- Make a finish line ramp and see if your car can climb a slight hill.

### Stuck?
- The car won't move: check the wheels. If they rub the cardboard, move the straws out a little.
- Air leaks out the neck: add more tape, or use a rubber band to seal it.
- No balloon at home: wind a rubber band around the back axle instead, and roll it back to power it up.

### Show it off
Mark a start line with tape. Challenge a grown-up to guess how far your car will go, then prove it.

### Clean up
Pick up caps and scraps. Keep your car on a shelf for the next race.

### For the grown-up
This builds early physics ideas (action and reaction, friction) and the habit of testing one change at a time. Balloons are fine at this age, but pop pieces are a choking risk for children under 8, so pick up any broken balloon right away if little ones are around. Do the hole poking in the caps yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "What slowed your car down the most, and how did you fix it?"

## 3. Flashlight Campfire Story

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Either | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a flashlight, paper, a pencil, a blanket or two, pillows, red, orange or yellow paper or tissue paper, tape

**Nice to have:** a paper towel tube, a jar of water for spooky sound effects, a stuffed animal audience

**Get ready the night before:** Ask who in the family can be your audience after dinner.

### The big idea
Long ago, people told stories around fires at night. The best ones had a slow build, a strange sound, and a big surprise. Tonight you will build a pretend campfire and tell a story that gives your family shivers. Not too scary. Just enough.

### Goals
- You write a short campfire tale with a surprise ending.
- You tell it by flashlight to an audience.

### Objectives
- I can plan a story with a beginning, a build-up, and a twist (a surprise turn).
- I can use my voice to show feeling, like whispering or pausing.
- I can add sound effects at the right moment.

### Get ready
Pick a corner for the "camp." Spread out a blanket and pile some pillows around it. Lay out paper and a pencil.

### Steps
1. Build the fire. Crumple the colored paper into flame shapes. Tape them around the flashlight, pointing up. Turn it on and see it glow.
2. Pick your setting. Choose one: an old lighthouse, a quiet lake, a snowy cabin, or a place you invent.
3. Plan your story on paper (15 minutes). Use three boxes: Beginning, Build-up, Twist. Write a few words in each.
4. Add three "creepy clues" to the build-up. Example: a door that was closed is now open. Footprints that stop in the middle of a field.
5. Write the twist. Funny twists are great. Maybe the monster just wanted its lost sock back.
6. Choose two sound effects. Tap the floor slowly for footsteps. Blow across a jar for wind.
7. Practice telling it out loud twice (15 minutes). Slow down before the twist. Pause. Then say it.

### Make it yours
- Tell the story from the monster's side, so the people are the scary ones.
- Leave the ending open. Let the audience vote on what happened next.
- Make it a story chain: you tell one part, and each listener adds a sentence.

### Stuck?
- No ideas: start with "Nobody ever went past the old ___ after dark. Except me."
- The story feels too long: cut it down to three clues and one twist. Short is spookier.
- Someone gets too scared: switch to a funny twist right away.

### Show it off
After dinner, dim the lights and invite your family to the campfire. Hold the flashlight under your chin for the twist.

### Clean up
Fold the blankets, put pillows back, and keep your paper fire for next time.

### For the grown-up
This builds story structure (beginning, rising action, twist), planning, and speaking with expression. The "fire" is only paper and a flashlight; keep real flames and candles out of it. If your child scares easily, the funny-twist version is just as much fun. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the creepiest clue in your story, and how did you come up with it?"

## 4. Family Escape Room

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puzzle | 90-120 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, pens, an envelope or two, a small box or bag with a zipper, tape, a book, a timer or clock, a "treasure" (a snack, a homemade award, or a note)

**Nice to have:** a combination lock, a flashlight, sticky notes

### The big idea
Your family is trapped! Well, pretend trapped. They have 20 minutes to solve a chain of puzzles and find the key to escape. You are the game designer. Every clue is yours. Can you make it hard, but not impossible?

### Goals
- You build a 4 or 5 puzzle escape room in one room of your home.
- Your family plays it, and you run the clock.

### Objectives
- I can design puzzles where each answer leads to the next clue.
- I can test my puzzles and fix the ones that are too hard or too easy.
- I can give hints without giving away the answer.

### Get ready
Pick one room. Ask a grown-up which spots are okay for hiding clues. Choose a story, like "a wizard locked the living room" or "the cat stole the TV remote."

### Steps
1. Plan the chain on paper (15 minutes). Draw five boxes in a row. Each box is a puzzle. The answer to one tells you where the next clue is.
2. Write puzzle one: a riddle. Example: "I have pages but I am not a tree. Look inside me." The next clue goes in a book.
3. Write puzzle two: a secret code. Swap each letter for the next one (A becomes B). "DPVDI" means COUCH.
4. Make puzzle three a hunt. Cut a clue into four pieces and hide them. Put together, they say the next spot.
5. Add one or two more puzzles of your own. A math lock works well. "Lock code: legs on a spider, then fingers on one hand." That is 85.
6. Put the treasure in the final box or bag. Hide every clue. Walk the whole chain yourself to test it (15 minutes).
7. Write three hint cards. Each hint gives a little help, not the answer.

### Make it yours
- Add a twist. One clue is a fake, and the real one is hidden under it.
- Give the players a role, like spies or space explorers, and write a one-paragraph "mission briefing."
- Make a two-team version with two chains that meet at the end.

### Stuck?
- A puzzle is too hard: add a picture hint or make the code shorter.
- Players finish in five minutes: add a puzzle that needs two clues put together.
- You run out of hiding spots: tape clues under chairs, inside shoes, or behind a picture frame.

### Show it off
Read the mission briefing out loud. Start the timer. Watch, give hints, and cheer when they escape.

### Clean up
Collect every clue so nobody finds one in a shoe next week. Keep your puzzles in an envelope.

### For the grown-up
Designing an escape room builds logic, reading, writing, and planning, and it means thinking from someone else's point of view. Agree on hiding spots ahead of time so nothing fragile gets moved. Playing it is a great after-work family game. **Ask at dinner:** "Which puzzle was hardest to make work, and how did you fix it?"

## 5. Clay Creature with a Backstory

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Art | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** modeling clay or play dough (or homemade dough: 2 cups flour, 1 cup salt, 3/4 cup water), aluminum foil, a toothpick, a plate or cutting board to work on, paper, a pencil

**Nice to have:** markers or paint for after it dries, beads or buttons for eyes, a lamp for dramatic lighting

### The big idea
Somewhere, deep in a jungle or under the ice, lives a creature nobody has ever seen. You get to discover it. Sculpt it, then write its story. Where does it live? What does it eat? Why is it so grumpy in the morning?

### Goals
- You sculpt one creature that could not exist anywhere but in your imagination.
- You write a "creature card" with its full backstory.

### Objectives
- I can build a sculpture on an armature (a foil skeleton inside).
- I can add texture with simple tools, like scales, fur, or bumps.
- I can write a short backstory that explains how my creature looks.

### Get ready
Cover your table with a plate, board, or old place mat. Wash your hands. Tear off a sheet of foil about 30 cm long.

### Steps
1. Think first (5 minutes). Pick two real animals and mash them together. An owl and an octopus. A turtle and a cactus.
2. Squeeze the foil into a skeleton. Make a body, a head, and legs or tentacles. Keep it smaller than your hand.
3. Press clay over the foil in thin layers. Smooth it with a wet finger.
4. Add the big features: horns, wings, a tail, a huge nose. Make one feature way too big. That gives it character.
5. Add texture with the toothpick (15 minutes). Poke dots for scales. Drag lines for fur. Press a fork for stripes.
6. Write the creature card. Include its name, home, food, one special power, and one weakness.
7. Write its backstory: three to five sentences on why it looks like that. Example: "Its ears are huge because it lives in a dark cave and hunts by sound."

### Make it yours
- Make a baby version and explain how it changes as it grows.
- Build a tiny habitat for it from things around the house.
- Make a rival creature and write the story of their first meeting.

### Stuck?
- A part keeps falling off: poke a toothpick through both pieces to pin them together.
- The clay is cracking: wet your fingers and smooth the cracks closed.
- No ideas: close your eyes, pick two animals from the list in your head, and go.

### Show it off
Set your creature on a shelf with its card. Give a "nature documentary" talk about it in a hushed voice.

### Clean up
Scrape crumbs into the trash, not the sink. Wipe the table and wash your hands.

### For the grown-up
This builds sculpting skills, creative writing, and the science idea that animals' bodies fit where they live (adaptation). Homemade dough air-dries in a day or two. Clay crumbs can clog drains, so scrape them into the trash. **Ask at dinner:** "What is your creature's weakness, and how did it get that way?"

## 6. Salt Dough Fossils

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 cups flour, 1 cup salt, 3/4 cup water, a bowl, a spoon, a rolling pin or a smooth bottle, "fossil makers" (shells, leaves, a fork, toy animals, chicken bones that were boiled clean), a plate, paper, a pencil

**Nice to have:** tea or coffee to brush on for an "old" look, a paintbrush, a magnifying glass

### The big idea
Millions of years ago, a creature stepped in soft mud. The mud hardened. Today, a scientist finds the print. That is a fossil! Today you make a set of fossils. Then someone has to guess what made each one.

### Goals
- You make at least six fossils: some prints and some "body" fossils.
- A family member tries to identify them like a real scientist.

### Objectives
- I can explain how a trace fossil (a print, like a footprint) forms.
- I can make a fossil and write a clue card for it.
- I can use clues to figure out what made a print.

### Get ready
Clear the table and dust it with a little flour. Wash your hands. Gather your fossil makers on a plate.

### Steps
1. Make the dough. Mix the flour and salt. Add the water a little at a time. Knead for 5 minutes until it is smooth, like soft clay.
2. Roll it out about 1 cm thick. Cut or tear it into six pieces, each about the size of your palm.
3. Make trace fossils. Walk a toy animal across one piece. Press a leaf into another. Drag a fork for "claw marks."
4. Make body fossils. Press a shell or a clean bone deep into the dough, then lift it out carefully.
5. Invent one fossil from a creature that never existed. Use a fork, a bottle cap, and a pencil tip to make its prints.
6. Write a clue card for each one (15 minutes). Example: "Three toes. Long stride. Probably ran fast."
7. Let them dry on a plate for two days. Or a grown-up can bake them at 120 C (250 F) for about 2 hours.

### Make it yours
- Make a "dig site." Bury dry fossils in a bin of sand or rice and dig them up with a spoon and brush.
- Tell a story with prints: a small creature's tracks, then a big one's, then only the big one's.
- Make a museum label for each fossil with a made-up age: "Found in layer 4. About 70 million years old."

### Stuck?
- The dough is sticky: add flour one spoon at a time.
- The prints are blurry: press straight down and lift straight up. Don't wiggle.
- Fossils crack while drying: they still look real. Real fossils crack too!

### Show it off
Set out your fossils with the clue cards face down. Can your family match each fossil to what made it?

### Clean up
Scrape dough bits into the trash, not the sink. Wipe the table and wash the bowl.

### For the grown-up
This is earth science: how fossils form, and how scientists read evidence. If you bake them, the oven is yours to run; this sheet is fine to air-dry instead. Salt dough is not food, so keep it away from pets (lots of salt is harmful to dogs). **Ask at dinner:** "What could a scientist tell about an animal just from its footprints?"

## 7. Straw Rockets

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Low | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, tape, scissors, a pencil, drinking straws, a ruler or tape measure, a few books, a notebook page for results

**Nice to have:** a wider straw or a paper tube to roll rockets on, a protractor, sticky notes for target markers

### The big idea
Real rocket scientists test, change, and test again. You can do the same thing with a straw and a scrap of paper. Blow into the straw and your paper rocket shoots across the room. Your mission: build the rocket that flies the farthest.

### Goals
- You build three or more paper rockets with different designs.
- You find the best launch angle and the best rocket shape.

### Objectives
- I can test one change at a time, like fin size or angle.
- I can measure and record each flight.
- I can explain why my best rocket flew farthest.

### Get ready
Find a long hallway or an open space outside. Make sure no one will walk into the flight path. Set a start line with tape.

### Steps
1. Cut a strip of paper about 12 cm by 8 cm. Wrap it around a pencil to make a tube. Tape the seam.
2. Slide it off. Fold and tape one end shut. That is the nose cone.
3. Cut out three or four small triangle fins. Tape them to the open end.
4. Slide the rocket onto your straw. Aim away from people. Blow hard and short!
5. Measure the distance. Write it in a chart with three columns: Rocket, Change, Distance.
6. Test angles (15 minutes). Stack books to hold the straw low, middle, and high. Which angle flies farthest?
7. Build two more rockets. Change only one thing on each: longer body, bigger fins, or no fins. Test each three times.

### Make it yours
- Add a "payload" (a small paper clip) and see how much weight your rocket can carry.
- Set a landing target 3 meters away and score points for hits.
- Design a two-stage rocket: a short tube inside a longer one.

### Stuck?
- The rocket won't slide on the straw: unroll it and roll it a little looser.
- Air leaks out the nose: add one more piece of tape across the top.
- Flights are all over the place: blow the same way every time, one short puff.

### Show it off
Hold a launch with a countdown. Show your chart and announce your champion rocket.

### Clean up
Collect every rocket, even crash landings. Throw away scraps and put the straws back.

### For the grown-up
This is the engineering design process: build, test, measure, improve. Your child also practices fair tests and simple data tables. Remind them never to aim at faces, and give each person their own straw. **Ask at dinner:** "What change helped your rocket fly farthest, and what did nothing at all?"

## 8. Dream Vacation on a Budget

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1 |

**Materials:** paper, a pencil, colored pencils or markers, a calculator if you want one, the prices list on this sheet

**Nice to have:** an atlas, globe or paper map, old travel magazines or brochures

### The big idea
Pack your bags! You have won a pretend prize: a 5-day trip anywhere in the world for your whole family. The catch? You only get 5,000 pretend dollars. Plan the trip, keep the budget, and write the diary of your adventure.

### Goals
- You plan a 5-day trip with a full budget that does not go over 5,000 dollars.
- You write a travel diary of the trip as if it really happened.

### Objectives
- I can add up costs and keep a running total.
- I can make choices when I can't afford everything.
- I can write a diary entry with real details about a place.

### Get ready
Clear a spot at the table. Fold two sheets of paper in half to make a little booklet. Use these made-up prices:
- Plane ticket: 400 per person. Train ticket: 150 per person.
- Hotel: 200 a night. Tent at a campsite: 40 a night.
- Meals: 50 per person per day. Big activity (a boat ride, a tour, a theme park): 100 per person.

### Steps
1. Pick your place. It could be a beach, a big city, a mountain, or somewhere you have always wondered about.
2. List who is coming. Count the people. That number matters for every cost.
3. Make your budget chart (15 minutes). Columns: Item, Cost, Running Total. Start with travel, then sleeping, then food.
4. Add activities until you get close to 5,000. Over budget? Cut something or switch the hotel for a tent.
5. Plan each day in your booklet. One page per day. Write where you go and what you do.
6. Write the diary (20 minutes). Pretend the trip happened. What did you eat? What went wrong? What was the best moment?
7. Draw a postcard on the back page with a short note home.

### Make it yours
- Add a surprise: a lost suitcase costs 150 dollars to replace. How do you change your plan?
- Plan two trips, one fancy and one cheap, and compare.
- Write the diary from your pet's point of view.

### Stuck?
- Way over budget: count people again, then try camping or a closer place.
- You don't know much about your place: write about what you imagine. Use clues from a map or book if you have one.
- Math gets messy: total each day first, then add the days.

### Show it off
Present your trip like a travel agent. Show the budget, read one diary page, and hand out your postcard.

### Clean up
Put away markers and keep your booklet in a safe spot. It's a great keepsake.

### For the grown-up
This mixes real math (adding, subtracting, money decisions) with geography and creative writing. Prices are made up, so no real money talk is needed. If your child picks a place with family meaning, this is a nice chance to share a story. **Ask at dinner:** "What did you have to cut from the trip, and was it hard to give up?"

## 9. Superhero Team Origin Comic

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-120 min | Indoors | Low | On your own | 1+ |

**Materials:** 3 or 4 sheets of paper, a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, markers or colored pencils, a black pen

**Nice to have:** a stapler, sticky notes for planning

### The big idea
Every superhero team has a first day. Before the capes, before the base, they were just ordinary people. Then something happened. Your job: invent a brand new team of heroes. Then draw the comic of how they met and got their powers.

### Goals
- You invent a team of three or four original heroes, each with a power and a weakness.
- You draw a 2 to 4 page comic that tells the team's origin story.

### Objectives
- I can plan a story in panels (the boxes in a comic).
- I can use speech bubbles, captions, and sound words.
- I can create characters with powers that work better together.

### Get ready
Rule your pages with a ruler. Make 6 boxes on each page, two across and three down. Leave a little gap between boxes.

### Steps
1. Invent your heroes (15 minutes). Give each one a name, a power, a weakness, and an everyday job. Example: "Static Sam. Shocks things. Weak to water. Works at a laundromat."
2. Make their powers fit together. One hero is strong but slow. Another is fast but weak. Together they win.
3. Pick the event that gave them powers. A strange storm? A science fair gone wrong? A mysterious old library book?
4. Plan the story on sticky notes or scrap paper. One sentence per panel. Page 1: normal life. Page 2: the event. Page 3: the first big problem. Page 4: they team up and win.
5. Sketch your panels lightly in pencil (30 minutes). Big actions get big boxes.
6. Add words. Speech bubbles for talking. Captions in boxes for "Meanwhile..." Sound words like ZZAP and KRUNCH.
7. Ink over the pencil with pen. Then add color.

### Make it yours
- Give the villain an origin too, from the same event.
- Make heroes with silly powers, like talking to houseplants or never losing socks.
- Draw a cover for "Issue 1" with the team name in big letters.

### Stuck?
- Drawing people is hard: use stick figures with big expressive faces. Many comics start that way.
- Too much story: cut it down to the four main moments, one page each.
- Words don't fit in the bubble: write the words first, then draw the bubble around them.

### Show it off
Read your comic out loud to your family. Do the voices for each hero.

### Clean up
Cap the markers and stack your pages. Staple them into a book if you have a stapler.

### For the grown-up

## 10. Mug Cake Science

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 45-75 min | Indoors | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** 2 microwave-safe mugs, flour, sugar, cocoa powder, baking powder, salt, milk, vegetable oil, measuring spoons, a fork, a microwave, oven mitts, a ruler, a spoon

**Nice to have:** chocolate chips, a toothpick, a timer

### The big idea
Why do cakes puff up? A secret ingredient makes tiny gas bubbles. You will bake two mug cakes, one with the secret and one without, and see what happens. Then you get to eat the evidence.

### Goals
- You make two mug cakes, one with baking powder and one without.
- You measure and compare them, then eat them.

### Objectives
- I can follow a recipe and measure carefully.
- I can run a fair test where only one ingredient is different.
- I can explain what baking powder does in a cake.

### Get ready
Ask a grown-up if you can use the microwave. Check with them about allergies. Wash your hands. Set out everything on the counter. Label the mugs A and B with tape.

### Steps
1. In each mug, mix 4 tablespoons flour, 2 tablespoons sugar, 1 tablespoon cocoa, and a pinch of salt. Stir with a fork.
2. Add 1/4 teaspoon baking powder to mug A only. Mug B gets none. This is the only difference.
3. Add 3 tablespoons milk and 1 tablespoon oil to each mug. Stir until there are no dry lumps.
4. Make a guess. Write it down: "I think mug A will be ___ cm taller."
5. Microwave mug A for 70 seconds. Use mitts to take it out. Hot! Then do mug B.
6. Let both cool for 3 minutes. Measure how tall each cake is with a ruler. Taste a bite of each.

- Turn it into a "cake contest" where family members rate each one from 1 to 5.

### Stuck?
- The cake is gooey in the middle: microwave 15 more seconds at a time.
- The cake is rubbery: it cooked too long. Try 10 seconds less next time.
- It overflowed: use a bigger mug or a little less batter.

### Show it off
Serve tiny tasting bites to your family. Show your measurements and ask them to guess which had the secret.

### Clean up
Soak the mugs in warm water, wash the spoons, and wipe the counter. Put every ingredient back.

### For the grown-up
This is kitchen chemistry: baking powder releases carbon dioxide gas when wet and heated, which puffs the cake. It also practices measuring fractions. Please check allergies first (wheat, milk). Mugs get very hot in the microwave, so make sure your child uses mitts, and give the okay before they start. **Ask at dinner:** "What did the baking powder do, and how could you tell?"

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | High | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** a 2-liter plastic bottle, a clear jar or glass, dirt, water, sand, small gravel or pebbles, cotton balls or an old sock, a coffee filter or paper towel, a cup, a spoon, white paper

**Nice to have:** activated charcoal from a fish tank kit, a second bottle for a better design, a timer

### The big idea
Imagine your team is stranded on an island. The only water is a muddy puddle. Can you build a filter that turns brown water clear? Engineers solve this problem for real around the world.

### Goals
- You build a layered water filter from things around the house.
- You test it, rate how clear the water is, and improve it.

### Objectives
- I can build a filter with layers that catch big and small bits.
- I can compare results with a clearness score from 1 to 5.
- I can explain why clear water still might not be safe to drink.

### Get ready
Work outside, in a tub, or over a tray. Ask a grown-up to cut the bottle in half for you. Fill a cup with water and stir in 2 spoons of dirt and a few leaf bits.

### Steps
1. Turn the top half of the bottle upside down. Set it in the bottom half like a funnel. Leave the cap off.
2. Plan your layers. Draw them first. What goes on the bottom? What goes on top?

### Show it off
Line up three jars: the muddy water, your first try, and your best try. Explain each layer.

### Clean up
Dump the sand and gravel outside or in the trash, never the sink. Rinse the bottle for recycling.

### For the grown-up
This is a classic engineering challenge that also teaches about clean water. The key safety point: filtered water is never safe to drink, because germs pass through. Please do the bottle cutting yourself (a knife makes the first slit easily). Dirt and sand will clog drains. **Ask at dinner:** "Your water looked clear. Why still not drink it?"

### The big idea
An automaton is a moving machine with a hidden mechanism. You turn a handle, and a figure on top bobs, waves, or hops. Inside is a cam, a lumpy wheel that pushes things up and down. Build your own cardboard machine and bring a character to life!

### Goals
- You build a box machine with a crank that makes a figure move.
- You design your own character and its motion.

### Objectives

- Write a tiny story that your machine acts out with each turn.

### Stuck?

- The cam slips on the skewer: add more glue or wrap tape around the skewer for grip.
- The figure barely moves: move the skewer hole closer to the edge of the cam.

### Show it off
Put on a show. Turn the crank slowly and tell the story, then let your family try the handle.

### Clean up
Throw away scraps and put the glue and scissors back. Let the glue dry overnight.

### For the grown-up
This builds real engineering ideas: cams, followers, and how machines change one kind of motion into another. It also takes patience and fixing. Please poke the holes yourself with a sharp pencil or skewer, and handle any hot glue yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you explain how the off-center hole makes your figure move?"

## 19. Kaleidoscope

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | Nearby | 1 |

**Materials:** a paper towel tube, a cereal box, aluminum foil, plastic wrap, a small bag or wrap scraps, tiny colorful things (beads, sequins, paper bits, colored plastic scraps), tape, glue stick, scissors, a ruler, a pencil

**Nice to have:** wax paper for a frosted end, reflective sticker paper, markers to decorate the tube

### The big idea
Look into a kaleidoscope and a few beads turn into a giant spinning star. The secret is mirrors. Light bounces between them again and again. You can build one with a tube and some foil, then discover a whole universe inside.

### Goals
- You build a working kaleidoscope with three mirror walls.
- You test which bits make the best patterns.

### Objectives
- I can explain that light reflects (bounces) off shiny surfaces.
- I can build a triangle-shaped mirror inside a tube.
- I can test and change my design to make clearer patterns.

### Get ready
Clear the table. Cut a flat panel from the cereal box. Smooth the foil out with your hand. Smoother foil makes sharper mirrors.

### Steps
1. Cut three strips of cardboard. Each one should be as long as the tube and about 3 cm wide. Measure the tube first.
2. Glue foil to one side of each strip, shiny side out. Smooth it with a ruler edge so there are no wrinkles.
3. Tape the strips together, foil on the inside, to make a long triangle. It should slide snugly into the tube.
4. Cover one end of the tube with plastic wrap. Tape it tight. That is the window.
5. Put a pinch of beads and bits on the window. Cover them with a second layer of plastic wrap, leaving a little room so they can tumble.
6. Cover the other end with cardboard. Poke a small eye hole in the center with a pencil.
7. Point it at a window or lamp, never the sun. Turn the tube slowly and watch (15 minutes of testing different bits).

### Make it yours
- Skip the beads. Look through the open end at real things, like a plant or your hand. That is a teleidoscope.
- Make a square mirror tube with four strips and compare the patterns.
- Draw the best pattern you see, as close as you can.

### Stuck?
- The pattern is blurry: the foil is wrinkled. Remake one strip with smoother foil.
- It is too dark: point it toward a bright window or lamp.
- The bits don't move: loosen the outer plastic wrap so there is more room.

### Show it off
Pass it around. Ask each person to describe the shape they see in just three words.

### Clean up
Sweep up loose beads and foil scraps. Put scissors and glue back.

## 22. Ice Cream in a Bag

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | 45-75 min | Either | Medium | Nearby | 1+ |

**Materials:** milk (or half-and-half), sugar, vanilla, salt, ice cubes (about 3 cups per batch), a small zip-top bag, a large zip-top bag, measuring cups and spoons, a towel or winter gloves, a spoon

**Nice to have:** a kitchen thermometer, cocoa powder, crushed cookies or fruit, a second set of bags for a test

### The big idea
How do you make ice cream without a freezer? Shake it! Salt makes ice super cold, colder than plain ice. That cold pulls heat out of the milk until it freezes. Get ready to shake, shake, shake and then eat your science.

### Goals
- You make a serving of real ice cream in a bag.
- You find out what salt does to ice.

### Objectives
- I can measure ingredients carefully.
- I can test ice with and without salt to compare.
- I can explain why the salty ice gets colder.

### Get ready
Ask a grown-up about allergies first. Wash your hands. Lay a towel on the counter. Measure everything before you start.

### Steps
1. In the small bag, put 1 cup milk, 1 tablespoon sugar, and 1/2 teaspoon vanilla. Squeeze out the air. Seal it tight.
2. Fill the large bag halfway with ice. Add 1/3 cup salt.
3. Put the small bag inside the big bag. Seal the big bag.
4. Wrap the bag in a towel or put on gloves. It will get very cold!
5. Shake for 5 to 10 minutes. Toss it, roll it, dance with it. Check every 2 minutes.
6. Want a test? Make a second big bag with ice but no salt. Hold one in each hand for 30 seconds. Which is colder? (Use a thermometer if you have one.)
7. When it is firm, take the small bag out. Rinse the outside with cold water so salt doesn't get in. Eat it!

### Make it yours
- Invent a flavor: add 1 teaspoon cocoa, a spoon of jam, or a pinch of cinnamon to the small bag.
- Race a family member. Who can make ice cream fastest?
- Make an ice cream shop menu with your flavors and silly names.

### Stuck?
- It is still soupy: add more ice and salt, and keep shaking 3 more minutes.
- It tastes salty: the small bag leaked. Seal it better and double-bag it next time.
- Your hands are freezing: switch to gloves or pass the bag to a helper.

### Show it off
Serve tiny tasting spoons to your family. Explain the salt secret while they eat.

### Clean up
Pour the salty water down the sink, throw away the bags, and wipe the counter.

### For the grown-up
This is kitchen chemistry: salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salted ice gets colder than 0 C and can freeze milk. Please check for milk or other allergies, and use a dairy-free milk if needed (it will be softer). The bag gets cold enough to hurt bare hands, so a towel or gloves matters. **Ask at dinner:** "Why did we need the salt?"

### The big idea

### Objectives

- I can write a rhythm with symbols so someone else can read it.

### Steps

- It sounds boring: add one rest (a silent box) in a surprising spot.
- Your score is confusing: add a key at the top showing every symbol.

### Show it off

### Clean up
Fold your score and keep it. Get a drink of water after all that stomping.

### For the grown-up
This builds rhythm, steady beat, and music writing (notation), plus the musical idea of form (ABA). It is quiet enough for an apartment if your child swaps stomps for leg pats. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you teach me your Section A?"

### Objectives
- I can write step-by-step commands in the right order.
- I can find a bug (a mistake) in my program and fix it.
- I can use a loop, like "repeat 3 times: step forward", to make a program shorter.

### Get ready
Clear a path in a room. Use tape or string to make a grid on the floor: 5 squares by 5 squares, each about one big
step wide. If you can't use tape, use sheets of paper as squares. Put the stuffed animal in one square and mark a
start square.

### Steps
1. Make your command cards. Write each one on its own card or scrap: FORWARD 1, TURN LEFT, TURN RIGHT, PICK UP.
2. Agree on the robot rules. The robot only moves when it hears a command. A turn means turn in place, no step.
3. Write your first program on paper (10 minutes). Plan every move from start to the stuffed animal. Read it over
   before you run it.
4. Run it! Read the commands one at a time. The robot does exactly what you say, even if it's wrong.
5. Did the robot crash? That's a **bug**. Find the line that went wrong, fix it, and run the program again.
6. Make it harder. Add "walls" (pillows) the robot can't step on, and write a new program around them (15 minutes).
7. Shorten it with a **loop**. If you wrote FORWARD 1 three times in a row, write REPEAT 3: FORWARD 1 instead.
8. Switch jobs. Now your partner programs and you are the robot.

### Make it yours
- Give the robot a personality. It beeps on every turn or talks in a robot voice. It can only follow commands, though!
- Invent a new command, like SPIN or JUMP, and decide exactly what it means.
- Make a delivery mission: the robot picks up the stuffed animal and brings it back to start.

### Stuck?
- Lefts and rights get mixed up: tape an L and an R on the robot's hands.
- The program is too long to keep track of: number every line so you can say "the bug is on line 6."
- No partner right now: be your own robot. Write the program, then walk it yourself exactly as written.

### Show it off
Challenge a grown-up to be your robot after dinner. Can they follow your program with no help?

### Clean up
Pull up the tape, put the pillows back, and keep your command cards in an envelope for next time.

### For the grown-up
This is "unplugged" coding: the same thinking as computer programming (order of steps, debugging, loops) with no
screen. It also builds left and right, direction words, and patience. Painter's tape comes off floors cleanly; test a
corner first on rugs. If the robot wears a blindfold, keep the grid clear of anything they could trip on. **Ask at
dinner:** "What was the worst bug in your program, and how did you find it?"
