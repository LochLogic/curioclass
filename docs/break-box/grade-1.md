## 1. Cardboard Castle with a Drawbridge

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a medium cardboard box (a shipping box), 4 paper towel or toilet paper tubes, a box cutter or sharp scissors (grown-up only), safety scissors, tape, string or yarn, a pencil, markers or crayons, paper

**Nice to have:** a brass paper fastener, aluminum foil, toy figures or small stuffed animals as knights

### The big idea
Read aloud: "A dragon has been spotted near the kingdom! The royal family needs a castle with tall towers and a drawbridge that goes up and down. Only a master builder can make it. That's you!"

### Goals
- Your child builds a castle with towers and a working drawbridge that raises and lowers with a string.
- Your child defends the castle in a story with toy knights.

### Objectives
- Your child can plan a building by drawing it first.
- Your child can make a moving part work by testing and fixing it.
- Your child can describe where things are using words like above, below, beside and inside.

### Get ready
Tape the box closed and set it on its side or bottom, whichever looks more like a castle. Clear a table or floor space. Set out the tubes, tape and markers. Keep the box cutter out of reach when you are not using it.

### Steps
1. **Draw a plan** (5 minutes). Your child sketches the castle on paper: where the towers go, where the door is, how many windows. This is the builder's blueprint.
2. **Cut the drawbridge.** On the front of the box, your child draws a door about 4 inches wide and 6 inches tall. You cut the top and both sides only, so it folds down at the bottom.
3. **Make it work** (10 minutes). Poke a small hole near the top of the door and another in the wall above the doorway (grown-up). Your child threads string through both and ties a knot on the door. Pull the string: the bridge goes up! Let go: it comes down.
4. Test it 10 times. Does it stick? Your child figures out the fix (a longer string, a bigger hole, folding the bottom crease back and forth).
5. **Towers** (15 minutes). Tape a tube to each corner. Your child cuts small notches in the top with safety scissors to make battlements (the bumpy tops).
6. **Decorate** (20 minutes). Your child draws stones, windows, flags and a coat of arms. What animal or symbol is on the royal flag?
7. **The dragon attack.** Your child tells a story with toy figures: the dragon comes, the bridge goes up just in time, and then what happens?

### Make it yours
- Add a moat made of a blue towel, and a paper boat to cross it.
- Build a secret tunnel out the back from a tube and a small box.
- Make the castle belong to someone surprising: a cat queen, a robot knight, a friendly dragon.

### Stuck?
- The drawbridge falls off: tape the hinge line on the inside to make it stronger.
- Towers keep falling: tape them on the inside of the box corner too, or stand them on a tape "foot."

### Show it off
When the family gets home, your child raises the drawbridge and tells the story of the dragon attack.

### Clean up
Your child picks up scraps and puts the castle on a shelf to keep playing with.

### For the grown-up
This builds early engineering (a moving hinge and a pulley-like string), planning and spatial language. It is also great storytelling play. Do all cutting with the box cutter or sharp scissors yourself; your child can use safety scissors for notches. **Ask at dinner:** "How did you make the drawbridge go up?"

## 2. Pom-Pom Wall Run

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 6 to 10 paper towel and toilet paper tubes, painter's tape or masking tape, scissors (grown-up cuts tubes), small soft things to roll: pom-poms, foil balls, crumpled paper balls, a cup or bowl to catch them

**Nice to have:** a cereal box, paper cups, a timer

### The big idea
Read aloud: "There's a fluffy ball that wants to go on the wildest ride ever! It needs a twisty, turny run down the wall from way up high to a cup at the bottom. Can you build a run so good that the ball never falls off?"

### Goals
- Your child builds a run on a wall or door that carries a ball from top to bottom.
- Your child changes the run to make the ball go faster, slower or farther.

### Objectives
- Your child can test, see a problem, and change the design.
- Your child can explain that steeper slopes make the ball go faster.
- Your child can compare two runs and say which works better.

### Get ready
Pick a wall, door or fridge where tape is okay (test a corner first). Cut some tubes in half the long way to make open chutes (grown-up). Make 6 small foil or paper balls about the size of a grape.

### Steps
1. Hold a tube against the wall, tilted. Drop a ball in. Ask: "What happens if we tilt it more? Less?"
2. **Start at the top** (10 minutes). Your child picks where the first tube goes, as high as they can reach. Tape it on a slant.
3. **Add the next piece.** Where does the ball come out? Put the next tube there, slanted the other way, so the ball zig-zags.
4. **Test after every piece.** Drop the ball each time. Did it fall off? Your child moves the tube until it works.
5. **Keep building** (20 minutes). Use whole tubes as tunnels and half tubes as chutes. Your child decides where the turns go. End with a cup taped at the bottom.
6. **Run tests.** Time the ball by counting. Make one part steeper. Is it faster now? Make it flatter. Does it get stuck?
7. **Add a surprise.** Your child adds one special part: a drop into a cup with a hole, a split where the ball goes left or right, a "waterfall" jump.

### Make it yours
- Make it a race: build two runs side by side and race two balls.
- Give the ball a story: it's a snowball rolling down a mountain, or a lost marble finding its way home.
- Build a run with a rule: it must have 5 turns, or it must be as slow as possible.

### Stuck?
- Tape won't hold: use more tape, or tape to a big flattened box leaned against the wall.
- The ball shoots off at the turns: tape a paper "wall" or a cup at each corner to catch it.

### Show it off
Give each family member a ball to drop when they get home. Your child explains how the run works.

### Clean up
Peel off tape slowly at a low angle so it doesn't take paint. Save tubes in a bag for next time.

### For the grown-up
This is a first engineering project and a look at slopes, speed and gravity. The real learning is the test-and-fix loop. Use painter's tape and test it on a hidden spot first. Use balls bigger than a grape if a younger sibling is around. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the hardest part of the run to get right?"

## 3. Superhero Me Comic

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1 |

**Materials:** 2 sheets of paper, a ruler, a pencil, crayons or markers, a towel or old pillowcase for a cape

**Nice to have:** a mirror, a clothespin or safety pin for the cape (grown-up attaches)

### The big idea
Read aloud: "What if you woke up tomorrow with a superpower? Could you fly? Talk to animals? Shrink to the size of an ant? Today you invent your hero self and draw your very first comic book adventure."

### Goals
- Your child invents a superhero version of themselves with a name, a power and a costume.
- Your child draws a 4-panel comic with a beginning, a problem and an ending.

### Objectives
- Your child can tell a story in order: first, next, then, last.
- Your child can draw characters and show feelings with faces.
- Your child can write or dictate words for speech bubbles.

### Get ready
On one sheet of paper, draw lines to make 4 boxes (fold it in half, then in half again, and trace the folds). These are the panels. Put on the towel cape for inspiration.

### Steps
1. **Invent the hero** (10 minutes). Ask: "What's your superpower? What's your hero name?" Ideas: Captain Bounce, The Whisper, Puddle Girl. Write it at the top of the second sheet.
2. **Design the costume.** On the second sheet, your child draws their hero self: mask, cape, a symbol on the chest, special shoes. Ask: "Why that symbol?"
3. **Plan the story.** Every hero needs a problem. Ask: "What goes wrong in the town?" (A cat stuck on the moon. All the ice cream melted. A giant sneeze blew away the playground.)
4. **Panel 1:** the hero at home, ordinary. **Panel 2:** the problem appears. **Panel 3:** the hero uses the power. **Panel 4:** the happy ending.
5. **Draw the panels** (25 minutes). Your child draws each box. Remind them to show faces: worried, surprised, proud.
6. **Add words.** Draw speech bubbles. Your child writes or tells you the words: "Help!" "I'll save you!" Add sound words like ZOOM and SPLAT in big letters.
7. Your child adds a title and writes "by" with their name on top.

### Make it yours
- Give the hero a sidekick: a pet, a sibling, a talking backpack.
- Make a villain with a silly power, like making everyone's socks itchy.
- Give the hero a weakness, like losing power when they hear a song, and make it part of the story.

### Stuck?
- Drawing people is hard: draw the hero as a circle with a cape. Stick figures are real comic art.
- Your child has too many ideas: pick one for today and write the others on a "next issue" list.

### Show it off
Your child wears the cape and reads the comic aloud to the family, acting out the sound words.

### Clean up
Put the comic in a folder labeled "Issue 1." Crayons go back in the box.

### For the grown-up
This builds story structure (beginning, problem, solution), sequencing, drawing and early writing, the same skills your child uses in first-grade writing. Speech bubbles are a fun, low-pressure way to write. If you pin on a cape, never tie it around the neck. **Ask at dinner:** "What is your superpower, and who did you save today?"

## 4. Restaurant Day

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** paper, crayons or markers, a notepad or scrap paper for orders, a towel for the waiter's arm, plates, cups, napkins, a simple real snack or lunch (bread, cheese, fruit, crackers, vegetables), a butter knife

**Nice to have:** an apron, a small bell, a tablecloth, a vase with a flower

### The big idea
Read aloud: "A brand-new restaurant is opening today, and you are the owner, the chef and the waiter! You'll make the menu, take the orders and serve real food to your customers. What will your restaurant be called?"

### Goals
- Your child runs a pretend restaurant with a menu and serves a real snack or lunch.
- Your child takes orders, serves them and makes a bill.

### Objectives
- Your child can make a menu with pictures, words and prices.
- Your child can listen to an order and remember or write it.
- Your child can add small numbers to make a bill.

### Get ready
Wash hands. Decide together what real food is on the menu: 4 or 5 simple things you already have. Prep anything that needs a sharp knife or heat ahead of time (grown-up). Set a table with a tablecloth or towel.

### Steps
1. **Name the restaurant.** Ask: "What kind of restaurant? Fancy? A diner? A dinosaur café?" Make a sign for the door.
2. **Make the menu** (15 minutes). Fold paper in half. Your child draws each food and writes or copies the names. Add prices: 1 to 5 dollars. Give each dish a fun name ("Cheese Mountain," "Rainbow Crunch").
3. **Set the table.** Your child places a plate, cup and napkin for each customer, plus a flower or a drawn centerpiece.
4. **Open for business.** You arrive as the customer. Your child greets you, shows you to your seat and hands you the menu.
5. **Take the order** (10 minutes). Your child writes your order on the notepad (pictures are fine). Then they "go to the kitchen" and make it: arrange crackers, spread butter with a butter knife, pour water.
6. **Serve.** Your child serves the food with the towel over their arm and says, "Enjoy your meal!"
7. **The bill.** Your child adds up what you ordered and writes the total. You "pay" with paper money and they make change.
8. Switch roles and you serve your child.

### Make it yours
- Make it a restaurant for monsters, with dishes like "Swamp Soup" (green grapes in a bowl).
- Add a drive-through window from a cardboard box.
- Serve the stuffed animals too, and make their special menu.

### Stuck?
- Adding is tricky: make every price 1 or 2 dollars and count on fingers together.
- Your child wants to skip to eating: serve first, eat together, and do the bill as dessert.

### Show it off
Make it a family dinner: your child serves the family a "restaurant night" with the same menu.

### Clean up
The staff clears the table, wipes it and puts dishes in the sink. Restaurants always clean up!

### For the grown-up
This builds writing for a purpose, adding money, listening and following directions, and real kitchen helping. Check allergies before planning the menu and keep it nut-free. Do any knife work beyond a butter knife and any cooking yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most popular dish at your restaurant?"

## 5. Backyard Weather Station

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a clear plastic bottle, a ruler, tape, a marker, scissors (grown-up only), a paper plate, a long strip of plastic bag or crepe paper, a straw, a pencil, small stones or sand, paper for a weather log, crayons

**Nice to have:** an outdoor thermometer, a compass, a clipboard

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Real weather scientists measure the rain, the wind and the clouds every day. Today you'll build your own weather station with a rain catcher and a wind sock. Then you can be the weather boss of your yard."

### Goals
- Your child builds a rain gauge and a wind sock and sets them up outside.
- Your child starts a weather log and records the weather for the rest of the break.

### Objectives
- Your child can measure in inches with a ruler.
- Your child can describe wind as calm, breezy or strong by watching the wind sock.
- Your child can draw weather symbols and record what they see each day.

### Get ready
Cut the top third off the plastic bottle (grown-up). Put a handful of stones in the bottom so it doesn't blow over. Pick a spot outside away from roofs and trees, or a balcony railing.

### Steps
1. **Rain gauge** (15 minutes). Turn the bottle top upside down and set it in the bottom part like a funnel. Tape a ruler to the side, or your child draws inch marks with the marker using the ruler. Mark 0 just above the stones; pour in water to the 0 line.
2. **Wind sock** (15 minutes). Your child decorates a paper plate ring (you cut the middle out). Tape 5 long strips of plastic bag or crepe paper to it. Hang it from a string.
3. **Set up the station.** Your child chooses the spot. Put the rain gauge where nothing drips into it and hang the wind sock where it can blow.
4. **Weather log.** Fold paper into 7 boxes, one per day. Your child draws symbols: sun, cloud, rain, wind.
5. **First reading** (10 minutes). Look at the sky, the wind sock and the rain gauge. Your child draws today's weather. Is the wind sock hanging (calm), waving (breezy) or straight out (strong)?
6. **Cloud check.** Name the clouds together: puffy (cumulus), flat and gray (stratus), thin and wispy (cirrus).
7. Your child makes a guess about tomorrow: "I think it will rain." Check tomorrow to see if they were right.

### Make it yours
- Add a weather vane with a paper arrow on a pencil to see which way the wind blows.
- Make a "puddle watch": draw chalk around a puddle and check how fast it shrinks.
- Have a weather contest: the family guesses tomorrow's weather, and the weather boss checks.

### Stuck?
- It isn't raining: pour a cup of water in and practice reading the ruler, then wait for real rain.
- No outdoor space: use a window sill and a wind sock hung by an open window, and record what you see.

### Show it off
Give a daily weather report to the family at dinner, using the log.

### Clean up
Tidy scraps. Empty the rain gauge after each reading and record it before you pour.

### For the grown-up
This builds measurement, observation, recording data over time and weather science (a first-grade topic). The cut edge of the bottle can be sharp; cover it with tape. Keep weather watching away from storms, and come inside when there is thunder. **Ask at dinner:** "What will the weather be tomorrow, and how do you know?"

## 6. Mystery Bag Guessing Game

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game | 45-75 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 2+ |

**Materials:** a pillowcase or cloth bag, 10 to 15 everyday objects (a spoon, a sock, a pinecone, a toy car, a sponge, an orange, a hairbrush, a key, a block, a cotton ball), paper, a pencil, a partner (a grown-up, a sibling or a friend over)

**Nice to have:** a scarf for a blindfold, a timer

### The big idea
Read aloud: "There's something hiding in the Mystery Bag! You can't look. You can only feel it. Is it soft? Bumpy? Cold? Can you use your detective fingers to guess what's inside?"

### Goals
- Your child guesses mystery objects by touch alone.
- Your child fills a mystery bag and gives clues for a partner to guess.

### Objectives
- Your child can use describing words: smooth, rough, soft, hard, round, pointy, heavy, light.
- Your child can give clues without saying the answer.
- Your child can make a guess based on evidence.

### Get ready
Without your child seeing, gather 10 objects. Make sure nothing is sharp. Put 5 in the bag. Write the list on paper and fold it.

### Steps
1. **Practice words.** Ask your child to touch things around the room and describe them: "The couch is soft. The window is smooth and cold."
2. **Round 1** (10 minutes). Your child reaches into the bag without looking and holds one thing. Before pulling it out, they describe it: "It's long. One end is round. It's cold." Then they guess. Pull it out to check!
3. Keep score: a point for each right guess.
4. **Round 2: Clue master** (15 minutes). Your child secretly picks an object and puts it in the bag. They give you three clues: "It's soft. You wear it. It goes on your foot." You guess.
5. **Make it harder.** Your child must give clues with no "using" words, only how it feels: "It's soft, stretchy and has a hole."
6. **Round 3: Whole house** (15 minutes). Your child hunts the house for the trickiest mystery object they can find and puts it in for you.
7. Add up points. The winner picks the next game.

### Make it yours
- Do a smell mystery instead: a lemon, cinnamon, soap, an orange peel, with eyes closed.
- Do a sound mystery: shake things in a closed box.
- Make it a story: the objects were stolen from a museum, and the detective has to find them all.

### Stuck?
- Too hard: let your child look for 1 second after 3 guesses.
- Your child says the answer as a clue: practice "it's a thing that..." sentences together first.

### Show it off
Play with the family after dinner. Your child is the game host and picks the objects.

### Clean up
Your child returns each object to its place. Can they remember where each one came from?

### For the grown-up
This builds describing vocabulary, reasoning from clues, and taking turns, plus sense of touch. Giving clues without saying the answer is a big thinking skill. Keep sharp, breakable or very small objects out of the bag. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the trickiest mystery object?"

## 7. Paper Airplane Airport

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 60-90 min | Either | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** 6 to 10 sheets of printer paper, tape, a measuring tape or ruler, markers, a laundry basket or box, paper for signs

**Nice to have:** paper clips, a hallway or yard, small stickers

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Welcome to the airport! Pilots need planes that fly far, planes that fly straight, and planes that can land on a target. Let's build a fleet and test them on the runway."

### Goals
- Your child folds at least 3 different paper airplanes.
- Your child tests them and finds out which flies farthest and which lands best.

### Objectives
- Your child can follow folding steps and then change a design.
- Your child can measure flight distance and compare numbers.
- Your child can explain one change that made a plane fly better.

### Get ready
Clear a hallway or go outside on a calm day. Tape a "runway" line on the floor where pilots throw from. Set a basket about 8 feet away as a landing target.

### Steps
1. **Build Plane 1: the Dart** (10 minutes). Fold paper in half the long way and open it. Fold the top corners to the middle line. Fold the new slanted edges to the middle again. Fold in half. Fold each wing down so its edge meets the bottom of the body. Your child names it and draws a logo.
2. **Test flight.** Throw from the runway line. Mark where it lands with a scrap of paper. Measure it with the measuring tape.
3. **Build Plane 2: the Glider** (10 minutes). Same as the Dart, but fold the tip down about 2 inches before the wing folds so the nose is blunt and the wings are wide.
4. **Test it the same way.** Which went farther? Which flew straighter? Write the distances on a chart.
5. **Your child's plane** (15 minutes). Your child changes something: bigger wings, wing tips folded up a pencil's width, a paper clip on the nose, a different shape. Ask: "What do you think will happen?"
6. **Airport challenges.** Who can land a plane in the basket? Who can fly through a doorway?
7. Award prizes: Longest Flight, Best Landing, Coolest Design.

### Make it yours
- Make it an airline: give planes passengers (drawn faces in the windows) and destinations.
- Build a control tower from a box, and you are the pilot asking for permission to land.
- Have a slow-flight contest: which plane stays in the air longest?

### Stuck?
- The plane nose-dives: fold the back edges of the wings up a tiny bit.
- It turns to one side: check that both wings are the same size and smooth out bends.

### Show it off
Hold an air show when the family gets home. Each person flies one plane and your child runs the tower.

### Clean up
Collect all the planes in a "hangar" box. Recycle the crumpled ones.

### For the grown-up
This builds measuring, comparing numbers, following steps and changing one thing at a time, which is how scientists test ideas. Folding builds fine motor control. Remind everyone not to throw planes at faces. **Ask at dinner:** "What change made your plane fly better?"

## 8. Nature Bracelet Walk

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside | 60-90 min | Outdoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** wide masking tape or packing tape, paper, crayons, a small bag

**Nice to have:** a magnifying glass, a hat for each walker, a water bottle

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Let's go on a walk and wear what we find! Your bracelet will be sticky side out, so every leaf, petal and feather you find can hop right on. By the end, you'll be wearing the whole walk on your wrist."

### Goals
- Your child makes a nature bracelet with at least 8 different treasures.
- Your child tells the story of the walk using the bracelet.

### Objectives
- Your child can find and name different leaves, seeds and petals.
- Your child can compare treasures by color, size and texture.
- Your child can retell events in order.

### Get ready
Wrap a strip of masking tape around your child's wrist, sticky side out. Make it loose enough to slide off. Make one for yourself. Plan a short walk: a yard, a block, a park, or a path near your home.

### Steps
1. Talk about walk rules: stay with your grown-up, pick only from the ground, and leave living flowers on plants unless they're yours to pick.
2. **The walk** (30 minutes). Your child looks for tiny treasures: a small leaf, a petal, a blade of grass, a seed, a feather, a bit of bark. Press each one onto the tape.
3. **Mix it up.** Ask: "Can you find something yellow? Something fuzzy? Something smaller than your fingernail?"
4. Let your child choose the path at every corner: left, right or straight.
5. **Back home, look closely** (10 minutes). Slide off the bracelet. Use the magnifying glass. Which treasure has lines? Which is the softest?
6. **Tell the walk story.** Your child goes around the bracelet in order: "First I found this leaf by the gate. Then a feather by the big tree..."
7. **Draw a map** (10 minutes). Your child draws the path you walked and tapes a picture of each treasure where it was found.

### Make it yours
- Make a color bracelet: find something in every color of the rainbow, in order.
- Make a crown instead, with tape around a paper band.
- Make a gift bracelet for someone at home with treasures they would love.

### Stuck?
- Tape stops sticking: add a new strip on top or wrap a fresh layer.
- No safe walking area: do it on a balcony with potted plants, in a courtyard, or with houseplant trimmings and kitchen herbs.

### Show it off
Wear the bracelet when the family gets home and tell them the walk story.

### Clean up
Lay the bracelet flat on paper to keep, or put it in a zip bag. Wash hands after the walk.

### For the grown-up
This builds observation, describing words, sequencing and early map-making. Keep your child away from mushrooms, berries, broken glass and plants you don't know; poison ivy and similar plants are common along paths. Wash hands after. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the story of your bracelet?"

## 9. Box Stage Puppet Show

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend | 60-120 min | Indoors | Medium | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a cardboard box (cereal box or larger), a box cutter or sharp scissors (grown-up only), paper, crayons or markers, tape, straws or craft sticks, a dish towel or fabric scrap for a curtain, safety scissors

**Nice to have:** string to make the curtain open, a flashlight for a spotlight

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Ladies and gentlemen, the show is about to begin! You are the puppet maker, the stage builder and the star of the show. Build a tiny theater, make your actors, and put on a play."

### Goals
- Your child builds a box stage and makes at least 3 stick puppets.
- Your child performs a short puppet play with a beginning, middle and end.

### Objectives
- Your child can plan a story with characters, a setting and a problem.
- Your child can use different voices and actions for different characters.
- Your child can perform for an audience and speak clearly.

### Get ready
Cut a large rectangle out of the front of the box to make the stage window, and cut the back off so hands can reach in (grown-up). Leave a frame about 1 inch wide around the window.

### Steps
1. **Decorate the theater** (15 minutes). Your child names the theater and draws on the frame: lights, stars, curtains. Write the name across the top.
2. **Plan the play** (10 minutes). Ask: "Who is in your play? Where does it happen? What goes wrong?" Example: a frog and a cat want the same lily pad, and a storm comes.
3. **Make the scenery.** Your child draws the background (a forest, the ocean, a kitchen) on paper. Tape it inside the back of the box so it shows through.
4. **Make the puppets** (20 minutes). Your child draws each character about the size of a hand and cuts them out with safety scissors (you help with tricky parts). Tape each to a straw or stick.

Read aloud: "Inside every bean is a tiny baby plant, sleeping and waiting. If you give it water, it will wake up and start to grow, and you can watch it happen right through the glass! Let's start your bean garden."

### Goals
- Your child sets up a bean jar and watches seeds sprout over the break.
- Your child keeps a bean journal with drawings and measurements.

### Objectives
- Your child can name the parts of a growing plant: seed, root, stem, leaf.
- Your child can measure growth with a ruler.
- Your child can make a guess and check it over several days.

### Get ready
Soak a few extra beans in water overnight if you can (it speeds things up). Fold 2 paper towels and wet them. Fold paper into 8 boxes for a bean journal.

### Steps
1. **Look inside a seed.** Take a soaked bean and gently split it open. With the magnifying glass, find the tiny baby plant inside.
2. **Set up the jar** (10 minutes). Your child stuffs the wet paper towels inside the jar, pressed against the glass. Slide 3 or 4 beans between the towel and the glass, halfway down, so you can see them.
3. Add a little water at the bottom so the towels stay wet but not soggy.
4. **Guess.** Ask: "Which will come first, the root or the leaf? Will it grow up or down?" Write the guess.

- Have a bean race: which bean grows tallest by the end of the week? Make a finish line.
- Give the beans a story: they're climbing to reach a castle in the clouds.
- Turn the jar upside down for a day once a sprout is up. Does the stem turn to grow upward?

### Stuck?
- No sprouts after 4 days: make sure the towels stay damp and the jar is somewhere warm.
- Mold appears: start over with fresh towels and use a little less water.

### Show it off
Share the journal at dinner each night: "Today my bean is 2 inches tall!" When it has leaves, plant it in soil.

### Clean up
Wipe up water drips. Your child puts the journal next to the jar.

### For the grown-up
This builds life science (plant parts and what plants need, a first-grade topic), measurement, and patience. Seeds usually sprout in 3 to 5 days. Some dry beans (like kidney beans) are toxic raw, so make sure your child doesn't eat them. **Ask at dinner:** "What came out first, the root or the leaves?"

**Nice to have:** wooden blocks, a ruler for a ramp, a timer, a bell at the end

### The big idea
Read aloud: "One little push, and the whole line goes CLACK, CLACK, CLACK! Today you build a chain reaction, where one thing knocks over the next and the next. Can you make a line so long that it goes all the way across the room?"

### Goals
- Your child builds a book domino line that falls all the way to the end.
- Your child adds a starting trick and a finish that rings, pops or drops.

### Objectives
- Your child can find the right spacing so each book knocks over the next.
- Your child can test, find where it stopped, and fix it.
- Your child can explain cause and effect: "this pushed that, which pushed that."

### Get ready
Clear a long space of floor, ideally on a hard floor or flat rug. Pile the books nearby. Move pets and siblings away from the building zone for now.

### Steps
1. **Test two books.** Stand two books up on their bottom edges, close together. Push the first. Does it knock the second? Move them farther apart. Still? Find the farthest space that works. (Usually about half a book's height.)

7. **The big run.** Count down from 5 and start it. Did it work? Rebuild and try again.

- Make a split path: the line splits into two and both sides fall at once.
- Add other "dominoes": tall boxes, cereal boxes, standing paper towel tubes.
- Make it a story: the books are a line of sleepy giants who wake each other up.

### Stuck?
- Books slide instead of falling: build on a rug or a towel, not slippery wood.
- The chain stops at the same place: that book is too far or tilted. Line it up again or move it closer.

### Show it off
Rebuild the chain and let a family member give the first push when they get home. Count down together!

### Clean up
Your child stacks books and returns them to the shelf by size: biggest on the bottom.

### For the grown-up
This builds cause and effect, early physics (pushes, falling, energy passing along a line), patience and problem solving. Use sturdy books you don't mind falling, and keep the line away from stairs. **Ask at dinner:** "Where did your chain keep stopping, and how did you fix it?"

5. **Dress the twin** (25 minutes). Your child colors in clothes: their favorite outfit, a costume, or something they dream of wearing. Glue on fabric scraps or yarn hair.
6. **The extra.** Your child adds one surprise: a cape, a pet at their feet, a speech bubble with their favorite saying, a magic wand.
7. **Cut it out** (grown-up) or leave it on the paper. Tape it to a door or wall.

### Make it yours
- Make it a "future me" twin: what will you look like as a grown-up?
- Fill the twin with drawings of all your favorite things: foods inside the tummy, songs in the head.
- Make a twin for a stuffed animal too, and compare sizes.

### Stuck?
- Your child wiggles while being traced: trace quickly and fix lines later, or count to 30 together while you trace.
- No big paper: tape together printer paper sheets, or make a half twin from the waist up.

### Show it off
Hang the paper twin on the front door so it greets the family when they come home. Your child can stand next to it.

### Clean up
Roll up scraps and recycle them. Your child caps markers and puts crayons away.

### For the grown-up
This builds body awareness, measurement, self-expression and drawing. It's a lovely keepsake to compare to next year's height. Do any big cutting yourself. **Ask at dinner:** "What's one thing about your paper twin that's just like you?"

## 29. Cloud Stories

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 45-75 min | Either | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a blanket or towel to lie on, blue paper or white paper with blue crayon, white crayons or chalk, cotton balls, glue, a pencil

**Nice to have:** a window with a view of the sky, a clipboard

### The big idea
Read aloud: "Look up! The sky is a giant picture book. One cloud looks like a dragon, another like an ice cream cone. They change shape while you watch! Let's find pictures in the clouds and turn them into stories."

### Goals
- Your child finds at least 5 cloud shapes and tells what they look like.
- Your child makes a cloud picture book and tells a story with it.

### Objectives
- Your child can describe shapes using words like puffy, long, round, wispy.
- Your child can imagine a story from a shape they see.
- Your child can tell a story with a beginning, middle and end.

### Get ready
Pick a day with some clouds. Lay a blanket outside on grass or on a balcony, or set up by a big window. Set out paper and cotton balls inside for later.

### Steps
1. **Cloud watching** (15 minutes). Lie on your backs and look up. Ask: "What do you see?" Point out shapes and take turns. "That one looks like a sheep with a hat!"
2. **Watch it change.** Pick one cloud and watch it for a minute. Did it get bigger? Did it turn into something else?
3. **Remember your favorites.** Your child picks 3 favorite cloud shapes to make into pictures.
4. **Make cloud pictures** (20 minutes). Inside, your child pulls cotton balls apart and glues them on blue paper in the shape of each cloud. Add details with crayon: eyes, a tail, a hat.
5. **Tell the story.** Line up the 3 pictures. Ask: "How are these clouds connected? What happens to them?" Example: "A cloud dragon flew to visit a cloud rabbit, but a cloud storm came..."
6. **Write it.** Your child tells the story and you write one sentence under each picture.
7. Staple or tape the pages together into a "Cloud Book" with a cover.

### Make it yours
- Make a cloud that rains on someone in the story, and what happens next.
- Make one cloud picture into a puppet on a straw for a cloud puppet show.
- Draw a cloud shape for someone else and see if they can guess what it is.

### Stuck?
- It's a clear blue sky: lie down anyway and look at tree shapes, or do it on another day. You can also make cloud shapes from cotton balls and guess each other's.
- Your child sees "just clouds": start with a fun one ("I see a giant shoe!") to get them going.

### Show it off
Read the Cloud Book to the family. Then go look at the sky together and find new shapes.

### Clean up
Shake out the blanket. Your child picks up cotton bits and puts the glue away.

### For the grown-up
This builds imagination, describing words, storytelling and early weather science (clouds change shape as wind moves them). Never look straight at the sun. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the most amazing thing you saw in the clouds?"

## 30. Family Time Capsule

| Kind | Time | Where | Mess | Grown-up | Players |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story | 60-90 min | Indoors | Low | With you | 1+ |

**Materials:** a shoebox or a large jar with a lid, paper, crayons or markers, a pencil, tape, a measuring tape or string, small keepsakes (a drawing, a list, a photo)

**Nice to have:** an envelope for each person, a sticker or ribbon to seal it

### The big idea
Read aloud: "A time capsule is a box full of things from today that you open far in the future! When you open it, you'll get to meet the you from right now. What do you want future you to know?"

### Goals
- Your child fills a time capsule with drawings, facts and a letter to their future self.
- Your child seals it and chooses a date to open it.

### Objectives
- Your child can tell facts about their life now: age, height, favorites.
- Your child can write a few words or a short letter.
- Your child can think about how things change over time.

### Get ready
Get a box or jar. Write these starters on separate papers: "My favorites," "Right now I am...," "Dear Future Me," "My family," "I hope..."

### Steps
1. **Talk about time.** Ask: "What were you like when you were a baby? What will you be like when you're 10?" Explain that the time capsule saves today.
2. **Measure.** Your child stands against the wall and you measure them with string or a tape. Cut a string their height, or write the number down. It goes in the capsule!
3. **Handprint and favorites** (15 minutes). Trace your child's hand on paper. On the "My favorites" page, your child draws or writes: favorite food, color, toy, book, animal, song.
4. **Letter to future me** (15 minutes). Your child tells you what to write: "Dear Future Me, I like to dance. Do you still like pizza?" They sign it.
5. **Family page.** Your child draws everyone they live with. Each family member can add a note or drawing later too.
6. **Decorate the capsule** (10 minutes). Your child decorates the box with a big label: "Do Not Open Until..."
7. **Seal it.** Put everything in. Your child chooses when to open it: in a year? On their birthday? Write the date on the box. Tape it shut and hide it somewhere safe.

### Make it yours
- Add a "guess the future" page: your child predicts what they'll be like in a year.
- Make a time capsule for a stuffed animal, telling about its life.
- Include a small object (a drawing, a leaf, a sticker) that means something today.

### Stuck?
- Writing is too hard: your child draws and you write their words.
- Your child wants to open it tomorrow: make one "mini capsule" to open at the end of the break, and the big one for a year.

### Show it off
Before sealing it, let your child show each item to the family. Everyone can add one thing.

### Clean up
Your child puts the supplies away and helps choose a safe hiding spot for the capsule.

### For the grown-up
This builds writing, self-awareness and an understanding of time (past, present, future), a first-grade social studies idea. It's a wonderful thing to open in a year. Put a reminder on your calendar. **Ask at dinner:** "What do you think you'll be like when you open the time capsule?"
