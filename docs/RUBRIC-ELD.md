## How it works

The workbook has four parts. **Part A** describes the materials (grade, subject, which ELD standards, scope,
links, any other reviews). **Part B** is a narrative on the framework's four Big Ideas; it is not scored.
**Part C** is the scored part. **Part D** sums up strengths, gaps and a growth plan, and repeats the self-rating.

Part C reads the materials against the framework's four components, each through three lenses:

- **Match:** the materials connect plainly and concretely to the ideas in that component.
- **Depth:** they carry its purpose, variety and growing complexity, planned so learners grow their language in
  purposeful and widening ways.
- **Breadth:** they do so consistently and on a system: across lessons, across units, and across the year, not
  here and there.

That makes 13 criteria (the standards statements have two match criteria: one for social and instructional
language, one for the language of the content areas). Each criterion has three indicators and a set of key
questions. A criterion scores 4 when the evidence clearly connects to all three indicators, 3 for two, 2 for one,
and 1 for none. A component's score is its lowest criterion score.

**The seal line**, for publishers only: at least 3 on three of the four components, and no component below 2. A 2
on two or more components is not yet eligible. Every criterion needs a claim, a justification and evidence with
page numbers and links.

**What may be submitted:** a full year's course in a core subject (language arts, math, science, social studies, or
an integrated course) built to the framework, or a full year of dedicated ELD that connects clearly and concretely
to grade-level content standards. Adjacent grades within one of the framework's grade clusters (K, 1, 2-3, 4-5,
6-8, 9-12) can be added by an extended review that shows the same approach holds. Supplements count only when tied
to core materials.

**How reviewers read:** at least two reviewers, a third if they disagree. The team reads a common set of units, and
each reviewer adds at least one more unit of their own choosing (a spot check). Each scores alone, then the team
reaches one score per criterion and writes a report. A local panel may follow the same steps to self-rate.

## The criteria, and what would satisfy each in a Curio Class unit or day

Ids are WIDA's. Wording is ours. "Evidence" is what a reader of a finished Curio Class ELD unit should be able to
point to; the rules themselves live in bar part 10.

| Id | What it asks, in our words | Evidence in a Curio Class ELD unit or day |
|---|---|---|
| **Match.ELD.1** (social and instructional language) | (a) The teacher is guided to value and use students' languages, cultures, experiences and identities. (b) Language for classroom life is supported: routines, negotiating, solving problems together. (c) That social language is used as a resource alongside the content-area language, not apart from it | Home language helps in section field 9; a named talk routine in field 7 that includes agreeing, disagreeing, asking for help and planning a shared task; the SI id planned in every unit next to its LA, MA, SC or SS id |
| **Match.ELD.2-5** (language of the content areas) | (a) Language is developed together with content. (b) The teacher is guided to help students share information and ideas and take part in the subject's practices in at least one content standard. (c) Interactive work and discussion build language and concepts at once | Each unit names its host core unit (unit field 3) and the content id it plans to; days whose task is the host unit's own investigation, text or problem; structured talk on that content before writing |
| **Depth.ELD** | (a) Strength-based teaching that draws on students' backgrounds and identities in the subject. (b) Many chances to interact with peers and adults. (c) Clear, systematic, explicit teaching of language that serves the subject. Key questions add metacognitive and metalinguistic skill: students think and talk about how language works | A form taught directly on most days (show, name, try, use); students name the form and its job in their own words; varied grouping (partner, small group, whole class, with the teacher) across a unit |
| **Breadth.ELD** | The above holds across lessons, across units and across the year | The scope and sequence's year map: SI and a content standard in every unit; the section plans of a sampled unit each show interaction and a taught form |
| **Match.KLU** (key language uses: narrate, inform, explain, argue) | (a) The key language uses are defined. (b) Their link to the content standards is shown. (c) Genre is explained as a way language is organised: different purposes, different patterns | A teacher note and a student page per unit that say which key language use the unit builds, what it is for, and which content standards ask for it; a model text with its stages labelled |
| **Depth.KLU** | (a) How each key language use works in a given subject (an argument in math differs from one in literature). (b) Explicit teaching of how it works across texts, tasks and purposes, showing its typical stages and language. (c) Language choices shaped by purpose, topic, situation, the speaker's identity and role, and audience. Key questions add how the uses blend and build on each other | A deconstruct-then-construct sequence each unit (take a model apart, build one together, write one alone); a task that has students say or write the same content for two different audiences or purposes; a note on how the use looks in the host subject |
| **Breadth.KLU** | Across lessons, units and the year: patterns explained, links to content shown, students taking texts apart and building them, and what they can do with each use growing | The year map shows each key language use the cluster's ids name recurring, with a harder text or task each time |
| **Match.LE** (Language Expectations) | (a) Language goals are stated for units and lessons. (b) They come from the content standards and practices. (c) Both modes are addressed: interpretive (listening, reading, viewing) and expressive (speaking, writing, representing) | Unit field 2 names the Language Expectation ids (in our words) drawn from the host unit's content standards; section field 2 states the day's language goal; each unit has interpretive and expressive tasks |
| **Depth.LE** | (a) Choices students can make with language widen through explicit teaching of the language functions inside an expectation. (b) And through the language features (sentence types, clauses, phrases, words) that carry out those functions. (c) The link between expectation, function and feature is made visible | Each taught form is presented as a feature that does a job (for example, a "because" clause to give a reason in Explain); the teacher page lists the unit's functions and the features for each; the language of the content is made visible to students on the page |
| **Breadth.LE** | Across lessons, units and the year: what students can do with the expectations grows, functions and features are explored, both modes are used | The year map lists functions and features per unit, with earlier ones coming back; both modes in every unit |
| **Match.PLD** (proficiency level descriptors, six levels) | (a) A range of language targets for learners at different levels. (b) Ways to watch language growth over time. (c) Scaffolds for content and language across the levels | Three entry points (levels 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6) with look-fors per entry point; the unit's end-of-unit assessment states how a student moves up; scaffolds named per entry point |
| **Depth.PLD** | (a) All three dimensions of language: whole text (discourse), sentence, and word or phrase. (b) The same thinking demand for every student, with the levels used to plan support, not to lower the task. (c) Scaffolds fitted to strengths, interests, prior experience, level, purpose and task. Key questions add monitoring growth in several ways | Look-fors at each entry point cover the whole piece, its sentences and its words; the three entry points do the same task on the same content; scaffolds that respond to the student in the moment (a prompt, a reread, a model) as well as planned ones; more than one kind of check (talk, writing, self-check) |
| **Breadth.PLD** | Across lessons, units and the year: a range of targets, growth watched, all three dimensions, the same demand for all, fitted scaffolds | The teacher's paper record sheet across units; every sampled unit shows entry points, three dimensions and same-task levels |
| **Part B, Big Ideas** (not scored) | Equity of access; content and language together; people working together (content and language teachers, families, specialists, students); a functional view of language as a social process growing over time, not isolated grammar | The approach page; a note per unit the ELD teacher can share with the host-course teacher; the family note; forms always taught for a job, on content |

## Where the bar uses this file

Bar part 10 cites the ids above against each of its criteria, so a reader can see which rest on PRIME and which are
ours. The rules are stated there once, not here.

**Proposed, not decided:** when a band course is finished, a private self-rating on all 13 criteria, read the way
PRIME reads (a common sample plus a spot-check unit per reader), recorded in a dated file under docs/reviews/. It is
never published and never called a seal or a claim of alignment. PRIME also reads core-subject courses built to the
framework, so the same ids could inform how C11 (English learner supports inside core lessons) is read; that is a
later question for the C11 gate, not settled here.
