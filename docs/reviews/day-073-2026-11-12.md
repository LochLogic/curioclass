## Verdict

Day 73 is correct mathematics and a sound fluency day: nine sums that grow in length and mix lengths on purpose,
an estimate before each, place names withdrawn mid-lesson, and the register's B and E fixed beside estimates. The
biggest risk is that the B fix is the worked example again, so on paper it is copying (the right sum and estimate
are a few inches above it on the same side), and the one written "how can you tell, without adding" that end-check
item 9.1c judges is never practised before day 77. The one change that matters most is finding 1: give Kai new
numbers and ask "how can you tell" in writing; keep 6,285 + 947 = 15,755 for the class contrast in the closing Ask
and on day 74.

## The writers' question: should the B fix reuse 6,285 + 947?

**For the class contrast, yes; for the student's item, no.** Showing the same sum lined up wrongly beside lined
up rightly is a sound move: the only difference is the alignment, so the class sees the error and nothing else
(the research on comparing a wrong and a right example of one problem supports this; from memory, Durkin and
Rittle-Johnson 2012, not reopened). That is what the closing Ask ("how do you know 6,285 + 947 is not 15,755?"),
the register's example and day 74's B reteach already do, and they should keep it. But as a two-minute task for a
student ("Estimate. Then find the right sum") it asks for two numbers the student wrote ten lines up, 7,200 and
7,232. It tests nothing, and it is the day's only B item a student works alone. With new numbers and a "how can
you tell" prompt, the same two minutes rehearse the reason end-check item 9.1c marks. Settled below.

  Nothing on the day says so, and the page "watch for" on A ("a sum short by 10, 100 or 1,000") does not tell

- **Evidence.** wrongs.csv `s09-F73-1` (7,222, 7,132); `s09-X73` A rows (340,205, 340,115 against an estimate of

  1, is close to 7,200 too. The estimate catches a wrong place; the columns catch a lost 1." Set 1's Check line
