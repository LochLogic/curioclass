## The expert's read

Read cold on 2026-11-11. I am not the second reader on rights. Nothing below rules on a licence. I checked that
the gathering is complete, accurate and honest, and I judged the findings that are not about rights.

### Verdict

The gathering is careful and mostly accurate: every address opened, and every quoted licence line matched its
page or file word for word, with one small wording note (F9). The biggest risk is not in the rights lines but in
the texts themselves: the pilot passage is not what its name says, the "Ramayana, condensed" is not a condensed
Ramayana, and the text for the end check is on no list at all. The one change that matters most is to fix the
pilot passage (Part II, chapters IV to VI, named for what it holds), give it an honest text record that says it
sits below the grade 6 to 8 band on the computed measure, and add the end-check myth to the list before the pilot
is planned.

### How I checked

- Fetched all six Project Gutenberg catalogue pages and plain-text files, the licence and permission pages, both
  LibriVox book pages and the public domain page, both Core Knowledge pages, both PDFs and the terms page. All
  returned 200 on 2026-11-11.
- Matched each quoted line against the fetched text by script, with white space collapsed.
- Counted words on the text between the Gutenberg start and end markers.
- Measured reading level myself (method under F2).
- Read Colum Part II, chapters IV to VI, in full. Read the opening and three later parts of Werner's chapter XIV,
  the head of the Ramayana part, and the first and last pages of Baldwin's "The Cruel Tribute". Skimmed the rest
  by headings.

### Findings

**F1. The pilot passage is misnamed, and the name hides a choice.** Colum's Part II, chapters IV to VI, is not
"Cyclops through Circe". Chapter IV is the Lotus-eaters and the Cyclops (about 2,380 words). Chapter V is Aeolus
and Circe (about 1,490 words). Chapter VI is Circe's warning, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Cattle of the
Sun, the wreck and the drift to Calypso (about 1,825 words). "Cyclops through Circe" taken at its word ends with
chapter V and is about 3,870 words, not 6,000. Colum also leaves out the Laestrygonians and the land of the dead,
which Church keeps.
- Evidence: the file at https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16867.txt.utf-8 , read in full for these chapters. Firm.
- Cost: a pilot planned on "Cyclops through Circe" either stops at 3,870 words, mid-tale, or teaches three
  episodes its name never mentions. The theme work depends on chapter VI: the Cyclops's curse in IV is paid out
  in VI, when every man is lost.
- Change: keep chapters IV to VI whole. It is Odysseus's own tale from start to finish, about 5,700 words, and it
  stands alone better than the shorter cut. Name it "Odysseus tells his own tale (Colum, Part II, chapters IV to
  VI: the Lotus-eaters to the wreck)" in RIGHTS.md row 1, in the spine's text table, the reading load and "The
  pilot". See "Settled by the expert".

**F2. The level: the passage sits below the grade 6 to 8 band on the computed measure, and the text record must
say so.**
- My measure: Flesch-Kincaid grade level, 0.39 x (words per sentence) + 11.8 x (syllables per word) - 15.59.
  Words are runs of letters. Syllables are counted by vowel groups with a silent-e rule. A sentence ends at a
  full stop, question mark or exclamation mark, plus any closing quote marks, followed by a space and a capital
  letter. Semicolons and colons do not end a sentence. Picture markers were removed.

  | Text | Words | Words per sentence | Flesch-Kincaid grade |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Colum, Part II, chapters IV to VI | 5,697 | 18.0 | 5.9 |
  | chapter IV | 2,383 | 19.5 | 6.6 |
  | chapter V | 1,491 | 17.3 | 5.4 |
  | chapter VI | 1,823 | 16.6 | 5.4 |
  | Colum, whole book | 53,912 | 19.3 | 7.0 |
  | Colum, the Reading Room copy (library/colum-odysseus/text.md) | 54,228 | 20.1 | 7.3 |
  | Church, chapters X and XI | 6,412 | 22.8 | 7.7 |

- The band, from the Supplement to Appendix A, PDF page 4 (figure 1): grades 6 to 8 is 6.51 to 10.34 on
  Flesch-Kincaid; grades 4 to 5 is 4.51 to 7.73. So the passage (5.9) is under the grade 6 to 8 floor and inside
  the grades 4 to 5 band. The whole book (7.0) is low in the grade 6 to 8 band. My figures agree with the
  drafter's (6.0 and 7.1). I could not reproduce the spine's 8.7 and 23.5 words per sentence by any reading of
  either copy.
- The same Supplement, PDF page 8, says qualitative judgment may outweigh the numbers, most of all for narrative
  fiction in the later grades. So the number does not settle the placement. It must still be reported.
- The qualitative read, from the passage itself:
  - Meaning: middling. The plot is plain. The theme is implied and has more than one layer: wit saves the men,
    the boast at the ship brings the curse, the crew's greed and hunger undo them twice, and the leader comes
    home alone.
  - Structure: middling to hard. It is a tale told inside a tale, in the first person, to a king the pilot's
    students have not met. Speech sits inside speech, with quote marks up to three deep. Chapter VI gives the
    dangers twice: first as Circe's forecast, then as they happen. Order of events is straight.
  - Language: the hardest part, and the part the formula cannot see. Old forms (thee, thou, thy, hast, dost,
    wilt, art, mayst and the like) come about 90 times in 5,700 words. Short old words carry the load: guilefully,
    suppliants, aught, bane, tarried, affrighted, hawsers, mast (the pig food), doublet, forbear, bade. Word
    order is often turned ("Different it is with our lord"). Sentences are not the problem: the middle sentence
    is 17 words, nine in ten are under 30, and the longest is 48.
  - Knowledge: middling to high. The Trojan War, Agamemnon and Priam, Zeus as guardian of strangers, Poseidon,
    Hermes, Jason and the Argo, the duty of a host to a guest, and about fifteen names.
- What the text record should conclude: "Computed measure 5.9 (Flesch-Kincaid), below the grade 6 to 8 band and
  within the grade 4 to 5 band; the whole book 7.0. Placed in grade 6 on qualitative grounds: old language and
  the knowledge it assumes. It is the easiest anchor passage of the year, placed first on purpose, so that the
  first teaching of theme and summary is not blocked by the text." It must not say or hint that the passage is
  in the band by measure.
- What it means for the pilot:
  1. The pilot goes ahead on Colum. The text does not change.
  2. The pilot cannot be offered as proof that the course teaches grade-band text. It tests the teaching of
     RL.6.2, not the text level. The climb has to be shown by later anchors.
  3. Scaffolds go to the old forms, the names and the frame (who is speaking, to whom, and where), not to a
     long-sentence routine. A one-page "thee and thou" sheet and a name chart do more than sentence work here.
  4. The end-check myth must match: a computed 5.5 to 7.5, a known source, and no harder in language (F3).
  5. The spine should drop "8.7" and "23.5" for Colum, replace them with 7.0 for the book and 5.9 for the
     passage, and drop the sentence that the year "opens on some of its hardest prose". It does not.
- Cost if left: an adoption reader who runs any tool on the passage finds grade 5 or 6 and a spine that says 8.7.
  That costs trust in every other figure. A teacher plans support for long sentences that are not there.
- Confidence: firm on the numbers (two independent measures agree, and the host's own score points the same
  way). The placement is a judgment call that I settle below. Church's chapters measure in the band (7.7), but
  the hero is "Ulysses" and the language is older still; I would not switch, so nothing here goes to Cory.
