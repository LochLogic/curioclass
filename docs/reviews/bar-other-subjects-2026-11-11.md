# Grounding the adoption bar outside math, ELA and science

Search date: 2026-11-11. Purpose: find published, citable instruments and research that could ground a quality bar
for the ten subject groups where Curio Class has no rubric on file. This is a search report, not a bar. Nothing here
says Curio Class was reviewed by, meets, or is endorsed by anyone, and nothing here should be read that way.

## How this was done, and how to read it

- Every source marked **opened** was fetched live on 2026-11-11 and read: PDFs by download and text extraction
  (pypdf), Word files by unzipping, web pages as HTML. Page counts are from the extracted file.
- **Abstract only** means the publisher page with the abstract was read and the full text was not.
- **Search result only** means the item showed up in a web search with a snippet and was not opened. Nothing may
  rest on these until someone opens them.
- **From memory, not opened** is said where it applies.
- Licence notes are what the file or page itself says. "None seen" means no licence line was found in the text read.
- All summaries are in our own words. No long passages are copied.
- Not repeated here: what docs/ADOPTION-BAR.md, docs/RUBRIC-MATH-K8.md and docs/APPROACH-SOURCES.md already hold
  (EdReports math, EQuIP science, the publishers' criteria, UDL, the WWC math and reading guides, ELSF math and ELA,
  the EdReports multilingual learner guides, the WIDA 2020 framework).

One pattern to know before reading: **the published instruments outside the core subjects come from four kinds of
body.** (1) Federal health agencies (CDC). (2) A handful of states that publish criteria for nearly every subject:
Texas, Oregon, California, Oklahoma, Ohio, Massachusetts, Louisiana, Maryland, Florida. (3) Subject associations,
which mostly publish standards and principles, not materials rubrics. (4) Program registries that rate evidence
(CASEL). EdReports itself lists tools only for ELA, math, science, multilingual learner supports, pre-K and interim
assessments (its review-tools page, opened); no plan for other subjects is stated there.

### Evidence

- **Tyner and Kabourek, Social Studies Instruction and Reading Comprehension**, Thomas B. Fordham Institute, 2020.
  https://fordhaminstitute.org/sites/default/files/publication/pdfs/09242020-social-studies-instruction-and-reading-comprehension.pdf
  (opened, PDF, 45 pp). Found: in a national sample followed from kindergarten to grade 5, students with 30 more
  minutes of social studies a day scored about 15 percent of a standard deviation higher in grade 5 reading, after
  controls; extra ELA time showed no such link. Limits: correlational, teacher-reported time, a think tank report.
  It supports "social studies builds knowledge that helps reading", not any one curriculum design.
- **Educating for Civic Reasoning and Discourse**, National Academy of Education, 2021.
  https://naeducation.org/educating-for-civic-reasoning-and-discourse-report/ (landing page opened; report not read).
  A consensus volume from eight panels. No effect sizes on the page read.
- No What Works Clearinghouse practice guide for social studies was found.

### (a) Build on

The EdReports and AIR criteria (short, national, six criteria) read alongside the Louisiana IMET (a working
gateway rubric with non-negotiables). Maryland's rubric supplies indicator-level detail.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

- Standards covered in depth, in an order that is explained.
- Knowledge built on purpose: topics sequenced so facts, concepts and vocabulary pile up.
- Real sources, primary and secondary, chosen with care and from more than one point of view.
- Questions that drive each unit, and students making claims backed by evidence from the sources.
- Disciplinary skills (cause and effect, judging a source, geographic and economic reasoning) taught, not assumed.
- Reading and writing supports that keep the content at grade level.
- Assessment that checks both knowledge and the skills, with guidance on what to do next.
- Teacher background on the content.

### (c) Unsupported

- **Which content to teach.** Every instrument leaves it to the state. There is no shared national content standard,
  and the two camps (inquiry first, knowledge first) weight the same criteria differently.
- **"Taking informed action."** Required by Maryland and the EdReports and AIR list; absent or contested elsewhere.
  No evidence was found for or against it as a materials feature.
- Any effect size tying a social studies materials feature to learning.
- Economics and geography have no materials rubric of their own; they ride inside the general ones.

### (d) Confidence: likely

Several state rubrics agree on structure. The content question and the evidence are open.

### Evidence

- **Review of Evidence: Arts Education Through the Lens of ESSA**, AIR for The Wallace Foundation, November 2018.
  https://wallacefoundation.org/sites/default/files/2023-08/Arts-Education-Evidence-Report.pdf (opened, PDF, 93 pp).
  Found: 88 studies with evidence in at least one federal evidence tier; 18 reached Tier II or III; the other 70
  had only a research-based rationale (Tier IV). A meta-analysis of the 20 well-designed studies gave a moderate
  average effect, described as about 15 percentile points for an average child, varying by art form and outcome.
  Limits: few rigorous studies; "interventions" are mostly programs and arts integration, not curriculum materials;
  the report itself calls for clearer definitions and better measures.
- **Bowen and Kisida, Investigating Causal Effects of Arts Education Experiences** (Houston Arts Access Initiative),
  2019. Full paper not reached (406 and 403). Read through Education Next, opened:
  https://www.educationnext.org/new-evidence-bolsters-argument-arts-education/ . Reported: 42 schools assigned by
  lottery, grades 3 to 8; 3.6 percentage points fewer discipline infractions, writing up 0.13 standard deviations,
  compassion up 0.08. Limits: it tests more arts exposure through partnerships, not a curriculum; short-term
  outcomes; one district. (No gain on math and reading tests: from memory, not opened.)

### (a) Build on

The Texas Fine Arts rubric, with the National Core Arts Standards' four artistic processes as the content spine.
Oklahoma's rubric is the cross-check.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

- All four processes present: students make, perform or present, respond, and connect.
- Skill and technique taught step by step, with practice, not only "express yourself" prompts.
- Arts vocabulary and literacy taught and used.
- Students judging work against criteria, and giving and getting feedback.
- Historical and cultural context for the works studied.
- Performance tasks as assessment, with scoring guides.
- A year plan, pacing for the short and rotating schedules arts classes really have, and a materials list.

### (c) Unsupported

- No rubric found treats the five arts disciplines separately; dance, theater and media arts ride on general wording.
- No evidence on what makes arts **materials** effective. The research is about access to arts teaching.
- Repertoire and artwork selection (what is worth studying) has no published criteria comparable to ELA text quality.
- How a screen-based tool can teach a performing art is not addressed anywhere read.

### (d) Confidence: likely for the instrument, thin for the evidence

### Evidence

- HECAT's own base: its overview says the 15 characteristics come from reviews of effective programs plus expert
  input, and it is candid that few health curricula have rigorous trials, that listed programs are hard to run as
  tested, and that the tool estimates how **likely** a curriculum is to change behaviour by checking for the
  features of ones that did. No effect sizes are given in the parts read. One finding it states flatly:
  teaching facts alone does not change behaviour; skills practice, norms and enough time do.
- No separate meta-analysis of health curricula was opened in this search.

### (a) Build on

HECAT 2021. Use Oregon's criteria as the model of how a state turns it into an adoption check.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

- Clear behaviour goals, with every lesson tied to one.
- Facts that are accurate, current and useful for a decision, not facts for their own sake.
- Skills taught in steps: why it matters, the steps, a model, practice in real situations, feedback.
- Work on beliefs, norms and social pressure, not just information.
- Age-fit content, inclusive examples, enough time, and skills revisited across grades.
- Ways to involve family and other trusted adults.
- Teacher information, and a check by someone qualified that the health content is medically sound.

### (c) Unsupported

- HECAT scores presence of features; it is not proof a curriculum works.
- It is written for teacher-led classes. Nothing in it speaks to a stand-alone digital tool.
- Sensitive topics (sexual health, drugs, mental health) carry "acceptability" checks that are local by design.
  No instrument can settle them for a national, open site.
- The two rival sets of national standards mean "aligned to the national standards" needs a named edition.

### (d) Confidence: firm

### Evidence

- **Educating the Student Body: Taking Physical Activity and Physical Education to School**, Institute of Medicine,
  2013. https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18314/chapter/2 (summary chapter opened as HTML). Found: recommends
  150 minutes a week of PE in elementary and 225 in secondary, with at least half of class time in moderate or
  vigorous activity; fitness-focused curricula produce more activity; a PE class gives only 10 to 20 minutes of
  such activity at best. Limits it names: much evidence is cross-sectional; monitoring is weak; dose and academic
  links are not well worked out. National Academies copyright.
- **Community Preventive Services Task Force, Enhanced School-Based Physical Education.** Not reached (the site did
  not resolve). Search result only: a recommendation on strong evidence, with about 10 percentage points more class
  time in moderate or vigorous activity. Unread; nothing may rest on it.

### (a) Build on

PECAT for the method, re-keyed to the 2024 standards, with Oregon's 2025 criteria as the current state example.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

- Every national standard covered in each grade band, with content and an assessment for each.
- Motor skills in a sensible sequence across grades.
- Most of class time spent moving.
- Accurate fitness and safety content.
- Lessons that work with the space, equipment and class sizes schools have, and adaptations for students with disabilities.
- Assessment of skill and knowledge, not of dress or attendance.

### (c) Unsupported

- PECAT has not been updated to the 2024 standards; any use needs a re-mapping we would have to do ourselves.
- Almost nothing on PE **materials** as such: the evidence is about time and activity levels.
- A screen-based tool cannot deliver movement time. What a no-account website can honestly offer in PE (plans, task
  cards, knowledge content) is not addressed by any instrument.

### (d) Confidence: firm for method, likely overall (dated standards)

### Evidence

- **Cultivating Interest and Competencies in Computing**, National Academies, 2021.
  https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25912/chapter/2 (summary opened as HTML). Found: experiences that feel real
  to the learner and reflect real practice may widen who takes part; many learners need several experiences over
  time; design must fit context. The report calls the computing-specific evidence "still emerging" and leans on
  research from other STEM fields. No effect sizes in the summary.
- **Scherer, Siddiq and Sanchez Viveros, The cognitive benefits of learning computer programming: a meta-analysis
  of transfer effects**, Journal of Educational Psychology, 2019. Not opened (repository copy timed out). As
  reported in a 2021 open-access follow-up by the same authors (Frontiers in Psychology, CC BY, opened:
  https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.559424/full ): 105 studies, 539 effects; large gains in
  programming itself (g about 0.75) and medium gains on other thinking skills (g about 0.47). Limits the authors
  give: many studies were not randomized, effects shrink with strong designs and active controls, and transfer
  has to be taught on purpose. The follow-up's reference list appears to mislabel this paper; check the original.

### (a) Build on

The Ohio rubric for structure and scoring, with the K-12 Computer Science Framework (concepts and practices) and the
current CSTA standards (already in the repo as data/standards/csta-2026-ids.txt) as the content side.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

- Standards coverage across all concept areas, not coding alone (data, networks, impacts, systems).
- The practices (collaborating, testing, abstraction, communicating) built in, not bolted on.
- A sensible progression of programming ideas, with unplugged and on-screen work.
- Technology that runs on what schools have, is accessible, and protects student data.
- Assessment that includes projects and code, with rubrics.
- Supports for beginners, for students with disabilities and for English learners.
- Strong teacher support, since most teachers of the subject are not trained in it.

### (c) Unsupported

- No national materials rubric exists; all are state documents.
- The research on what makes K-12 computer science materials work is thin by the field's own account.
- Claims that coding improves general thinking rest on studies with weak designs; do not promise it.
- The CSTA standards are mid-revision; any rubric keyed to the 2017 set will age.

### Evidence

- **What We Know About the Impact of Career and Technical Education: A Systematic Review of the Research**, CTE
  Research Network (AIR), 2024. Read through AIR's announcement, opened as HTML:
  https://www.air.org/news/announcement/new-report-finds-positive-effects-career-and-technical-education-high-school
  . Found (28 causal studies): positive effects on high school achievement, completion, employability skills and
  college readiness; more two-year college enrolment and employment; no effect on four-year enrolment, degree
  completion or earnings; none on attendance or discipline. Effect sizes were not on the page. Limit: about taking
  CTE programs, not about materials.
- **Kaiser and Menkhoff, Financial Education in Schools: A Meta-Analysis of Experimental Studies**, discussion paper
  (September 2019 version; later in Economics of Education Review, 2020).
  https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/69210/1/187.pdf (opened, PDF, 41 pp; abstract and summary read). Found: 37

  NBER Working Paper 27057, 2020. https://www.nber.org/papers/w27057.pdf (opened, PDF, 40 pp; abstract read). 76
  randomized trials, over 160,000 people of all ages; positive effects on knowledge and behaviour. A working paper;
  short quotation allowed with credit.

### (a) Build on

Personal finance: the CFPB tool, re-keyed to the 2021 national standards. CTE: the Texas CTE rubric for materials,
with the ACTE framework's curriculum, instruction and assessment elements as the wider frame.

### (b) A reviewer would look for

Personal finance:
- All topic areas covered at the right grade, with decisions and skills, not only terms.
- Accurate, current content with no product or brand pushed, and the funder named.
- Usable by a teacher with no finance background.
- Honest statement of whether the curriculum itself has been studied.

CTE:
- Technical skills tied to industry standards, built in depth and in order.
- Academic and employability skills woven in.
- Career exploration and planning.
- Hands-on and applied problem solving, with safety.
- Performance assessment.

### (c) Unsupported

- CTE quality is mostly about things outside materials: equipment, employer partners, work-based learning,
  credentials. No instrument says what a materials-only CTE offer should be held to beyond the Texas rubric.
- Each CTE field has its own technical standards; there is no single content bar.
- Personal finance effects on behaviour are small. A bar should not promise changed behaviour.
- The CFPB tool's efficacy dimension needs studies of the curriculum itself, which a new curriculum will not have.

### (d) Confidence: likely for personal finance; likely to thin for CTE

## Ranked: where the grounding is strong and where it is weak

1. **Health education: strong.** A federal, purpose-built, free analysis tool (HECAT 2021) plus state criteria.
2. **Physical education: strong method, dated content.** PECAT is the same kind of tool but keyed to 2014 standards.
3. **Social and emotional learning: strong criteria and the largest evidence base**, with a catch: half the bar is
   a study of the program itself.
4. **World languages: strong instrument.** The Texas LOTE rubric is detailed and covers ASL. Evidence is thin.
5. **Social studies: several agreeing state rubrics and a national criteria list.** The content question is open.
6. **Personal finance: a real federal review tool (2015) and two meta-analyses.** Needs re-keying to 2021 standards.
7. **Computer science: state rubrics only.** The field says its own research base is thin.
8. **Arts: state rubrics (Texas, Oklahoma, California).** Evidence is about access, not materials.
9. **Career and technical education: one materials rubric (Texas).** Quality is mostly outside materials.
10. **English language development as its own course: thin.** Supports inside content courses are already grounded.
11. **Library and information literacy: thin.** No materials instrument; one good trial.

Three things that cut across subjects:

- **A subject-neutral core exists.** Texas reuses the same three opening parts (design at course, unit and lesson
  level; progress monitoring; supports for all learners) in fine arts and CTE. Massachusetts reuses one "classroom
  application" domain in every rubric. California reuses five categories in arts, health and world languages. A
  Curio Class bar could have one shared core plus a short subject part, and cite these as the pattern.
- **Standards moved in 2024 to 2026.** PE (2024), health (2024), the can-do statements (2026), school library
  standards (second edition) and the CSTA standards are all newer than the tools keyed to them.
- **Almost no evidence is about materials.** Outside SEL and personal finance, the research found is about time,
  access or whole programs.
