## What I studied

The community's own voice first, then federal primary texts and agency pages (free sources only, as Cory settled
2026-11-15), then a Native journalists' guide.

1. **Since Time Immemorial (tribally developed curriculum, endorsed by all 29 federally recognized tribes in
   Washington), S:\CurioClass-sources\since-time-immemorial\**, high school US history units 1 to 6 level 1 and unit
   2 level 2, and licence\ospi-sti-main.html. Taught me the frame the nations' own curriculum asks for: tribes as
   sovereign governments in the present tense, a place-based approach ("tribes in their own communities"), land
   policy read through treaties, reservations and cessions, and purpose separated from result ("Differentiate between
   the purpose of Allotment and the results"). It reads Native authors in their own words (Robert J. Lyttle,
   Cheyenne/Arapaho, on sovereignty) rather than only federal texts.
2. **Rosebud Sioux Tribe, History and Culture**, https://www.rosebudsiouxtribe-nsn.gov/history-culture. The nation
   names itself Sicangu Lakota Oyate, of the Oceti Sakowin; it uses "Sioux" in its legal name and "Lakota" for the
   people. It says an 1860s treaty "gave away Lakota lands along the Republican and Platte Rivers" and that the 1868
   treaty "was not honored by the United States". Taught me that the Platte country is part of Lakota history, and
   that the nation tells treaty history as broken promises, not as a closed transaction.
3. **Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes (Oklahoma), official site**, https://www.cheyenneandarapaho-nsn.gov/. Names itself
   "Tsistsistas | Hinono'ei", and speaks of ancestors who "sacrificed, prayed and fought for our existence today".
   Taught me the nations' own names and that the government today is one tribe of two peoples.
4. **Eastern Shoshone Tribe, About and treaty pages**, https://easternshoshone.org/about/ and
   https://easternshoshone.org/fort-bridger-treaty-of-1868/. The tribe has lived in its homelands "since time
   immemorial"; the Fort Bridger treaty of 1863 covered about 44 million acres; the transcontinental railroad led
   the United States to make a new treaty and a reservation of 4.4 million acres, later cut to 2.2 million. The
   newsletter's name, Newe' News, carries the people's own name. Taught me that a nation tells the railroad as the
   direct cause of its land loss, and names its leader (Washakie) as a treaty-maker.
5. **Northern Arapaho Tribe, official site**, https://northernarapaho.com/. Today on the 2.2 million acre Wind
   River Reservation with the Eastern Shoshone. Confirms the Arapaho are two present-day governments (Oklahoma and
   Wyoming), so "the Arapaho" in 1862 maps to more than one nation now.
6. **Treaty of Ruby Valley, October 1, 1863, United States and the Western Bands of the Shoshonee Nation**, 18 Stat.
   689 to 692 (scan read in full); National Archives, Ratified Indian Treaty 326, catalog id 178907665,
   https://catalog.archives.gov/id/178907665. Article III: "it being understood that provision has been made by the
   government of the United States for the construction of a railway from the plains west to the Pacific ocean, it
   is stipulated by said bands that the said railway or its branches may be located, constructed, and operated, and
   without molestation from them, through any portion of country claimed or occupied by them." Signed by Te-moak and
   other leaders. No article cedes land. Taught me that the federal government itself had to negotiate with a named
   nation, one year after the 1862 act, to run the line.
7. **Treaty of Fort Laramie, 1851**, University of Nebraska treaties portal,
   https://treatiesportal.unl.edu/treatyoffortlaramie1851/treaty.tfl.laramie73.html (Kappler, vol. 2, pp. 594 to
   596). Parties: "the Sioux or Dahcotahs, Cheyennes, Arrapahoes, Crows, Assinaboines, Gros-Ventre Mandans, and
   Arrickaras". Article 5 recognizes "The territory of the Cheyennes and Arrapahoes" between the North Platte and the
   Arkansas, east of the Rocky Mountains, its east edge at the forks of the Platte. Article 2 recognizes United States
   roads and posts "within their respective territories". Taught me which nations the line west from the 100th
   meridian had to cross under the United States' own recognition.
8. **Treaty of Fort Laramie, 1868**, National Archives milestone document,
   https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty. Article XI: the bands of the Sioux Nation and the
   Arapaho "will withdraw all opposition to the construction of the railroads now being built on the Plains".
   Confirms Lakota and Arapaho opposition to the line, in a federal text.
9. **Pacific Railway Act, 1862**, National Archives transcript,
   https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/pacific-railway-act. Section 1: the line runs "from a point on the one
   hundredth meridian ... in the Territory of Nebraska, to the western boundary of Nevada Territory"; section 2 (the
   "Indian titles" sentence, checked to its end); section 3: "five alternate sections per mile on each side". The
   page itself says the companies met "American Indians, whose ancestral lands were transected by the railroads".
10. **National Park Service, Homestead National Historical Park, "Native Americans and the Homestead Act"**,
    https://www.nps.gov/home/learn/historyculture/native-americans-and-the-homestead-act.htm. "To settlers,
    immigrants, and homesteaders, the West was empty land. To Native Americans, it was home." Treaties and laws had
    already pushed many nations onto reservations before 1862; responses "were as complex and varied as the Native
    Americans themselves". Taught me the federal park's own wording of error K, and that Homestead land was land
    already ceded, unlike the railway grants.
11. **Library of Congress, Royce, Indian Land Cessions in the United States (1899)**, https://www.loc.gov/item/13023487/
    (the page refused an automated read; cited for its state cession maps, public domain). The checkable map for
    a teacher who wants to see which cessions the Homestead land in Kansas, Nebraska and Minnesota came from.
12. **National Park Service, Appomattox Court House, Ely S. Parker**, https://www.nps.gov/apco/parker.htm (via search
    excerpts). Parker, Seneca, Grant's military secretary, made the formal ink copy of the surrender terms. Taught me
    a Native person already inside this section's own story (Grant), as an actor.
13. **Native American Journalists Association (now the Indigenous Journalists Association), Tribal Nations Media
    Guide (2020)**, https://indigenousjournalists.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/2020-NAJA-Tribal-Nations-Media-Guide-1.pdf.
    "No two tribal nations are the same"; ask "How do tribal members refer to themselves"; for treaties ask "Have they
    been adhered to?" and "How do they continue to impact the tribe?". Taught me: name the specific nation, use its
    own name where it uses one, and treat treaties as live agreements between governments. (The association's older
    terminology guide is no longer online; I could not read it.)

**What the sources ask of outsiders, and where they differ.** Name the nation, not "Indians" in general; use the
nation's own name where it uses one, while accepting that several nations keep "Sioux", "Indian" or "Tribe" in their
legal names; keep "Indian" for quotation and law; speak of nations as present-day governments; read treaties as
agreements between nations whose terms were often broken; avoid the vanishing frame (Native people only in the past,
only as loss). Members differ on names (Sioux, Lakota, Oceti Sakowin; Shoshone, Newe) and on whether old treaties
ceded land at all (the Ruby Valley treaty has no cession article). Common errors: "empty land", one undifferentiated
"Plains Indians", land loss told as natural or finished, Native people shown only as obstacles or victims.

## Verdict

Pass with fixes. The day names the error K danger and quotes section 2 well, but it asks students to name nations no
student source names, names a pair (Lakota and Cheyenne) that is not the best-supported one, and says the land was
already "taken" in 1862 when the act only promised to take it; Native people appear only as land that was lost.

### 2. The named nations and the tense are wrong in the key, the opener and Words and supports. Origin: day (d079.md), section (opener.md), items.csv. Severity: major.

- **Where.** items.csv s05-X79 samples: "The Pacific Railway Act gave railroads federal land and promised to
  extinguish Indian titles, so Washington took and handed out Lakota and Cheyenne land." opener.md Native land: "gave
  out land the United States took from Native nations, among them the Lakota and Cheyenne." d079.md Words and
  supports: "the Lakota and Cheyenne held land along the railroad's route."
- **Why it matters.** In July 1862 the railway grants were a promise: section 2 says the United States "shall
  extinguish" the titles, future tense, because the nations still held them. Saying the land was already "taken"
  repeats the "settled, finished" frame the sources argue against, and a student reading Source E closely would be
  marked against the text. The best-supported pair along the line is the Cheyenne and Arapaho (1851 treaty, article 5)
  and the Shoshone (1863 treaties); the 1851 treaty put Lakota territory north of the North Platte, so "the Lakota
  held land along the route" is arguable and not checkable from any page the class has.
- **Fix.**
  - items.csv s05-X79, `answer`: "... the Pacific Railway Act shown granting land that Native nations still held,
    with the United States' promise to extinguish their titles (or the Homestead Act shown granting land already ceded
    by Native nations), and at least one nation named from Source H."
  - items.csv s05-X79, first sample, last sentence: "The Pacific Railway Act gave the railroad companies land and a
    right of way and promised to extinguish the Indian titles, so the federal government planned to take land that
    the Cheyenne, Arapaho and Shoshone still held, as Source H and its gloss show."
  - items.csv s05-X79, `steps`, last look-for: "the land named as a named nation's land (Source H), not empty land".
  - opener.md, Native land: "**Native land.** The Pacific Railway Act granted land and a right of way across land
    Native nations still held, among them the Cheyenne and Arapaho on the Platte and the Shoshone farther west, and
    promised in section 2 that the United States would extinguish 'the Indian titles'. The Homestead Act gave out land
    the United States had already taken by treaty cession, often under pressure, or by force. The Lakota and Arapaho
    fought the line; in the 1868 Fort Laramie treaty they agreed to withdraw opposition to it. Read section 2 and
    Source H with students."
  - d079.md Words and supports, the second sentence: "Name nations, not 'Indians' in general: Source H names the
    Western Shoshone, and its gloss the Cheyenne and Arapaho, whose land the line crossed. Say 'Indian' only when
    reading the law's words."

### 4. The "extinguish" gloss reads as if the law itself ended ownership. Origin: section (sources-79.md, Source E). Severity: moderate.

- **Where.** "Gloss: *extinguish the Indian titles*, end Native nations' legal ownership of the land".
- **Why it matters.** A gloss in the past-completed sense hides who would do the ending and how, which is the point of
  the sentence; and "legal ownership" can suggest Native nations' claim to the land existed only in United States law.
- **Fix.** "Gloss: *extinguish the Indian titles*, end the Native nations' ownership of the land, which United States
  law recognized, by treaty purchase or by force. The law is a promise to the railroad that the government will do
  this; it does not say how; *right of way*, the strip the railroad runs on."

### 5. Native people appear only as land that was lost. Origin: section (u05-2.md section 1, "what the teacher page must explain"). Severity: moderate.

- **Where.** Across the five days the only Native presence is "Indian titles" and the error K line; no Native person
  acts, speaks or serves.
- **Why it matters.** The brief asks that people be shown with their own agency, not only as a loss or a lesson for
  others. The nations' own pages tell the railroad years through named leaders and treaty-making (Washakie, Te-moak,

- **Fix.** (a) Source H already does part of this: read its source line with the leader named, and say in the

  Service, Appomattox Court House)." Record the NPS page in sources.csv as a teacher-only row. (c) In u05-2.md
  "what the teacher page must explain", change the last item to: "That the Pacific Railway Act promised to take land
  that named Native nations still held, and that those nations negotiated, resisted and govern themselves today."

### 6. Words and terms. Origin: template (house wording) and day. Severity: minor.

- **Where.** "Indian titles" is used unquoted in the section plan's error table ("the Railway Act promised to
  extinguish Indian titles") and the X79 sample; errors.csv K example ends "so the land was Native nations' first".
- **Fix.** Put "Indian titles" in quotation marks wherever it is the law's phrase, and use "Native nations' titles"
  in our own voice. In errors.csv K example, replace "so the land was Native nations' first" with "so the land

## Could not settle

- **The 1861 Fort Wise treaty.** Some Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders signed away most of the 1851 territory in
  1861; many Cheyenne and Arapaho did not accept it, and Sand Creek (1864) followed. The facts line above says only
  what the 1851 treaty recognized, which is safe either way. Whether to name Fort Wise in the teacher page needs a
  Cheyenne and Arapaho source I could not reach (the Tribes' site has no history page); to the lead.
- **Western Shoshone land today.** The Ruby Valley treaty has no cession article, and whether Western Shoshone land
  was ever lawfully taken is still argued; I found no Western Shoshone government page to quote. The fix keeps to what
  the treaty text shows.
- **Royce map numbers.** The Library of Congress page refused an automated read; the map plate for the Platte route
  is not recorded. Not needed for the fixes above.
