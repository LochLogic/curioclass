## Verdict

The day is well aimed: the Lost Cause is tested, never balanced; the worked example pairs a postwar claim with a
founder's 1861 statement; the sourcing language (who, when, purpose, what a source cannot show) is right for grade
11, and the source F figures are right. It is not teachable yet, and a busy teacher would struggle with the printed
pieces more than with the history: the gates pass a day with a placeholder source, the teacher page prints whole
stems and answers in mid-sentence, and the exit's unseen sources sit on the sheet students hold all lesson. The one
change that matters most is a sharper exit source pair (Source E with the clause that binds the states, and Source F
with secession dates), because the exit is the only individual evidence on the day's goal.

### 3. Source F cannot answer the question the key expects without secession dates, and its columns mislead

- **What.** The key's model question is "did the states with the most enslaved people leave first?" The table groups
  states before and after Fort Sumter but lists them by share, not by date, and gives no dates, so a student reads the
  list order as the order of leaving. "Stayed in the Union" lists only the four slave states, which a student can read
  as "all the states that stayed". Virginia's 31% includes the western counties that broke away and joined the Union
  as West Virginia in 1863.
- **Why it matters.** The exit is the day's only individual evidence, and its look-for "Source F actually used"
  depends on the table being readable for the question. Texas (30%, left first) is below North Carolina (33%) and
  Virginia (31%, left after); students need the grouping to be honest to handle that exception well.
- **Fix.** sources-82.md Source F: add a date column in each group (South Carolina Dec. 20, 1860; Mississippi Jan. 9,
  1861; Florida Jan. 10; Alabama Jan. 11; Georgia Jan. 19; Louisiana Jan. 26; Texas Feb. 1; Fort Sumter April 12 to
  14; Virginia April 17; Arkansas May 6; Tennessee May 7; North Carolina May 20), order each group by date; rename the
  third column "Slave states that stayed in the Union" and add one line under the table: "The free states, which also
  stayed, had almost no enslaved people. Virginia's figure includes the counties that became West Virginia in 1863."
  Every share I checked from memory matches the 1860 census to the nearest percent (Arkansas 25.5 and Kentucky 19.5
  round up, so check those two first against the volume). Confidence: firm on the dates and shares (from memory,
  consistent with the cheap and sensitivity reads), likely on the misreading.
- LAYER day. CATCH expert. REPEAT new. CHEAP missed. **ORIGIN section.**

### 8. The Stephens source line hides a sourcing fact the day needs

- **What.** Source B is the newspaper reporter's version reprinted by Cleveland in 1866. After the war Stephens
  himself said the report was imperfect and that he had not revised it, and in his postwar writing he argued the war
  was about states' rights.
- **Why it matters.** On a day about memory-making this is the best single example: a founder recasting his own 1861
  words after defeat. Leaving it out also lets a sharp student find it later and distrust the lesson. It does not
  weaken the source: the speech was widely reported in 1861 and matches the Confederate constitution.
- **Fix.** sources.csv 82B note and the sheet's source line: "From a reporter's notes, reprinted in 1866. After the
  war Stephens said the report was not exact; he did not deny the cornerstone passage's meaning at the time." One
  K82-1 look-for gains "(Stephens's own later account is itself memory-making)" as an extension in Shorter or longer.
  Confidence: likely (from memory: his 1865 prison diary and A Constitutional View of the Late War Between the States,
  1868 to 1870); check before printing.
- LAYER day. CATCH expert. REPEAT new. CHEAP missed. **ORIGIN unit.**

### 9. A teacher with no prep gets two sentences on what the Lost Cause was

- **What.** The day's whole goal is the Lost Cause, but the teacher's background is one line in the opener ("Historians
  call the later story the Lost Cause") and one in the worked example. No date, no names, no how it spread.
- **Why it matters.** A student will ask "who said this, and where did I hear it?" The teacher needs three facts:
  the name comes from Edward Pollard's The Lost Cause (1866); veterans' groups and the United Daughters of the

  1910s, as Black Southerners lost the vote (the AHA statement the sensitivity read cites). This is factual background,
  separate from the sensitivity read's finding 1 on Black scholarship, and both fit in one opener bullet.
- **Fix.** Opener "What the teacher needs to know", replace the last bullet with about 70 words carrying those facts
  (recorded as background rows in sources.csv), and the worked example's first sentence points to it.
