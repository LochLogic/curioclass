# Social studies: which content standard the 14 courses teach

Written 2026-11-12 for NEXT-SPINES item 5. For Cory's decision. Revised once after the expert read
(docs/reviews/social-studies-expert-read-2026-11-12.md). Research only: nothing else in the repo was changed. Web
sources are listed at the end; repo facts cite the file.

## The short version

- **The real choice is a course-list choice.** Our course order is California's almost exactly. Path 1: anchor to
  Massachusetts and take its course order (its terms clearly allow our use; about 8 of our 14 course outlines are
  redrawn, and since no social studies scope and sequence is written yet, that costs topic lists and retagging, not
  finished work). Path 2: keep our order and anchor to California (or Tennessee's 2024 set), which needs a
  permission request or confirmed terms if we go beyond codes, our own paraphrase and a link.
- **Every one-state anchor needs a plan for state-history grades** (see "State and local study"): a national site
  cannot teach every child Massachusetts or California history.
- Today Curio Class has only C3 loaded. C3 names inquiry skills (questions, sources, claims) and broad discipline
  ideas, not what happened in 1787 or who Hammurabi was. A content standard is the missing half, and FOUNDATION's
  end goal ("would stand up to a state adoption review") needs it: rubric row C1 asks that every standard of a
  course be named, loaded and taught.
- Three options were asked for, plus a fourth worth naming. All four keep C3 for skills. They differ in where the
  content list comes from.
- The hardest fact for every option: a state reviewer checks materials against **their own state's** standards.
  No option passes every state without a crosswalk. Since formal adoption is not being pursued (FOUNDATION 4), the
  real test is "would a careful reviewer from a strong state find the content complete and sound".
- Strong and freely usable is a short list. The states rated best (Fordham 2021) are Alabama, California,
  Massachusetts, Tennessee and the District of Columbia. Of these, only Massachusetts was confirmed to grant free
  copying for non-commercial educational use with credit. California requires written permission to reproduce any
  part (a fee for full reprints). Texas restricts use outside Texas. The others were not confirmed either way.

Three cautions on this table:

1. **Ratings are of older editions.** Alabama, the District of Columbia and Virginia have replaced the sets Fordham
   graded; Tennessee has adopted a replacement. Oklahoma (B+ / B+ in 2021) wrote a new set in 2025 that its supreme
   court voided in December 2025, so the 2019 set is back, and a new draft without the contested lines was under
   way in 2026. State sets move.
2. **Fordham is one reviewer with a point of view,** and it has been criticised (for one, by the National
   Association of Scholars). It covers only civics and US history. Its world history review is from 2006
   (A grades then: California, Georgia, Indiana, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, South Carolina, Virginia).
   No well-known review covers economics, geography or psychology standards.
3. **Licence matters less than it looks,** because Curio Class does not need to reprint a standards document. It
   needs the codes, a short paraphrase in our own words and a link (the C3 file is ids only today). Whether that
   use needs permission was not settled here and is not legal advice. So licence decides between equally good
   anchors, not between a better and a worse fit. Massachusetts is the only one where the question does not arise.

National subject standards fill the gaps every option has: economics (Council for Economic Education, Voluntary
National Content Standards, 2nd ed., 2010, copyright CEE), personal finance (NSPFE 2021, already cited on 3
topics), geography (NCGE's Geography for Life, 2nd ed., 2012, edition to confirm) and psychology (APA National
Standards for High School Psychology Curricula, 2022 revision, edition to confirm).

Two more reference points for any option, read and linked only:
- **The college-level course frameworks** for high school US history, world history, US government, economics and
  psychology. They are the working high watermark for high school content in most districts. A check for grades 9
  to 12, never named in anything a student, teacher or family reads.
- **The Educating for American Democracy Roadmap (2021),** a federally funded national history and civics guide
  built by about 300 scholars. Not standards, but the nearest national content reference for (b) or (d). Terms
  not checked.

### Notes on each option

**(a) One state's set.** Massachusetts is the only strong set whose terms we have read and that clearly allows our
use. Its cost is the course order at most grades (see "What it means for each course"): for example our grade 7
(medieval to early modern) is its high school World History I, and our 1877 split of US history does not match its
World War I split. Either our courses follow its order, or its standards are taught in other grades (high school
codes in grade 7), which breaks the trace. California and Tennessee fit our course list almost as is, but
California's licence needs a request, and Tennessee is between two editions until 2027-28.

**(b) Unified set.** It answers Cory's wish for something better than one state, and it lets us avoid one state's
weak spots. The cost is real and recurring: we become a standards writer, and standards writing is a public,
contested job in every state. Without a named outside reviewer the result is our own opinion with codes. A school
in any one state still needs a crosswalk to its own standards, so the work of (a) is done anyway, four times.

**(c) High watermark.** "More demanding" has three meanings for content, and they pull apart: earlier grade (a
topic taught in grade 4 rather than 5), more depth (the Constitution's articles versus the whole document), and
more topics (the union of all named people and events). Of the three, only depth can be ranked. Earlier grade is
a sequencing choice the anchor settles (and can suit the age less); more topics only adds. A pure
union of 4 strong states would overfill every year, so it needs a cap tied to C1 and C2: the year's teaching days
are fixed first, and an addition must displace something weaker. With that cap, (c) becomes a judgment per topic,
which is close to (d).

**(d) Hybrid.** This is the high watermark applied as a check, not as the source. The code set stays one
published, traceable state; strong states are used to find what it leaves out or teaches thinly. It is named
because it keeps most of what Cory wants from (b) and (c) (no single state's gaps) at close to the cost of (a),
once the anchor's course order is taken (under Massachusetts, about 8 of 14 course outlines change). Its
weak point is the same as (c): someone must judge "essential", so it needs the expert read the brief already
plans.

## State and local study

Most states give a year (usually grade 4, sometimes 3 or 7) to their own state's history and government. Our grade
4 has State History and State Government. Massachusetts grade 3 names Massachusetts people and places; California
grade 4 is California. Any one-state anchor, taken literally, gives every other state's child a study of that
state, and a teacher in Ohio cannot use it as written.

Proposed for every option: the state study is taught as a method ("your state": its founding, government, peoples,
land and economy), with the anchor's codes for the skills and structure, worked examples drawn from several
states, and a link to each state's own standards for the content. This is a scope question for Cory, because it
changes what grade 3 or 4 teaches.

## The values question

Cory's lean: where state instruments conflict, "follow the higher quality or rigor". The rule works where two
instruments ask for more or less of the same thing. It stops where they ask for opposite things. Three real cases:

| Case | What the states differ on | What the rule does | Does it work? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Florida's 2023 African American history standards** | A Florida benchmark note says enslaved people built skills that in some cases could be used for their personal benefit. The American Historical Association and NCSS criticised the strand's framing as inaccurate; the National Association of Scholars endorsed it. Other states frame slavery without that note | Rigor in history means accuracy and full context. Curio Class teaches the skills enslaved people had and the brutality of the system that held them, plainly, which promise 7 already asks. It can also take Florida's strength: a whole African American history strand is more content, not less | **Mostly.** Accuracy can be ranked, but scholarly bodies split here, so "which is more accurate" needs our own named historian, not a vote of organisations |
| **Oklahoma's 2025 standards** | They asked students to identify "discrepancies" in the 2020 election results and named a contested account of COVID-19's origin. Voided by the state supreme court in December 2025 on open-meetings grounds; the 2019 set is back | A claim the discipline's evidence does not support is not "more rigorous". Curio Class teaches how elections are run, counted and challenged, and how to check a claim against sources, which is the rigorous version | **Yes.** Factual claims can be tested. The case also shows why a single-state anchor is risky: state sets change through courts and boards in months |
| **LGBTQ history** | California (2011), New Jersey, Illinois (2019), Washington (2024) and a few others require it in social studies. Florida restricts formal classroom instruction on sexual orientation and gender identity (a 2024 settlement clarified that discussing LGBTQ people is not barred), and several other states have similar limits | Neither side is more rigorous. One requires inclusion, the other restricts it. Taking "more content" here is not ranking rigor, it is choosing a side. The conflict is narrower than it looks: naming a person's role where it was historically significant (step 3 below) likely sits inside both | **No.** The rule cannot rank a requirement against a restriction |

### Where the rule stops, and what governs past it

Things the rule can rank: accuracy against evidence, depth, use of real sources, more than one account of a
contested event, specific over vague. Things it cannot rank: which groups get attention beyond historical
significance, the moral framing of the nation's story (pride or critique), and whether a topic suits an age.

Many values mandates are additive and compatible (Holocaust education, Asian American history, Native nations'
history, the history of communism). A union of these is a content question, settled by the C1 and C2 cap like any
other addition. The true conflicts are a requirement in one state against a prohibition in another.

For those, a proposed order, for Cory to accept or change:

1. **The discipline's evidence first.** If a claim fails the evidence, it is out, whichever state wrote it.
2. **FOUNDATION promise 7** ("History and science are not softened; they are told with care for the people
   involved"): hard facts are told plainly, with care, at the grade's level, and a fresh agent reads sensitive
   content before it goes live.
3. **Rubric row C12** (many voices and places, no one group carries every example): people who were part of an
   event appear in it where they were historically significant, for example court cases a government course
   already teaches.
4. **Acceptability is local** (ADOPTION-BAR-SUBJECTS): each course page says which units a school may leave out
   without breaking the course, as health already does.

Put simply: "higher rigor" where rigor can be ranked; promise 7 and C12 where it cannot; the local leave-out note
for what a community decides. Curio Class keeps not claiming to satisfy any state's values criteria.

## If we had to choose (recommendation, clearly marked)

First, the fork, because the anchor choice is a course-list choice:

- **Path 1: Massachusetts anchor, Massachusetts course order.** Terms are certain. About 8 course outlines are
  redrawn; no social studies scope and sequence exists yet, so the cost is topic lists and retagging, not finished
  work.
- **Path 2: California anchor (or Tennessee 2024), our course order kept.** Almost no course changes. Needs a
  written permission request to the California department (or Tennessee's terms confirmed) if we go beyond codes,
  our paraphrase and a link.

Recommended: option (d) on Path 1. C3 for skills; Massachusetts as the content anchor (strong in the main
independent review, the newest of the exemplary sets that is in force and unrevised, a coherent K-12 design
reviewers know, its grade 8 civics year is what Fordham asks states to add, and its terms clearly allow our use);
checked unit by unit against a named few (Tennessee, Alabama 2024, DC 2023, California, New York, the college-level
frameworks for 9 to 12, the Roadmap), with every addition recorded and kept inside the year's days. Economics adds
CEE, psychology loads the current APA set, geography names NCGE as reference. State study is taught as "your state"
(above). A reviewer can trace it. This is a judgment call; Path 2 is fully defensible if Cory values the current
course list.

What would change this: (1) if Cory wants our course order kept, Path 2, with California if it grants permission;
(2) if Tennessee's terms turn out free and its 2024 set reads well, it fits our course list best, including
psychology; (3) if a named historian or civics expert will sign a merged set, option (b) becomes credible; (4) if
the unit-level check finds Massachusetts thin in a course, that course takes a different anchor.
