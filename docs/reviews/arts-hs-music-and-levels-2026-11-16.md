# Arts, high school: the music strand and the level structure

Research for Cory's two answers of 2026-11-16 (Desk card q-later-arts-health-pe):

1. High school music is composition and theory, but music class is often built around instruments, so this may be
   a stub course; research the best way forward.
2. High school arts levels are tied to what a student has done (Proficient, Accomplished, Advanced), with an
   optional fourth year; use pedagogically backed data where it exists, and the best available version where it
   does not.

Every claim carries a tag. **SOURCE**: read at the source named (or "search only" where only a search summary was
seen, because the page refused the fetch). **DERIVED**: our reasoning from sources. **ASSUMED**: our judgment with
no source behind it. Research notes in our own words, not legal advice. Short quotes only.

## Part 1. High school music without a band or orchestra

### 1.1 What the national standards offer (the four strands)

- The 2014 music standards give high school four strands that mirror the kinds of music courses secondary schools
  run: traditional and emerging ensembles, harmonizing instruments (guitar, keyboard and the like), composition
  and theory, and music technology. **SOURCE** (coalition introduction pages, nationalartsstandards.org nodes 779
  and 780.)
- Only the ensemble and harmonizing instrument strands have the two preparatory levels (Novice, nominally grade 5;
  Intermediate, nominally grade 8), because students often start those classes before high school. Composition
  and theory and technology start at Proficient. **SOURCE** (NAfME, Appendix A descriptors, read in full.)
- Composition and theory: 21 performance standards per level, 63 in all, ids of the form `MU:Cr1.1.C.Ia`, across
  all eleven anchors, so all four artistic processes. **SOURCE** (the strand's "at a glance" sheet, read in full;
  ids counted from it.)
- Composition and theory does not require an instrument. The presenting standard lets a student share music
  "through notation, performance, or technology"; the performing standard asks for live or recorded performances
  of the student's own and others' works; the responding standards ask for listening and score analysis. Nothing
  names an instrument. **SOURCE** (same sheet, standards Cr3.2 and Pr6.1.)
- Music technology: three levels; its creating and performing standards are written around digital tools from
  Proficient up (generate ideas using digital tools, present using digital tools). **SOURCE** (the strand's sheet,
  read.)
- Ensembles and harmonizing instruments assume an instrument per student, rehearsal with others and a teacher who
  hears and corrects technique in the room. **DERIVED** (from the strand names and the preparatory-level
  rationale.)

### 1.2 How many students the instrument model reaches

- A large share of US teenagers never enrol in a school ensemble; Williams (2011, Music Educators Journal) named
  this the "elephant in the room" and argued for courses for the students ensembles do not reach. **SOURCE**
  (search only: abstract and citations.)
- Elpus and Abril built national profiles of ensemble students (2011, 2019, Journal of Research in Music
  Education): ensemble students are more advantaged than their peers; the 2024 paper models persistence across
  years. A transcript study puts students who take any music course in high school at roughly a third. **SOURCE**
  (search only: abstracts; the percentages were not read in full text.)
- About 14 percent of responding US high schools offered a technology-based music class (Dammers 2012, Bulletin of
  the Council for Research in Music Education); more than half of those without one thought it feasible.
  **SOURCE** (search only: abstract.)
- Teachers and researchers report growth when schools add composing, production and popular-music classes beside
  ensembles; most of this is case study, not controlled comparison. **SOURCE** (search only: Colquhoun 2019
  dissertation, Bell 2015 dissertation, practitioner case studies.)
- Composition taught to high school students with no composing background produced usable pieces and reported
  gains in understanding and interest (Menard 2015, Journal of Research in Music Education, a band sample; the
  author names general music as untested). **SOURCE** (search only: abstract.)
- No study compares a composition course built on free tools with an instrument course on outcomes. **SOURCE**
  (none found in three searches.)

### 1.3 What online and other non-ensemble programs do

- A large state virtual school runs a one-credit high school music course that satisfies the fine arts graduation
  requirement, studies world traditions and fundamentals (pitch, rhythm, harmony, form), and asks for an
  instrument that may be "virtual", a keyboard app, plus a recording device. **SOURCE** (course page, read.)
- The national advanced theory course expects students to read and write notation and to have basic performance
  skill on voice or an instrument; sight-singing is a course skill. Voice is enough. **SOURCE** (College Board
  student page, read; exam sight-singing from search only.)
- The international diploma music course asks students to work as researcher, creator and performer, with
  experimentation and finished works assessed through submitted recordings and writing. **SOURCE** (search only:
  the subject brief refused the fetch.)
- Free tools that run on any device exist for every part of the strand: browser notation editors with free tiers,
  a free open-source desktop notation program, free browser sequencers and audio workstations (several need an
  account to save), and keyboard apps. **SOURCE** (search only: vendor and teacher pages.) Accounts for minors are
  a privacy cost. **ASSUMED**
- Curio Class can build its own step sequencer and staff tool that keeps work on the student's device, which the
  approach page already permits (rhythm reading, note names). **DERIVED**

### 1.4 The options weighed

| Option | Claims which ids | Needs | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Composition and theory, full course | `.C` strand, all four processes | Voice, body and found percussion, a keyboard app or our own tool | Meets the bar's four processes; no instrument; serves the students ensembles miss. **DERIVED** |
| B. General music or music appreciation | No high school strand exists for it; would borrow responding ids | Listening only | Weak on creating and performing (bar criterion 1); its content fits inside A's responding units. **DERIVED** |
| C. Music technology | `.T` strand | A device with audio, a production tool, often an account | Viable, but every standard assumes digital production; heavier device and account needs. A good later sibling. **DERIVED** |
| D. Ensembles or harmonizing instruments | `.E` or `.H` strands | Instruments, a group, a teacher who hears technique in the room | A curriculum page cannot deliver this at the bar; it belongs to the local program. **DERIVED** |
| E. Stub course only | None | Nothing | Leaves most students with no high school music at all. **DERIVED** |

### 1.5 Recommendation (music)

**Build music 9 to 12 as a full composition and theory course (option A), not a stub. Add a short ensemble
companion page inside it for students who also play or sing in an ensemble. Do not claim ensemble, harmonizing
or technology ids.** **DERIVED**

Reasons:

1. The strand is written so that work can be shared by notation, performance or technology, and performed live
   or recorded; no instrument is required (1.1). **SOURCE**
2. It starts at Proficient, so it follows our grade 8 general music year with no gap (1.1). **SOURCE**
3. It reaches the large group that ensembles do not (1.2), which matches a free site's audience. **DERIVED**
4. Online programs already run credit-bearing high school music on a keyboard app and a recorder (1.3).
   **SOURCE**
5. It covers what an appreciation course would, through its responding standards. **DERIVED**
6. It prepares for the national theory exam, which asks only for voice or an instrument. **DERIVED**

How it is taught without instruments **(DERIVED)**: the voice (singing, solfege, sight-singing), body and found
percussion, a free keyboard app or a Curio Class tool on any device, and the student's own instrument whenever
they have one. Performing standards are met by live or recorded performance by the student or a small group, and
by the tool playing back the score where the standard is about presenting the composition. A level with no
keyboard of any kind still works: the tool gives pitch playback.

What the ensemble companion contains (the "stub", if Cory wants one named) **(DERIVED)**:

- How to find an ensemble near you: school, community and youth bands, choirs and orchestras, places of worship,
  community music schools, and informal groups. No company names.
- A practice log and a weekly self-check against a few look-fors.
- "Write for your section": how a composition and theory assignment can be written for the student's own
  instrument or ensemble, so the two feed each other.
- A plain note that ensemble and instrument standards are met, and assessed, by that ensemble and its teacher;
  Curio Class claims none of them.
- It is a page in the music 9 to 12 course, with no days and no ids, so it is not a counted course. **ASSUMED**

Loading note: the strand's 63 ids would be loaded by hand, ids only. Our standards checker
(scripts/standards.py) accepts `MU:Cr1.1.Ia` but not the strand letter in `MU:Cr1.1.C.Ia`; the pattern needs a
`.C` segment before loading. **SOURCE** (read the pattern; not changed here.)

## Part 2. Sequencing high school arts by level

### 2.1 What the national descriptors say

- Proficient: what most students reach after one high school arts course (one credit, about 120 hours) built on
  good pre-kindergarten to grade 8 learning. **SOURCE** (Appendix A, read.)
- Accomplished: what most students reach after "a rigorous sequence" of further courses beyond Proficient; they
  solve problems with little help and can keep making art after school. **SOURCE**
- Advanced: well beyond Accomplished, at honors or college depth, self-directed, able to lead. **SOURCE**
- The descriptors are achievement levels, not years. They do not say how many courses each takes. **SOURCE**
- A College Board review of college-level arts learning and the advanced placement arts courses informed the
  coalition's work, which is where the honors or college anchor for Advanced comes from. **SOURCE** (coalition
  research page, read; the report itself not read.)

### 2.2 How states turn levels into years

| Framework | Mapping | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| New York (music association descriptors) | Novice: first year(s); Intermediate: 2 to 4 years; Advanced: typically after five units of high school study | **SOURCE** (read) |
| New York (diploma rules) | Every district must offer a three- or five-unit arts sequence; five units earns an advanced designation | **SOURCE** (search only) |
| Arizona (2015 standards) | Proficient about one year; Accomplished 2 to 4 years; Advanced honors or college entry | **SOURCE** (search only; page refused the fetch) |
| California (2020 framework, chapter 9) | Sequential yearlong courses carry students through Proficient, Accomplished, Advanced; a four-year sequence; advanced courses offered every year; advanced placement and international diploma pathways on top | **SOURCE** (read) |
| California (same chapter) | Students who missed sequential K to 8 arts reach high school underprepared and teachers must fill the gap first | **SOURCE** (read) |
| Texas | Art, music, theatre and dance run Level I to IV; levels are achievement, not grade, so a Level I class mixes grades; Level IV needs Level III | **SOURCE** (search only) |
| North Carolina | Courses at Accomplished and Advanced count as honors | **SOURCE** (search only) |
| South Dakota | Two high school levels only (Proficient, Advanced) | **SOURCE** (search only) |

### 2.3 Advanced courses at the top

- The national art and design portfolio course has no formal prerequisite, but schools commonly place it after two
  to four studio courses or a portfolio review. **SOURCE** (College Board student page, read; school catalogues,
  search only.)
- The international diploma arts courses run over the last two years of high school. **SOURCE** (search only.)
- Both are portfolio or sustained-project years, which is the shape of our optional fourth level. **DERIVED**

### 2.4 Research on sequence

- No peer-reviewed study was found comparing sequenced high school arts courses with survey courses on outcomes.
  **SOURCE** (none found in two searches.) The NAEP arts assessment tests grade 8 only and gives no high school
  levels. **ASSUMED** (from prior knowledge, not read this session.)
- The best support for sequence is indirect: the national descriptors define Accomplished as reached through a
  sequence, every state framework read builds levels as a sequence of yearlong courses, and California reports
  the cost of missing sequence. **DERIVED**
- Skill in an art builds through repeated, increasingly demanding practice over time, which a survey course cannot
  give. **ASSUMED** (general learning principle, not an arts-specific study.)

### 2.5 Recommendation (levels)

**Keep one 9 to 12 course per discipline, made of three level years plus an optional fourth, each level a full
160-day year: Level 1 Proficient (`.I`), Level 2 Accomplished (`.II`), Level 3 Advanced (`.III`), Level 4
optional, a portfolio or production year that claims `.III` again on new, self-directed work.** **DERIVED**

Reasons and entry rules:

1. One level per year matches the national descriptor (Proficient in one course) and the yearlong-course
   frameworks (California, Texas). **SOURCE**
2. Three-level states put Advanced at three or more years of study (Arizona, New York) and treat it as honors.
   Level 3 is therefore honors depth, and Advanced is claimed by assessed work, not by finishing the year. A
   student who ends Level 3 at "partly" has Level 4 to reach it. **DERIVED**
3. The optional fourth year takes the shape of the portfolio courses at the top (2.3): a sustained investigation
   or a production the student leads. **DERIVED**
4. Entry: every student new to a discipline starts at Level 1, whatever their grade (Texas, national
   descriptors). **SOURCE** Level 1's first unit assumes no K to 8 arts, because many students arrive without it
   (California). **DERIVED**
5. Placement above Level 1: by a placement showing scored against the previous level's end-of-course
   assessment, which mirrors the portfolio review or teacher permission schools use. Otherwise each level
   requires the one before (Texas). **DERIVED**
6. One level is a complete course. A school or family may stop after Level 1 (Proficient), which is the national
   aim for every student in at least one art. **SOURCE** (aim: search only.)

Count: this keeps the 9 to 12 band as one course per discipline, so the arts stay at 20 courses (five
disciplines by four bands). **DERIVED** Music 9 to 12 becomes composition and theory; the ensemble companion is a
page, not a course.
