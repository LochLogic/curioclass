## Verdict

Day 12 is correct mathematics on a sound plan: one question that has a yes and a no, a same and a not-same worked
example, four pairs that sort the B and D errors apart, Jo and Kai, a written task, and a clean hand-off to day 13.
The biggest risk is that the student never makes a cut: every line on the page is printed already cut into every
denominator, so "prove it with a drawing" becomes "see if two printed ticks line up", which is the opposite of the
plan's key moment 2 (cuts into 3, 5 and 10 marked by hand), and the one not-same worked example (2/5 against 5/12)
differs by 1/60 of the line, about 2 millimetres, which no hand-drawn mark can prove. The changes that matter most
are findings 1 and 2: students draw the cuts on the coarser line before they look at the finer one, and Example 2
becomes 2/5 against 6/12.

## Findings, most serious first

### 1. Students never cut: every line arrives cut, so the drawing proves nothing they made

- **What is wrong.** The line stack prints halves, thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths, tenths and twelfths, every
  tick drawn; the four pair figures print both lines fully ticked. In Example 1 step 2 says "Cut each fourth into
  3 equal parts", but the twelfths line is already there and nothing tells students to draw on the fourths line;
  the frame "Each part was cut into __" is filled by copying the display. The pairs are judged by whether two
  printed ticks line up. Kai's talk asks students to "prove it with the fifths and tenths lines", both printed.
  The only place a student must cut is the written task (the stack has no eighths line), and the page does not
  say so. The plan's key moment 2 for this day (u03-1.md field 5) is "cuts that cannot be folded (into 3, 5 or 10)
  ... printed number lines the student marks by hand", and days.csv says "Printed lines: cuts into 3, 5 and 10".
  On the page as built, no student cuts into 3, 5 or 10.
- **Evidence.** d012-student.pdf side 1 (the stack) and both sides (pair figures); items.csv figure cells for
  P12-5 to P12-8; u03-1.md field 5; s03-W12 look-for 1 ("says how each part was cut"), which the day never has
  students practise before the written task. The standard's own wording (4.NF.A.1, "with attention to how the
  number and size of the parts differ") puts the cut at the centre; the Progressions make the same point from the
  number line (from memory, not reopened).
- **Cost.** Students can get every pair right by alignment and still fail the written task and end-check item
  3.1b, which ask how each part was cut. The day's goal ("prove it with a drawing") is met by reading a drawing,
  not making one.
- **Change.** Keep the stack (it is a good check), but make the cut the student's job first. Teacher page,
  Teaching first, after "Students mark the line stack and fill the frames on side 1 as you go": "In Example 1,
  students draw 2 marks inside each fourth on the fourths line before anyone looks at the twelfths line; the
  twelfths line is the check." Same or not?: "On pair 1, draw a mark in the middle of each sixth first, then mark
  10/12; the twelfths line checks your cuts." Kai's claim: "Students draw a mark in the middle of each fifth on
  the stack's fifths line, then check on the tenths line." Written task: "There is no eighths line: cut each fourth
  in 2 on the fourths line." Student page: under "The line stack", add "Draw your cuts on the top line of a pair.
  The line below is the check." (12 words; side 1 has room.)
- **Confidence.** Firm on what the page does; likely on the cost (the taught run will show it).
- **LAYER:** day. **CATCH:** expert. **REPEAT:** new. **CHEAP:** missed.

### 2. Example 2's two points are 2 millimetres apart, so the drawing cannot prove "not the same"

- **What is wrong.** 2/5 is 4.8 twelfths. On the printed stack the fifths tick at 2/5 and the twelfths tick at
  5/12 are 8.6 of 640 units apart: about 0.09 inch (2.3 mm), with the sixths and tenths lines between them.
  A grade 4 student's hand-placed point and a pencil laid "straight down" are less accurate than that. The day's
  own rule is "the points do not line up", and here a careful student will see them line up. The steps then
  explain with "5 × 2 = 10 and 5 × 3 = 15, so a fifth is a bit more than 2 twelfths", which needs 12 ÷ 5 thinking
  that grade 4 has not met, and "2 fifths are a bit more than 4 twelfths, not 5" understates it (4.8 is nearly 5).
- **Evidence.** Measured from the stack's SVG (fifths x = 299.2, twelfths x = 307.8 of 640, drawn at 6.6 inches);
  d012-student.pdf side 1; s03-K12-2 steps.
- **Cost.** The one not-same worked example either fails in students' hands ("they do line up") or is taken on
  the display's word, which teaches that the drawing is not to be trusted. That undercuts the whole day.
- **Change.** Example 2 becomes `s03-K12-2v2`, 2/5 against 6/12 (see "Settled by the expert"): the gap is a tenth
  of the line (about half an inch), the reason is grade 4 (6/12 is halfway; 2 fifths is short of halfway, since
  half of 5 fifths is 2 and a half), and it previews day 17's benchmark without teaching it. Its figure draws both
  points, fifths line on top, so the steps say "right below" truthfully. The warm-up's "Leave the fives up: Example
  2 uses them" becomes "Leave the fives up: the question uses them (5 × 2 = 10)". This replaces the cheap read's
  repair of the old figure and step 4.
- **Confidence.** Firm (measured).
- **LAYER:** day. **CATCH:** expert. **REPEAT:** new. **CHEAP:** missed (the cheap read caught the figure and the
  step wording, listed at the end, not the size of the gap).
