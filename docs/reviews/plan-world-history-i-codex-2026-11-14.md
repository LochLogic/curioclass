Add look-fors and contrasting answers. Credit an explanation connecting a feature to a bounded inference; reject “it is ancient, so it proves what everyone believed.” Keep the distinction between an ancient building and a modern photograph documenting it.

## Unit 2: Belief and empire

### F3. Serious: the religious-continuity key does not demonstrate the relationship it claims

**Location:** [s02.md](S:/CurioClass/docs/courses/world-history-i/s02.md:116), field 5, A4, U1 and its sample answer; field 4, E22.  
**LAYER:** plan. **Confidence:** firm on the coverage problem.

Students describe ancestors, spiritual beings and other features of named traditions, but the credited continuity examples concern language, reused sites and court admonitions. The key does not connect the described religious practices to their later Christian or Islamic forms. E22 correctly warns that linguistic or social continuity alone cannot establish survival of earlier religious beliefs.

Consequently, a student can receive full credit without explaining how the practices described survived and shaped either adopted religion. That relationship is the substance of T2.4. [Massachusetts framework, printed page 141](https://www.doe.mass.edu/frameworks/hss/2018-12.pdf).

**Exact fix:** Keep the Ethiopian and Malian material as medieval evidence of local agency and evidentiary limits. Replace U1’s assessment role with two explicitly dated, practice-to-practice comparison cards, one Christian and one Muslim.

A usable development source is Ayodeji Ogunnaike’s July 2026 account. Its public descriptions include ancestral traditions among some Yoruba Muslims and Ndebele ancestor ceremonies adapted within Christian contexts. Present these as later comparisons, attribute them to the author, and include no ceremonial instructions. [Ogunnaike, “Africana Religions”](https://cswr.hds.harvard.edu/news/2026/07/14/africana-religions).

Replace A4’s continuity instruction with:

> Identify an inherited religious practice in each card. Explain how it is retained or reinterpreted within a Christian or Muslim setting. State why these later observations cannot establish the same practice in medieval Ethiopia or Mali.

Change the key and sample answer accordingly. Keep the existing seven-minute allowance by using two short cards.

## Unit 4: Rulers and ruled

### F6. Serious: rebuilding temples after warfare has disappeared from the Kamakura standard

**Location:** [s04.md](S:/CurioClass/docs/courses/world-history-i/s04.md), field 2; E52; field 5, A2; days 52 and 62.  
**LAYER:** plan. **Confidence:** firm.

The published T3.2f includes restoration of temples destroyed in war. That component is absent from the local paraphrase, progression description, teaching goal, exit and assessment. Sculpture and calligraphy do not by themselves cover rebuilding. [Massachusetts framework, printed page 142](https://www.doe.mass.edu/frameworks/hss/2018-12.pdf).

This omission starts below the unit plan, so checking against the local paraphrase alone would miss it.

**Exact fix:** Restore this component in the standards paraphrase and the existing day 52 work. Use Tōdai-ji as the named example: destruction in 1180, rebuilding begun under Chōgen, and the rebuilt Great Buddha Hall completed in 1195. The temple’s own records support those dates. Distinguish that rebuilding from the present hall, which is later. [Tōdai-ji history](https://www.todaiji.or.jp/en/history/), [Great Buddha Hall](https://www.todaiji.or.jp/en/information/daibutsuden/).

Integrate the example with the existing sculpture study. Add to E52 and A2:

> Explain how rebuilding a temple after warfare helped restore religious and cultural life.

Add that connection to the answer and look-fors, then update the progression, day goals and generated text. No additional day is needed.
