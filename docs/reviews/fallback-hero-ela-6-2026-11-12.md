# The fallback second hero for grade 6 ELA, unit 2.5: our own search

Written 2026-11-12, after the owner said "lets do our own research and find our own preferred document."

**This report gathers. It does not rule.** Every licence statement below is marked **found, not yet second-read**.
Nothing here is a legal judgment. No text named here may be taught from until a second reader, a person, has
checked its licence line and its source (FOUNDATION, section 7). Nobody has compared any file with a printed
copy.

## The need

Unit 2.5 has two class days for a second hero from a tradition other than the Greek. The named fallback, "the
Ramayana, condensed", is not on file: what is there is about 28,100 words of rhymed verse from Book I only
(docs/reviews/rights-ela-6-s2-2026-11-11.md, finding F4). The need, as given to me: prose a grade 6 class can
read, one complete episode of roughly 1,500 to 3,000 words (or cleanly cuttable to that), with a theme a student
can state and support from the text. It must be free and legal to copy and adapt in the United States, from a
source anyone can open.

## The short answer

- **Preferred document:** Yei Theodora Ozaki, *Japanese Fairy Tales* (Project Gutenberg book 4018). The episode
  is **"The Ogre of Rashomon"**, whole, 2,244 words. A second whole episode in the same file, "My Lord Bag of
  Rice" (2,337 words), is there if the first does not pass its sensitivity read.
- **Runner-up:** Sister Nivedita (Margaret E. Noble), *Cradle Tales of Hinduism* (1907), "The Cycle of the
  Ramayana". The episode is **Hanuman finds Sita**, a cut of 1,866 words from the tale "The Conquest of Lanka".
- The Ramayana can be had in young readers' prose. It did not come first because every copy I found is scanned
  text that needs proofing, each needs a cut and a lead-in, the best one measures above the band, and it is a
  sacred story of a living religion that needs an outside reader first. A fallback should be the text with the
  fewest things still owed.

## How I checked

- I searched Project Gutenberg's own search page and the Internet Archive's search endpoint. I opened every
  catalogue page and plain-text file named below. I counted words by script on the named part.
- **Level.** I used the expert's Flesch-Kincaid method from F2 of the rights review: 0.39 x (words per
  sentence) + 11.8 x (syllables per word) - 15.59, words as runs of letters, syllables by vowel groups with a
  silent-e rule, sentences ending at a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. To test my script I ran it
  on Colum, Part II, chapters IV to VI. It gave 6.0 on 5,730 words. The expert got 5.9 and the drafter 6.0. So
  my figures can sit beside theirs. The band for grades 6 to 8 is 6.51 to 10.34.
- **Scanned text.** The Internet Archive files are machine-read scans with errors (broken quote marks, page
  headers in the text). I stripped page headers by script before counting. Counts and levels on those files are
  rough: trust them to within about 50 words and about half a grade.
- **Reading.** For each candidate I say how much I read. "Read whole" means every word of the named episode.

### 1. Yei Theodora Ozaki, *Japanese Fairy Tales*

- **What it is.** Twenty-two Japanese tales retold in English for young readers. The file's title page says
  "Compiled by Yei Theodora Ozaki" and "Profusely Illustrated by Japanese Artists". It shows no publisher. The
  dedication and the preface are both signed "Tokio, 1908". I believe the book first came out in 1903 under
  another title; that is from memory and not confirmed. The whole file is 71,526 words.
- **Who retold it.** A biographical sketch in another of her books at Project Gutenberg (*Warriors of Old
  Japan*, book 41437) names "her father, Baron Saburo Ozaki, the descendant of a Kyoto samurai". Her preface
  here says the tales "have been translated from the modern version written by Sadanami Sanjin", that they
  "are not literal translations", and thanks five Japanese helpers by name, her brother among them. She says
  plainly: "I have followed my fancy in adding such touches of local color or description as they seemed to
  need". So this is a retelling for Western children by a writer of Japanese family living in Tokyo, not an
  outsider's digest.
- **Where the free copy lives.** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4018 . Plain text:
  https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4018.txt.utf-8 .
- **Why it is free, in the source's words (found, not yet second-read).** The catalogue page says "Public
  domain in the USA." The file opens with the same passage RIGHTS.md quotes for rows 1 to 6 ("This eBook is for
  the use of anyone anywhere in the United States and most other parts of the world at no cost and with almost
  no restrictions whatsoever..."). Paragraph 1.C in the file has the same "unprotected by copyright law"
  sentence. I checked both by script.
- **The episode: "The Ogre of Rashomon", whole.** 2,244 words with its title. It is the third story from the end of the
  file. Nothing needs cutting. The plot: knights at a feast argue over a rumour of an ogre at the city gate.
  Watanabe calls it "some old woman's story". He "could not bear the thought that his companion should believe
  he was afraid", so he rides out in a storm to prove it false. The ogre seizes him. He fights it and cuts off
  its arm. He is feasted as a hero, locks the arm in an iron-banded box and swears to show it to no one. An old
  woman comes at night saying she was his nurse. He refuses her three times, then gives in. She is the ogre. It
  takes the arm and escapes through the roof.
- **Level.** 8.6 by my measure on 2,244 words: 23.1 words per sentence, middle sentence 22 words, longest 82.
  That is inside the grade 6 to 8 band. The catalogue page shows "Reading ease score: 84.0 (6th grade)" for the
  whole book. One old form ("durst"). My judgment, marked as judgment: the language is plain modern English and
  the load is in a few long sentences and four Japanese names (Raiko, Hojo, Watanabe, Oni).
- **Theme work.** A student can state and support more than one theme. (a) Courage wins the fight but does not
  finish it: "he knew that the ogre to whom it belonged was still alive". (b) A hero can be beaten through his
  kindness when strength fails: the ogre cannot win at the gate, so it comes as the nurse who "had been like a
  foster-mother to him". (c) Pride starts the trial: he rides out so that no one will think him afraid. It pairs
  well with the anchor. Odysseus beats the Cyclops and then loses by his boast at the ship. Watanabe beats the
  ogre and then loses by giving in. Both stories let a class ask what a hero's weak point is. For RL.6.3 the
  hero changes three times: scoffer, fighter, careful keeper, and then he yields.
- **What a class should not be handed without a note.** The first two paragraphs speak of an ogre that ate its
  victims and an ogre chief "who instead of wine drank the blood of human beings". The knights are drinking sake
  at a feast. An arm is cut off. "Some old woman's story" is used as a put-down. None of this is graphic, and
  the cave scene in the anchor is harsher. The plain-text file prints italics as capitals ("SAKE", "ME"), which
  must be fixed in a class copy.
- **The second episode: "My Lord Bag of Rice", whole.** 2,337 words, 8.7 by my measure, the first story in the
  file. A warrior walks over a dragon on a bridge without fear. The dragon is the Dragon King, who asks him to
  kill a giant centipede. Two arrows fail. He wets the last arrow in his mouth and it kills. He is given a bag
  of rice that never empties. It is clean and complete, and I found nothing that needs a note beyond the
  monster's death and wine at the feast. The theme is simpler (courage and a cool head when the first tries
  fail), and the hero does not change, so it gives less for RL.6.3. Read whole.
- **Care.** Japan is a living culture. These are folk and warrior legends, not scripture. The reteller is of
  the culture. The library plan's outside reader is asked for a retelling of ours; this is not one. A reader
  with knowledge of Japanese literature would still be a good check on the names and on the short note a
  teacher gets.

### 2. Sister Nivedita (Margaret E. Noble), *Cradle Tales of Hinduism*

- **What it is.** Longmans, Green, London and New York, 1907. The scan's title page reads "1907" and "All rights
  reserved". Four tales under the heading "The Cycle of the Ramayana": "The City of Ayodhya" (2,984 words), "The
  Capture of Sita" (1,783), "The Conquest of Lanka" (3,442), "The Ordeal of Sita" (2,650). That is about 10,860
  words in all. Her preface says the four "are intended to form a brief epitome of that great poem". So this is,
  in fact, a condensed Ramayana in prose.
- **Who retold it.** An Irish-born woman who joined the Ramakrishna order in Calcutta. The preface is signed
  "Nivedita, of Ramakrishna-Vivekananda, Calcutta, June 1907". She thanks "the Hindu lady, Jogin-Mother" and
  "the Swami Saradananda" for help, and says the tales are "genuine Indian nursery-tales". She writes from
  inside the faith, as a convert. The tone is reverent, never mocking.
- **Where the free copy lives.** https://archive.org/details/cradletalesofhin00noblrich . Full text:
  https://archive.org/download/cradletalesofhin00noblrich/cradletalesofhin00noblrich_djvu.txt . A second scan is
  at https://archive.org/details/cradletalesofhin00niveuoft .
- **Why it is free, in the source's words (found, not yet second-read).** The Internet Archive record for each
  scan carries "possible-copyright-status: NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT". The first adds: "Evidence reported by judyjordan
  for item cradletalesofhin00noblrich on May 29, 2007: no visible notice of copyright; stated date is 1907."
  That note says "no visible notice", yet the scanned text reads "All rights reserved". The second reader should
  look at that. The plain ground is the printed date, 1907.
- **The episode: Hanuman finds Sita.** A cut from "The Conquest of Lanka", from "The first point was to find out
  the whereabouts of Sita" through "had been seen at one bound to leap across the sea." 1,866 words. Hanuman
  leaps the sea, shrinks to the size of a cat, searches the city all night, finds Sita in the grove, hears her
  defy Ravana, wins her trust with Rama's ring, offers to carry her home and is refused, and leaves after
  wrecking the orchards. It needs a lead-in of about 150 words to say who Rama, Sita and Ravana are. That would
  be Curio Class writing and must be labeled as ours.
- **Level.** 10.8 by my measure on 1,866 words: 25.6 words per sentence, longest 103. That is just over the top
  of the band (10.34) and close to the Monkey King cut (10.5 by the expert's measure). The whole chapter
  measures 10.9. Scanned text, so rough. No old forms.
- **Theme work.** Loyal service: Hanuman does the task he was sent to do and leaves the honour of the rescue to
  Rama ("my master also would desire for himself the honour of liberating you"). Patience under trial, in Sita.
  A class can support either from the text. Hanuman is a fair match for the Monkey King the fallback replaces.
- **What a class should not be handed without a note.** Rama is called "the Lord" and "the Lord of the Universe"
  throughout. This is a devotional telling of a sacred story of a living religion. A public school class needs
  a teacher note that says so, and the project needs a reader from the tradition before it is used. Sita is
  "under sentence of execution". Ravana is "sleeping off the night's intoxication". "Alas, for the instability
  of the monkey-nature!" sits just before the cut. The fourth tale, the ordeal of Sita, raises questions about
  the treatment of a wife that I would not open in two days; it is outside the cut.
- **How much I read.** "The Capture of Sita" and "The Conquest of Lanka" whole. The preface whole. The other
  two tales by their openings only.

### 3. Richard Wilson, *The Indian Story Book*

- **What it is.** Macmillan and Co., London, 1914 (the scan's title page reads "MACMILLAN AND CO., LIMITED, ST.
  MARTIN'S STREET, LONDON, 1914"). Tales from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata "retold by Richard Wilson" for
  young readers. Story I, "Rama's Quest", is one unbroken tale of about 11,300 words with no chapter breaks.
- **Who retold it.** An English writer for children. The preface says he kept names few "out of consideration
  for the youth of my readers" and worked from English books, among them Richardson's *The Iliad of the East*
  and Oman's summaries. The dedication is "to that spirit of kinship and understanding which unites the hearts
  of the best in India with the hearts of the best in Britain". An outsider's telling, friendly in intent.
- **Where the free copy lives.** https://archive.org/details/indianstorybook015651mbp . Full text:
  https://archive.org/download/indianstorybook015651mbp/indianstorybook015651mbp_djvu.txt . A second scan is at
  https://archive.org/details/TheIndianStoryBook .
- **Why it is free (found, not yet second-read).** **The source gives no rights line.** Neither Internet
  Archive record carries a copyright status field. The only ground I can show is the printed date, 1914. The
  word "COPYRIGHT" appears on the page after the title page. A LibriVox recording of the book exists
  (https://archive.org/details/indian_story_book_1702_librivox), and its record carries the public domain mark
  for the recording, but that speaks for the audio, not this scan. This is the weakest rights line of the seven.
- **The episode: Hanuman's leap.** A cut from "After a long and somewhat painful march they came to the shore"
  through "he heard the welcoming cries of the Monkey army." 1,666 words. Hanuman offers to go, leaps, kills
  the sea monster, shrinks, searches the palace, despairs, finds Sita, speaks with her, wrecks the grove, is
  caught, has his tail set alight, burns a palace and leaps home. It is the liveliest telling I found, and
  Hanuman is plainly the hero of it.
- **Level.** 9.1 by my measure on 1,666 words, inside the band. The whole tale measures 11.0. Twelve old forms
  in the cut ("thee", "thou"), all in speech. Scanned text, so rough.
- **Theme work.** Courage and wit, then rashness: his rage in the grove gets him caught, which the text itself
  marks ("This behaviour was not calculated to advance the cause of Sita").
- **What a class should not be handed without a note.** Just before my cut the monkey generals "agreed to put an
  end to their own lives" and a vulture waits to eat them. The cut starts after that on purpose. Inside the cut
  Hanuman says "nothing remains but to become a hermit" and Sita says "Why should I care for death". The telling
  plays the monkeys for laughs (a king who weeps rivers, "these intelligent animals"). Hanuman is a revered
  figure, so a reader from the tradition may find the comic tone disrespectful. I cannot judge that. The scan
  has many reading errors and must be proofed against the page images.
- **How much I read.** From the forest exile to the bridge, about 5,500 of 11,300 words, and the opening page.

### 4. George Bird Grinnell, *Blackfeet Indian Stories*, "The First Medicine Lodge"

- **What it is.** Charles Scribner's Sons; the file shows 1915 (I believe first published 1913; not confirmed).
  https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13833 . Catalogue page: "Public domain in the USA." and "Reading ease score:
  87.2 (6th grade)". Licence wording as in RIGHTS.md rows 1 to 6 (found, not yet second-read).
- **The episode.** The story of Scarface, 3,887 words with Grinnell's four opening paragraphs, about 3,600
  without. A poor, mocked young man is told he may marry only if the Sun allows it. He travels until his food
  and moccasins are gone, is helped by animals, passes a test of honesty, saves Morning Star, and comes home
  with the scar gone. As a hero's journey with a theme (worth is not wealth; honesty and endurance), it is the
  best story of the seven. Read whole.
- **Level.** 4.1 by my measure on 3,895 words: 13.7 words per sentence. That is below the grade 4 to 5 floor
  (4.51), and far below the band.
- **Care.** This is the origin story of a ceremony of a living people, told by an outsider. Grinnell's opening
  says those who pray "tried to prove their prayers sincere by sacrificing their bodies, torturing themselves"
  and compares it to "ancient times". The Sun says of the young woman "you belong to me". It should not be used
  without a reader from the Blackfeet Nation, and the opening should not be handed to a class as it stands.
- **Why it is not the pick.** The level, and the weight of the care it needs.

### 5. Zitkala-Sa, *Old Indian Legends*, "The Badger and the Bear"

- https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/338 . Catalogue page: "Public domain in the USA." and "Reading ease score:
  92.7 (5th grade)". The file shows no publisher or date (I believe Ginn, 1901; not confirmed). Licence wording
  as above (found, not yet second-read). The author was Dakota, so this is a telling from inside.
- **The episode.** 2,174 words. A bear drives a badger family from their home. A young avenger born from a clot
  of blood sets it right. I read the opening, the middle and the ending, about a third.
- **Level.** 4.7 by my measure on 2,183 words. Below the band.
- **Why it is not the pick.** The level. The hero enters two thirds of the way in and acts once, so there is
  little trial to trace. It is an animal tale more than a hero's tale. The spine already uses this author
  elsewhere.

### 6. Elizabeth D. Renninger, *The Story of Rustem, and Other Persian Hero Tales from Firdusi*

- https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57827 . The file's title page reads "Copyright, 1909, by Charles Scribner's
  Sons" and "Published September, 1909". Catalogue page: "Public domain in the USA." and "Reading ease score:
  70.5 (7th grade)". Licence wording as above (found, not yet second-read).
- **The episode.** "Rustem the Wonder Child", 3,574 words: the boy kills a white elephant with one blow and
  takes an enchanted fort by a trick. The better-known "Seven Labors" chapter is 12,104 words, too long.
- **Level.** 11.2 by my measure, above the band. 52 old forms ("unto", "thee", "hath") in 3,574 words.
- **Why it is not the pick.** Too long for the need, above the band, and written in a mock-old style with a
  chatty narrator ("like a great gas-inflated balloon"). The hero does not struggle, so the theme is thin. I
  read the opening, the middle and the ending, about a third. I saw nothing disrespectful to Persians in what I
  read. Killing the elephant is the one hard image.

### 7. Hamilton Wright Mabie (editor), *Legends That Every Child Should Know*, "Beowulf"

- https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6622 . Catalogue page: "Public domain in the USA." and "Reading ease score:
  70.2 (7th grade)". The file shows no publisher or date in its first pages (I believe 1906; not confirmed).
  Licence wording as above (found, not yet second-read).
- **The episode.** 3,938 words: Grendel, Grendel's mother and the dragon, the whole life in one telling.
- **Level.** 6.0 by my measure, just under the band floor (6.51).
- **Why it is not the pick.** It is over the length. It is three fights told fast, close to a digest. The file
  does not say who retold it. Beowulf is not Greek, but it is another European hero, so it adds less breadth
  than the others. Christian wording is laid over the tale ("the good things God had given him"). I read about
  a third.

## Looked at and set aside

- **Nivedita and Coomaraswamy, *Myths of the Hindus and Buddhists*** (Holt, New York, 1914;
  https://archive.org/details/mythshindusbudd00nive ; the record says "Public domain. The Library considers that
  this work is no longer under copyright protection"; found, not yet second-read). The Hanuman sections run
  about 3,780 words at 13.1 by my measure. They hold "the rape of Sita" (the old sense, carrying off), "amorous
  dalliance" and a city pictured as a woman's body. For adults. I read about half of the Hanuman sections.
- **Kate Milner Rabb, *National Epics*** (1896; https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8072 ). "The Story of the
  Ramayana" is the whole epic in 2,968 words, at 13.4 by my measure. It is a summary, not a telling, and its
  tone toward the poem is light ("cudgeling his brains"). The same fault the expert found in the Monkey King
  chapter, and worse. I read the opening, the middle and the ending.
- **John Campbell Oman, *The Great Indian Epics*** (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/73417 ). The Ramayana story
  is 15,641 words, an adult summary with a scholar's comments. Headings only.
- **W. D. Westervelt, *Legends of Ma-ui*** (Honolulu, 1910; https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/32601 ). "Maui
  Snaring the Sun" is 3,830 words at 6.8, but it is a study, not a story. It opens with a Tongan tale in which
  a child is cut in two, moves between island versions, and stops to explain. A class could not read it as one
  episode. I read the opening, the middle and the ending.
- **Ozaki, "Momotaro"** (4,437 words, 6.2) and **"The Story of Prince Yamato Take"** (5,356 words), both in the
  preferred file. Too long whole. Not read.
- **Grinnell, "Kut-o-yis, the Blood Boy"** (4,717 words, 6.4). Too long. Not read.
- **Downloaded, mapped by headings, not read:** Mackenzie, *Indian Myth and Legend* (Gutenberg 47228; its
  Ramayana chapters run about 16,000 words); Ebbutt, *Hero-Myths and Legends of the British Race* (25502;
  "Cuchulain" is 5,646 words, "Beowulf" 13,227); Monro, *Stories of Indian Gods and Heroes* (1911, Internet
  Archive; "The Tale of Rama and Sita" runs about 60 pages); Eastman, *Wigwam Evenings* (28099; all tales under
  1,000 words); Rolleston, *The High Deeds of Finn* (14749); Pyle, *Wonder Tales from Many Lands* (48351).
- **Seen in search results, not opened:** Hull, *The Boys' Cuchulain* (1910, Internet Archive); Dhan Gopal
  Mukerji, *Rama, the Hero of India* (the Internet Archive record shows a 1931 London printing; its United
  States date and status are not known to me, so I did not go further).
- **Nothing found.** I found no free prose telling for young readers of Sundiata, Mwindo or Gilgamesh. The only
  Gilgamesh at Project Gutenberg is a scholar's edition of a tablet. For Sundiata and Mwindo my search was thin
  (Project Gutenberg's search page only), so "nothing found" is not "nothing exists". I did not search
  Wikisource. Anansi tales turned up only as trickster tales, not hero tales.

## What I could not open

- The gutendex.com search service timed out on most queries. It is a search aid, not a source. I used Project
  Gutenberg's own search instead. Nothing in this report rests on it.
- Every address named above opened. I opened no paywalled or library-only source, signed up for nothing, and
  downloaded only plain text.

## Ranking

1. **Ozaki, "The Ogre of Rashomon".** In the band by measure. The right length with no cut. Clean text. The same
   host and licence wording as six rows already in RIGHTS.md. A reteller of the culture. A theme that answers
   the anchor's.
2. **Nivedita, Hanuman finds Sita.** A true condensed Ramayana, told with respect from inside the tradition,
   with a rights line on the host's record. Held back by its level (10.8), the scan, the cut and lead-in it
   needs, and the outside reader it needs.
3. **Wilson, Hanuman's leap.** Easier (9.1) and livelier than Nivedita, with Hanuman at the centre. Held back by
   having no rights line at its source, by its comic tone toward a revered figure, and by the scan.
4. **Ozaki, "My Lord Bag of Rice".** As clean as the first, with less to teach.
5. **Grinnell, Scarface.** The best story, far below the band, and a sacred story told by an outsider.
6. **Zitkala-Sa, "The Badger and the Bear".** A telling from inside, below the band, thin as a hero's trial.
7. **Mabie's "Beowulf"** and **Renninger's Rustem.** Each misses on length and level, and on form or style.

**Preferred: Ozaki, *Japanese Fairy Tales*, "The Ogre of Rashomon".** It is the one candidate that could be put
in front of a class with the fewest steps still owed: a second read of one licence line, a sensitivity read of
2,244 words, and a proofed copy.

**Runner-up: Nivedita, *Cradle Tales of Hinduism*, Hanuman finds Sita.** If the owner wants the fallback to be
the Ramayana, as the spine first named it, this is the telling I would gather. If the level worries the unit
writer, Wilson's cut is the easier one, provided the second reader can settle its rights from the 1914 date
alone.

## What I am least sure of

1. **Whether "Rashomon" carries enough for two days.** It is 2,244 words. The unit has room for about 3,600. The
   theme work is real but the story is short and the hero is not a hero of a great epic. The unit writer may
   want both Ozaki stories (4,581 words together, which is over), or may judge that a class deserves the
   Ramayana by name. That is a teaching call and the owner's call, not mine.
2. **The levels on scanned text.** The Nivedita and Wilson figures come from machine-read scans. They could be
   off by half a grade or more. The Gutenberg figures are firmer.
3. **Printing dates.** The Ozaki file shows "Tokio, 1908" and no publisher. Grinnell, Zitkala-Sa and Mabie
   dates are partly from memory and are marked so. I opened no page scan of any title page except through the
   machine-read text.
4. **Tone toward a living tradition.** I can report what the words say. I cannot say how a Hindu reader hears
   Wilson's jokes or Nivedita's reverence, or how a Japanese reader hears Ozaki. That needs the outside reader.
5. **The sensitivity read.** My notes on what a class should not be handed are a first look by the gatherer.
   They are not the fresh sensitivity read the spine asks for.
