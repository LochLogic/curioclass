# Sensitivity read: World History I, section 3.4 (days 42 to 46), 2026-11-15

Fresh agent read, following docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md. Communities and topics: the peoples of Ghana,
Mali and Songhai (Soninke, Mande, Songhai and their descendants today), the Swahili coast and Kilwa, Axum and
Ethiopia, the Mongols and the peoples they conquered, and Islam as practised in these states.

## What I studied

Community voices first (at least two per major community), then scholarship, then style guidance.

**West Africa (Mali, Senegal, Guinea; Mande and Soninke)**
- [Ahmed Baba Institute](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Ahmed_Baba_Institute) and [Africanews on the manuscripts' return, 2025](https://www.africanews.com/2025/11/19/timbuktus-ancient-manuscripts-return-home-but-security-threats-remain/): Mali's state institute, named for a Timbuktu scholar; Timbuktu's families and staff saved tens of thousands of manuscripts in 2012 to 2013. Timbuktu is a written, living tradition, not a past one.
- Souleymane Bachir Diagne (Senegal), *The Ink of the Scholars* and "Toward an Intellectual History of West Africa" in *The Meanings of Timbuktu* ([CODESRIA](https://publication.codesria.org/index.php/pub/catalog/download/141/1221/4139?inline=1); [review](https://art50.net/en/the-ink-of-the-scholars-reflections-on-philosophy-in-africa-souleymane-bachir-diagne/)): West Africa wrote its own history (Tarikh al-Sudan, Tarikh al-fattash); the "oral only" picture of Africa is the error he names.
- Djibril Tamsir Niane (Guinea) and the griot Mamoudou Kouyate; the Manden Charter of Kurukan Fuga on UNESCO's intangible heritage list, 2009 ([Kurukan Fuga](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Kurukan_Fuga); [The Standard, Gambia](https://standard.gm/mande-charter-kouroukan-fouga-1235); [Birmingham on the Sunjata epic](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/documents/college-artslaw/history/brihc/2017/the-collection-and-analysis-of-the-sunjata-epic.pdf)): the Mande keep their own history through jeli (griot) families; Mande scholars disagree on how literally to date the charter (1236 is Niane's date and is treated with caution).
- Niane, UNESCO General History of Africa vol. IV ch. 6 ([UNESDOC](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000060258)), already named in s03 field 10: open, by a Guinean historian.

**Swahili coast, Kilwa, Great Zimbabwe (Kenya, Tanzania, Zimbabwe)**
- [National Museums of Kenya, Gede](https://museums.or.ke/gede-museum/): "a Swahili town, typical of most towns along the East African Coast"; Kilwa named as a source of later migration up the coast.
- Chapurukha Kusimba (Kenya; formerly National Museums of Kenya), ancient DNA study with Reich and *Swahili Worlds in Globalism* ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-03-life-uncovers-ancient-dna-swahili.html); [Antiquity review](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/57937C9267B76AF02D83520A40BEA79E/S0003598X24002114a.pdf/chapurukha-m-kusimba-2024-swahili-worlds-in-globalism-cambridge-cambridge-university-press-978-1-009-49508-0-hardback-pound4999.pdf)): Swahili towns were African-led and cosmopolitan; calling them either purely African or non-African is a colonial-era simplification. Kusimba also works on the archaeology of slavery on the coast.
- [National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe](https://www.nmmz.co.zw/world-heritage-sites/): Great Zimbabwe, 11th to 15th centuries, "a unique testament to the Bantu civilization of the Shona."
- [Kilwa Chronicle record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilwa_Chronicle) and [Saad, Kilwa dynastic historiography](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/kilwa-dynastic-historiography-a-critical-study/6378AC68CFD88648D91BA961D185C401): Kilwa wrote its own chronicle (Kitab al-Sulwa), with a Shirazi founder tradition. No free English translation of the Arabic text found.
- I found no Tanzanian heritage body page I could read; Abdul Sheriff's (Zanzibar) work did not come up in search. Logged under "Could not settle".

**Axum and Ethiopia**
- Sergew Hable Selassie (Ethiopia), *Ancient and Medieval Ethiopian History to 1270*, Addis Ababa 1972 ([catalogue record](https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/000171640/Description)): the record and a review only, not the text. Ethiopian historians write Aksum as the root of a continuous Ethiopian and Eritrean Christian state.
- [National Geographic Education, Aksum](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/kingdom-aksum/) and [UEN open world history, Aksum and Ethiopia](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/worldhistory1/chapter/aksum-and-ethiopia/): Ezana's conversion in the 300s, coins with the cross; "Aksum" is the spelling closer to Ge'ez, "Axum" also common.

**Mongolia and the peoples conquered**
- [National Museum of Mongolia, the Mongol Empire](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/mQUxDh8p8iesaw): "Chinggis Khaan", unifier, founder of law and the "Great Mongolian State"; conquest described in restrained terms; the killing of a Mongol caravan and envoys at Otrar is given as the cause of the Central Asian war.
- Shagdaryn Bira (Mongolia), "Historical Science in the Mongolian People's Republic", Journal of Asian Studies ([Cambridge](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/historical-science-in-the-mongolian-peoples-republic/314C81C8FCD92B46194222AB08ECA000)): the Secret History of the Mongols as the Mongols' own first history.
- [Stanford Daily, a Mongolian-American writer on Chinggis Khan](https://stanforddaily.com/?p=1135059) and [Columbia, the Mongols in world history](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/mongols/history/history.htm): Mongolians disagree among themselves about how much to dwell on the killing; "barbarian" is the conquered peoples' and Europe's frame, not a neutral word.
- [Secret History, de Rachewiltz shorter version, Western CEDAR](https://cedar.wwu.edu/cedarbooks/4): open, but CC BY-NC-ND, so not usable under our rights rule.

**Style guidance (Africa and Islam)**

- Africa No Filter, *How to Write About Africa in 8 Steps* ([African Union copy](https://www.au.int/sites/default/files/documents/41636-doc-How_to_Write_About_Africa.pdf)): unequal power in who tells the story; local context; return the story to the community.

## Verdict

Pass with fixes. The section names enslavement and conquest plainly, gives no death total, bans role play, frames
every traveller as an outsider and says clearly that Africans built and ruled these states; the quotations are real
and checked. But every primary voice is an outsider's, one slavery claim goes beyond its source, one translation
date is wrong, and the read-aloud and gloss guidance needs tightening before the section ships.

## Findings, most serious first

**1. Only outsiders speak; the communities' own record-keeping is never named. LAYER: unit.**
Days 42 to 46, all sheets. Every quoted source is a visitor or an enemy: Ibn Battuta (Morocco), Polo and Rubruck
(Europe), Ibn al-Athir (Mosul), the Catalan Atlas (Majorca). The nearest insiders are Mongke Khan through Rubruck's
interpreter and Dugha, whose song is reported but not quoted. The opener says rightly that enslaved people's voices
"do not survive in these sources", but says nothing of the peoples who did write and sing their own histories. A
Malian, Swahili, Ethiopian or Mongolian student would see their ancestors only through others' eyes, the single-story
pattern Wainaina and Diagne name. Why it matters: the brief requires people shown "with their own voices and agency";
s03 field 10 already asks for "griot transmission" and "oral alongside written history".
Fix, in the pieces now (section): add to opener.md "Outsiders, all of them" a second sentence: "These peoples kept
their own histories too: Mande griots (jeliw) such as Dugha's successors still sing Sundiata's story; Timbuktu's
scholars wrote the Tarikh al-Sudan; Kilwa wrote its own chronicle; Ezana's inscriptions at Axum are in Ge'ez; the
Mongols wrote the Secret History of the Mongols. None has a free English translation we could check, so this sheet
holds only visitors. That is a limit students should name." Add the same limit, one line, to each option sheet's
header and to day 42's "Teaching first" after the outsider paragraph. Add to the A2 and W44 look-for "a limit of the
sources named" the sample "no source here is by the people of Mali themselves".
Fix, in the plan (unit): s03 field 10 and sources.csv gain a row for one insider or community-historian passage per
option before unit 3 ships, for example Niane's UNESCO chapter (Guinea; open on UNESDOC, licence to check) for option
a. See "For Cory" for the cost question.

**2. "Source C's raids took people captive" is not in Source C. LAYER: section.**
Days 42, 43, 44, 45, 46; sources-42b.md; opener.md. Lines: d042 "Source C's raids took people captive"; d044 "Source
C connects Kilwa's ruler with raids taking people"; d045 and d046 Error J "people taken in Kilwa's raids"; opener
"Sources A, C and P1 show enslaved people ... people taken in Kilwa's raids"; sources-42b gloss "*booty*, goods and
people taken in raids". Gibb's Kilwa passage says only "the booty made on his expeditions"; it names no captives
(checked against the Fordham copy). Why it matters: the unit's rule is that nothing a source does not record is
reported as recorded, and students are scored on keeping evidence apart from inference.
Fix: sources-42b gloss becomes "*booty*, what raiders seized; raids of this time often took captives, but this
passage does not say who or what was taken." Opener: "Sources A and P1 show enslaved people: armed men at the court
and salt miners at Taghaza. Source C records raids on inland neighbours; raids like these often took captives, but
the passage does not say so." d042: "Source C records raids on inland peoples; it does not say whether people were
taken, and we do not claim it." d044: "Source C connects Kilwa's ruler with raids on inland neighbours." d045 and
d046 Error J: replace "people taken in Kilwa's raids" with "the inland peoples Kilwa raided".

**3. Browne's translation is dated 1902; the volume is 1906. LAYER: unit.**
sources.csv 42E ("London: T. Fisher Unwin, 1902"); sources-42d.md twice ("Browne's 1902 translation", "translated by
Edward G. Browne (1902)"); opener.md ("Browne's 1902 translation"); u03-4.md field 9 ("Browne's 1902 'Tartars'").
Volume 2 of *A Literary History of Persia* (the volume holding pp. 427 to 431) has its preface dated 16 May 1906;
volume 1 was 1902. Why it matters: an invented or wrong date breaks the unit's sourcing rule and the rights line.
Fix: "1906" in every place listed; the public-domain basis is unchanged.

**4. Reading "negroes" and "blacks" aloud, over and over. LAYER: section.**
opener.md "Translators' words"; sources-44 (G), sources-45 (P1, F1); u03-4.md field 9. Every source is read aloud,
and Source G and the day 45 packet repeat "the negroes" five times. "Glossed once" covers the printed page but not
the teacher's voice. A Black student hears an outdated label said by their teacher, again and again, in a test.
Fix, opener.md, after "glossed once": "When you read aloud, read the translator's word once where it first appears,
say what it means and why we do not use it, then say 'the people of Mali' in its place for the rest of the reading.
The printed text stays as printed." Mirror the sentence in d044 and d045 "Words and supports" (item-level choice; I
decided it).

**5. Source G gives only Ibn Battuta's praise, and Mali's Islam is measured by a visitor. LAYER: section.**
sources-44.md (G), d044 discussion, opener. Ibn Battuta, a judge of one school of law, also condemned Malian customs
in the same passage (women at court uncovered, foods he thought unlawful; Fordham copy). The selection is fine to
keep, but read alone it lets a visitor's verdict stand as the measure of a people's faith, and the "faith" column on
day 44 leans on it. Why it matters: CAIR and ING guidance on teaching about Islam, and Diagne's point that West
African Islam had its own scholars and ways.
Fix: add to sources-44 G's gloss: "In the same chapter Ibn Battuta also condemns customs he thought wrong. His praise
and his blame both measure Mali by his own training; Mali's Muslims, scholars among them, practised Islam in their
own way beside Mande customs." Add one line to d044 "Expect": "Source G is a visitor's verdict on Mali's faith, not
the people's own."

**6. Two item samples say more than the sheets do. LAYER: unit.**
items.csv s03-W44 sample: "Ibn al-Athir describes whole cities killed". The sheet's excerpt says "spared none,
slaying women and men and children" and "devastations"; "whole cities" is not on the sheet, and a sample is what
students copy toward a total. items.csv s03-P42-1 sample: "The gold cap shows wealth"; Source A as printed has a gold
bird and gold guimbris, no cap (the cap is in the cut). Fix: W44 sample "Ibn al-Athir records the killing of women,
men and children"; P42-1 sample "The gold bird and gold instruments show wealth".

**7. Day 42's student page turns enslaved men into "armed guards". LAYER: day.**
d042 Side 1: "Armed guards accompany the ruler." Source A says "three hundred armed slaves", and the teacher page
says they were enslaved. The student statement drops it, softening what the source shows. Fix: "Three hundred armed
enslaved men walk behind the ruler."

**8. The Massufa gloss: "Berber", and who was enslaved. LAYER: section.**
sources-45.md P1 gloss: "*Massufa*, a Berber people of the desert who guided caravans". Beside "the slaves of the
Massufa tribe" a student may read the Massufa as the enslaved. "Berber" is an outside name many Imazighen reject.
Fix: "*Massufa*, an Amazigh (Berber) people of the desert who guided caravans; the salt diggers were people the
Massufa held in slavery."

**9. The fact cards name states but not peoples. LAYER: section.**
sources-42a fact card. Ghana is "Ghana (Wagadu)" with no Soninke; Mali has no Mande or Maninka; Songhai is a state
only. The communities' own names are part of the brief. Fix: "Ghana (Wagadu), a kingdom of the Soninke, ...";
"Mali, a kingdom of the Mande (Maninka) peoples, rose under Sundiata Keita about 1235"; "Songhai, a kingdom of the
Songhai people from Gao, ...". Add: "Their descendants live in Mali, Mauritania, Senegal, Guinea, Niger and beyond
today."

**10. Gloss accuracy: Zanj and the Koran. LAYER: section.**
sources-42b gloss: "*heathen Zanj*, his word for inland peoples who were not Muslim." Zanj was the Arabic writers'
name for the Black peoples of the East African coast and interior; "heathen" carries the "not Muslim". Fix:
"*Zanj*, Arabic writers' name for East African peoples; *heathen*, his word for people who were not Muslim; *Koran*,
the Qur'an." (Our own text already avoids "Koran"; the gloss gives the spelling Muslims use.)

**11. Mongke's "one God" and "Moal". LAYER: section.**
sources-44 F gloss: "*Moal*, Mongols, in the Khan's word". Rockhill's own note says "Moal" is Friar William's
transcription. "One God" is the friar's Christian rendering; Mongol rulers prayed to Eternal Heaven (Tengri), the
Mongols' own faith. Fix: "*Moal*, Rubruck's spelling of Mongol; *one God*, Rubruck's words, through an interpreter,
for Eternal Heaven (Tengri), whom Mongol rulers worshipped."

**12. "Tartars" glossed as a name only. LAYER: section.**
sources-42d header and gloss. Browne's own footnote (p. 428) says the European form was chosen to link the Mongols to
Tartarus, the underworld. Fix, header: "Browne's translation says 'Tartars', a European name that linked the Mongols
to Tartarus, the underworld; Browne himself notes it. The Mongols called themselves Mongol."

**13. Error K's repair could erase the coast's mixed heritage. LAYER: section.**
sources-42b fact card; d043 and d044 error K lines ("not foreign builders"). Kusimba's work shows the towns were
African-led and also cosmopolitan; Kilwa's own chronicle traces its sultans to a Persian (Shirazi) founder, and many
Swahili families hold that heritage. Fix, fact card after "each under its own ruler": "Their people were coastal
Africans; Muslim traders from Arabia and Persia settled and married into the towns, and Kilwa's own chronicle traces
its sultans to a founder from Shiraz in Persia. Language, building and government were made on the coast."

**14. The Swahili and Mongol cards say nothing of enslaved people. LAYER: section.**
sources-42b and 42d fact cards. Option a's card names the trade in enslaved people plainly; option b's and d's do
not, so a student mapping them can score full marks on economy without it. Fix, 42b, after "ivory went out too":
"and some enslaved people, taken from the interior and sold across the Indian Ocean". Fix, 42d, after Khwarazm:
"Skilled captives from conquered cities were carried off to work for the khans; Rubruck met one, a goldsmith from
Paris, at Karakorum." (Rubruck's Boucher is in the Rockhill text already in sources.csv.)

**15. The Mongol card gives the war no cause. LAYER: section.**
sources-42d fact card. Mongolia's national museum gives the killing of a Mongol trade caravan and its envoys at Otrar
(1218) as the cause of the war on Khwarazm. Naming it gives the Mongols agency without excusing the killing. Fix,
before "destroyed the cities of Khwarazm": "after the governor of Otrar killed a Mongol trade caravan and its envoys
in 1218,". Also add "and gave the Mongols written law and a single state" after "proclaimed Chinggis Khan in 1206".

**16. Culture "beyond the ruler" leaves out women. LAYER: day.**
d043 "Teaching first": "Model culture beyond rulers: Source A's musicians; option b's Swahili language and Shona
builders; Source D's merchants". Source B shows village women selling food to travellers, the one place women act in
these sheets. Fix: add "Source B's village women, who fed and traded with travellers;" after "Source A's musicians;".

**17. Family note frames visitors as the people "who saw these places". LAYER: section.**
family.md: "We read travellers who saw these places". Fix: "We read visitors who wrote about these places for readers
at home, among them Ibn Battuta in Mali and Kilwa and Marco Polo in China, and ask what each could and could not know.
The peoples of these places kept their own histories too, in griots' songs, chronicles and manuscripts, and their
descendants live there today."

**18. Page numbers for the Mali court passages. LAYER: unit.**
sources.csv 42A, 44G and 45-F2 cite "the Mali section (from p. 317)". The Fordham copy heads the Mali court passages
"pp. 323-335"; pp. 317 to 323 is the Sahara and Walata. Fix: "pp. 323-335" in those three rows (42B, 45-P1, 45-F1
stay "pp. 317-323").

## Could not settle

- Whether Ibn Battuta's full text says the Kilwa raids took captives (Gibb 1929 selection does not; a later fuller
  translation might). Finding 2's wording holds either way.
- A free, checkable English text by an insider for any option: the Secret History open edition is CC BY-NC-ND; the
  Kilwa Chronicle's Arabic edition (Strong 1895) has no free English; Tarikh al-Sudan's English is in copyright.
  Theal's *Records of South-Eastern Africa* (1898 to 1903, public domain) has the Portuguese version of the Kilwa
  chronicle in English, but that is through Barros, not Kilwa's own voice. Niane's UNESCO chapter's licence is to check.
- Mali's capital: P42-2's sample names Niani; its identification is debated. Suggest "Mali's capital (its site is
  debated)" if the answer-key read agrees.
- Whether the "three hundred armed slaves" were royal enslaved soldiers with rank (as Mande royal jonw could be); the
  section rightly says enslaved, and I found no source to add nuance safely.
- No Tanzanian heritage body page or Abdul Sheriff text was reached.
