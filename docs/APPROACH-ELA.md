# The Curio Class approach to teaching English language arts

This page says how English language arts carries out the shared principles in [APPROACH.md](APPROACH.md). It does
not repeat that page; principle numbers point back to it. The same rules hold: nothing here has been tried with
children, every claim rests on a source opened for this page, and the rules are rules for unit plans. Evidence
ratings (strong, moderate, minimal) are the federal practice guides' own. A rating belongs to the grades and the
students the guide studied, and those are named each time, because most do not match a whole grade 6 class.

First draft, 2026-11-11 (Sara's list, piece 2). Not yet approved. Written for the grade 6 pilot first; what it
says about grades 3 to 5 is thinner and marked.

**The role of the standards.** The Common Core State Standards for English Language Arts set what is learned and
leave the teaching to us. Three ideas from the writers' criteria for publishers (grades 3 to 12) shape everything
below: students read texts of the right difficulty and learn from them, questions and writing are answered from
the text, and reading builds knowledge. The spine carries the first and third. The unit plan carries the second.

**The text is the unit (principle 1).** In math a unit is built on a standard. Here it is built on a text or a
set of texts, and the standards are taught through them. The reading standards ask for this: RL.6.1, "Cite
textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the
text." The publishers' criteria say eighty to ninety percent of the reading standards in each grade need this
kind of work, and ask for a like share of questions. *Rule:* no unit is planned until its stretch's texts are
named, checked for rights and on file. A unit plan holds the text record, the question set and the word list
before it holds days. The share of questions that cannot be answered without the text is counted.

**How hard a text is.** The standard sets the level: RL.6.10, "read and comprehend literature ... in the grades
6-8 text complexity band proficiently, with scaffolding as needed at the high end of the range." Appendix A of
the standards gives three parts to weigh together: a computed measure, a person's judgment of what makes the
text hard (meaning, structure, language, the knowledge it assumes), and the reader and the task. The 2012
supplement gives the bands for six measures (grades 6 to 8: 925 to 1185 on Lexile, 6.51 to 10.34 on
Flesch-Kincaid) and says the human judgment then places a text in a grade. Appendix A argues that school texts
had become too easy; that is an argued case, and we did not read the studies under it. *Rule:* every anchor has
a text record with all three parts and a reason for where it sits in the year. Texts need not climb in order
through a year.

**Supports that keep the text (principle 9).** The publishers' criteria ask that help lets every student meet
the text and never stands in for it: no summary that makes reading unnecessary. For students who struggle with
reading in grades 4 to 9, working through hard "stretch" text with a teacher's help is rated moderate (WWC 2022,
recommendation 4). That guide studied small-group help, not whole classes. *Rule:* a unit plan names what makes
its anchor hard and gives support before, during and after reading that matches it. Audio sits beside the text;
it does not replace reading on a reading check.

**Text sets that build knowledge.** What a reader already knows about a topic strongly affects what they
understand. We read this in Appendix A and in a three-page annotated list of studies from the standards' authors;
we read none of the studies themselves. For students with reading difficulties in grades 4 to 9, a set of
practices that starts with building knowledge of the world and of words is rated strong (WWC 2022,
recommendation 3). Whether topic-based sets beat a mixed reader for a whole class, we did not read. The rubric on
file scores it either way ([RUBRIC-ELA.md](RUBRIC-ELA.md), indicator 2d). *Rule:* a stretch is one topic and one
set of texts, with a word list that carries through it. A second text is chosen for what it adds to the first.

**Volume of reading.** Appendix A argues students must read a lot, and the publishers' criteria ask for several
longer texts a year and for time reading without help. For kindergarten to grade 3, reading connected text every
day is rated moderate (WWC 2016, recommendation 4). We opened no rated source on how much a sixth grader should
read, or on silent independent reading. *Rule:* the spine states the words read in class and at home for every
stretch, and offers a long on-topic book each stretch. The weekly reading block rests on judgment, and the spine
says so.

**Kinds of day (principle 2).** An ELA day is marked by its lead strand: read, write, talk, language or check.
Most days mix them; the mark names the main work.

**Modelled reading and writing (principle 3).** The shared page says modelling in reading and writing is common
practice with weaker evidence. The sources opened for this page are stronger than that in three places, each
with a limit:

- Writing, grades 6 to 12: teaching writing strategies directly, in a cycle of model, practise, reflect, is
  rated strong (WWC 2016 secondary writing, recommendation 1).
- Writing, elementary: teaching the writing process and writing for different purposes is rated strong (WWC
  2012, recommendation 2).
- Reading, kindergarten to grade 3: teaching comprehension strategies, handing the work over step by step, is
  rated strong (WWC 2010, recommendation 1). For grades 4 to 9 the like finding is for students with reading
  difficulties (WWC 2022, recommendation 3).

What stays thin is modelling the close reading of one text for a whole middle school class. The publishers'
criteria add a limit: strategies serve the understanding of this text and are not the goal of the lesson.
*Rule:* the teacher models on a passage or a sample piece, the class does the next one together, and the day the
help is withdrawn is named. A strategy is taught where a text needs it, never as a unit of its own. This finding
about the shared page goes to Cory; this page does not change that one.

**The writing process and the writing calendar.** W.6.5: "develop and strengthen writing as needed by planning,
revising, editing, rewriting, or trying a new approach." W.6.10: "Write routinely over extended time frames ...
and shorter time frames." So a year needs both pieces taken through every step and pieces done in one sitting.
Linking reading and writing, with model texts that show a kind of writing's features, is rated moderate (WWC
2016, recommendation 2). Using students' writing to decide what to teach next is rated minimal (recommendation
3). The elementary panel asks for an hour of writing a day from grade 1; that rests on minimal evidence, and a
50-minute period cannot give it. The publishers' criteria ask for about 35 percent argument, 35 percent
informative and 30 percent narrative in middle school. *Rule:* the spine holds a writing calendar that says, for
each piece, its type, process or one sitting, and the texts it draws on. A process piece has a model piece, a
rubric and sample student work on paper. A tool cannot judge open writing (principle 5).

**Sentences.** The elementary guide rates teaching sentence construction, with spelling, handwriting and typing,
moderate (WWC 2012, recommendation 3; its studies were of grades 1 to 4). We opened no source on grammar taught apart
from writing; the spine's line on it is from memory and stays marked. *Rule:* sentence work uses sentences from
the anchor text and from students' own drafts.

**Vocabulary from the texts.** L.6.6: "Acquire and use accurately grade-appropriate general academic and
domain-specific words and phrases." L.6.4: "Determine or clarify the meaning of unknown and multiple-meaning
words." Appendix A sorts words into three tiers and puts the weight on the second, the general academic words
found across written texts, and says words are learned through repeated meetings in varied settings. Teaching a
small set of academic words in depth is rated strong for English learners (shared page, principle 9). *Rule:* a
stretch has one word list, drawn from its texts, mostly general academic words. Each word is met before, during
and after reading and comes back in a later stretch. Ways to work out a new word are taught on words the text
supplies.

**Foundational skills in the lower grades.** Grades 3 to 5 still have standards for reading words: RF.4.3 and
RF.5.3, "Know and apply grade-level phonics and word analysis skills in decoding words." For kindergarten to
grade 3, teaching sound and letter links and teaching decoding and word parts are each rated strong (WWC 2016,
recommendations 2 and 3). For students behind in grades 4 to 9, teaching the reading of long words is rated
strong (WWC 2022, recommendation 1). The rubric on file makes this part must-pass for grades 3 to 5 and rules
out teaching children to guess a word from a picture or the sentence. *Rule:* a grade 3 to 5 ELA spine starts
from a sequence for long words, word parts and fluency. Grade 6 has no such standards; it teaches roots and word
parts, and a one-minute oral reading check in the first two weeks flags students who need more than this course
gives. Curio Class is not a reading intervention.

**"Fluency" means reading fluency.** In math the word means quick, accurate calculation. Here it is RF.4.4:
"Read with sufficient accuracy and fluency to support comprehension", and it includes expression. Purposeful
rereading and reading with expression are rated strong for students behind in grades 4 to 9 (WWC 2022,
recommendation 2). *Rule:* fluency practice is rereading a short passage of the anchor aloud with a partner. No
tool shows a student a countdown timer (shared rule). In the oral reading check the teacher holds the watch.

**Talk (principle 5).** SL.6.1: "Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions." Discussion of what
a text means is rated minimal, and that for kindergarten to grade 3 (WWC 2010, recommendation 3). We keep it
because the standards require it. *Rule:* a unit plan names the discussion routine, two or three planned
questions about the text, and a look-for sheet.

**The daily evidence (principle 8).** The day ends with a written question that can only be answered from the
day's text. This is W.6.9, "Draw evidence from literary or informational texts", done daily, and it is the
routine writing W.6.10 asks for. The stretch's process piece, scored with a rubric, is the other half.

**Practice that returns (principle 7).** Words and sentence skills come back in the openers. Reading standards
come back on new texts, which is why a cumulative check uses a text read cold.

## Where the ELA evidence is thin

- Topic-based text sets against other ways of choosing texts, for a whole class. Argued; studies unread.
- How much reading, and independent silent reading. Nothing rated was opened.
- Modelled close reading for typical middle school readers. Common practice.
- Discussion. Minimal, and for younger grades.
- Daily writing time. Minimal.
- Grammar apart from writing. Nothing opened.
- Most strong ratings above are for younger grades or for students who struggle with reading.

## What ELA adds to the unit plan fields

Field numbers are those of [courses/UNIT-PLAN.md](courses/UNIT-PLAN.md). They were fixed on math; the pilot is
the first ELA plan and may change what ELA fills. Proposed, to be tried on the pilot:

- Stretch sheet, field 2: the text set, with each text's record, rights line and status; the word list.
- Stretch sheet, field 5 (end check): a new text read cold, named, with its record.
- Unit plan, field 3: the exit question for every day, in full, and whether it needs the text.
- Field 4 (expected errors): expected misreadings of this text, in place of wrong answers to a calculation.
- Field 5 (what is carried): the model passage or model piece, and the day the help is withdrawn.
- Field 6: each day marked read, write, talk, language or check, with the pages or words read.
- Field 7: the discussion routine; the written task and its type.
- Field 8: the words and the sentence skill that come back.
