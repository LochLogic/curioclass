## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How many rectangles can you make with a given area?"
**Hook, day 1:** Build rectangles with ten squares for five minutes. Ask the compelling question, then connect side lengths to the area product. This adapts base lesson 1.
**Supporting questions:** How can we know the pair list is complete (day 2)? What makes a number prime (day 3)? Which totals fit equal groups (day 4)? Why do particular turns change a locker (day 5)?
**Closing claim:** I can find every factor pair, name the multiple relationship, test equal grouping and justify prime or composite status. Rectangle Search and Locker Changes prepare this claim; items 1 to 5 check it on day 7.
**Teaching:** Model a systematic search on day 2, including unsuccessful divisors. Compare student searches before defining prime on day 3. Step back during days 4 and 5; connect explanations to equations afterward.

<!-- skel -->
- **4.OA.B.4:** Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100. Recognize that a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors. Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1–100 is a multiple of a given one-digit number. Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1–100 is prime or composite. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.C.7:** Fluently multiply and divide within 100, using strategies such as the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8) or properties of operations. By the end of Grade 3, know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers. (The "such as" list gives examples, not required parts.) Parts, by verb: whole standard.

**Scoring:** Record each component separately: complete pair list; completeness reason; each multiple statement; each grouping decision with evidence; each classification with evidence; the tray-size list and completeness reason. Award a component only for correct mathematics. Do not infer a missing part from another answer.

**Teacher-judged look-fors and samples:** Item 1 needs the complete list, a justified stopping point and composite classification. Meets: "The pairs are (1, 77) and (7, 11). I checked possible smaller sides 1 through 8. Nine times nine exceeds 77, so another pair would repeat a smaller side already checked. Its four distinct factors make 77 composite." Does not: "I remembered two pairs, so there are no others" (A). Item 2 needs both multiplier witnesses. Meets: "84 is a multiple of 6 because 6 times 14 is 84, and of 14 because 14 times 6 is 84." Does not: "6 is a multiple of 84" (C). Item 3 needs exact grouping or bounding products. Meets: "Two works because 2 times 43 is 86. Seven does not: 84 and 91 are consecutive multiples around 86." Does not: "Seven works because 84 is close" (D). Item 4 needs distinct positive factors, with a complete search for 59. Meets: "59 has only 1 and 59; checking 2 through 7 gives no factor and 8 times 8 is greater. 59 and 2 are prime; 1 is neither. Two has factors 1 and 2; 1 has only the factor 1." Does not: "Two is composite because it is even" (F). Item 5 needs all tray sizes and a completeness reason. Meets: "Sizes 1, 3, 9 and 27 work. Pairs (1, 27) and (3, 9) give all sizes; checking smaller sides 1 through 5 finds no others, and 6 times 6 exceeds 27." Does not: "The tray sizes are 27 and 54" (G).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | The search stops before all possible smaller sides are tested. | Recall has replaced a completeness method. Repair: check consecutive candidate sides until the next side squared exceeds the total. | From memory. |
| B | A reversed factor pair is counted as a new pair. | Orientation is confused with a new pair. Repair: rotate one array and record its pair once. | From memory. |
| C | The factor and multiple relationship is reversed. | The product and its inputs are confused. Repair: label a multiplication equation and read both relationships aloud. | From memory. |
| D | Nearly equal groups are accepted when no leftovers are allowed. | Approximation replaces exact grouping. Repair: draw the leftover beside two neighbouring multiples. | From memory. |
| E | One is classified as prime. | One distinct factor is mistaken for two. Repair: list distinct positive factors beside the definition. | From memory. |
| F | Two is classified as composite because it is even. | Parity replaces the prime definition. Repair: count its distinct positive factors. | From memory. |
| G | Multiples of the total are listed where its factors are required. | The factor relationship is reversed. Repair: test whether each proposed number divides the total exactly. | From memory. |
| H | The total itself is omitted from its factor list. | The final possible factor is excluded. Repair: divide the total by itself and include that factor. | From memory. |

Each letter has one repair. Other wrong answers receive individual feedback rather than a forced letter.
