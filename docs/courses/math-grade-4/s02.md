## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can two different fractions be the same amount?"

**Hook, day 9:** Compare half of one strip with two fourths of an identical strip for five minutes. Ask the compelling question before retrieval. This adapts base lesson 1.
**Supporting questions:** Why can one point have different names (day 12)? What changes when parts are cut (day 16)?
**Closing claim:** I can explain and generate equivalent fractions, then justify comparisons for the same whole. Cut and Regroup and Comparison Claims prepare the claim, checked on day 24.
**Teaching:** Model subdivision on day 14 and its reverse, including division of both counts, on day 16. Step back for the critique afterward. Teach common-numerator reasoning on day 20, then rehearse it before day 21's argument. Day 20 also models renaming both fractions with a shared multiple, replacing repeated practice. Days 17, 18 and 22 consolidate established methods.


**Practices:** MP3 develops through day 16's Cut and Regroup critique and day 21's Comparison Claims defense. MP7 develops on day 14 by connecting cuts to part counts in equations. Day 24 checks both.
**Fluency:** These standards set none. Untimed mixed practice gives feedback without new content.
**Balance:** Understanding takes 600 minutes, fluency 180 and application 120, respectively 67, 20 and 13 percent of teaching time. Major work receives 660 of 900 minutes, 73.3 percent; 12 of 15 lessons and one of one units center grade 4 major work. Days 9, 10 and 13 earn none. Each remaining teaching day earns 55 minutes, excluding its five-minute earlier-grade warm-up.
**Limits:** Given denominators are 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 100; generated denominators may differ. Use positive whole-number scale factors. No simplest-form requirement, least common denominator, cross multiplication, fraction multiplication or general unlike-denominator arithmetic. Read fractions as counts of unit fractions.
**Carried:** Tie strips and area diagrams to fraction notation on day 9 and the number line to equations on day 11. Students subdivide and regroup these models through day 18. Withdraw supplied strips on day 19, area diagrams on day 20 and partitioned lines on day 23; students may draw their own. Restore models for day 25 repairs. Supporting partitions explain subdivision on day 11; factors and multiples practise major equivalence on days 17 and 18.

**Teacher-judged look-fors and samples:** Item 1 needs a faithful model, correct new counts, smaller unit parts and unchanged amount. Meets: "I shaded thirteen tenths across two equal wholes. Halving each tenth gives twenty parts per whole and twenty-six selected parts; each part is half as large, and no shading changed." Does not: "26/20 has more pieces of the original size" (J).

Item 2 needs both grouping arguments and correct equalities. Meets: "Three sixths make one half, so fifteen sixths make five halves at the same point. Pair the twelfths: twenty-two become eleven groups and twelve per whole become six, so 22/12 = 11/6." Does not: "15/6 = 5/6 because I divided the top by three" (D).

Item 3 needs opposite sides of one half, a consistent shared line and the comparison symbol. Meets: "Four tenths is left of five tenths, one half; seven twelfths is right of six twelfths, one half. Therefore 4/10 < 7/12." Does not: "My line has four tenths to the left, so 4/10 > 7/12" (G).

Item 4 needs correct equivalent fractions and an equal-part comparison. Meets: "28/40 < 30/40, so 7/10 < 3/4." Does not: "Both exceed one half, so they are equal" (I).

Item 5 needs a common numerator and reasoning about part size. Meets: "7/4 = 14/8; fourteen eighths exceed fourteen tenths, so 7/4 > 14/10." Does not: "14/10 is greater because fourteen exceeds seven" (C).

Item 6 needs the distinction between comparing numbers and comparing amounts of different wholes. Meets: "The numbers have that order, but a shorter whole could make two thirds shorter than three fifths of the other strip. We need whole lengths." Does not: "The second marked length must be greater because two thirds is greater" (F).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Unequal parts are counted as equal fractional units. | Part count has replaced equal measure. Repair: partition one fixed rectangle into equal areas before naming parts. | From memory. |
| B | Tick marks are counted instead of intervals from zero. | Positions are confused with unit lengths. Repair: trace and count each interval starting at zero. | From memory. |
| C | A larger numerator is treated as proof of a greater fraction. | The size of the fractional unit is ignored. Repair: align equal wholes and compare the actual marked lengths. | From memory. |
| D | Only one count is changed when renaming a fraction. | The selected count is disconnected from parts per whole. Repair: track both counts while subdividing or regrouping every equal part. | From memory. |
| E | A larger denominator is said to make each unit fraction larger. | Whole-number order is applied to part size. Repair: compare one eighth and one tenth of identical strips. | From memory. |
| F | Numerical fraction order is assumed to decide amounts from unspecified different wholes. | The common unit is missing. Repair: draw different whole lengths that reverse the marked-length order. | From memory. |
| G | The written comparison symbol reverses the stated order. | Symbol meaning is insecure. Repair: read the inequality aloud beside ordered points. | From memory. |
| H | All pieces shown beyond one are redefined as one whole. | The unit changes during counting. Repair: outline the original whole repeatedly without changing its size. | From memory. |
| I | Fractions on the same side of a benchmark are called equal. | A broad location substitutes for a comparison. Repair: locate both distinct points within that region. | From memory. |
| J | Subdivision is said to leave each part's size unchanged. | The cut is disconnected from the unit fraction. Repair: overlay one original part with its smaller pieces. | From memory. |
