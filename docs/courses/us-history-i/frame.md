# Course frame: US History I (1763 to the First World War)

Course id `us-hist-1`, grade 11, draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame names no single day.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 9 units, in time order (1754 to 1920, from the Seven Years' War to the Senate's vote on the League of Nations).
- **Days.** 160 teaching days: 148 in units and 12 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year. 27 rows in 148 unit days is about 5.5 days a row, more than World History II, because three rows are sustained products: the research cycle (us1-research-inquiry), the reform research project (us1-reform-movements) and the several-page argument written three times (us1-argument-writing). us1-empire carries ten Massachusetts codes as a survey with one research report, which is also the year's third argument (one product). The year has 12 check and respond days.
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 United States History I, topics USI.T1 to USI.T7, is the content anchor: every USI code is owned by one row and every row sits in one unit below. The news/media literacy codes MA.NML.T1.5, T2.1, T2.2, T3.2 and T4.1 to T4.3 are placed in this course (the rest sit in US Government rows). C3 9 to 12 indicators carry the skills. Each unit plan is checked against California, Tennessee and New York and the college-level United States history course framework; the "Check set adds" column lists candidates with their source, confirmed or dropped in the unit plan's comparison table, within the unit's days. Check set names never appear in student, teacher or family copy.
- **Base text.** None. Partial base: the Since Time Immemorial high school US history units (Washington State, OSPI). Selected: from its unit 1, the one-period Level 1 constitutional case (founding documents and treaty rights) as one example in unit 2, after the constitutional principles, named as a Washington case whose Boldt decision is a later Pacific Northwest application; from its unit 2, the two-period Level 2 allotment source analysis for the Dawes Act part of unit 6, after a retrieval task. Its other levels are left out of the required path (they add days units 2 and 6 do not have); its units 3 to 6 fall after 1920 and belong to US History II. The scope and sequence records each guide, kept or left out with the reason, in the course's source map, and every linked third-party reading is rights-checked on its own (the guides' licence does not cover them). The publisher asks that adaptations keep the content's design; the licence is CC BY 4.0 and the request is a courtesy, honoured where a unit adapts a whole lesson, with the usual credit. Primary sources (founding documents, treaties, court opinions, speeches, letters, the 1930s narratives of formerly enslaved people) come from public domain and open archives.
- **Native nations in the present tense.** Every unit that meets a Native nation says where it governs today and uses its own published materials (the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Lakota, Haudenosaunee, Odawa nations and Native Hawaiians among others), and court holdings that still stand are named (Worcester v. Georgia, McGirt v. Oklahoma, the 1980 Black Hills ruling).
- **Writing and research arc.** The research cycle is taught in full in unit 1 and used all year; the several-page sourced argument is written three times (unit 5 on why war came and on Reconstruction, and unit 9's empire research report written as the third, with a shorter argument in unit 1), each draft returned with feedback before the next. Unit 5 also teaches the sourced causal explanation (D4.2) as a companion section with its own criteria.
- **One example in depth.** us1-press-history, us1-jim-crow, us1-progressivism, us1-women-work-suffrage, us1-empire and us1-slavery-resistance ask for one case: a short survey of all options for everyone, one studied in depth, and survey pages complete enough that a student working alone needs no classmates' reports. us1-reform-movements is the one place where "the class shares findings": the survey pages carry each reform so a student alone meets them all.
- **Informed action.** Once in the year, in unit 8 (us1-progressivism): each student chooses one present-day local problem like one the Progressives took on and decides whether and how to act; the course picks no cause, and anything sent outside goes through the teacher or parent, using adult contact details.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh-agent sensitivity read, and every unit also needs a qualified reader from the community concerned before it ships: each holds Native or hard-history content (the Sullivan campaign, constitutional slavery protections, removal, slavery, racial terror and lynching, anti-Chinese violence, boarding schools, imperial war). Each unit's last column names its reads. Who reads, and any pay, is Cory's.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 band; 18th and 19th century prose glossed and excerpted with care; period texts that hold slurs are excerpted around them (rule 6), with a teacher note on reading aloud.
