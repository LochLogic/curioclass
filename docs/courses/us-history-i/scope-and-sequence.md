# Scope and sequence: US History I (1754 to 1920)

Course `us-history-i` (catalogue id `us-hist-1`), grade 11, full year (160 days), draft (2026-11-14). The days live in data/plans/us-history-i/days.csv; this page gives the judgment behind them.

The course meets every school day (50 minutes; a timer only where it has a grounded purpose), so course day n is school day n and the `school_day` column is blank. Week = (day - 1) // 5 + 1. Every unit closes with a one-day end-of-unit assessment built on unseen sources (bar item 7), and the next day is its respond day (the response time, counted once). No unit is over 25 days, so none holds a mid-unit assessment. Days with Native, slavery, racial terror, disability or hard-history content carry a sensitivity flag in the note column; the sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md) comes with each unit plan. Literacy codes (RH, WHST) are also-met and sit only in notes. Personal finance is touched once, inside day 127 (the 16th Amendment read on a sample pay stub), with no added day. Every end-of-unit assessment and course-level check asks about each part of each code it lists, with a short prompt per part; the day's goal in days.csv names the parts (the blueprint), with no day added. Where a unit's sustained product (the inquiry proposal, the reform paper, the essays, the empire report) was done on its own days, the assessment scores it as a named component.

The partial base is Since Time Immemorial (Washington OSPI, CC BY 4.0), on three days only: the one-period Level 1 constitutional case from its high school US history unit 1 (day 25) and the two-period Level 2 allotment analysis from its unit 2 (days 104 and 105). Their content stays as written, in our structure and tone, with one credit line saying the content is the tribes'. data/plans/us-history-i/source-map.csv records every Since Time Immemorial unit and level and why the rest are cut (more days than the units have, Washington's own assessments, or after 1920 and so US History II's).

Readiness checks come before units 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8, each on a grade 5, 8 or 9 row last used two or more grades back, given before the unit opens (Cory, 2026-11-14): in the last ten minutes of the respond day before it (days 19, 34, 50, 71, 96 and 122), and for unit 1 in the last ten minutes of day 1, after the hook, so each unit's first day opens on its question (unit 1's check adds grade 8's news literacy and grade 9's significance judgment, both last used in grade 9). Unit 7 and unit 9 open with retrieval tasks on World History II (the year before), and a row met earlier this year (unit 3's Native sovereignty on day 102, unit 6's Jim Crow on day 133, the reform ideas of day 55 on day 123, news literacy on day 144) is retrieved, not checked again. Each student keeps an enduring question and a research portfolio from day 2 to day 160.

The course shares one research product with ELA 11 in unit 4: the reform research paper. History supplies the question set and sources and scores historical reasoning; ELA teaches the process and citation. Milestones: question by the end of week 12 (day 56), source list in week 13 (day 61), draft in week 14 (day 66), paper and talk in week 15 (the talk in ELA 11's days; day 71 scores it here).

## Unit 1. Why break with Britain? 1754 to 1783 (days 1 to 19, weeks 1 to 4)

**Topics taught:** colonial self-government before 1763; the five source checks and an enduring question of one's own; supporting questions and lateral reading to choose sources; the economic, intellectual and cultural forces behind the Revolution; the French and Indian War inside the Seven Years' War; Pontiac's War, the Proclamation of 1763 and the Stamp Act read from three positions; the Tea and Intolerable Acts; Patriot responses; the causes in a chain closing with the Second Continental Congress and the Olive Branch Petition; the Declaration as an argument and its equality claim tested against 1776; the war from Lexington to Yorktown with the people in it (Loyalists, Black soldiers, Native nations, women); significance over time; a short sourced argument on whether the Revolution was radical.

Arc: four days on the inquiry and its tools (two scholarly interpretations compared, supporting questions tied to them, sources chosen with an expert appraisal), six on causes, three on the Declaration, four on the war and the argument. The research habits come first because the portfolio runs all year.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 1, after the hook, on grade 5's French and Indian War, grade 8's road to independence and news literacy, and grade 9's significance judgment (used again on day 16 with a worked example). Day 2 retrieves grades 9 and 10's source checks and contested questions.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 18: the causes weighed, the Declaration and the war, with a source-judging task on unseen sources; the inquiry proposal (days 2 to 4) and the source-selection rationale (day 13) are scored as its components. Respond day 19.
- Candidate: colonial self-government before 1763, kept, part of day 1, 0 added days. Candidate: the Second Continental Congress and the Olive Branch Petition, kept, part of day 10, 0 added days.

## Unit 2. A government on trial, 1781 to 1815 (days 20 to 34, weeks 4 to 7)

**Topics taught:** the Articles of Confederation and the Northwest Ordinance; Shays' Rebellion and the call for a convention; the Convention, its compromises and its leaders; the three-fifths, slave trade and fugitive slave clauses named plainly; ratification state by state; treaties as the supreme law of the land in a Washington case (Since Time Immemorial Level 1); the spread of information from the press to the Federalist essays, with one journalism case in depth; Washington's presidency (Hamilton's plan, the first parties, the Whiskey Rebellion, neutrality and the Jay Treaty); Adams (the Alien and Sedition Acts and the crisis with France; the election of 1800 and the first peaceful transfer of the presidency between rival parties in 1801); Jefferson (Marbury v. Madison with McCulloch recalled, the Louisiana Purchase); a sourced claim on the compelling question.

Arc: six days on making and ratifying the Constitution ending in the treaty clause case, three on the press, four on the first three presidencies and the claim. The constitutional case sits right after the supremacy clause it applies.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 19, on grade 8's Articles, Convention, ratification, press freedom and courts, and grade 5's first presidents. The Bill of Rights is recalled from grade 8, not retaught.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 33: the Articles, the Constitution and ratification, the press case and the first three presidencies' effects on federal power and foreign policy, with an unseen source. Respond day 34.
- Base: Since Time Immemorial high school US history unit 1, Level 1, on day 25.
- Candidate: the Northwest Ordinance, kept, part of day 21 beside Shays' Rebellion (day 20 opens the unit), 0 added days. Candidate: the Whiskey Rebellion, kept, part of day 29, 0 added days.

## Unit 3. Expansion and removal, 1800 to 1854 (days 35 to 47, weeks 7 to 10)

**Topics taught:** Jackson's presidency and the spoils system; the Nullification Crisis; the removal debate of 1829 to 1838 and the Removal Act's vote; Worcester v. Georgia and removal anyway, the forced marches; the five nations today as governments, with McGirt v. Oklahoma; expansion from the Louisiana Purchase to the Gadsden Purchase on maps; the War of 1812, Tecumseh and the Shawnee-led confederacy, and the Monroe Doctrine; Manifest Destiny from its 1840s sources; the war with Mexico and the 1848 treaty, with the people already living there; a map sequence from 1800 to 1854; who paid for growth.

Arc: five days on Jackson and removal (ending with the nations today), six on expansion ending in the map sequence and the compelling question. Removal first so the maps of expansion are read knowing whose land they show.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 34, on grade 5's Louisiana Purchase, War of 1812 and Native land and self-government.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 46: Jackson and removal, expansion and the war with Mexico, with a map-as-evidence task on unseen maps. Respond day 47, with review of units 1 to 3.
- Candidate: Tecumseh and the Shawnee-led confederacy, kept, part of day 41, 0 added days.

## Course-level check 1 (days 48 to 50, week 10)

## Unit 4. Mills, cotton and reform, 1800 to 1860 (days 51 to 71, weeks 11 to 15)

**Topics taught:** the Transportation Revolution; New England's textile and machinery industries; the women mill workers' own magazine and their strikes; Irish and German immigration and nativism; the Second Great Awakening and Republican Motherhood as roots of reform; every reform surveyed over two days (abolition; women's rights, Seneca Falls and wider schooling for women; Mann's public schools, literacy and print; schools for disabled students and asylums; the Transcendentalists), with temperance and prison reform as brief connected examples; a reform research question and source plan; the cotton gin and slavery's growth after 1800 with the domestic slave trade, compared with the Caribbean and Brazil; Northern mills, banks and insurers profiting from slavery; what the 1930s narratives of formerly enslaved people can and cannot show; excerpts of Douglass's Narrative; a primary source task on resistance; abolition and its opponents; the reform paper drafted, revised and finished.

Arc: four days on the market revolution (day 54 on immigration and nativism), two on the reform survey (day 55 opens with the Awakening and Republican Motherhood; day 56 ends with the question), three on slavery's economy, four on the resistance sources, then the paper and the compelling question, closing with the assessment and the paper scored. The research days fall on the shared brief's weeks, so the slavery and resistance days sit between the question and the draft.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 50 (course-level check 1's respond day), on grade 5's spread of slavery with cotton and the people who fought it.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 70: the market revolution with each part of T3.2, slavery's economy with the regional comparison, the religious and social trends, common survey questions on every reform strand, and an unseen source to judge; the resistance write-up (day 64) and the reform paper are its components. Respond day 71 (week 15), where the reform paper is also scored for historical reasoning. A school without ELA 11 hands the paper in on day 69; the talk is ELA's.
- Candidate: temperance and prison reform, kept as brief connected examples on the survey pages of day 55 (asylums are taught on day 56 under MA.USI.T4.2d), 0 added days. Candidate: the Lowell mill workers' magazine, kept, the source of day 53, 0 added days.

## Unit 5. The Union breaks and is rebuilt, 1820 to 1877 (days 72 to 92, weeks 15 to 19)

**Topics taught:** the Missouri Compromise and expansion into the Midwest; the Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act and Kansas-Nebraska; Dred Scott, the Lincoln-Douglas debates and John Brown's raid; the election of 1860 and the seceding states' own declarations; a sourced causal explanation; Lincoln's presidency and the Emancipation Proclamation; Davis, Lee and Grant, Antietam, Gettysburg and Vicksburg, the Homestead and railroad acts; the men and women who served, with the United States Colored Troops; the Lost Cause tested as history; the war's consequences; the 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments, the Freedmen's Bureau and what freedpeople built; Klan terror, the Colfax massacre and 1877; historians' accounts of Reconstruction critiqued; two several-page essays, each in three drafts.

Arc: six days on the crisis ending in essay 1's first draft, six on the war with drafts 2 and 3 between, seven on Reconstruction ending in essay 2's first draft (day 88), then the assessment (day 89) and its respond day (day 90), then essay 2's second and third drafts (days 91 and 92). Written feedback is returned between every draft: at least two school days before each second draft, and overnight feedback on one criterion before essay 2's final.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 71, on grade 5's causes of the Civil War and grade 8's constitutional issues.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 89: the sectional crisis, the war and Reconstruction, with a causal explanation on unseen sources of how and why Reconstruction ended; the two essays are its components (the composite is complete after day 92). Respond day 90; day 92 closes with review of units 1 to 4 for the mid-year check.
- Candidate: the Lincoln-Douglas debates and John Brown's raid, kept, part of day 74, 0 added days. Candidate: the Homestead Act and the 1862 railroad acts, kept, part of day 79, 0 added days.

## Unit 6. After Reconstruction: Jim Crow and the West, 1865 to 1900 (days 97 to 109, weeks 20 to 22)

**Topics taught:** the Jim Crow system (segregation laws, disfranchisement); racial terror and lynching from the record; one Jim Crow practice in depth (school segregation or Plessy v. Ferguson); what Black Americans built (historically Black colleges, and Dunbar and Chesnutt from short excerpts); Huckleberry Finn's impact from a short slur-free excerpt; the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, the Black Hills, the 1980 ruling and the Lakota nations today; the Plains wars and Wounded Knee (1890); the Dawes Act and allotment from the sources (Since Time Immemorial Level 2, two periods); boarding schools from the federal government's 2022 report and survivors' accounts; the 14th Amendment's reach (Elk v. Wilkins, Wong Kim Ark, Yick Wo v. Hopkins), with the Chinese and Mexican American West on the survey pages as the cases' setting.

Arc: five days on Jim Crow and Black responses, six on the West through treaties, war, allotment and boarding schools, ending in the citizenship comparison. The compelling question is answered inside the assessment, so the unit has no separate claim day.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 96 (the mid-year check's respond day), on grade 5's Black life under Jim Crow. Day 104 opens with a retrieval task on unit 3's removal and grade 5's Native sovereignty before the allotment analysis.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 108: one Jim Crow practice, Black responses, and the West from treaties and laws, answering the compelling question with unseen sources. Respond day 109.
- Base: Since Time Immemorial high school US history unit 2, Level 2, on days 104 and 105.
- Candidate: the Chinese and Mexican American West, kept, on the survey pages of day 107 (Chinese exclusion is taught on day 118), 0 added days. Candidate: the Populist movement and the Omaha Platform, dropped: no day is free in unit 6; the Omaha Platform is named on day 123 as context for Progressivism only.

## Unit 7. Industry and newcomers, 1865 to 1920 (days 110 to 122, weeks 22 to 25)

**Topics taught:** the causes of industrial growth after 1865 (war demand, railroads, technology, inventors and entrepreneurs); supply and demand for steel and oil prices; trusts and the distrust of monopolies; cities, tenements, pollution and political machines; unions and the great strikes (1877, Haymarket, Homestead, Pullman) with the Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911 as the bridge to reform; why Germans, Irish, Italians and Eastern Europeans came, from letters; Chinese, Korean and Japanese immigrants and their work; Ellis Island, nativism, the Chinese Exclusion Act and the Rock Springs massacre; photographs and interviews as sources; who built industrial America.

Arc: six days on industry ending in one cause and consequence chain, five on immigration ending in the compelling question. Industry first because the immigrants are the workforce it drew.

- Readiness: none. Day 110 retrieves World History II's Industrial Revolution; day 116 its imperialism unit.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 121: industry's causes and consequences and immigration, with unseen sources. Respond day 122.
- Candidate: political machines, kept, part of day 113, 0 added days; a separate Gilded Age party-system treatment dropped to protect the industry and immigration days. Candidate: the Triangle Shirtwaist fire, kept, part of day 114, 0 added days.

## Unit 8. The Progressive Era, 1890 to 1920 (days 123 to 139, weeks 25 to 28)

**Topics taught:** what Progressivism meant, with the Omaha Platform as context; a Progressive's own text or photographs (Jane Addams, Upton Sinclair, Jacob Riis or Theodore Roosevelt), with the Triangle fire recalled; how investigative journalists developed and checked facts; one Progressive policy researched and judged (child labour laws, the Pure Food and Drug Act, the 16th and 17th Amendments, the initiative, referendum and recall); the Progressives' support for eugenics and segregation; women entering paid work; one women's political organization; suffrage, its opponents and the 19th Amendment; who still could not vote after 1920; Booker T. Washington and W. E. B. Du Bois; the Niagara Movement, the NAACP and Ida B. Wells; how far Black Americans reached their goals by 1920, with the Red Summer of 1919; an informed action on one present-day local problem, taken and looked back on.

Arc: six days on Progressivism and its limits, four on women, three on Black civil rights, then two on the year's informed action. Day 126 starts the action (each student notes a present-day local problem like the one the researched policy targeted); day 136 investigates it and compares the options; day 137 decides and takes the action through an adult (or records a reasoned choice not to act); day 160 looks back. When Government runs the same year and supplies the action, days 136 and 137 revisit its evidence, compare it with Progressive-era responses and evaluate the outcome. The limits (eugenics, segregation, who could not vote) close each part so no reform reads as complete.

- Readiness: the last ten minutes of day 122, on grade 5's women's rights work around the 14th and 15th Amendments; day 123 retrieves World History II's industrial-era reform ideas (met on day 55). Day 133 retrieves grade 5's Jim Crow row and unit 6.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 138: Progressivism, women's work and suffrage, and civil rights in the early 1900s, with unseen sources. Respond day 139.
- Candidate: the 16th and 17th Amendments and the initiative, referendum and recall, kept, on the survey pages of day 126, 0 added days. Candidate: the Red Summer of 1919, kept, part of day 135, 0 added days.

## Unit 9. Empire, news and the First World War, 1867 to 1920 (days 140 to 155, weeks 28 to 31)

**Topics taught:** expansion from 1867 to 1914 (Alaska, Hawaii's annexation, with Native Hawaiians and Alaska Native peoples today); the Spanish-American and Philippine-American Wars, Cuba, Puerto Rico and Guam; the Roosevelt Corollary, the Monroe Doctrine recalled, the Panama Canal; anti-imperialist arguments of 1898 to 1900 and a report question; news against opinion; lateral reading on one current website; assertion against verification in the Maine coverage; the empire report in three drafts; why the United States entered the First World War; its role (the Harlem Hellfighters, the home front); the influenza pandemic and the Fourteen Points; a class debate on joining the League of Nations.

Arc: four days on empire ending in the report question, three on news reading (the third is also the report's research day), then the war woven between the report's drafts, with the final draft due day 153 (week 31). The news days sit before the war so its propaganda is read with them.

- Readiness: none. Day 140 retrieves World History II's imperialism; day 148 its First World War.
- End-of-unit assessment, day 154: the empire report (day 153) as its component, news against opinion and assertion against verification on an unseen 1898-style pair, and the First World War. A site's sponsors and credibility (NML.T4.1, T4.2) are tested on day 158 on earlier content. Respond day 155, with review of the year.
- Candidate: anti-imperialist arguments, kept, part of day 143, 0 added days. The Red Summer of 1919 is kept in unit 8 (day 135).

## Shorter path

The year is 160 days of 50 minutes. Two shorter routes keep every owned code, skill and assessment, every readiness check, all three argument cycles, the research portfolio's eight sources and the shared paper's milestones, and the history path for a school without ELA 11; the unit plans check them against real minutes, so neither is yet shown to fit.

- **45-minute periods, same 160 days.** Each teaching day keeps its source work, its product and its exit ticket, and uses the shorter excerpt of each source, trims the second worked example and shortens the whole-class share; drafting days keep their full writing time and lose the opening model instead; assessment and respond days stay whole, and no day drops its second account of a contested event.
- **About 155 days.** These days fold into a neighbour, because each code they carry is taught on another day too: 8 (the Tea and Intolerable Acts, into 7 and 9), 27 (the journalism survey, into 28, the chosen case), 44 (the map sequence, into 40 and 45), 115 (the industry chain, into 120's claim), and 119 (photographs and interviews, into 116 to 118).
