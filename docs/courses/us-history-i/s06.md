- **S1, Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education, 1899, court record and opinion:** Black taxpayers challenged funding white high schools after their public high school closed. The board said the money served more Black primary pupils. The Supreme Court declined the requested relief. A stated budget justification did not give those pupils equal secondary access. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/175/528), opening petition and concluding reasoning, public domain. Reserve this case entirely.
- **S2, Brown v. Board of Education, 1954, Court's conclusion:** Racially separate public schooling denied equal protection even where tangible resources were equal. [Transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/brown-v-board-of-education), final education reasoning, public domain. Day 99 teaches the holding; reserve this passage.
- **S3, Howard's congressional charter, March 2, 1867:** The university was authorized to teach liberal arts and sciences. Its named departments included teacher education, law, medicine, agriculture and theology. A charter authorizes provision; it does not document attendance or employment. [Sections 1 and 5](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-14/pdf/STATUTE-14-Pg438.pdf), printed pages 438 and 439, public domain. Day 100 uses Fisk's history instead. Add a labelled context sentence identifying Howard as a historically Black university.
- **S4, anonymous review, Cambridge Tribune, December 23, 1899:** The reviewer welcomed Dunbar and Chesnutt as authors representing Black people's own experiences and read Chesnutt's stories as exposing racial injustice. This is one reviewer's assessment, not evidence that racial inequality ended. [Original review](https://chesnuttarchive.org/item/ccda.rev00221), The Cambridge Tribune 22 (December 23, 1899): 2; page loaded and matched 2026-11-17; public-domain original, modern editorial material excluded. Its period terms are not reproduced; quote only the sentence crediting Dunbar and Chesnutt, with the term replaced. Day 100 uses Dunbar's poem and Chesnutt's story, not this review.
- **S5 and S6, opposing reviews reprinted in The Critic, March 28, 1885:** A Springfield reviewer supported the Concord library's rejection of Huckleberry Finn as morally harmful. A Saturday Review writer praised its humor, tenderness and Huck's struggle over betraying Jim. These judgments conflict; neither measures changes in readers' conduct. [Transcripts](https://twain.lib.virginia.edu/huckfinn/concordcon.html), public-domain newspaper originals, modern notes excluded. Use original summaries without slurs; if one sentence is quoted (Huck's struggle over betraying Jim), replace the period term with [Jim] and do not read the page's wording aloud. Day 101 instead uses chapter 31 and the April 4 Hartford Courant defense, which challenges unsupported claims that libraries unanimously rejected the book. Distinguish its antebellum setting from its later publication and reception. Later racial-language challenges are different from these early objections; their exact source selection remains to make.
- **W1, Fort Laramie Treaty, 1868, article XII:** A land cession required signatures from three-fourths of the adult men occupying or interested in the reservation. [Treaty transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/fort-laramie-treaty), public domain. Teach article II on 102; reserve XII.
- **W2, United States v. Sioux Nation, 1980, factual record and holding:** The Court records that only about one-tenth of adult men signed the 1876 agreement implemented by Congress in 1877. It upheld compensation with interest for the taking of the Black Hills. It did not order the land returned. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/448/371), 448 U.S. 371, public domain. Quote the consent sentence and the holding sentence from the Court's opinion, not the reporter's syllabus; part and page to be chosen. Teach the holding on 102; reserve these consent figures for comparison with W1.
- **W3, Dawes Act, 1887, section 5:** After allotment, the Secretary could negotiate purchases of remaining reservation land, requiring congressional ratification; land released was directed toward settler homes. This made land available beyond the individual allotments. [Transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/dawes-act), public domain. Use other sections and quotations on 104 and 105; reserve this provision for the assessment. The law states a mechanism, not its full results.
- **C1, 14th Amendment, section 1:** Citizenship is defined through birth or naturalization and jurisdiction. State due-process and equal-protection duties concern persons. [Text](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/14th-amendment), public domain. Supply this familiar reference beside new applications.
- **C2, paired case passages:** [Elk v. Wilkins](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/112/94), 1884, rejected Elk's claimed automatic citizenship after leaving his tribal community; the opinion also acknowledges citizenship through express treaties and statutes. [Wong Kim Ark](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/169/649), 1898, upheld citizenship at birth for a San Francisco-born man whose Chinese parents were domiciled residents, not diplomats. Reserve the opinions' comparisons of citizenship routes; day 107 uses their facts and holdings.
- **C3, Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 1886:** The Court held that discriminatory enforcement of a laundry ordinance violated equal protection, including for Chinese subjects. Citizenship was not required for that protection. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/118/356), concluding application to discriminatory enforcement, public domain. Reserve this passage; teach with the opening case facts.
- **C4, citizenship statutes:** The [1855 act, section 2](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-10/pdf/STATUTE-10-Pg604.pdf) treated a woman eligible for naturalization who married a citizen as a citizen. The [1870 act, section 7](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-16/pdf/STATUTE-16-Pg256.pdf) extended existing naturalization laws to African nativity and descent. Give the existing white-person eligibility rule as context. Teacher note, from memory, source to add before use (Revised Statutes section 2169): Chinese immigrants stayed excluded from naturalization; unit 7 owns the exclusion laws. European immigrants were not automatically citizens simply by arriving. These are public-domain statutes.
- **C5, Minor v. Happersett, 1875:** A woman was a citizen, but the Court held citizenship did not itself confer suffrage. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/88/162), concluding distinction, public domain. On 107 teach with a male European naturalization example. Reserve C4 and C5 for this changed application: a German-born woman eligible for naturalization married a citizen in Missouri in 1890. Was she a citizen, did she have protection as a person, and did citizenship alone entitle her to vote? Answer: yes, yes, no. These historical rules are dated, not presented as present law.

**Teacher background for the section plans (dated lines checked 2026-11-14):**

- **Birthright citizenship, days 107 and 108.** As of November 2026: in Trump v. Barbara (No. 25-365, decided June 30, 2026) the Supreme Court held that children born in the United States to parents unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the 14th Amendment, so the 2025 executive order limiting birthright citizenship was unconstitutional. The Court kept the rule of Wong Kim Ark. The judgment was 6 to 3 (Chief Justice Roberts wrote for five justices; a sixth agreed with the judgment).
