## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Who wins and who loses when a country trades more?"
- **Hook, day 52.** Contrast Anthony Stone's lost steel job with cheaper imported materials for buyers. Ask the compelling question before teaching trade's benefits.
- **Supporting questions.** Write together: Why specialize (52)? What can observed trade prove (53)? Who bears protection's costs (54)? What does a stronger currency buy (55)? How are gains and resources distributed (56)?
- **Closing claim.** Day 56: answer the compelling question in two paragraphs using the trade table, tariff study and copper sources below. Explain a mechanism, distinguish national totals from distribution, answer a counterclaim and name missing evidence.
- **Teacher background, tariffs today.** Read frame.md, "Teacher background, as of November 2026", unit 7, before teaching.
- **Teaching moves.** Model opportunity-cost ratios on 52, sourcing on 53 and a sourced argument on 54; withdraw worked examples on 56. Source every card and corroborate accounts with data. Teach specialization, absolute and comparative advantage, tariff, quota, trade balance, balance of payments, exchange rate and labor productivity, with constant-cost assumptions, adjustment costs and reciprocal exchange rates.

**Withheld pack, supplied with the assessment.**

- **A, invented production possibilities.** With equal fixed labor endowments, A can produce 96 tiles or 48 remote design visits; B can produce 36 tiles or 27 visits. Assume constant opportunity costs, transferable labor within each country, no transport costs and no other goods. A visit is a service delivered across borders. Intermediate combinations lie on straight frontiers.
- **B, unseen primary trade data.** The federal goods-trade table for Chile, 2023 and 2024, reports United States exports of 19,272.5 and 18,370.0 million dollars, imports of 15,579.8 and 16,413.0, and balances of 3,692.7 and 1,957.0. These are nominal goods values, not seasonally adjusted; they exclude services and household incomes. Compare changes without treating nominal values as quantities or causal evidence. Teaching uses 2021 and 2022 only.
- **C, invented policy proposal.** A heater tariff is proposed to preserve domestic production capacity. A model predicts higher domestic output and import prices, while heater buyers and businesses using heaters face higher costs. It omits retaliation and the value of emergency capacity. Distinguish predictions from observations.
- **D, unseen primary productivity table, a dated historical table.** The December 6, 2012 federal manufacturing comparison, table 1, supplies item 9's two annual observations for each country. The statistics bureau has since discontinued this international comparison; the card says so and gives the table's date, so students read it as a historical record, not current data. Its technical notes warn that these United States estimates differ from the regular domestic productivity release. Teaching uses the separate 2026 international compendium, not this table.
- **E, unseen copper evidence.** Original summary of Chile's copper commission's 2024 water report, executive summary: surveyed operations account for 97.9% of national copper production. Operational water consumption increased 2.7% and copper production 4.8% from 2023. Reported water extraction rose 20.8%, partly reflecting broader reporting coverage. This does not establish every community's water access or mining's sole causal effect. Teaching uses the 2023 report only; do not show this summary beforehand.
- **F, invented application, not a Chilean policy record.** A Chilean mining town's mining workers' average real earnings rise from 100 to 108 index points while other residents' remain at 100. United States copper users report cheaper inputs. (Real note on this invented card: semi-finished copper products have faced a 50% United States tariff since 2025; refined copper was exempt when this card was written.) Residents request water monitoring and participation in allocation decisions; workers seek bargaining protections. A proposed export contract guarantees copper supply in exchange for financing recycling and water monitoring. Local and national authorities dispute how to allocate scarce water. Both countries dispute the contract's costs. No actual court ruling or community position is represented by this card.

**Teacher judgment.** Record each component as secure, partial or missing. Judge evidence and reasoning, not agreement with a policy. For every open item, use these look-fors and contrasting samples:

- **1.** Three scales and an opportunity-cost mechanism. Meets: "People can focus on lower-cost work; regions and countries can do likewise and exchange for more varied consumption." Does not: "Trade makes resources unlimited."
- **2.** Both absolute advantages, four ratios and a beneficial exchange. Meets: "A is absolutely better at both. Tile costs are half and three-quarters of a visit; visit costs are two and four-thirds of a tile. Three tiles for two visits improves on each country's own production cost." Does not: "B cannot gain because it produces less."
- **3.** Analyze goods and services using relative costs; separate the trade observations. Meets: "A gives up fewer visits per tile and B fewer tiles per visit, so both can gain. Source B has values, not opportunity costs." Does not: "A surplus proves superiority."
- **4.** Distinct tax and ceiling definitions. Meets: "The tariff taxes imports at 18% of value; the quota limits annual imports to 2,000 heaters." Does not: "A quota taxes each heater."
- **5.** Two reasons and a cost. Meets: "A government may protect jobs and strategic capacity, but buyers pay more and exporters may face retaliation." Does not: "Barriers always create national wealth."
- **6.** Evidence, opposing interests and a qualified judgment. Meets: "I prefer free trade because the model predicts higher buyer costs under protection. Capacity matters too, so I need evidence of emergency needs and alternatives before ruling out a targeted measure." Does not: "Tariffs are good because I like local jobs."
- **7.** Correct balance, account scope and classification. Meets: "The trade deficit is 5. Income and remittances affect the current account, while bonds enter the financial account; the balance of payments is broader." Does not: "Bond sales cancel imports in the goods balance."
- **8.** Both computations and reciprocal effect. Meets: "The item falls from 60 to 50 dollars; the other rises from 45,000 to 54,000 pesos. The same exchange movement helps dollar buyers abroad and hurts peso buyers of dollar goods." Does not: "A stronger dollar makes everything cheaper for everyone."
- **9.** Compare every trend with units and limits. Meets: "All slowed; the declines in growth were 9.2, 1.8 and 3.1 percentage points. German growth was fastest in the second interval, but levels and effort remain unknown." Does not: "The United States became 9.2% less productive."
- **10.** Six labeled effects, both nations, observed evidence distinguished from the scenario. Meets: "Trade can support Chilean output and United States input users. F suggests unequal earnings and disputed participation and bargaining rights. E shows production and water consumption rising together, not household welfare. Resources and income need distribution evidence in both countries." Does not: "The surplus proves both countries' citizens gained equally."
- **11.** Explain conflict and cooperation at both scales and evaluate a response. Meets: "Water allocation can divide domestic users, while monitoring could support cooperation if residents participate. International supply dependence can produce disputes or a financing agreement; we need its costs and enforcement terms to judge it." Does not: "Scarcity means war, and contracts solve everything."
