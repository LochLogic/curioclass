## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do we know whether the economy is doing well, and for whom?"
- **Hook, day 37.** An invented dashboard shows sales rising while quantities stay unchanged and job seekers remain unemployed. Students propose what else they need before calling this prosperity.
- **Supporting questions.** Write together: What counts as production (day 37)? What do measures miss (day 38)? Why can prices rise (day 39)? What can indicators predict (day 40)? How do choices connect (day 41)? How have events changed cycles (day 42)?
- **Closing claim.** Day 42 revises day 40's short argument using the September employment release, measurement guides and historical accounts below. Answer the compelling question, address a counterclaim and identify missing evidence. Day 43 transfers this reasoning to withheld sources.
- **Teaching moves.** Teach real and nominal, final output, labor force, price index, aggregate, frictional, structural and cyclical. Model sourcing and circular flow on day 37, denominators on day 38, graph axes on day 39 and conditional forecasts on day 40. Students lead comparisons on day 42. Teacher background distinguishes accounting identities from causes, price levels from inflation rates, and output from welfare.

**Sources A to C, current snapshot as of November 14, 2026.** These are original summaries of the linked federal releases, not invented observations. A, the national economic accounts agency, October 29 advance release: third-quarter real GDP grew at a 3.4% seasonally adjusted annual rate, about 0.8% over the quarter; investment, consumption and government purchases increased, while exports decreased and imports increased. B, the labor statistics agency, November 6 release: October unemployment was 4.1%; payroll jobs rose by 165,000. The unemployment rate counts people, while payroll jobs come from a different survey. C, the same agency's November 10 release: October consumer prices rose 0.4% from September, seasonally adjusted. No all-items annual comparison was published because the comparable October 2025 index was unavailable. These observations have different periods and can be revised; do not equate annualized quarterly growth with monthly change. They do not locate output relative to potential or establish whose welfare improved. Refresh observations, dates and keys before future teaching; preserve this dated version for reproducibility.

**Source D, invented measurement exercise for one economy.** Annual final expenditure is household consumption 87, investment 23, government purchases 31, exports 17 and imports 26, all in money units. A separate transfer of 7 is not a purchase. The previous year's nominal GDP was 120 and its GDP price index 100; the current index is 110 on the same base. There are 144 employed people, 16 available people actively seeking work, and 40 others outside the labor force. A fixed consumer basket cost 200 last year and 212 this year. A person applying between jobs, a worker whose occupation was displaced by automation, and a worker laid off during economy-wide contraction provide three classification cases.

**Sources E and F, historical evidence withheld until day 43.** E is our summary of the institutional history linked in field 10, a secondary account by Gary Richardson: following U.S. entry into World War II in December 1941, military procurement expanded and financing supported purchases of ships, aircraft and other war goods and recruitment of millions of service members. This identifies demand and resource reallocation, not a controlled estimate of the war's effects. F is primary testimony from Betty Reid Soskin (1921 to 2025), a Black wartime union clerk, reflecting later on a public-history planning meeting. In the park service's published transcript she recalls being "the only person of color in the room" and recognizing "sites of racial segregation." This recollection challenges an inclusive-prosperity claim; it cannot measure national output. The underlying testimony date is not established on the page, so do not label it wartime testimony. Both sources address wartime experience from different positions.

- **3.** Look for both causes, mechanisms and conditional gains and losses. Meets: "Spending can outpace capacity; input costs can reduce supply. Unexpected inflation reduces fixed debt's real value, helping its borrower relative to its lender, while fixed cash loses purchasing power." Does not: "Inflation helps everyone who owes anything."
- **4.** Look for dated evidence from A to C, a conditional forecast, a counterclaim and a limit. Meets: "Quarter-three output expanded at a 3.4% annualized rate, October unemployment was 4.1%, and monthly prices rose 0.4%. Continued spending could support output, but these different periods cannot guarantee next quarter's jobs or everyone's welfare." Does not: "Growth proves everyone is better off and prices will fall."
- **5.** Look for all three actors and links to income, employment and prices. Meets: "Reduced household visits, firms' investment and bridge purchases cut receipts. Firms may cut jobs and payments to households, further reducing demand. Prices may be lower than otherwise if supply stays unchanged." Does not: "Government creates all income by printing money."
- **6.** Look for both labelled diagrams and unemployment reasoning. Meets: "The axes are real GDP and the overall price level. Demand falling lowers both along unchanged short-run supply and threatens jobs. Supply falling lowers output but raises the price level. This illustrates price pressure, not a continuing measured rate." Does not: "The horizontal axis is the price of meals, and every price rise means more jobs."
