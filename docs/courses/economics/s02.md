## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Do prices set by markets serve people better than prices set by law?"
- **Hook, day 9.** Two fictional bicycle-rental notices contrast changing prices with capped prices and waiting lists. Ask which serves people better and whose experience could differ. Keep competing answers visible.
- **Supporting questions.** Write these together, allowing revisions: What do curves represent (day 9)? How do prices coordinate choices (days 10 and 11)? What changes equilibrium (days 12 and 13)? How responsive are quantities (day 14)? Who gains access under controls (day 15)? What evidence would justify a policy (day 16)?
- **Closing claim.** Day 16 answers the compelling question using the two wage reviews below, with a counterclaim. Day 17 transfers the argument to unseen sources A and D. Either policy conclusion can earn full credit.
- **Teaching moves.** Model author, date, purpose and limits before reading. Teach demand, supply, equilibrium, shift, elasticity, ceiling, floor and marginal explicitly. Model graphs on days 9 and 10; compare markets on day 11; withdraw examples on day 13. Distinguish prediction from observation on day 15; students corroborate evidence and write independently on day 16.

**Unseen source B, fictional woodworking labor schedule.** At hourly wages 18, 24 and 30, employers demand 44, 32 and 20 labor hours per week, while workers supply 20, 32 and 44. A training program increases hours offered by 24 at every wage, only for item 7. No inference about workers' worth follows from the price of labor.

**Unseen source C, fictional market for otherwise identical one-year loans.** At annual interest rates 2%, 4% and 6%, borrowers demand 90, 70 and 50 thousand money units; lenders supply 50, 70 and 90 thousand. New investment plans increase borrowing demand by 40 thousand at each rate, only for item 7. Risk and maturity are fixed.

**Unseen source D, fictional council policy memo and interested statements.** The council proposes capping A's rental price at 12 to improve affordability. A user supports cheaper access; a supplier warns of offering fewer rentals. These are forecasts, not measured effects, and describe only the additional shift, not the whole policy. The memo does not establish who obtains the limited rentals.
