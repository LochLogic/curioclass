## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Do prices set by markets serve people better than prices set by law?"
- **Hook, day 9.** Two fictional bicycle-rental notices: changing prices against capped prices and waiting lists. Which serves people better, and whose experience differs? Keep competing answers visible.
- **Supporting questions.** What do curves represent (9)? How do prices coordinate choices (10, 11)? What changes equilibrium (12, 13)? How responsive are quantities (14)? Who gains access under controls (15)? What evidence would justify a policy (16)?
- **Closing claim.** Day 16 answers the compelling question using the two wage reviews below, with a counterclaim. Day 17 transfers the argument to unseen sources A and D. Either policy conclusion can earn full credit.
- **Teaching moves.** Source every card before reading. Teach demand, supply, equilibrium, shift, elasticity, ceiling, floor and marginal. Model graphs on 9 and 10; compare markets on 11; withdraw examples on 13; prediction against observation on 15; students corroborate and write alone on 16.

**Unseen source A, fictional tool-rental market register.** Prices are money units per tool per week; quantities are weekly rentals of identical tools. At prices 12, 18 and 24, quantities demanded are 72, 54 and 36; quantities supplied are 36, 54 and 72. A new repair method increases quantity supplied by 36 at every price. Apply that change only in item 7. All other questions use the original schedule. These are illustrative competitive-market schedules, not observations.

**Unseen source B, fictional woodworking labor schedule.** At hourly wages 18, 24 and 30, employers demand 44, 32 and 20 labor hours per week, while workers supply 20, 32 and 44. A training program increases hours offered by 24 at every wage, only for item 7. No inference about workers' worth follows from the price of labor.

**Unseen source C, fictional market for otherwise identical one-year loans.** At annual interest rates 2%, 4% and 6%, borrowers demand 90, 70 and 50 thousand money units; lenders supply 50, 70 and 90 thousand. New investment plans increase borrowing demand by 40 thousand at each rate, only for item 7. Risk and maturity are fixed.

**Unseen source D, fictional council policy memo and interested statements.** The council proposes capping A's rental price at 12 to improve affordability. A user supports cheaper access; a supplier warns of offering fewer rentals. The policy office estimates that one additional allocation clerk shift would produce benefits valued at 31 and use resources costing 37. These are forecasts, not measured effects, and describe only the additional shift, not the whole policy. The memo does not establish who obtains the limited rentals. Author, purpose and assumptions must accompany each printed source.
