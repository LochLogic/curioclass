## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Who should fight a recession or inflation: Congress or the Federal Reserve?"
- **Hook, day 45.** The December 2008 policy minutes: purchases and jobs fell as credit tightened. Students ask which institution could interrupt that chain, before money is taught.
- **Supporting questions.** What makes money useful (45)? How do banks change balances (46)? Who controls taxes and spending (47)? How do monetary tools change demand (48)? Which response fits which evidence (49)?
- **Closing claim.** Day 49: about a page answering the compelling question from the 2010 majority and Hoenig dissent, the June 2022 statement, one or two quoted sentences of the Congressional Budget Office's estimates of the 2009 Recovery Act, and the abstract of one published skeptic (Taylor, 2011). The 2008 minutes stay with the hook, so the pack is no longer. Before defending either actor or a joint response, cite at least one fiscal record and one central-bank record. Answer a counterclaim; state a limit.
- **Teacher background, the Federal Reserve today.** Read frame.md, "Teacher background, as of November 2026", unit 6, before teaching.
- **Teaching moves.** Model sourcing and a causal chain on 45, a loan ledger on 46, a sourced policy paragraph on 47; partners compare mechanisms on 48, ending with an eight-minute May and June M2 moment; withdraw models on 49. Teach medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, liquidity, deposit, reserves, fiscal policy, monetary policy and stabilization. Day 48: administered rates hold the target; reserve requirements, now zero, are not a tool. Keep deposits apart from reserves, goals from guaranteed outcomes, and demand stabilization from long-run capacity.


- **D2.Eco.12.9-12:** Evaluate choices of fiscal and monetary policy across different economic conditions. Parts: evaluate each policy type; compare conditions.
- **D2.Eco.10.9-12:** Use current observations to explain how spending, production and money-supply changes influence economic conditions. Parts: use data; explain each influence. The local file lists codes only; the [published indicator table, page 38](https://www.socialstudies.org/sites/default/files/c3/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.pdf) supplies the wording. Unit 5 teaches and checks spending and production; this unit completes money supply.
- **Limits.** No money-multiplier formula, trading advice or exact forecasts. Current definitions replace obsolete textbook ones. Reserve requirements are zero (checked November 14, 2026); banks still face capital, liquidity, risk and borrower-demand limits.
- **Carried.** Keep unit 5's circular flow and aggregate graph and unit 4's public budget and claim-evidence-reasoning. Add bank credit on 46; tie taxes, purchases and interest-sensitive spending to their arrows on 47 and 48. Students rebuild the diagrams alone in day 51's transfer.

**Teacher judgment and samples.** Score each named component separately as secure, partial or missing; policy agreement earns no credit by itself.

- **1.** Look for three functions correctly connected. Meets: "Paying for rehearsal uses exchange; comparing rental prices uses a unit of account; keeping earnings for repairs stores purchasing power, imperfectly if prices rise." Does not: "Money makes instruments."
- **2.** Look for correct membership and no double counting. Meets: "Public cash and both deposit types enter M1 and M2; the small time deposit and retail fund add to M2. Shares and unused credit are excluded." Does not: "Only notes count."
- **3.** Look for institutional roles, balanced entries and a lending constraint. Meets: "Banks and credit unions provide payments and credit; insurers pool risk; investment funds pool savings. A new loan adds a loan asset and matching deposit liability, not free wealth. Capital limits lending." Does not: "Banks lend without limit because deposits cost nothing."
- **4.** Look for the governing board, regional banks, policy committee and distinct work. Meets: "The Board governs, twelve Reserve Banks operate regionally, and the committee sets monetary policy. The system supports payments, supervision and stability; it does not enact taxes." Does not: "Twelve private banks vote taxes."
- **5.** Look for both definitions, owners and mechanisms. Meets: "Federal fiscal policy uses taxes and spending through Congress and the President; monetary policy uses the central bank's interest and credit tools to influence demand." Does not: "Both mean government spending."
- **6.** Look for both tax and spending directions, all three goals, and a constraint. Meets: "In R, temporary purchases or tax reductions can support demand, jobs and real output, with inflation risk if capacity tightens. In I, lower purchases or higher taxes restrain demand but can slow growth and hiring; productive investment can expand capacity later. Delays and borrowing costs matter." Does not: "Cut taxes in both cases because growth always eliminates inflation."
- **7.** Look for each tool's transmission, the near-zero limit in R and the tightening direction in I. Meets: "In R the rate is near zero, so bond purchases can lower longer yields and lending to banks can preserve liquidity and payments. In I, a higher rate target, supported by higher administered rates, restrains borrowing. Neither guarantees full employment or lasting productivity growth." Does not: "Every extra dollar of reserves becomes a loan."
- **8.** Look for both conditions, source-specific evidence, an alternative and uncertainty. Meets: "R's weak demand supports fiscal purchases or bond purchases; the 2012 committee sought easier financing when hiring was weak. I calls for restraint, as the 2023 committee judged, but a port closure also needs a supply response. Fiscal action can target needs; monetary action can move faster, yet effects lag. Either can impose costs." Does not: "The central bank is always right because it issued the statements."
- **9.** Look for dated observations, a mechanism and a causal limit. Meets: "August to September M2 rose by 113.0 billion dollars in the October 27 release. More deposit money can support spending and, with spare capacity, production and hiring; near capacity, prices may face pressure. This rise alone proves neither stimulus nor inflation." Does not: "Money rose, so prices must rise by the same percentage immediately."
