**P1 and P2 samples.** Meets P1 analysis: "Washington's rise initially makes economic progress seem a path to recognition. The three concessions and subsequent losses complicate that path: civic standing becomes a condition for durable economic and educational progress. The final qualified support joins aims that the program separated." Separate summary: "Du Bois describes Washington's rise and program, acknowledges its appeal and criticizes its concessions on rights and education. He connects those concessions with losses and argues for training alongside political rights and higher education." Meets P2: "Du Bois says Washington's teachings accelerated, rather than directly caused, disfranchisement and lost support for higher education. He explains two distinct dependencies: workers and property owners need voting rights for protection, while industrial schools need teachers trained in colleges. Washington's concessions undermine the conditions his program needs." Does not meet P1: "Education is the topic" (A). Does not meet P2: "Washington rose, rights were lost, and Du Bois disagreed" (D).

**P3 samples.** Meets: "Du Bois's acknowledgment of Washington's achievement near the opening clarifies their common economic aim for informed readers, makes the later objections more credible and engages them through a disagreement within shared commitments. An unfamiliar reader gains needed context and a reason to hear the criticism rather than dismiss it. The later numbered losses clarify the stakes and give that reader concrete grounds for conviction and continued attention; informed readers can test the proposed connection rather than merely recognize names. Woolf's contrasting meals in chapter 1 turn unequal resources into visible experiences. Informed readers see how familiar institutions shape thought; the contrast clarifies the claim, makes material inequality tangible and engages through sensory detail. For unfamiliar readers, that same sequence supplies context and an accessible reason to follow the argument. Chapter 3's historical search followed by the invented sister clarifies the documentary gap for both audiences, makes the proposed constraint imaginable and sustains interest through a life story. Informed readers may find the illustration persuasive as a hypothesis; unfamiliar readers need the invention identified to avoid mistaking engagement for historical proof." Does not meet: "Both authors have better flow" (E).

**M1 samples.** Meets: "Wollstonecraft links independent inquiry with equal companionship. Adult answers can produce reliance, while peer inquiry encourages thought and trust. Her criticism of existing schools complicates that connection; the combined arrangement preserves family affection and peer exchange." Separate objective summary: "Wollstonecraft argues that dependence on adult answers limits inquiry and that exchange among equals can foster independent thought. She criticizes existing schools as well as isolation at home, then proposes combining peer education with family affection, illustrated by the remembered day-school child." Does not meet: "Education." That substitutes a topic for two interacting ideas (A).

**M2 samples.** Meets: "The parent cannot become an age-mate. Reliance on adult answers limits inquiry, while exchanges among equals permit disagreement and confidence. Her movement from rejecting existing schools to combining settings develops the proposal; the recalled day-school child connects intellectual and domestic life." Does not meet: "First there are parents, then there are schools, then a boy goes home." This supplies order without explained relationships (D).

**S1 samples.** Meets: "Keep A-B-C-D for the learned society. A's greeting and naming of the scholar first clarify the occasion and task, lend the speaker a shared purpose that strengthens his appeal and engage listeners through direct address. B's fable before C's occupational examples gives those listeners a clear analogy, makes the diagnosis plausible through recognizable cases and sustains interest by moving from story to their society. D after C then clarifies their intellectual responsibility, makes it a consequence of the diagnosis and engages them personally. For an unfamiliar reader I prefer C-B-A-D: C first makes the problem concrete and engaging, though the bodily imagery is initially puzzling; B next explains that imagery and makes the examples more convincing as a coherent argument. A in third place delays useful context and interrupts momentum, weakening clarity and engagement. D still gives the reader a clear conclusion and a reason to see the scholar's role as relevant. Its conviction depends on accepting the analogy, which illustrates a claim rather than proving it historically. The reordered version therefore gains an accessible opening for this reader but sacrifices some continuity and contextual credibility." Does not meet: "The original is convincing because its sentences sound better." This gives no structural evidence (E).
