## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What do these writers show about how money decides who marries whom, and how does laughing at it make the point?"
Open day 39 on Swift's title and opening paragraph after the content note. Ask whose benefit the proposed remedy promises. Introduce the compelling question before retrieving earlier work.
Supporting questions: whose interests govern Swift's calculations (39 to 41)? Whose certainty does Austen's opening voice expose (42)? How does Wilde turn social approval into a comic obstacle and resolution (43 to 45)? Does the ending overturn the marriage market or admit its protagonists to it (46 and 47)?
On day 47 each student writes a qualified closing claim about money and marriage, citing Swift, Austen and Wilde. The teacher models persona against author on day 39, narrator against community judgment on day 42 and structure on day 44; students lead the comparison and seminar on days 46 and 47.
The teacher page explains sustained persona, dramatic irony, the difference between laughing at cruelty and sharing it, and how an ending changes both meaning and the audience's pleasure.

**The Upright Room, Curio Class, 2026. A complete short scene with alternative openings and endings.**

Opening A: An exhibition room. CURATOR MERE stands beside a curtained map. He puts his certificate over a maintenance note as VISITOR ADA and TECHNICIAN NERI enter.

Opening B: An exhibition room. CURATOR MERE reads a maintenance note: "Map hung upside down this morning. Turn frame." He draws a curtain over the map and covers the note with his certificate before ADA and NERI enter.

The same scene follows either opening.

ADA: Your map sent me to a cupboard. Where is the exit?

MERE: The map has passed my inspection. You must have misunderstood the room.

NERI: May I look behind that certificate?

MERE: There is nothing behind a certificate that a visitor needs to know.

ADA: Perhaps we could turn the frame?

MERE: I have made a million corrections today. I am a scrupulous curator.

NERI: The inspection book records two questions. Neither has an answer.

MERE: Questions are corrections waiting for someone else's attention. Mine is required for the opening ceremony.

ADA: Does the ceremony require us to find the exit?

MERE: It requires everyone to admire the room from the proper position.

NERI draws the curtain. The map's lettering is upside down.

ADA: Which position is that?

Either ending may follow.

Ending A: NERI turns the frame. ADA points to the exit. MERE uncovers the maintenance note, then turns his certificate face down. "The room is now ready." Curtain.

Ending B: MERE hangs a sign: "Visitors must invert their view." ADA and NERI tilt their heads. MERE applauds. "At last, everything is upright." Curtain.

**Circular for Universal Consultation, Curio Class, 2026. A complete short satirical circular.**

The Office of Consultation will hear every voice by requiring silence. At previous meetings one speaker's words prevented others from speaking. Our new procedure removes this inequality: nobody may speak at all.

The chair will read the proposed decision. Everyone will then observe a minute of silence. Silence will be entered as agreement. Those who disagree should remain silent too, so that their objections do not disturb the consultation.

We ask every cooperative resident to follow this procedure. Suggestions for changing it may be written on the reverse of the meeting notice. The notice is fixed inside a locked glass case, where suggestions will be safe from interference.

Our first consultation produced no audible objection. The chair therefore reports universal support for continuing the procedure. The minutes record that everyone was heard.

**Director's commentary, Curio Class, 2026, invented for practice.** Annotate the numbered claims with evidence, retaining or revising each inference.

1. The curator's certificate proves his accuracy, and the circular's report proves that every resident supports its procedure.
2. Moving the maintenance note into the opening changes nothing. Both endings simply correct an error, so their effects on the whole work and audience are identical.
3. The million corrections reports a precise total. Hearing every voice through silence is an ordinary factual description. Neither wording creates a comic or critical effect.
4. Replacing scrupulous with careful and cooperative with compliant would preserve every implication.

Reserve each exit's passage or task until the final five minutes. It may have been read, but is not analyzed in that day's model, discussion or writing assignment. Reprint the source lines. - **E39.** In Swift's sentence introducing his thoughts with the promise of no objection, how does the promise prepare a reader to misjudge the proposal, and which earlier detail challenges his benevolence? Look for the persona's confident modesty against commodification. Sample: "He promises an unobjectionable remedy, but calling children a saleable commodity exposes what his assurance hides." Wrong: the assurance proves the author agrees (A).
- **E40.** In the final profession of public service, what does the speaker's lack of children do to his claim of disinterest, and how does its restrained tone help expose his values? Look for money as the speaker's ethical test and the role of understatement. Sample: "He measures innocence by whether he can earn a penny; the calm financial disclaimer makes the absence of moral concern more shocking." Wrong: no personal profit proves benevolence (A).
- **E41.** Using the reserved sentence offering to accept an equally innocent, cheap, easy and effectual alternative, write a short analysis of how the persona gives itself away; explain what innocent would lose if replaced by harmless. Look for a source-based account of the moral evasion and nuanced substitution. Sample: "He calls his scheme innocent while demanding that alternatives satisfy his own conditions. Innocent suggests freedom from guilt; harmless more directly tests its consequences for victims." Wrong: a claim without source detail (F); the adjectives have identical implications (D).
- **E42.** In the chapter 3 paragraph beginning with Darcy drawing attention, how does the change in communal judgment expose the narrator's irony, and what would pleasant lose if replaced by amiable in the description of Bingley's countenance? Look for wealth-based admiration overturned by manners and contextual nuance. Sample: "The room first admires money and appearance, then condemns his manner. Pleasant describes the agreeable impression of a face; amiable would more strongly attribute a kindly disposition." Wrong: the narrator simply endorses the crowd (A); no changed implication (D).
- **E43.** In Jack's reserved description of Lady Bracknell as a monster without being a myth, how does the figure expose the marriage rule he resents; use a changed form of exclude correctly to explain that rule. Look for metaphor as hostile judgment and accurate derivation. Sample: "The impossible creature makes her gatekeeping seem inhuman; exclusion is based on recognized parentage rather than Jack's wishes." Wrong: a literal creature (C); treating exclusion as a verb (E).
- **E44.** In the reserved muffin argument at the end of act 2, how do the men's claims of love sit beside their actions, and how does placing that dispute after exposure of their identities affect the larger comedy? Look for stated seriousness undermined by appetite and an anticlimax. Sample: "They claim devotion but compete for muffins; the trivial quarrel postpones repair and makes their self-importance ridiculous." Wrong: taking their romantic self-description as proof of selflessness (A); retelling without an effect (B).
- **E45.** How does the last exchange about triviality and earnestness change the meaning of Jack's earlier deceptions and the audience's response to the completed plot? Look for the pun and resolution's backward effect. Sample: "His accidental true name gives earlier lies a comic escape; the solemn final claim invites laughter because truth has been obtained through deception." Wrong: the final line proves an unqualified moral conversion (A); listing the discovery only (B).
- **E46.** Test this alternative ending pair, invented for practice: Hamlet closes before Fortinbras enters, and Earnest before Jack's final pun. Wrong: ungrounded generalization (F); ignoring an opposing view (G). Record revised participation during the seminar for P3's day 48 score; this written exit cannot replace observed turns.

**Teacher background.** Before Swift, locate the 1720s: hunger, economic restriction and colonial power. The 1845 to 1852 Great Famine is a later event, not this pamphlet's occasion.
