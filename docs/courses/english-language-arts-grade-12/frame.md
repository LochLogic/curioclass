# Course frame: English Language Arts Grade 12

Course id `english-language-arts-grade-12`, grade 12, draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame names no single day.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 8 units, each built on one named set of texts: Shakespeare, British and American writers, the early twentieth century, and the law, closed by the senior research project.
- **Days.** 160 teaching days: 149 in units and 11 for course-level checks and respond days (three checks, each followed by respond days). A full year of 32 weeks, five lessons a week of about 50 minutes, with home reading of about 20 to 30 minutes on school nights while a long text is open (Hamlet across about five weeks; The Great Gatsby, about 48,000 words, in under four). Days follow load, not row count: unit 2 holds a whole play, three of the year's hardest reading rows and a process argument; unit 8 is the senior project of about eight weeks (the row's length), which shares its question with US History II's capstone from about day 123; unit 5 is short because it reads two essays in excerpt for three structural rows. Each unit's days include its readiness check, its end-of-unit assessment on a text not read in the unit, and its respond time.
- **Standard.** Common Core ELA, grades 11 to 12 band (data/standards/ccss-ela-standards-full.txt). Grade 12 owns every 11 to 12 code, all 30 rows: grade 11 started each one (marked ~ there), and this course brings each to the band's end. Every lettered part is taught here, and the unit that teaches it says so (W.11-12.1a to e, 2a to f, 3a to e, 9a and 9b; SL.11-12.1a to d; L.11-12.1a and 1b, 2a and 2b, 3a, 4a to d, 5a and 5b), as are the parts grade 11 left to this year: Shakespeare in RL.11-12.4 and RL.11-12.7, the legal reasoning in RI.11-12.8, the early twentieth century in RL.11-12.9. The bar is docs/RUBRIC-ELA.md (the 6 to 12 criteria) and docs/APPROACH-ELA.md. The "Check set adds" column lists candidate additions from the college-level English language and English literature course frameworks and New York's Next Generation 11 to 12 standards (see For the lead); each is confirmed or dropped in the unit plan, and check-set names never appear in student, teacher or family copy.
- **Base text.** None. ELA 6 to 12 is Curio Class's own (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md, "Our own"). Every anchor is public domain in the United States (published 1930 or earlier), a US government work (court opinions, presidential addresses), or a Curio Class original; each text's date is checked in its text record. CommonLit is not used. Under Cory's 2026-11-13 rule, anchors are picked for being recognizable and as recent as the licence allows: The Great Gatsby (1925), A Room of One's Own (1929), Hughes, McKay and Cullen, Hurston's 1928 essay, and US government works through the present (Tinker, 1969; Mahanoy, 2021; the most recent inaugural address). Short quoted passages of well-known recent writers may sit inside Curio Class lessons beside an anchor (candidates named in units 1 and 6). No student is sent to a library or a paid copy: every anchor has a Curio Class print copy.
- **What this year anchors on.** Hamlet and The Great Gatsby read whole; The Importance of Being Earnest read whole as the comic pair to Hamlet; the court opinions and The Federalist in excerpt. The independent long book: Pride and Prejudice (1813) is offered first, carrying on from its opening chapters in unit 3; the shelf adds The Age of Innocence (1920), A Farewell to Arms (1929), The Sun Also Rises (1926), Mrs Dalloway (1925), Passing (1929), Jane Eyre (1847), Great Expectations (1861), all public domain in the United States, with log and conference (e12-reading-band). Frankenstein is not offered: grade 10 reads it whole.
- **Reading level and supports.** Anchors sit at the high end of the 11-CCR band (about 1185 to 1385 Lexile, 2012 supplement to Appendix A). Gatsby (about 1070) and Woolf are placed for their qualitative demand (an unreliable narrator; a wandering structure); Hamlet and the poems rest on the qualitative read. Supports come before, during and after reading and never replace the text; audio sits beside the text, never in place of it on a check. Routine supports fade across the year with independent checkpoints (the first cold read without routine supports in check 1, the last in check 3), so that by the senior project students read new texts alone. Targeted instruction and access supports continue for students who need them and are recorded apart from evidence of independent proficiency (the row's rule).
- **Anchor text records.** Each anchor's record (data/plans/<course>/texts.csv, fixed with the first ELA pilot plan) carries the computed measure taken from the full public-domain text, what makes it hard under the four headings (meaning, structure, language, what the reader needs to know), the task, and the reason for its place, which the Units table gives here in short. Shares on anchors, reported not scored: about half the reading days on informational texts (essays, addresses, The Federalist, court opinions, the policy set, the research sources), half on literature; the year's anchors range across four centuries, British and American, Black and white writers, men and women. Writing across the year about 40 percent argument, 40 informative and 20 narrative, the band's end ratio.
- **Routines all year** (each starts in unit 1 and runs in every unit): the daily exit question and the weekly one-sitting analytic paragraph, with three one-sitting essays on cold texts that draw on a literary and an informational text at once (W.11-12.9a and 9b together, e12-routine-writing); a seminar at least twice a unit, one of them led by students who set its goal, deadline and roles (e12-seminar); the unit word list of about 15 words and the student's own log (e12-academic-words); the independent book (e12-reading-band); the long-sentence routine from grade 11 on one anchor sentence a week, now led by students (review of grade 11's e11-syntax-effect until unit 2 takes it up). No timer is shown to students; the teacher keeps time.
- **Process writing calendar.** Five process pieces: the reflective narrative (unit 1, about 650 to 1,000 words, due about day 12, in college application season), the literary argument on Hamlet (unit 2, about 1,500 words, about day 35), the informative piece on an idea from another course (unit 6, about 1,500 words, about day 99), the mock opinion (unit 7, about 1,200 words, about day 117), the senior research essay and talk (unit 8, about 2,500 words, the year's end). Each has a model piece, the year's rubric for its type and a revision plan the student writes (e12-process). The portfolio of all five, with one piece rewritten with a new approach, closes the year.
- **Paper path and accounts.** Every task that publishes, uses a recording or a digital tool has a paper path: a posted copy and a class binder for publishing and updating; a printed transcript or the teacher reading aloud where audio is not available; a printed slide handout for the talks. No student account anywhere; research uses open archives, government data and the school library's own access.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh-agent sensitivity read. Units that also need a qualified or community reader: unit 1 (Hurston's essay on race and identity: a Black community reader), unit 2 (Hamlet's suicide content and Ophelia's death: a school counselor or like qualified reader), unit 3 ('A Modest Proposal' and the Irish famine: an Irish community or Irish history reader), unit 4 (the Harlem Renaissance poems, McKay's answer to 1919, Gatsby's racist speech by Tom Buchanan: a Black community reader), unit 7 (Plessy's reasoning from segregation: a Black community reader). Who, and any pay, is Cory's.
