## 1. Standards and practices

<!-- skel -->
- **4-PS3-3** (days 7, 8, 9, 10): Ask questions and predict what happens to energy when objects crash into each other.
- **4-PS3-1** (days 9): Explain, using evidence, how an object's speed is related to how much energy it has.
- skill s4-collisions (grade 4)
- skill s4-speed-energy (grade 4)

Unit plan field 2, the lines naming this section's days:

> Model safe release on day 1 and measurement on day 3. Let students propose controls on day 2, justify patterns on days 4 to 6 and question predictions on days 7 to 12. Use notebooks to select the next explanation to discuss.
>
> Group the question board by evidence and destinations. Days 1 to 3 ask, "Was speed the difference?" Fair comparisons raise "Does it happen repeatedly?" Days 4 to 6 use repeated observations to explain speed, energy and transfer. That raises "Where does the energy go?" Days 7 to 10 ask testable questions and predict motion, sound and warming destinations. Days 11 to 13 test the explanation elsewhere and attach evidence to answered questions; retain unresolved questions. Transfer: a rolling spool strikes a hanging cardboard flap, unseen before day 14.
> Carried: cause and effect and claim, evidence and reasoning begin on day 1; days 4 and 5 revise them from repeated-trial patterns. Force arrows return on day 7; day 8 observations locate pushes at contact. Energy arrows begin on day 7; days 8 and 10 add receiving-object motion, sound and warming from collision evidence and the sourced account. Day 12 distinguishes force arrows acting on an object from energy arrows between destinations. All reach consensus on day 13 and inform day 14 models; none is withdrawn.

### U3. Exit questions

<!-- skel -->
> (shared across the unit) Each exit takes five minutes, is unscored and uses a fresh paper case, printed "invented for practice". Reserve these cases for the exit, apart from worked examples, tool trials and the assessment. Read prompts aloud as needed. Accept labelled drawings or spoken explanations. Use field 6 repairs next lesson if more than a third miss an exit; otherwise confer during independent work. Day 13 misses shape feedback, not reteaching on day 14.
>
> | Exit, day and code | The question | Answer and likely wrong answers |
> |---|---|---|
> | E1, day 1, 4-PS3-1 | Two picture sequences show the same toy train rolling into the same paper tower. In one, the train moves faster and knocks more pieces away. (a) Describe a difference you notice and draw a possible explanation. (b) What question could help test your explanation? | Notice faster motion and more displaced pieces; a tentative drawing might connect the train's motion to the tower's change. Ask whether repeating faster and slower rolls gives different tower changes. Energy vocabulary is not expected. Wrong: "My prediction proves what repeated trials will show" (I). |
> | E2, day 2, 4-PS3-1 | A ring rolls into a lid. How would you plan a fair comparison of the effects of faster and slower rolls? Name what changes, what stays the same and what you observe. | Change ring speed; keep ring, lid, surface and contact arrangement fixed; observe lid motion. Wrong: use a different lid for the faster roll (G); ask only "Which speed is best?" (F). |
> | E3, day 3, 4-PS3-1 | A drawing shows a washer target starting at the ruler's 6-centimetre mark and ending at 15 centimetres. A repeat moves it 9 centimetres. (a) Record both travel distances with units. (b) Put both trials on a labelled line plot. | Record 9 centimetres twice; put two marks above 9 on an axis labelled distance in centimetres. Wrong: record the first travel as 15 centimetres (H); label the axis energy in centimetres (B). |
> | E4, day 4, 4-PS3-1 | A table and matching line plot show the same rolling disk moving a target: slower trials 2, 3, 2 centimetres; faster trials 7, 8, 7 centimetres. What repeated pattern do both displays show, and what does it suggest about speed and energy? (Motion energy is the energy an object has because it is moving.) | Every faster trial moves the target farther than every slower trial; the faster disk has more motion energy. Wrong: equal motion energy at both speeds (A); the distances measure energy in centimetres (B). |
> | E5, day 5, 4-PS3-1 | A cylinder rolls into a folded paper target. Each of three faster trials moves it farther than each of three slower trials. Explain which cylinder has more motion energy, using the repeated evidence and explaining why target motion matters. | The faster cylinder has more motion energy; all faster outcomes exceed all slower outcomes; transfer at contact makes the target move, so the controlled difference supports more energy available to transfer. Wrong: same cylinder means equal motion energy (A). A distance-only answer needs the explanation completed. |
> | E6, day 6, 4-PS3-1 | A rolling hoop repeatedly moves the same cardboard tube farther at faster speed. A student writes, "The faster hoop has more energy because fast means more energy." How would you strengthen this explanation with evidence and reasoning? | Cite the repeated larger tube movements and explain that transferred energy changes tube motion, linking the controlled comparison to more energy available in the faster hoop. Wrong: hoop size is unchanged, so motion energy is unchanged (A); tube travel is an energy measurement (B). |
> | E7, day 7, 4-PS3-3 | A rubber ball rolls toward an empty carton. (a) Ask a testable question comparing two ball speeds with this setup. (b) Predict both objects' motion after contact and show where the carton receives a push. | Ask whether a faster ball moves the same carton farther; predict the ball slows and the carton moves, with a contact-force arrow on the carton in the ball's direction. Wrong: "Which speed is best?" alone (F); energy disappears when the ball slows (C); draw the contact-force arrow carrying energy into air (J). |
> | E8, day 8, 4-PS3-3 | You predicted a rolling can would keep its motion unchanged after hitting a cork. New pictures show the can slowing and the cork starting to move. How would you revise the prediction and energy model using these observations? | Predict that a repeat can slow the can and start cork motion; show energy passing from can motion to cork motion at contact, supported by both observed changes. Wrong: slowing makes energy disappear (C); the can's slowing was only predicted, so it does not count yet (I). |
> | E9, day 9, 4-PS3-1 and 4-PS3-3 | For the same rolling wheel and box, three slower collisions are described as quiet, quiet, quiet; three faster collisions as loud, loud, loud. Pictures also show greater box motion in every faster trial. (a) Record listening tallies by speed. (b) How does this evidence support an energy explanation? | Three quiet slower trials and three loud faster trials; consistent sound and motion differences support more motion energy before faster collisions and transfer to box motion and sound. Wrong: all energy becomes sound (D); the same wheel has equal starting motion energy (A). |
> | E10, day 10, 4-PS3-3 | A wooden sphere slows after moving a paper cone, with a soft click. A background card says objects and nearby air warm slightly, below our equipment's detection. Revise a before-and-after energy drawing to account for these records and the card. | Show initial sphere motion energy and transfers to cone motion, sound and slight warming; distinguish observed motion and sound from the supplied warming account. Wrong: energy vanished (C); all became sound (D); no felt warmth means no warming (E). |
> | E11, day 11, 4-PS3-1 and 4-PS3-3 | A small wagon repeatedly moves the same carton less at slower speed than at faster speed. (a) Ask a testable follow-up question and predict a slower repeat. (b) Explain the speed-energy link using these observations. | Ask whether another slower roll again moves the carton less; predict less transferred motion energy, wagon slowing and less carton movement; repeated controlled differences support more motion energy at faster speed. Wrong: an undefined "best speed" question (F); equal starting motion energy at both speeds (A); energy vanishes as the wagon slows (C). |
> | E12, day 12, 4-PS3-1 and 4-PS3-3 | A rolling bead slows after striking a small card. Its model says "energy vanished" and uses a contact-force arrow pointing from the card into the air. (a) Repair the energy account. (b) Explain how the two kinds of arrows should differ. | Energy transfers to card motion, sound and slight warming. Force arrows show pushes on objects at contact; energy arrows connect initial energy to its destinations. Wrong: retain vanishing energy (C); use contact-force arrows for energy travelling into air (J). |
> | E13, day 13, 4-PS3-1 and 4-PS3-3 | The same orange rolls into the same empty bottle in three trials at each speed. Every faster trial moves the bottle farther and sounds louder. (a) Use the class explanation to explain these records. (b) The orange now hits two bottles set in a row; predict what each bottle does and where the energy goes. | Repeated greater effects support more initial motion energy in the faster orange. Predict the first bottle moves and passes some motion to the second, with sound and slight warming; the orange slows as energy transfers, without vanishing. Wrong: equal initial motion energy (A); energy disappears (C); all energy becomes sound (D). |
