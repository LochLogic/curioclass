# Course frame: Science Grade 4

Course `science-grade-4` (catalogue id `science-k5`), grade 4, draft frame, written 2026-11-13, aligned 2026-11-15 with [the scope and sequence](scope-and-sequence.md) and [day record](../../../data/plans/science-grade-4/days.csv).

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 6: speed and collisions, energy on the move, waves and codes, the changing land, Earth's patterns and
  hazards, then senses and structures.
- **Days.** 120 course days: 111 in units and 9 at course level (6 for three course-level checks, 3 respond
  days). Elementary science shares the day, so the course assumes one science lesson of 45 minutes on four days a
  week (180 minutes a week) across 30 weeks (a school year of about 36 weeks, of which about 30 carry science
  lessons here; the rest hold breaks, the school's own testing and events). Science has 120 sessions in 30 weeks of four sessions each; their school days come from docs/courses/CALENDAR.md and data/plans/calendar.csv. The year owns
  14 rows (15 performance expectations, with 4-PS4-3 and 3-5-ETS1-2 in one row). Two rows are design work with
  building and testing (s4-energy-device, s4-information-codes) and one is a third design comparison
  (s4-hazard-solutions), so the year weighs a little more per row than grade 3; about 111 days in units fits it, and
  the full 160-day yardstick is not needed. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment and response, counted once.
  A row used the year before, or earlier the same year, opens as a retrieval task inside a day; every other
  earlier-grade row the unit assumes (last used two or more grades back) gets a readiness check. Count by grade
  number: a row last taught or assessed in grade g gets retrieval in grade g+1 and readiness in grade g+2 or later;
  passing retrieval or a maintenance claim does not reset that grade. The scope and sequence lists every assumed
  earlier-grade row and relevant part. Readiness is in the last ten minutes of days 15, 41, 56, 76 and 93, before
  units 2 to 6; one-lesson responses are inside days 18, 43, 59, 78 and 95, preserving hook days. Unit 1 has no
  readiness rows; where a unit 1 check is needed, it follows the hook in day 1's last ten minutes. Math leans use
  retrieval by the lead's rule, with a bridge for untaught same-year math.
  A school with about 120 minutes a week runs the year at three lessons a week across the same 30 weeks (90
  days) by taking the shorter path the unit plans name in each unit; no expectation is dropped (Cory, Desk
  2026-11-14: the time a day stays as picked; the frame keeps 120 days).
- **Standard.** The grade 4 performance expectations of data/standards/ngss-pes.txt, each taught in full, plus
  3-5-ETS1-2, the band's engineering expectation this grade owns; 3-5-ETS1-3 is started here in s4-energy-device
  (test and improve) and completed in grade 5. The bar is docs/RUBRIC-SCIENCE.md. Practices and crosscutting
  concepts are named per unit in the C3 column (see "For the lead"). Plain facts (BASELINE rule 5, values rule b):
  the great age of the Earth is taught as the evidence shows; energy resources are neither cheered nor blamed
  beyond what backed sources show.
- **Base text.** None. Science K to 5 is Curio Class's own (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md, "Our own"): the elementary
  units in the source store are NonCommercial and reference only. Phenomena are chosen to be real, observable or
  measurable in class where possible, puzzling at grade 4 and explainable with the unit's core ideas; each unit
  names its anchor, and a teacher may swap in a local phenomenon that needs the same ideas (a local road cut or
  river bank for unit 4, the state's own power plants for unit 2). Investigations are our own designs. Data, maps
  and pictures come from open and public-domain material only: USGS earthquake and volcano data and topographic
  maps, NOAA ocean-floor maps and tsunami pages, the US Energy Information Administration's state energy data,
  NASA images, public-domain photographs, and open simulations released before 2026-03-29. Curio Class's own
  online tools serve as simulations where a class cannot run the investigation (listed per unit, built later).
- **Materials.** Classroom-cheap: marbles, toy cars, cardboard ramps, cups, AA batteries, wire, small bulbs or
  buzzers, rubber bands, coiled springs or rope, trays of water, flashlights, mirrors, sand, soil, a sloped tray as a
  stream table. Every investigation names a household substitute where safe (a baking tray and sand for a stream
  table; aluminum foil strips for wire) and a no-kit path where not (a class data set or a Curio Class
  simulation), said plainly to be weaker (PRODUCTION-PATH: substitutes, then no-kit). Where a class can afford one
  set only, the teacher or one pair runs it at the front while the class predicts, records and explains. The
  investigations whose expectation says make observations, test or design (energy moving and the device in unit 2,
  sending codes in unit 3, the erosion and weathering tests in unit 4, the hazard tests in unit 5) are first-hand
  core: a demonstration is the least that keeps them honest, and the no-kit path does not meet them alone. Every
  simulation has a paper path (the same data on a printed sheet) and needs no student account. The scope and sequence states provisional quantities and costs for 30 using one shared apparatus, within the existing approximately 30-dollar ceiling per unit beyond named stock; unit plans verify actual prices and setup.
- **Safety.** A course safety page for the year: low-voltage batteries only, never outlets, a bulb or buzzer always
  in the loop (a short circuit gets hot); never look at the sun, even in a mirror, and no lasers; rolling objects
  slow and on the floor, no throwing; water and sand on covered tables, spills wiped at once; hand washing after
  soil. Eye protection when rubber bands are stretched and whenever dry sand is moved by air; mirrors acrylic, not
  glass; anything frozen in plastic, never glass; warm water, never hot; wet sand and soil never down a sink (let it
  settle in a bucket and empty it outdoors); nothing in science class is eaten. Every hands-on day carries its own
  Safety and set-up section.
- **The storyline.** Every unit opens on its anchor and builds a question board; each lesson set answers a question
  the last one raised. One energy story runs through units 1 to 3: the energy transfer arrows start in unit 1,
  follow energy through sound, light, heat and current in unit 2, and follow a wave across water in unit 3. Each
  unit has an initial model, named revision points and a consensus model; the end-of-unit assessment uses a
  phenomenon the class has not met.
- **Design work.** Grade 4 owns 3-5-ETS1-2 (generate and compare several solutions): taught in full in unit 3
  (codes: research the problem first, generate at least two solutions, share proposals, test them under two
  conditions and compare them on the data), used again in unit 5 (hazards). Unit 2's device begins 3-5-ETS1-3 (test, find where it fails, improve)
  without the planned fair test that grade 5 completes.
- **Nature of science (how science works).** Taught on purpose in unit 4 (the rock record is read by assuming the same processes worked
  in the past as now) and unit 6 (a common idea, that light goes out from the eye, tested and revised by evidence).
  The other connections the standards place beside this grade's expectations each get a named moment: scientists
  and engineers work in teams and science affects everyday life (unit 2, the device); science findings rest on
  recognizing patterns (unit 3, waves; unit 5, the map belts); knowing the science matters in engineering (unit 2,
  resources; unit 3, the encoding reading before the codes); engineers improve technologies to cut risk (unit 5).
- **Watch for.** The common idea a teacher should expect to hear, where each unit's teacher page starts: "the energy
  got used up in the crash" (unit 1); "the battery runs out of electricity", "one wire is enough to light the bulb"
  (unit 2); "the wave carries the water along with it" (unit 3); "rocks do not change", and weathering and erosion
  used as one word (unit 4); "earthquakes and volcanoes can happen anywhere, by chance", "a big enough wall stops a
  flood" (unit 5); "eyes send out sight", "a cat's eyes glow by themselves", "a cat can see with no light at all"
  (unit 6).
- **Schools that teach science in blocks.** Many grade 4 classes teach science only some weeks, alternating with
  social studies. Units run as blocks in this order and close with their own end tasks. After a gap, retrieve the last model inside a teaching day; give any required older-row readiness at the last actual meeting before the next unit, never weeks ahead, and respond on the named later lesson rather than its hook day.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md);
  no outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Unit 5 (earthquakes, tsunamis and floods students may have
  lived through): the agent first studies published guidance on children and disasters (a national child
  traumatic stress network, a school psychologists' association, a geological survey's and an emergency agency's
  pages for children). Unit 2 (communities whose work depends on one energy source): it first studies coal, oil,
  gas, wind and solar communities' own published voice (miners' and energy workers' unions and town pages, energy
  education bodies) and a journalism style guide on energy and climate reporting. Every unit is hands-on, so
  every unit also gets the safety agent's read, against published school science safety guidance (unit 2's
  circuits and solar heaters and unit 5's shake table first), before the course is called finished; the course's
  checks get the answer-key agent's read.
- **Reading level.** Science texts are short (one to three pages), written at a grade 3 to 4 band with diagrams;
  words are taught when the class needs them.
