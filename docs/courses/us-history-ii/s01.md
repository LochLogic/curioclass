## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Who should steer the economy: markets, the Federal Reserve or elected leaders?"
**Hook, day 1.** Read Roosevelt explaining the bank holiday. Ask who could restore access to deposits and what each response might cost. Introduce the compelling question before teaching scarcity.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write these: What must a choice give up (day 1)? Whose goals compete (day 2)? How do markets coordinate production and investment (days 3 to 5)? What do indicators hide (day 6)? Why can banks need help (days 7 and 8)? Who gains from economic change (day 9)? Which response fits which crisis (day 10)?
**Closing claim, day 10.** Recommend a division of responsibility among markets, the Federal Reserve and elected leaders. Use the monetary-policy history and recovery law named below; answer a counterclaim and identify who bears a cost.
**Teaching.** Model sourcing on day 1, a graph on day 3, and a causal explanation on day 8. Before every source, identify maker, date, audience and purpose. Compare institutional explanations with affected people's accounts. Withdraw writing frames on day 10. Practise D2.His.14.9-12 there; connect indicators and prose for RH.11-12.7 on day 6. Distinguish financial investment from productive capacity, liquidity from solvency, and historical tools from current practice. The organization chart describes the modern system, not its 1913 form.

## U3. Daily evidence for section 1.1

The unit assessment is in field 5. Each teaching day ends with the corresponding exit ticket below. All scenarios and document selections are reserved from instruction. The answer key names the required reasoning; other supported answers are possible. Each first and last teaching day also leaves one open question for the capstone notebook.

- **E1, MA.USII.T1.1.** A case (invented for practice): a drama club has $36. A banner and a sound kit each cost $24. The club chooses the kit and ranks the banner next. Explain its scarcity and opportunity cost. Answer: both cost $48, above the budget; the forgone banner is the opportunity cost. Wrong: the opportunity cost is the entire $36 (A).
- **E2, MA.USII.T1.2 and T1.10.** Case, invented for practice: a town pays for mosquito control and enforces a bicycle-rental contract. (a) Name each role, goal and one payer. (b) Explain a conflict between two policy goals. Answer: shared pest control supports security through a public good; enforcement protects property rights; taxpayers and contracting parties pay. Funding pest control can improve security but leave unequal tax burdens, raising equity concerns. Wrong: both are redistribution (T); every resident gains equally (B); security and equity mean the same thing (AC).
- **E3, MA.USII.T1.3 and T1.3b.** (a) Define supply and demand and draw separate graphs for cheaper glass used in jars and for more buyers wanting jars; explain each price and quantity change. (b) Explain how a jar price rise could change access for buyers with different budgets. Answer: supply is quantities offered at different prices; demand is quantities wanted and affordable at those prices. Cheaper inputs shift supply right, lowering price and raising quantity; more buyers shift demand right, raising both. Higher prices may exclude buyers with smaller budgets. Wrong: both shocks move along existing curves (X); budgets never affect access (AF).
- **E4, MA.USII.T1.3a, T1.3c and T1.4.** Cash record (invented for practice): June 1, savers pay $900 for new farm bonds; June 3, $900 buys irrigation equipment; September 30, crop receipts are $700 and operating costs $850; October 2, an existing bond is resold for $860. (a) Identify the financial investment and which payment finances production, contrasting resale. (b) Calculate the operating result and explain the risk to expected returns. Answer: bonds commit money for possible interest and repayment; the new issue finances equipment, while resale pays the former holder. Operations lose $150, so expected profit can attract investment without ensuring profit or bond repayment. Wrong: resale adds $860 to the farm's funds (L); returns are guaranteed (E).
- **E5, MA.USII.T1.4 and T1.5.** An unseen case (invented for practice): a asset changes from $50 to $45 after buyers revise expected earnings downward. Calculate its change and return; explain how buyers and sellers produce the new price. Answer: a $5 loss, or 10%; lower bids and accepted offers establish a lower market price. Wrong: the issuer must have set the price (Q); a rebound is certain (E).
- **E6, MA.USII.T1.8 and RH.11-12.7.** An unseen case (invented for practice): a panel shows real output rising 2%, unemployment unchanged at 8%, and inflation falling from 6% to 3%. Does it show falling prices or gains for every household? Connect one spending decision to an indicator. Answer: prices still rise; neither output nor unemployment describes everyone's experience. More business equipment purchases can support output and jobs. Wrong: lower inflation means cheaper goods (D); output growth guarantees equal gains (K).
- **E7, MA.USII.T1.6.** A case (invented for practice): a bank holds $15 in cash and $85 in sound loans against $90 in deposits. Depositors request $30 immediately. Explain its problem, a possible consequence for businesses, and why an insurer's role differs. Answer: assets exceed deposits but immediate cash is insufficient; restricting loans may cut production. Insurance pools covered risks rather than providing ordinary payment deposits. Wrong: the run proves insolvency (N); every financial institution holds payment deposits (V).
- **E8, MA.USII.T1.7.** Case, invented for practice: a council asks regional banks to cut local taxes and says all twelve presidents vote on rates. (a) Explain the 1913 banking problem and correct both claims on a system diagram. (b) Trace a central-bank bond sale to credit and jobs. Answer: recurrent panics prompted the system; seven governors and five presidents vote; all presidents participate. Taxing belongs to elected fiscal authorities. Sales can tighten credit, spending and hiring. Wrong: regional banks levy taxes (G); all twelve presidents always vote (R); the system addressed no banking crisis (AE).

## U7. Talk and writing

Ask on day 2, "Whose goal does this policy advance?" On day 7, ask, "Whose account could challenge this explanation?" On day 10, ask, "What evidence would favor another response?" Judge the argument by field 5's A8 look-fors. Students can disagree with a source or each other while explaining the evidence accurately.

## U8. Practice that returns

Day 3 builds graph fluency through varied shocks, explanations and corrections, without speed scores. Days 6 to 10 repeatedly distinguish a level from its change and a correlation from a cause.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach scarcity, equity, investment, liquidity, solvency, fiscal and monetary through examples and contrasting nonexamples. Offer "The source shows __; this could affect __ because __." Allow home-language annotations. Keep original grade 12 excerpts to about 250 words per sitting, with glosses and teacher read-aloud; source labels remain visible. Describe graphs aloud, enlarge labels, and allow movable cards, a scribe or oral explanations. Extension tests the same chain against contrary evidence.

## U10. Two rooms

For one child, the adult asks the competing view and records the student's response. Keep assessment answers covered until work is complete; use day 12 to discuss misses together. With one projector, display sources while every student annotates a paper copy; with no device, read the same copies aloud.
