## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Who should steer the economy: markets, the Federal Reserve or elected leaders?"
**Hook, day 1.** Read Roosevelt explaining the bank holiday. Ask who could restore access to deposits and what each response might cost. Introduce the compelling question before teaching scarcity.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write these: What must a choice give up (day 1)? Whose goals compete (day 2)? How do markets coordinate production and investment (days 3 to 5)? What do indicators hide (day 6)? Why can banks need help (days 7 and 8)? Who gains from economic change (day 9)? Which response fits which crisis (day 10)?
**Closing claim, day 10.** Recommend a division of responsibility among markets, the Federal Reserve and elected leaders. Use the monetary-policy history and recovery law named below; answer a counterclaim and identify who bears a cost.
**Teaching.** Model sourcing on day 1, a graph on day 3, and a causal explanation on day 8. Before every source, identify maker, date, audience and purpose. Compare institutional explanations with affected people's accounts. Withdraw writing frames on day 10. Practise D2.His.14.9-12 there; connect indicators and prose for RH.11-12.7 on day 6. Teacher notes must distinguish financial investment from new productive capacity, liquidity from solvency, and historical tools from current practice. The organization chart describes the modern system, not its 1913 form.

## U3. Daily evidence for section 1.1

The unit assessment is in field 5. Each teaching day ends with the corresponding exit ticket below. All scenarios and document selections are reserved from instruction. The answer key names the required reasoning; other supported answers are possible. Each first and last teaching day also leaves one open question for the capstone notebook.

- **E1, MA.USII.T1.1.** A fictional drama club has $36. A banner and a sound kit each cost $24. The club chooses the kit and ranks the banner next. Explain its scarcity and opportunity cost. Answer: both cost $48, above the budget; the forgone banner is the opportunity cost. Wrong: the opportunity cost is the entire $36 (A).
- **E2, MA.USII.T1.2 and T1.10.** Take one new tax-funded proposal, storm warnings or income support (your choice). Name its goal, the government role it plays and who pays. Then classify one of these: contract enforcement or a pollution charge. Answer: warnings promote security through a public good; income support promotes equity or security through redistribution; taxpayers pay. Contract enforcement protects rights; the charge addresses a spillover. Wrong: everyone gains equally, or every case is redistribution (B).
- **E3, MA.USII.T1.3 and T1.3b.** Draw separate graphs for cheaper glass used in jars and for more buyers wanting jars. Define both curves and explain each new price and quantity. Answer: cheaper inputs shift supply right, reducing price and increasing quantity; more buyers shift demand right, raising both. Wrong: both changes move along the original supply curve (C).
- **E4, MA.USII.T1.3a, T1.3c and T1.4.** A fictional pottery workshop issues shares to buy a kiln. Explain the investor's commitment, how the money reaches production, and why expected profit does not remove risk. Contrast a later resale. Answer: funds buy a claim on possible future returns and finance equipment; costs may exceed receipts. A resale pays the earlier owner. Wrong: every sale buys the workshop another kiln, with guaranteed profit (E).
- **E5, MA.USII.T1.4 and T1.5.** An unseen fictional asset changes from $50 to $45 after buyers revise expected earnings downward. Calculate its change and return; explain how buyers and sellers produce the new price. Answer: a $5 loss, or 10%; lower bids and accepted offers establish a lower market price. Wrong: the issuer must have set the price, or a rebound is certain (E).
- **E6, MA.USII.T1.8 and RH.11-12.7.** An unseen fictional panel shows real output rising 2%, unemployment unchanged at 8%, and inflation falling from 6% to 3%. Does it show falling prices or gains for every household? Connect one spending decision to an indicator. Answer: prices still rise; neither output nor unemployment describes everyone's experience. More business equipment purchases can support output and jobs. Wrong: lower inflation means cheaper goods, or output growth guarantees equal gains (D).
- **E7, MA.USII.T1.6.** A fictional bank holds $15 in cash and $85 in sound loans against $90 in deposits. Depositors request $30 immediately. Explain its problem, a possible consequence for businesses, and why an insurer's role differs. Answer: assets exceed deposits but immediate cash is insufficient; restricting loans may cut production. Insurance pools covered risks rather than providing ordinary payment deposits. Wrong: the bank has no assets or every financial institution holds deposits in the same way (F).
- **E8, MA.USII.T1.7.** Why was the system created in 1913? Why can a President not order a tax cut through regional banks? Diagram its Board, regional banks and voting committee; trace a bond sale to credit and jobs. Answer: recurrent panics prompted reform; elected fiscal powers differ from monetary authority; seven governors and five regional presidents vote. Sales can tighten financial conditions, slowing spending and hiring while reducing inflation pressure. Wrong: twelve regional presidents all vote, or the central bank sets taxes (G).
- **E9, MA.USII.T1.9, T1.9a and T1.9b.** Using fresh evidence cards of at most 25 words each, choose one 19th-century case (canals or railroads) and one 20th-century case (space technology or the internet). Give one causal link and one uneven consequence for each. Answer key, all cases plus the Civil War card: war orders and destruction differ by region; cheaper water freight changes inland markets; rail links expanded markets while taking Native nations' lands (for example the Lakota nations') and relying on dangerous, underpaid labor, including Chinese workers on the Central Pacific; satellite applications change communications unevenly; internet exchange lowers some costs while displacing some work. Wrong: every case creates the same permanent, universal gain (H). The card selections must differ from A7's evidence.
- **E10, MA.USII.T1.10 and T1.11; D2.His.14.9-12.** Submit the independent policy argument for A8. Then name its strongest counterclaim and one observation that could change the conclusion. Answer: a specific competing mechanism or distributional cost and relevant outcome evidence. Wrong: no conceivable evidence could change the claim (H). Add an open question to the capstone notebook.

## U7. Talk and writing

Ask on day 2, "Whose goal does this policy advance?" On day 7, ask, "Whose account could challenge this explanation?" On day 10, ask, "What evidence would favor another response?" Judge the argument by field 5's A8 look-fors. Students can disagree with a source or each other while explaining the evidence accurately.

## U8. Practice that returns

Use U6's retrieval tasks; never replace the opening hook with a readiness check. Day 3 builds graph fluency through varied shocks, explanations and corrections, without speed scores. Days 6 to 10 repeatedly distinguish a level from its change and a correlation from a cause.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach scarcity, equity, investment, liquidity, solvency, fiscal and monetary through examples and contrasting nonexamples. Offer "The source shows __; this could affect __ because __." Allow home-language annotations. Keep original grade 12 excerpts to about 250 words per sitting, with glosses and teacher read-aloud; source labels remain visible. Describe graphs aloud, enlarge labels, and allow movable cards, a scribe or oral explanations. Extension tests the same chain against contrary evidence.

## U10. Two rooms

For one child, the adult asks the competing view and records the student's response. Keep assessment answers covered until work is complete; use day 12 to discuss misses together. With one projector, display sources while every student annotates a paper copy; with no device, read the same copies aloud.
