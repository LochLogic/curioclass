## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What did winning the war cost, at home and in Japan?"
**Hook, day 57.** Pair the 1941 fair-employment order with a Japanese American account of forced removal. Ask how mobilization could expand opportunity while taking away liberty.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Who gained work, and where (57 and 58)? Whose rights were denied (59)? How did service strengthen demands for change (60)? - **Day 59, Korematsu.** In Trump v. ## U3. Checks, written first

Every invented caption or display is labeled "invented for practice". New source selections are withheld from teaching and other checks. Every exit ticket uses a new passage or claim, not the day's model or practice passage. Sources remain labeled with maker, date, audience and purpose. Students first identify those features, then explain what the evidence supports. The following prompts and keys fix the evidence needed; separate passage selections remain to make.

- **E57, MA.USII.T3.8a and b.** A new caption says, "War factories attracted Black immigrants from the South, so all Americans benefited equally." Correct its movement term, explain one push and one pull factor and connect federal spending to jobs. Answer: these were internal migrants; Jim Crow and racial violence pushed while industrial jobs pulled; federal purchases raised production and hiring, but unequal access continued (A, B). Add an open capstone question.
- **E58, MA.USII.T3.8c and d.** A new display says, "Roosevelt ended military segregation in 1941; women first entered paid work as combat pilots." Revise both claims. Explain Randolph's leverage and distinguish the work and service status of a woman pilot from one other role (your choice). Answer: organizing and the march threat helped secure Order 8802 against defense hiring discrimination; enforcement was limited and military segregation continued. Women already worked. Civilian pilots ferried or tested aircraft; programmers calculated and coded; clerks administered records; nurses treated patients; munitions workers manufactured weapons. Civilian and uniformed roles differed (C, D, K).
- **E59, MA.USII.T3.8e.** A fresh caption says, "Both governments detained only foreign enemies; Korematsu rejected the policy in 1944 and compensation ended its effects." Correct it with one United States and one Canadian detail, then distinguish Korematsu from the 1988 law (the 2018 statement is in the key). Answer: citizens were also targeted; Canadian property sales and continued exclusion differed from the American course. Korematsu upheld exclusion; 1988 legislation apologized and provided redress; the 2018 Court repudiated Korematsu. Payments did not erase lost liberty, homes or opportunities (P, R, L).
- **E60, MA.USII.T3.8f.** A fresh argument says, "Two wars gave women and Black Americans equal rights, and everyone of Mexican ancestry had the same wartime role." Explain why evidence of work and service instead strengthened demands, naming an example for each world war. Contrast recruitment under the Bracero program with the Zoot Suit attacks. Answer: contributions strengthened existing organizing, including voting claims and Black veterans' demands after World War I, and employment and Double V campaigns during World War II. Recruitment of Mexican contract labor coexisted with racist attacks on Mexican American youth; ancestry did not determine a single status (F, M).
- **E61, MA.USII.T3.7; RH.11-12.6.** On an exit-only paragraph of Truman's statement or the Franck report, withheld from teaching, identify its maker and audience. Which reason or alternative does it establish was offered, and what would it fail to establish about necessity? Answer: cite the selected paragraph accurately; an official's surrender or lives-saved claim establishes a justification, while Franck's demonstration proposal establishes a proposed alternative. Neither proves the untried outcome. Date the record before or after each bombing (G, H, N).
- **E62, MA.USII.T3.7.** From a previously unused, non-graphic survivor passage, identify a specific consequence missing from an official reason for bombing. What can you infer, and what can you not infer? Answer: attribute the detail to that survivor and city; distinguish bodily injury, bereavement or disrupted life from officials' stated goals. One testimony cannot establish every experience, official motive or alternative outcome (I).
- **E63, MA.USII.T3.7; D2.His.7.9-12.** A new caption says, "Nuclear debate was identical in 1945 and 1963; the treaty abolished bombs." Correct it using one intervening development. Then explain how a 1995 exhibit participant's purpose could shape which 1945 evidence they highlighted. Answer: Soviet testing and expanding arsenals strengthened deterrence claims, while fallout and escalation strengthened arguments for limits; the treaty banned specified testing environments, not weapons. A participant might emphasize service or civilian harm for commemorative reasons; establish their position from their actual statement, not their identity alone (J, O).
- **E64, MA.USII.T3.7 and T3.8; D2.His.10.9-12.** Collect W64 separately. Case, invented for practice: an official commemoration calls one payment complete repair, while an unused family record documents continuing housing loss. How should the claim change? Answer: acknowledge a legal benefit while bounding restitution; one family cannot establish every experience (L, I). Add an open capstone question.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach mobilization, migration, incarceration, redress, deterrence, fallout and interpretation in context. Offer: "This source establishes __ because __, but cannot establish __." Allow home-language planning and oral rehearsal; compare spoken reasoning with writing to distinguish language from content gaps. Limit independent excerpts to 250 words per sitting; read dense laws aloud. Provide large print, transcripts, described maps and scribed responses. Extension tests a contrary source on the same question.

Give advance content notes and an equivalent quiet text task. Name discrimination, racist violence, forced removal, deaths and radiation injury plainly without graphic detail, slurs, reenactments or forced disclosure. Do not ask students to defend incarceration's racial premise or speak for an ancestry group. Explain civilian casualties alongside Japan's wartime aggression without collective blame. Present women's paid work as older than this war, and computer programming as specialized civilian work supporting it.
