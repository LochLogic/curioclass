## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What did winning the war cost, at home and in Japan?"
**Hook, day 57.** Pair the 1941 fair-employment order with a Japanese American account of forced removal. Ask how mobilization could expand opportunity while taking away liberty.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Who gained work, and where (57 and 58)? Whose rights were denied (59)? How did service strengthen demands for change (60)? What reasons and alternatives did officials consider (61)? What do survivors add (62)? Why did nuclear arguments and public memory change (63)?
**Closing claim, day 64.** Answer the compelling question using the employment order, community accounts, the Interim Committee and Franck Report, Truman, survivor testimony and the test-ban record. Explain gains, unequal costs and a counterclaim.
**Teaching.** Model spending-to-jobs causation on 57 and source purpose on 61 (RH.11-12.6). Pairs compare evidence on 60 and 62. Model present-day interpretation on 63 (D2.His.7.9-12); withdraw frames on 64 (D2.His.10.9-12). Distinguish reasons offered from demonstrated necessity.

**W64, collected on day 64; T3.7 and T3.8.** Write 300 to 400 words answering the compelling question. Use a home-front primary source, a 1945 decision document and a survivor account, with secondary context. Explain a lasting home-front effect, reasons given and human costs; answer contrary evidence. Choose evidence independently rather than repeat the teacher's model argument. Look-fors: qualified claim, accurate chronology, three attributed details linked to reasoning, lasting consequence and counterclaim answered. Meets: Order 8802 prohibited defense hiring discrimination after organizing pressure, but opportunity did not mean equal liberty. Momo Nagano recalled arriving at unfinished barracks in 1942, showing incarceration's disruption. Chieko Kiriake recalled repairing uniforms instead of attending school in Hiroshima, revealing educational costs. Truman's August 6 announcement threatened further destruction to obtain acceptance of surrender terms. Although that aim supports a claim of military urgency, Franck's proposed demonstration shows an alternative was considered; neither document proves its untried outcome. Winning widened some opportunities while leaving losses that later redress could not fully repair. Does not meet: Everyone benefited at home, and the bomb was necessary because we won. Positions on the decision are not scored.

## U3. Checks, written first

Every invented case is labeled "invented for practice". Exit evidence below is withheld from teaching and other checks. Historical passages retain maker, date, audience and purpose; source links and selection boundaries are in field 10. Original summaries are reading supports, never quotations.

- **E57, MA.USII.T3.8a and b.** Case, invented for practice: a federal ship order leads a yard to add shifts; a Black family moves from Louisiana to California after discriminatory restrictions and a job offer, but encounters housing exclusion. Explain the spending-to-jobs chain, movement type, push and pull factors, and why growth need not mean equal gains. Answer: orders raise production and hiring; internal migration combines exclusion with job opportunity; housing barriers limit gains. Wrong: victory alone created jobs (S); foreign immigration (B); jobs alone caused the move (T); all prospered equally (A). Add an open capstone question.
- **E58, MA.USII.T3.8c and d.** Case, invented for practice: organizers threaten a defense-employment march; officials create a hiring complaints process, but a rejected applicant still needs enforcement. A civilian woman ferries aircraft, another makes shells, and a third programs firing calculations; uniformed women keep military records and treat patients. Explain Randolph's leverage and the 1941 order's reach using this case. Then explain all five contributions, service status and the unequal access the case leaves unresolved. Answer: collective pressure helped secure Order 8802; complaints required enforcement. Ferrying, manufacture, programming, records and care supported mobilization; pilots and programmers could be civilians while clerks and nurses served in uniform. Wrong: order integrated armed forces (C); paid work began in 1941 (D); all were combatants (K); expanded work ensured equality (F).
- **E59, MA.USII.T3.8e.** Case, invented for practice: an American citizen loses liberty under an exclusion order; a Canadian-born family loses its home through a government sale and remains barred from the coast after fighting ends. A 1988 payment does not restore their former neighborhood. Explain one shared harm and one policy difference. Place the 1944 Korematsu holding and 1988 redress in that explanation. Answer: ancestry-based coercion harmed citizens in both countries; Canada forcibly sold property and prolonged exclusion under its own government. Korematsu upheld exclusion; 1988 brought apology and redress, not complete restitution. The key adds the 2018 repudiation. Wrong: only foreigners were targeted (P); Canada followed the American order (Q); the Court rejected exclusion in 1944 (R); payment erased every loss (L).
- **E60, MA.USII.T3.8f.** Case, invented for practice: a World War I woman war worker presses an existing suffrage demand, and a returning Black veteran demands protection from racial violence; a World War II woman factory worker demands lasting job access, and a Black serviceman joins Double V organizing. Explain all four contribution-to-demand links and why wartime leverage differs from a movement's origin or automatic equality. Then contrast a Mexican worker recruited on contract with a Mexican American youth attacked by servicemen. Answer: contributions strengthened existing claims to votes, protection and work; organizing and barriers continued. Recruitment and racist attacks show unequal wartime inclusion, not interchangeable roles. Wrong: war created the movements (W); service automatically granted equality (F); all Mexican experiences were identical (M).
- **E61, MA.USII.T3.7; RH.11-12.6.** Read only Truman's August 6, 1945 announcement paragraph beginning "It was to spare", through its threatened sea and land forces. Identify maker, audience and the justification offered, including what it cannot prove about necessity. Then date the announcement relative to both bombings. Answer: Truman addresses the public and Japanese leaders; he presents the Potsdam ultimatum as averting greater destruction and threatens further attack to force acceptance. This establishes a justification, not proof no alternative existed; it follows Hiroshima on August 6 and precedes Nagasaki on August 9. Wrong: the threat proves necessity (H); it followed both bombings (X).
- **E62, MA.USII.T3.7.** Read Yamawaki's exit-only paragraph about waiting overnight for his father, ending with the brothers setting out to find him. Provenance: retrospective testimony by Yoshiro Yamawaki, Nagasaki, hosted in the city's English survivor collection; publication date and translator unstated. What consequence does it add to an official surrender justification, and what cannot follow from it? Answer: an eleven-year-old and his brothers waited anxiously for an absent parent and searched the next morning. This establishes family disruption in Nagasaki; it cannot establish all survivors' experiences, officials' motives or an untried alternative's outcome. Wrong: everyone experienced the same loss (I); it proves official motives (U); it proves a demonstration would have succeeded (N).
- **E63, MA.USII.T3.7; D2.His.7.9-12.** Case, invented for practice: a 1945 argument stresses American monopoly; a 1963 argument confronts rival arsenals and fallout while negotiators ban atmospheric, outer-space and underwater tests but permit contained underground tests. Explain how one intervening development changed an argument and identify the treaty's limit. Then read Heyman's January 30, 1995 statement paragraphs on veterans expecting commemoration and his simpler aircraft-and-crew display. How did that purpose affect his selection of 1945 evidence? Answer: Soviet testing in 1949 ended monopoly; rivalry or fallout strengthened deterrence or limitation arguments. Testing restrictions left weapons in place. Heyman prioritizes honoring veterans' sacrifice, helping explain his proposed focus on the aircraft and crew; his stated purpose does not settle the 1945 decision. Wrong: debate never changed (J); the treaty abolished weapons (O); commemoration proves necessity (H).

Case, invented for practice: an official commemoration calls one payment complete repair, while an unused family record documents continuing housing loss. How should the claim change? Answer: acknowledge a legal benefit while bounding restitution; one family cannot establish every experience (L, I). Add an open capstone question.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach mobilization, migration, incarceration, redress, deterrence, fallout and interpretation in context. Offer: "This source establishes __ because __, but cannot establish __." Allow home-language planning and oral rehearsal; compare spoken reasoning with writing to distinguish language from content gaps. Limit independent excerpts to 250 words per sitting; read dense laws aloud. Provide large print, transcripts, described maps and scribed responses. Extension tests a contrary source on the same question.

Give advance content notes and an equivalent quiet text task. Name discrimination, racist violence, forced removal, deaths and radiation injury plainly without graphic detail, slurs, reenactments or forced disclosure. Do not ask students to defend incarceration's racial premise or speak for an ancestry group. Explain civilian casualties alongside Japan's wartime aggression without collective blame. Present women's paid work as older than this war, and computer programming as specialized civilian work supporting it.
