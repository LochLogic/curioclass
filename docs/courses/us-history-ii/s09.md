## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What makes a social movement succeed?"

**Hook, day 114.** Contrast the 1966 women's equality statement with the later marriage ruling. Does a legal victory establish success?
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Who defines success (114)? How does organizing change institutions (115 and 116)? Why these tactics and opponents (117 to 119)? Success for whom (120 and 121)?
**Closing claim, day 121.** Compare two movements using field 10's primary records and outcome histories; answer a counterclaim.
**Teaching.** Model sourcing on 114, causation on 115 and comparison on 116. Teach perspective on 117 (D2.His.5.9-12); analyze tactics on 118 (D2.Civ.14.9-12). Step back on 119; collect work on 120 and 121. Section 9.1 owns all options.


**Limits.** Survey six options; investigate one fully. Chicano and Asian American comparisons sit on 116. Assess reasoning, never beliefs. Unit 12 owns informed action.
**Carried.** Keep unit 8's sourcing, perspective, causal chains and evidence reasoning. Students revise comparison records on 117 to 120; withdraw argument frames on 121. Retain chronology.

**Daily exits.** Allow five minutes after teaching. Reserve questions and evidence from worked examples. Answers cite evidence. All address T4.8 and applicable options.

**W121, teacher judged, T4.8.** In 250 to 350 words, answer the compelling question using at least two movements and three attributed sources, including primary evidence from each movement and an outcome record. Explain a causal mechanism, compare contexts and answer a counterclaim. Look-fors: defensible criterion of success; accurate content; evidence interpreted in context; reasoning across movements; qualified response to counterevidence. Meets: "Protest can help a movement succeed when organizing connects a demand to an institution able to act. I judge success by an enforceable change that addresses an identified exclusion, while treating everyday implementation as a further question. Disability rights and women's rights illustrate both the value and the limits of that criterion.

Kitty Cone's retrospective account of the 1977 Section 504 sit-in describes people with different disabilities organizing to obtain regulations for a law already passed. Occupying a federal office made delay visible and sustained pressure on the department responsible for issuing regulations. The account gives a participant's explanation of strategy, not independent proof that every participant had identical aims. The National Organization for Women's 1966 Statement of Purpose instead addresses a broad public with demands for full participation and equal opportunity. Its organizational purpose helps explain why it emphasizes collective action across several institutions rather than a single remedy.

A separate outcome record, Congress's Title IX, Education Amendments of 1972, section 901(a), prohibits sex discrimination in federally assisted education programs. That provision establishes a bounded legal protection. It does not establish that every workplace or school became equal. Comparing the records shows why the same tactic cannot explain both histories by itself: disability organizers pressed for administrative implementation, while women's organizing addressed wider exclusions through several routes, including legislation.

A counterclaim is that any enacted protection proves complete success. The statute's limited scope and Cone's account of implementation struggles qualify that claim. Conversely, incomplete equality does not erase a real legal change. These records support a conditional explanation involving organized pressure and institutional authority; they cannot isolate protest as the sole cause or measure everyone's lived access." Does not meet: "Any large protest succeeds because everyone agrees with it." No single political position earns credit.
