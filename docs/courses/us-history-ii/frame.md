# Course frame: US History II (the 1920s to today)

Course `us-history-ii` (catalogue id `us-hist-hs`), grade 12, draft frame (2026-11-13). The days are set in docs/courses/us-history-ii/scope-and-sequence.md and data/plans/us-history-ii/days.csv.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 12, in time order from the 1920s to today, opened by an economics toolkit and closed by a researched argument.
- **Days.** 160 course days (a full year): 154 in units and 6 for three course-level checks, each an assessment day and a
  respond day. One lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week, across 32 weeks. Each unit's days include its
  end-of-unit assessment and one respond day (a unit over 25 days also holds a mid-unit assessment); a row used the year
  before or earlier this year opens as a retrieval task inside a day, and a row last used two or more grades back opens
  its unit with a readiness check on the first day, with a reteach station for students it flags (units 1, 3 to 6, 8, 9
  and 11; the table is in the scope and sequence).
- **Senior calendar.** Grade 12 may end early. The capstone question is chosen on unit 9's respond day (about day 123)
  from the exit-routine notes, and units 10 and 11 each give part of one day to the student's source set. Unit 12 then
  needs 10 days at most: a school that ends seniors early keeps unit 12's audience and action and cuts unit 11's
  check-set additions first, in the order unit 11 gives.
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 USII.T1 to USII.T5 is the content anchor: every numbered code is owned by one row and
  every row sits in one unit below, so the whole topic range has a unit. Where a code lists options (USII.T2.4a to k,
  T4.8a to f), the row names one case studied in depth with the rest surveyed. Skills cite C3 (D2.Geo.8, D2.Geo.9,
  D2.Civ.12 and D4.3 to D4.5 are owned here; the history and inquiry indicators the course practises are owned by
  grades 9 to 11 and are named as practice). Each unit plan compares the unit with California, Tennessee and New York
  and with the college-level US history course framework, and adds inside its days what a strong set treats as
  essential; the column "Check set adds" names the likely additions. Check-set names never appear in student,
  teacher or family copy. The values rule governs: court rulings as the higher court decided them, claims from backed
  sources, LGBTQ people and every group present where the history includes them, depth set by a grade 12 reader.
- **Historical reading and writing.** Reading sources as a historian (sourcing, comparing accounts, weighing evidence)
  and writing historical arguments and explanations are taught in this course's own units, whether or not a school
  pairs it with ELA 12; the paired project shares a question and sources, not the teaching.
- **Base text.** None for the course (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md, P2); every day is Curio Class's own, written with
  references. Partial bases where a unit can use one: Washington OSPI's Since Time Immemorial high school US history
  units (CC BY 4.0, store folder `since-time-immemorial\`) for unit 3 (unit 3 of that set, the Indian Reorganization
  Act), unit 6 (its unit 4, termination and relocation), unit 9 (its unit 5, Indian civil rights and
  self-determination) and unit 11 (its unit 6, nation-building and self-determination today), each after the
  sensitivity read and as the Since Time Immemorial bullet below says; the retired CC BY Principles of Economics 2e
  (store folder `openstax-retired-ccby\`, chapters 2, 3, 17, 27 and 28 and sections 19.1, 21.1, 22.1 and 30.4) for unit 1,
  and its chapter 25 (the Keynesian perspective) for unit 3's comparison of two accounts of the Depression, the monetary
  account sourced separately (the Federal Reserve's dated history of the Great Depression); chapter 26 (the neoclassical
  perspective) only as named further context. No lesson-by-lesson source map: the
  unit plan says which pieces it adapts, each with a one-line credit on the day.
- **Inquiry arc.** Every unit runs on its compelling question, with supporting questions per section, primary sources
  sourced (who, when, for whom, why) before use, and an end-of-unit task done from sources the student has not seen.
  Writing starts from a full sourced argument (unit 2) and runs to the capstone (unit 12); the counterclaim is retrieved
  from grades 8 to 11 and required in every argument from unit 2.
- **Since Time Immemorial.** Where a unit adapts Since Time Immemorial (Washington OSPI's tribally developed curriculum, CC BY 4.0), the content is kept as written (the facts, the tribes' own words and framing); the lesson shape and voice follow Curio Class; each adapted day carries one credit line saying it is adapted from Since Time Immemorial and that the content is the tribes' (Cory, 2026-11-14). Here: unit 3 (its unit on the Indian Reorganization Act), unit 6 (its unit 4, termination and relocation),
  unit 9 (its unit 5, Indian civil rights and self-determination) and unit 11 (its unit 6, nation-building and
  self-determination today).
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md)
  before it ships; no outside human reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). The agent studies first, for: unit 2, Black
  historians and institutions on the Tulsa Race Massacre and the Klan, LGBTQ history archives and scholarship, and
  disability history and a disability language guide for eugenics and Buck v. Bell; unit 3, Mexican American scholarship
  on the 1930s removals, Jewish community histories of 1930s antisemitism, and the Native nations' own pages with Since
  Time Immemorial; unit 4, Holocaust museums' and survivor testimony collections' teaching guidance and the Navajo
  Nation's own account of the Code Talkers; unit 5, Japanese American community archives and the 1983 commission report
  on incarceration, and the survivors' (hibakusha) own testimony collections; unit 6, the Native nations terminated in
  the 1950s (the Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin and the Klamath Tribes among them) from their own pages, with Since
  Time Immemorial, and LGBTQ history scholarship on the Lavender Scare; unit 7, the movement's own organizations' records
  and Black scholarship on the movement and on Boston's desegregation; unit 8, Vietnamese American, Hmong and Cambodian
  American community histories and veterans' oral history collections; unit 9, the movement studied in depth from its
  own organizations (women's, LGBTQ, disability, environmental, farmworker or Native nations' groups) with a disability
  language guide and Since Time Immemorial's unit 5; unit 11, Muslim American community organizations' accounts of the
  backlash after 2001 and the Native nations' own pages for self-government today; unit 12, the community each student's
  question concerns, from that community's own published voice.
- **Paper path.** Every source, map, data set and task has a printed version; no day needs a device, an internet
  connection or a student account. The capstone's second form can be a printed briefing and a spoken talk; a
  documentary or site is an option, never required.
- **Literacy standards (also met).** The unit work also meets these Common Core literacy standards for history and
  social studies (data/standards/ccss-literacy-6-12.txt; the rows' notes name them), with no added days: unit 1,
  RH.11-12.7; unit 5, RH.11-12.6; unit 7, RH.11-12.7; unit 12, WHST.11-12.4, WHST.11-12.5, WHST.11-12.7,
  WHST.11-12.8, WHST.11-12.9. Across the year: RH.11-12.2, RH.11-12.4 and RH.11-12.10 (every unit reads and summarizes
  sources at the band's complexity) and WHST.11-12.10 (routine short and long writing). WHST.11-12.6 is not claimed:
  the paper path keeps every product off a device.
- **Review roles.** Review roles follow FOUNDATION section 7.3, revised 2026-11-14: the sensitivity, safety and answer-key agents do the content reads, each cold and after studying its sources; the cases and sources named here stay binding. The course-level checks get the answer-key agent's read before the course is called finished.
- **Informed action.** Once a year, in unit 12: from the capstone's findings the student chooses whether and how to act
  on a present-day question it raises, through a safe public route. Never a cause the course picks.
- **Economics in two courses.** Unit 1 is a review toolkit (Cory, Desk card q-ss-progression-1): supply and demand and
  opportunity cost come back from grades 7 to 10 with a retrieval task, and the toolkit is used in units 3, 6, 10 and
  11. Neither this course nor Economics assumes the other: a student who took Economics in grade 11 meets unit 1 as
  retrieval at a higher level, and the days stay the same.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 primary sources, excerpted and glossed where the original is long; one secondary
  account per contested event at least, beside a second telling.
- **Topics.** The course's existing topics cover units 2 to 7, 10 and 11 (Roaring Twenties and the Great Depression,
  World War II and the Home Front, Cold War America, Suburb Boom, Civil Rights Movement, Modern America) and the
  source skills (Missing Voices Archive, Behind the Documentary); units 1, 8 and 9 have no topic of their own, and
  the scope and sequence names the three topics to add in its Topic pages table (data/plans/later.csv row L8).
- **The grade 12 timetable** (Cory, 2026-11-14: yes, as picked). US History II takes one period all year. Government and Economics are half years open to
  grade 11 or 12, so a grade 12 student taking both has them in a second period, one each half:

| Period | Fall half (days 1 to 80) | Spring half (days 81 to 160) |
|---|---|---|
| US History II | Units 1 to 5, course-level check 1 and unit 6 to day 80 | The rest of unit 6 (days 81 to 83), the mid-year checkpoint (days 84 and 85, week 17), units 7 to 12, the third course-level check, capstone at the end |
| Second social studies period (suggested) | US Government and Politics (80 days), ending in its local action | Economics (80 days), after this course's unit 1 toolkit |

  The suggested order keeps Government's action (its unit 9) away from this course's capstone and lets Economics
  deepen a toolkit the student met in September. Either order works, since no row in the three courses assumes
  another. A grade 11 student takes US History I all year and Government or Economics in one half.
