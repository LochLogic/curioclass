# Course frame: US History II (the 1920s to today)

Course id `us-hist-hs`, grade 12, draft frame (2026-11-13). The scope and sequence sets the days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 12, in time order from the 1920s to today, opened by an economics toolkit and closed by a researched argument.
- **Days.** 160 teaching days (a full year): 154 in units and 6 for three course-level checks, each a check day and a
  respond day. Each unit's days include its opening retrieval task, its end-of-unit assessment and its own respond day.
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 USII.T1 to USII.T5 is the content anchor: every numbered code is owned by one row and
  every row sits in one unit below, so the whole topic range has a unit. Where a code lists options (USII.T2.4a to k,
  T4.8a to f), the row names one case studied in depth with the rest surveyed. Skills cite C3 (D2.Geo.8, D2.Geo.9,
  D2.Civ.12 and D4.3 to D4.5 are owned here; the history and inquiry indicators the course practises are owned by
  grades 9 to 11 and are named as practice). Each unit plan compares the unit with California, Tennessee and New York
  and with the college-level US history course framework, and adds inside its days what a strong set treats as
  essential; the column "Check set adds" names the likely additions. Check-set names never appear in student,
  teacher or family copy. The values rule governs: court rulings as the higher court decided them, claims from backed
  sources, LGBTQ people and every group present where the history includes them, depth set by a grade 12 reader.
- **Base text.** None for the course (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md, P2); every day is Curio Class's own, written with
  references. Partial bases where a unit can use one: Washington OSPI's Since Time Immemorial high school US history
  units (CC BY 4.0, store folder `since-time-immemorial\`) for unit 3 (unit 3 of that set, the Indian Reorganization
  Act), unit 6 (its unit 4, termination and relocation), unit 9 (its unit 5, Indian civil rights and
  self-determination) and unit 11 (its unit 6, nation-building and self-determination today), each after the
  sensitivity read and keeping that set's content design as its terms ask; the retired CC BY Principles of Economics 2e
  (store folder `openstax-retired-ccby\`, chapters 2, 3, 17 and 27) for unit 1. No lesson-by-lesson source map: the
  unit plan says which pieces it adapts, each with a one-line credit on the day.
- **Inquiry arc.** Every unit runs on its compelling question, with supporting questions per section, primary sources
  sourced (who, when, for whom, why) before use, and an end-of-unit task done from sources the student has not seen.
  Writing builds from a sourced paragraph (unit 2) to a full argument with a counterargument (unit 7 on) to the
  capstone (unit 12).
- **Informed action.** Once a year, in unit 12: from the capstone's findings the student chooses whether and how to act
  on a present-day question it raises, through a safe public route. Never a cause the course picks.
- **Economics in two courses.** Unit 1 is a review toolkit (Cory, Desk card q-ss-progression-1): supply and demand and
  opportunity cost come back from grades 7 to 10 with a retrieval task, and the toolkit is used in units 3, 6, 10 and
  11. Neither this course nor Economics assumes the other: a student who took Economics in grade 11 meets unit 1 as
  retrieval at a higher level, and the days stay the same.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 primary sources, excerpted and glossed where the original is long; one secondary
  account per contested event at least, beside a second telling.
- **Topics.** Pending(ss:us-history-ii): the course's existing topics cover units 2 to 7, 10 and 11 (Roaring Twenties
  and the Great Depression, World War II and the Home Front, Cold War America, Suburb Boom, Civil Rights Movement,
  Modern America) and the source skills (Missing Voices Archive, Behind the Documentary); units 1, 8 and 9 have no
  topic of their own, and the scope and sequence names the topics to add (data/plans/later.csv row L8).
- **The grade 12 timetable.** US History II takes one period all year. Government and Economics are half years open to
  grade 11 or 12, so a grade 12 student taking both has them in a second period, one each half:

| Period | Fall half (days 1 to 80) | Spring half (days 81 to 160) |
|---|---|---|
| US History II | Units 1 to 6 and the first course-level check (about day 80 falls in unit 6) | Units 7 to 12, two course-level checks, capstone at the end |
| Second social studies period (suggested) | US Government and Politics (80 days), ending in its local action | Economics (80 days), after this course's unit 1 toolkit |

  The suggested order keeps Government's action (its unit 9) away from this course's capstone and lets Economics
  deepen a toolkit the student met in September. Either order works, since no row in the three courses assumes
  another. A grade 11 student takes US History I all year and Government or Economics in one half.

**Shared research brief with ELA 12** (us2-research-paper and e12-research, cross-read finding 8). Where a school teaches
both, one project serves both: one question and one source set; ELA 12 owns the writing (W.11-12.7 and W.11-12.8, about
2,500 words over its research weeks) and the presentation standards; US History II owns the historical question, the
source sourcing and corroboration and D4.3 to D4.5, scored on its own rubric. ELA 12's research weeks should end at or
near this course's unit 12, so the two finish together. Each course still teaches the whole cycle for a school that
uses it alone.
