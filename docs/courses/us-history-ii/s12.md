## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What question from the past century still needs an answer, and what do the sources say?"

**Hook, day 148.** Read the Library of Congress description of Marian Anderson's 1939 concert beside this proposed claim: "One concert ended segregation." What could the records establish?
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Which sources deserve confidence (148 and 149)? How do argument and explanation differ (151 and 152)? What must change for another audience (154 to 156)? What action follows, if any (157 and 160)?
**Closing claim, day 158.** Each student answers the chosen question using the named sources in their approved register and answers counterevidence. The teaching example weighs Anderson's concert as a challenge to exclusion, using the library's secondary organizing account, concert description and the museum's training excerpt, without claiming segregation ended.
**Teaching.** Model source independence on 149, argument critique on 151, causal explanation on 152 and audience adaptation on 154. Withdraw the model during each independent task. Keep unit 11's perspective, causal chains, continuity and change, and evidence reasoning. Source every record before use. Corroboration returns in revision on 153 and independent presentation on 156; withdraw its organizer on 158.


**Owned indicators, in our words.** D4.3.9-12: adapt and present arguments and explanations with engaging ideas and perspectives for varied audiences and venues beyond class, using print, speech and possible digital forms. D4.4.9-12: critique how claims and evidence establish an argument's credibility. D4.5.9-12: critique an explanation's reasoning, sequence and supporting details. Section 12.1 owns all parts. Digital production is optional under the course's paper path.
**Limits.** Synthesis, rather than another content survey. Assess historical reasoning and communication, never political agreement or successful persuasion. Standalone: 1,500 to 2,000 words, six sources including three primary. Paired: one existing English language arts draft, about 2,500 words, eight sources including three primary; no second paper.
**Daily pacing.** Days 149 to 155, except 153, use opening 5, model 10, work 30 and exit 5 minutes. Day 153 uses feedback 5, revision 40 and exit 5: ten per critique and twenty for the argument. Day 148 uses hook 5, retrieval 5, model 10, audit 25 and exit 5. Days 157 and 158 use opening 5, instruction 5, work 35 and exit 5. No student timers.

**Ten-day option.** Keep the normal 13-day schedule above. If the school needs the scope and sequence's shorter path, combine the two critiques as separate 20-minute tasks after ten minutes of modeling, combine forms and rehearsal, and combine presentation and hand-in. Place action before that combined hand-in so its record is complete. Keep assessment and response separate. Use prior drafts and source records; do not reduce the two forms, audiences or critique criteria. Field 5 gives the ten-session table and writing totals.

**E152, original teaching explanation.** "Anderson's concert became a public challenge to racial exclusion because her artistry and organized support made an alternative venue possible. Roosevelt alone caused its success. Anderson's April concert caused Eleanor Roosevelt's earlier February resignation from the Daughters of the American Revolution, and the large audience proves discrimination disappeared."

Prompt: Using the museum biography, the library account and the concert description, critique its reasoning, sequence and details, then repair it. Answer: preserve the explanation's link between exclusion, organizing and an alternative venue; reject sole causation and the reversed sequence: Roosevelt resigned in February 1939, and Anderson sang on April 9, 1939. Audience evidence establishes public reach, not the disappearance of discrimination. The teacher models the procedure first with a different original explanation about Anderson's earlier musical training, using the museum's training excerpt specified below.

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Two retellings from one original provide independent corroboration. | Confuses repetition or status with independent support. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 149: trace both retellings to their common origin. |
| B | A crowd's size proves equality everywhere. | Overgeneralizes or ignores counterevidence. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 150: contrast a bounded audience claim with universal equality. |
| C | New headings alone adapt an argument for another audience. | Confuses repackaging with adaptation. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 154: compare identical paragraphs with an explanation of unfamiliar context. |
| D | Agreement or proofreading is sufficient credibility critique. | Substitutes agreement or proofreading for credibility. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 151: test a claim against its cited evidence. |
| E | One person alone caused a complex historical outcome. | Replaces an interacting causal chain with a single actor. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 152: build a supported chain involving several actors. |
| F | A later event caused an earlier decision. | Ignores chronology when testing explanation. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 152: reconstruct the dated sequence. |
| G | No reply proves an action failed. | Confuses action quality with agreement or visible change. | Anticipated, from memory. Day 157: compare a reasoned process with its uncertain external result. |
| H | Announcing a policy proves it achieved its aim. | Intention substitutes for implementation evidence. | From memory and assessment records. Day 152: compare a proposed action with a documented result. |

If more than a third miss an exit, use the next day's first ten work minutes for the listed repair, then recheck with a different source. Otherwise conference during work. Teach the repair before observing. Students without that gap test a rival interpretation.
