## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Were the 1920s a decade of new freedoms or of new fears?"
**Hook, day 13.** Read Eastman's opening beside the 19th Amendment. Ask whose freedoms each establishes.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Whose voice grew (days 13 and 14)? What was contested (15 and 16)? Who shared prosperity (17)? Who belonged (18 to 20)? Who contested authority (21)? What changes our answer (22 and 23)?
**Closing claim, day 23.** Argue freedoms or fears, specifying for whom. Use four sources across culture and restriction: Hughes, Hurston, Eastman, the immigration law, Franklin or Buck. Answer the strongest counterclaim. Either conclusion can succeed.
**Teaching.** Model sourcing on day 13, corroboration on 19 and counterclaims on 22. Practise D2.His.4.9-12 on 21 and D3.1.9-12 on 22. Teacher notes explain chronology and overlapping traditions and modernities. Eugenics claimed modern science while enforcing exclusion. Study faith historically without personal disclosure.

**E17, teacher judged.** Look for correct payment totals, an economic mechanism, cultural and landscape effects, and the distinction between policy and corruption. ## U3. Daily evidence for section 2.1

The unit assessment is in field 5. Reserve these prompts and source passages from teaching examples. Accept other supported conclusions; diagnose with field 6's letters.

- **E13, MA.USII.T2.1c.** Read Eastman's later paragraph about occupational barriers, not used in the hook. How could a nurse's or telephone operator's wartime contribution coexist with that complaint after suffrage? Answer: skilled service, pay, recognition and voting access were different questions; sex-based voting exclusion ended constitutionally, while other barriers remained (C). Add an open question for the capstone.
- **E14, MA.USII.T2.1a.** Read the reserved closing paragraph of Hurston's essay beside Hughes's Mother to Son (1922), both with labels. What different voices do they construct, and why might business networks and patrons matter to their circulation? Answer: Hurston asserts an individual identity beyond imposed categories; Hughes's speaker counsels persistence through hardship. Publishers, venues and financial support could extend audiences without authoring the works. One author's experience cannot represent all Black Americans (A, B).
- **E15, MA.USII.T2.1b and T2.1g.** Would a favorable review establish that everyone welcomed cubism? Would a different verdict in Scopes establish scientific truth? Explain the limits of both inferences, placing the Armory Show before the trial and Darwin's book before both. Answer: one review cannot establish universal reception; a verdict determines a legal charge, not scientific validity. Order: 1859, 1913, 1925. Identify experimental form and biblical authority as different stakes in the conflicts (A, G).
- **E16, MA.USII.T2.1h and T2.1i.** Could a crowded city ball photograph establish nationwide equality for gay and lesbian people, or prove that Prohibition was being violated? What additional evidence would each claim need? Answer: gathering shows presence, not equal access; examine laws, policing and community testimony. A ball or jazz performance alone does not establish prohibited liquor manufacture, sale or transport (C).
- **E17, check-set additions.** A fictional 1920s radio costs $72 in cash or twelve $7 payments. Explain the extra cost and why access is not income. Then connect mass production, cars, radio and film to one cultural change and one landscape change. Contrast Harding's normalcy, Coolidge's business-friendly policies and the Teapot Dome bribery investigation. Answer: $84 total, $12 extra; repayment reduces later spending. Cheaper output and mass communication spread consumption and culture; roads and settlement patterns change. Policy preference differs from evidence of corrupt leasing. Wrong: installments create wages, or every business-friendly policy is bribery (I). Score this evidence with the unit assessment; no family finance disclosure.
- **E18, MA.USII.T2.1d and citizenship addition.** The 1924 citizenship law preserves tribal property rights. Can that sentence establish that Native nations disappeared, that every Native person first became a citizen then, or that voting barriers vanished? Explain. Then distinguish a quota's admissions rule from a refugee's need for protection and a Red Scare accusation. Answer: no to all three claims; sovereignty and earlier citizenship persisted, and discriminatory barriers remained. Legal admission, displacement and political accusation differ (C, D).
- **E19, MA.USII.T2.1e.** A map shows Black population growth in northern cities. What can it establish about migration, and what can it not establish about motives or safety? Name evidence needed to connect racial terror, the revived Klan and Tulsa to that pattern without claiming a single cause. Answer: settlement change; migrant testimony, employment records and local violence histories are needed for motives and conditions. Northern discrimination continued; Tulsa's white attackers destroyed Greenwood, whose residents also resisted and rebuilt (E).
- **E20, MA.USII.T2.1f.** A label claims eugenics concerned only surgery. Correct it using segregation and immigration policy, then distinguish Buck's legal outcome from a scientific conclusion. Answer: false hereditary classifications also supported institutional separation and exclusion; the Court upheld compulsory sterilization in 1927 but did not establish sound science. Later rejection is not formal overruling (F).
- **E21, MA.USII.T2.1j; D2.His.4.9-12.** Why might a textile worker, a police striker and a defendant's supporter give different accounts of public order? Anchor each in Lawrence, Boston, and Sacco and Vanzetti, with dates and a specific concern. Answer: wages in 1912, union recognition and conditions in 1919, and evidence, prejudice and a fair murder trial in 1921 before execution in 1927. Perspective explains a claim without proving its truth or guilt (H).
- **E22, MA.USII.T2.1; D3.1.9-12.** Identify a source that most challenges your draft claim. What does its author establish, what remains uncertain, and how must your claim change? Answer: accurate attribution, a relevant contrary detail and a qualified revision rather than dismissal (A, B).
- **E23, MA.USII.T2.1; WHST.11-12.10.** Submit A12, then identify one source your argument still needs and why. Answer: a specific missing perspective or counterexample, not another source merely agreeing (A, B). Add an open capstone question.
