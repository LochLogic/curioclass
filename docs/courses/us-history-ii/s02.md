## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Were the 1920s a decade of new freedoms or of new fears?"
**Hook, day 13.** Read Eastman's opening beside the 19th Amendment. Ask whose freedoms each establishes.
**Supporting questions.** Co-write: Whose voice grew (days 13 and 14)? What was contested (15 and 16)? Who shared prosperity (17)? Who belonged (18 to 20)? Who contested authority (21)? What changes our answer (22 and 23)?
**Closing claim, day 23.** Argue freedoms or fears, specifying for whom. Use four sources across culture and restriction: Hughes, Hurston, Eastman, the immigration law, the museum's secondary account of Franklin or Buck. Answer the strongest counterclaim. Either conclusion can succeed.
**Teaching.** Model sourcing on day 13, corroboration on 19 and counterclaims on 22. Practise D2.His.4.9-12 on 21 and D3.1.9-12 on 22. Teacher notes explain chronology and overlapping traditions and modernities. Eugenics claimed modern science while enforcing exclusion. Study faith historically without personal disclosure.

## U3. Daily evidence for section 2.1

The unit assessment is in field 5. Reserve these prompts and source passages from teaching examples. Accept other supported conclusions; diagnose with field 6's letters.

- **E13, MA.USII.T2.1c.** Read Eastman's later paragraph about occupational barriers, not used in the hook. How could a nurse's or telephone operator's wartime contribution coexist with that complaint after suffrage? Answer: skilled service, pay, recognition and voting access were different questions; sex-based voting exclusion ended constitutionally, while other barriers remained (AE). Add an open question for the capstone.
- **E14, MA.USII.T2.1a.** Read Hurston's reserved final bag paragraph beside Hughes's reserved Mother to Son (1922), both with labels. What different voices do they construct, and why might business networks and patrons matter to their circulation? Answer: Hurston's bag metaphor asserts shared humanity across imposed categories; Hughes's speaker counsels persistence through hardship. Publishers, venues and financial support could extend audiences without authoring the works. One author's experience cannot represent all Black Americans (W).
- **E15, MA.USII.T2.1b and T2.1g.** Case, invented for practice: in 1924, a local gallery advertises portraits built from intersecting planes, while a school board bars a biology chapter to protect biblical authority. Explain the distinct stakes in each conflict and one limit of inferring unanimous public agreement from these two institutional notices. Answer: experimental representation contests artistic conventions; the board contests the authority governing science instruction. Neither notice establishes all viewers' or Christians' positions (AC, G).
- **E16, MA.USII.T2.1h and T2.1i.** Case, invented for practice: a city club's rule sheet welcomes same-sex couples to a jazz ball but records nothing about liquor. Does this establish nationwide equality or a Prohibition violation? Explain what evidence each inference lacks. Answer: a local welcome cannot establish legal equality; examine laws, policing and community testimony. Jazz alone proves no prohibited liquor manufacture, sale or transport (C, R).
- **E17, check-set additions.** Case, invented for practice: a radio costs $72 cash or twelve $7 payments; a lawmaker favors lower business taxes, with no evidence of bribes supplied. Explain the credit cost and why access is not income; does the tax preference establish corruption? Answer: $84 total, $12 extra; repayment commits future income. A policy preference is not evidence of bribery. Wrong: credit creates wages (I); favorable policy proves bribery (P). Permanently unscored practice. Cultural and landscape effects remain in the day's practice and CE22. No family finance disclosure.
- **E18, MA.USII.T2.1d and citizenship addition.** Case, invented for practice: a Native citizen retains tribal property but a registrar refuses a ballot; a displaced refugee is denied a quota place. What does each case show about citizenship, sovereignty and access? Answer: citizenship need not end sovereignty or voting barriers; refugee need does not guarantee legal admission. Wrong: citizenship dissolves a nation (AB); citizenship guarantees a ballot (AF).
- **E19, MA.USII.T2.1e.** Case, invented for practice: a northern city counts 1,000 Black residents among 10,000 residents in 1910 and 3,000 among 20,000 in 1920. What changed, and what additional sources would test migration, motives and safety, including possible connections to the revived Klan and Tulsa? Answer: the count and share rose, from 10 to 15 percent; counts alone do not separate migration from births or establish motives. Seek migrant testimony, job records and violence histories; Tulsa's white mob terror does not explain every move, and northern discrimination persisted (E, L).
- **E20, MA.USII.T2.1f.** Case, invented for practice: a 1926 state commission calls hereditary rankings scientific and proposes separate schools, restricted entry and compulsory sterilization. A 1928 legal digest cites Buck as support. Explain how the commission's proposals share one false premise; distinguish what the digest can establish about law from what it establishes about science. Answer: false inherited rankings rationalize segregation, immigration exclusion and sterilization; Buck upheld coercion in 1927, but legal approval does not validate science (AD, F).
- **E21, MA.USII.T2.1j; D2.His.4.9-12.** Two account cards, invented for practice: a newspaper editor blames a robbery suspect solely because he is an anarchist; a neighbor defending him offers an alibi but cannot recall the date. Explain how each position could shape the account, and what further evidence would help test guilt. Answer: the editor substitutes political hostility for crime evidence; the neighbor's support does not establish an accurate date. Seek independent timelines and evidence of conduct. Wrong: political beliefs prove guilt (O).
- **E22, MA.USII.T2.1; D3.1.9-12.** Case, invented for practice: an unsigned 1920s advertisement claims every worker could afford its product, while an independent wage record shows a gap. Which record would you seek next, and how should the draft claim change? Answer: investigate authorship, audience and comparable wages or purchases; qualify the universal claim. Wrong: dismiss the contrary record because it is unwelcome (V).
- **E23, MA.USII.T2.1; WHST.11-12.10.** Collect W23 separately. Case, invented for practice: a library exhibition counts ticket sales as proof of equal access but has no admissions-policy record. Name a missing source and explain its value to a revised argument. Answer: a dated policy or excluded visitors account could test access; sales alone cannot establish equality (C). Add an open capstone question.
