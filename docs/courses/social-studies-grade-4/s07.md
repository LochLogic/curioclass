**Assessment materials and directions.** These are draft original source summaries and constructed diagnostic records, not quotations. Keep their details out of teaching, practice, review and exit tickets. Final historical selections need the source verification named in field 10. The strips supply information, not completed student responses. Teach source reading beforehand with different people, contributions and cases. A region includes differences within communities and relationships across boundaries. Contributions do not justify colonization or forced labor.

**Regional strips C, unseen historical summaries:**

- **Northeast:** Wampanoag communities sustain language learning. Africans, both enslaved and free, worked as skilled artisans in port towns. Early Dutch settlers developed trading towns. Later Irish immigrants organized workers. More recent Somali arrivals developed farms and markets in Maine.
- **Southeast:** Cherokee people developed writing in their own language. African Americans established schools. Spanish colonists built forts in Florida on Native homelands. Cuban immigrants developed cigar-making communities in Tampa.
- **Midwest:** Menominee people manage forests for future generations. African Americans developed newspapers in Chicago. European immigrants established farms. Hmong arrivals developed vegetable markets in Minnesota.
- **Southwest:** Pueblo peoples developed farming communities. African Americans founded towns in Oklahoma. Spanish settlers developed irrigation works drawing on several peoples' knowledge. Mexican communities sustained ranching skills. Vietnamese arrivals built fishing communities on the Texas coast.
- **West:** Makah people maintain canoe knowledge. African Americans built the town of Allensworth. Spanish colonists established missions using Native labor, including forced labor. Mexican communities developed ranching. Chinese workers built levees. Japanese immigrants formed farm associations. Filipino immigrants organized agricultural workers.

The strips represent specific examples, not inherited traits or complete group histories. Spanish and Mexican are dated political and cultural identities that can overlap; neither means a single race. The teacher must name the source community and date on each final card. The five short sentences students write describe five regional examples; underlining checks the remaining named groups without requiring twenty-five paragraphs.

**M, constructed movement maps:** print two labelled diagrams, with the same river, rail line, farm area, port and town sites. In the nineteenth-century diagram, arrows show families moving toward river towns and grain traveling downstream; a newspaper travels along the rail line. In the twenty-first-century diagram, arrows show arrivals joining relatives near the rail-connected city, produce traveling to city markets, and language teachers traveling between communities. Both have dates, titles and a key. Students explain the arrows; they do not have to draw a new map. Ask: "What stayed connected? What changed? How did land and community connections affect people, goods and ideas?"

**S, constructed settlement diagrams:** in an environmental case, river flooding makes lowland houses unusable; arrows lead to an uphill district and a neighboring city, whose temporary housing fills. In a technological case, a maintained reservoir wall fails after a structural defect; arrows lead from waterside houses to another town. A separate arrow shows disrupted water delivery to a downstream town. Labels say what happened, without giving a causal paragraph. Ask: "Compare settlement and migration before and after both events. Explain an effect on another place in each case. What can these diagrams not tell us about individual families' choices?" Key: the diagrams show displacement and connections, but not each person's reasons or eventual return. No invented counts are represented as historical data.

**P, constructed council record:** "Floodwater blocked the only shelter entrance. Residents asked the council for another route. Council members heard residents and emergency workers, compared costs, and voted for a second entrance. After an access test and more public comments, the council added a step-free route." Ask: "Trace how this policy developed. What challenge remained, and what opportunity did the response create?" Key: restricted access and costs are challenges; public input and more usable shelter access are opportunities. Do not claim step-free access serves only one kind of person.

**J, unseen New Zealand summary:** after earthquakes damaged central Christchurch, its council asked residents what rebuilding should include. Ideas informed a draft recovery plan. Time was short, residents were coping with disruption, and projects needed priorities and funding. The draft still required debate and decisions. This original summary uses the New Zealand Audit Office's account of community participation and the government's August 2011 draft-plan announcement. Ask: "Compare a challenge and an opportunity with P. How do location and public decisions matter? What shared global problem do these local rebuilding cases illustrate?" Key: rebuilding after disasters combines limited resources, location-specific damage and public choices in places around the world. Teaching on 154 uses Japan, a different event and response.

**Teacher notes:** teach slavery as forced labor and denied freedom, with resistance, skills and lives built. Never describe contributions as payment for rights or as making colonization beneficial. Native governments and contemporary people appear beside historical examples. Use communities' own names and public materials; omit sacred knowledge and craft imitation. Spanish missions include Native labor and coercion. Exclusion targeted Chinese labor immigration in 1882, with exceptions and restrictions on citizenship; it was not a ban on every Asian person. Japanese American incarceration denied freedom to people targeted for ancestry, including citizens; teach it with the later federal acknowledgment and apology. Do not debate whether a child or community deserved rights. No role play of exclusion, forced labor, removal or confinement, and no slurs from primary documents.

Disaster sources avoid graphic injury, frightening soundtracks and reenactment. Do not ask about family losses, immigration or legal status. Offer quiet source work with the same goal. Adults handle emergency actions; student proposals concern evidence, communication and decisions, not rescue work, purchases or entry into hazard areas. Make access part of public planning, without asking a child to represent a disability. No child is blamed for housing, transport or resource constraints.
