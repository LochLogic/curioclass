# Scope and sequence: Grade 4: North America, Its Land and Peoples

Course `social-studies-grade-4`, grade 4, draft, written 2026-11-14 from the frame, revised the same day from the expert and Codex sequence reads (docs/reviews/sequence-3-5-fixes-2026-11-14.md) (docs/courses/social-studies-grade-4/frame.md). The days are in data/plans/social-studies-grade-4/days.csv; this page gives the judgment behind them.

## The year at a glance

- 160 course days, one lesson of about 40 minutes every school day (course day n is school day n): 150 in seven units, 10 at course level. Units, owned rows and day counts are the frame's, unchanged.
- Standard: Massachusetts 2018 grade 4 (4.T1 to 4.T4e) as content anchor, every code on a day; C3 (3 to 5 band) for skills.
- Every unit runs the C3 arc: the compelling question and supporting questions on the unit's first day, content taught in order, sources weighed, a claim with evidence from two sources, then the end-of-unit assessment (a task from sources not seen before) and one respond day.
- Grade 4's steps up: sources chosen by two tests (unit 2), who made a source, when, for whom and why (unit 4), two sources compared with the check that one did not copy the other (unit 4), a timeline drawn to scale (unit 4).
- Own-place method: the student's own state in its region (day 113), its peoples (day 144) and its hazards (days 155 to 157).
- Informed action: once, in unit 7 (days 155 to 157): a written proposal to the school or town on one local hazard response, judged against the policy the class traced.
- Readiness checks: none. Every row the year assumes is a grade 3 row used the year before, so each opens as a retrieval task inside a day (named in the note column), not a readiness check (rule 2); the frame's Days bullet now says so.
- Shorter path (FOUNDATION section 5): 14 days are marked "compressible (shorter block)" in the note column (days 8, 13, 22, 34, 36, 55, 57, 75, 77, 98, 101, 119, 128, 130): practice, evidence-gathering and review days. A school on a shorter block folds each into the day before or after it, the work moving with it (a gathering day folds into its claim day, a review into the assessment's opening); no owned code lives only on a compressible day, and no assessment or respond day is compressible.
- Writing grows across the year, in five steps named in the note column: one modelled paragraph on a full frame (day 14); a focused claim from organized notes (day 35); two linked paragraphs with reasons tying evidence to the claim on a reduced frame (days 56 and 76); an independently organized response with a checklist (days 100, 129 and 158); a proposal drafted and revised for its chosen reader (days 156 to 157). Oral rehearsal and the frames stay available as supports.
- Every assessment day (end-of-unit, mid-unit, course-level) lists every code it assesses, and its goal asks about each part of each code, a short prompt per part; codes judged from a product (unit 2's report and presentation, unit 7's proposal) say so in the note and are scored with the unit rubric.
- Sensitivity: every unit names Native nations or Indigenous peoples; each day with Native, hard-history, exclusion or disaster content says "sensitivity read" in its note (added on days 49 to 51, 54, 71, 140 and 146 to 150 at the fix pass), for the fresh agent's read with the unit plan.
- Personal finance: one light touch, no added day (day 125, a worker's pay split between needs, wants and saving).

## The topics, unit by unit (later.csv L8)

Grade 4 lost topics to other years in the redraw, so each unit's topics are named here in full; together they fill the year inside the frame's units and days.

1. North America on the map: the physical map of North America (rivers, lakes, oceans, mountains, deserts) with title, key, directions and scale; North America among the Western Hemisphere's physical regions; distances by ruler and parts of a scale bar, converted to miles or kilometres; one place at three scales; Canada's provinces and territories, Mexico's states, the Caribbean nations and the United States; continent, nation, country, state, province, county, city; Native nations as nations; latitude and longitude in whole degrees.
2. Canada and Mexico: kinds of sources; the two tests for a source set; a source's setting; fact and opinion; one fact checked in two sources; research on people and languages (First Nations, Inuit, Métis, the Indigenous peoples of Mexico), government (compared with the United States), cities, land and industries; a short report presented and critiqued; why the three countries trade, what crosses the borders, who gains and who loses.
3. The first peoples: how archaeologists work (digs, artifacts, climate data, teamwork); the deep past on a timeline in thousands of years; the land bridge and the coastal route weighed against dated evidence; Native nations' own origin accounts; culture regions; tools, pottery, mounds and cliff dwellings; farming and settlement; Mesa Verde and the Pueblo nations; Cahokia and the Osage and other nations; why preserved sites matter.
4. Voyages and conquest: how historians study voyages and what each kind of source adds; sourcing (who, when, for whom, why); the Norse at L'Anse aux Meadows; why rulers paid for voyages, and compass, astrolabe and ship design; Columbus and the Taíno; two sources on one landing; the exchange of plants, animals and diseases; Cortés, his Native allies and the Mexica capital; Taíno descendants and Nahua people today; Cartier; Coronado; the to-scale timeline and the paired Coronado and Cartier timeline.
5. How the United States grew: the gains of 1791 to 1898 mapped and dated; the Louisiana Purchase and Oregon; canals and roads (the Erie Canal) and railroads; why people moved west, wagon trains and the Oregon and California trails; causes and effects of moving west; Mexico's northern lands; Texas independence, annexation and the war of 1846 to 1848 from several sides; Mexican Americans who stayed; Alaska (1867), the Hawaiian Kingdom (1893, 1898), Puerto Rico (1898) and their peoples today; coming by choice and by force; the division over slavery's spread that led to the Civil War.
6. The regions, states, land and resources: the states of the five regions, the fifty states and capitals, and the territories; the admission timeline; why statehood beat colonial status and what a territory lacks; our own state's admission; a Northeast region map and four state maps (Southeast; Midwest resources and farming; Southwest homelands, the Long Walk, reservations and settlement; West landmarks and parks); how people adapted to each region; resources, towns, industry and trade; specialization, one worker and one business; Washington, D.C., its three branches' buildings, its civic resources and its residents' missing vote.
7. The regions, peoples and hazards: the peoples of each region and what they built and gave (Native nations, African Americans, Spanish and Mexican settlers, Europeans, the Gold Rush, Chinese and Japanese Americans with what they built, exclusion and incarceration (two days), later and recent arrivals); our own region's peoples; movement compared across regions; hurricanes, floods, tornadoes, drought and wildfires; the Johnstown Flood beside a natural disaster; who was displaced; how a far disaster reaches us; government and citizen responses; one response traced as a policy; one international comparison; the hazard proposal.

## Unit 1. North America on the map. "What can a map tell us, and what does it leave out?"

Days 1 to 16, weeks 1 to 4. Codes: MA.4.T1.1, MA.4.T1.2; D2.Geo.3.

Physical map first (days 2 to 4), then measuring and converting (5 to 6), then scale as a choice (7 to 8), then the political map and its words (9 to 11), then latitude and longitude (12 to 13). Physical before political because the political map's borders are read over the land; scale before the three-scale comparison because students must measure before they can say what a scale hides.

- End-of-unit assessment: day 15, every owned code: physical features read with the map's parts and a ruler distance, political places and the place words, latitude and longitude, what a scale shows and hides, and a claim about what an unseen map leaves out. Respond: day 16.
- Candidate (New York grade 5, physical regions of the Western Hemisphere): kept, one map task on day 4, no added day.
- Leans: math 4.MD.A.2 comes later (math unit 6, days 77 to 95); days 5 and 6 carry the frame's bridge (whole and half bar lengths, a given conversion table, doubling and adding only).

## Unit 2. Our neighbors: Canada and Mexico. "How are Canada, Mexico and the United States tied together?"

Days 17 to 38, weeks 4 to 8. Codes: MA.4.T1.3; D1.5; D3.1, D3.2; D4.5; D2.Eco.14, 15.

The research routine is taught before it is used (days 18 to 22: kinds of sources, the two tests, setting, fact and opinion, one fact in two sources), then four research days, one per topic (23 to 26), a draft, a presentation and a critique (27 to 29). Trade follows (30 to 33) because the research has just shown each country's industries, which is why they trade.

- End-of-unit assessment: day 37, every owned code: kinds of source, the two tests and a source's setting, fact and opinion and one fact checked twice, a research question answered from an unseen set, a given explanation critiqued, why the countries trade and who gains and loses, and a claim; each student's report and presentation (days 27 to 28) also scored with the unit rubric. Respond: day 38.
- Candidate (New York grade 5, comparing the governments): kept, one table on day 24.
- Leans: ELA W.4.7 comes later (ELA unit 2, days 39 to 77); day 23 onward use the unit's own note-taking frame and day 27 a report frame. ELA SL.4.4 runs in ELA unit 1 now.

## Unit 3. The first peoples of North America. "How do we know about people who lived here thousands of years ago?"

Days 42 to 59, weeks 9 to 12. Codes: MA.4.T2.1 to 4.

How we know comes before what we know: archaeology's methods (day 43) and the deep-time timeline (44), then the two theories and the dated evidence (45 to 46), the nations' own accounts beside them (47), and the first claim weighing archaeological migration theories (48). Then the material evidence (49 to 51) and the preserved sites with the nations who govern today (52 to 54).

- End-of-unit assessment: day 58, every owned code: a theory weighed against unseen dated evidence, a culture-region map and an artifact photograph read, how archaeologists build a theory, why a preserved site matters, and a claim. Respond: day 59.
- Candidate (California grade 5, pre-Columbian settlements and farming): kept, inside day 51.
- Leans: science 4-ESS1-1 comes later (science unit 4, school days 72 to 92); day 43 teaches in one lesson that deeper layers are usually older.

## Unit 4. Voyages and conquest. "What did the voyages of the 1400s and 1500s mean for the people already here?"

Days 60 to 79, weeks 12 to 16. Codes: MA.4.T3.1 to 3; D2.His.1, 9, 11.

Sourcing is taught first (days 61 to 63), deepening grade 3's primary and secondary sort and unit 2's two tests: these are the year's first primary sources of the past, and audience and purpose are inferred from a clue inside the source, not read from a label. Then the voyages in time order: the Norse (64), why rulers paid and how ships sailed (65), Columbus and the Taíno (66) with the two-source comparison on that landing (67), the exchange between hemispheres (68), Cortés and the Mexica capital (69), the peoples today (70), Cartier (71) and Coronado (72), then the to-scale timeline (73) and the paired timeline (74). Each voyage day traces its route on one accumulating class map.

- End-of-unit assessment: day 78, every owned code: what each kind of source adds, an unseen source sourced with audience and purpose inferred, the Norse evidence, two routes traced and why rulers paid, a to-scale timeline with two events at the same time compared, and a claim from two sources. Respond: day 79.
- Candidates: California grade 5 (navigation tools): kept, inside day 65. New York grade 5 (the exchange of plants, animals and diseases): kept, day 68, inside the unit's 20 days.
- Leans: ELA RI.4.6 comes later (ELA unit 4, days 120 to 156); day 62 reteaches first-hand and second-hand with one voyage account of each kind.

## Unit 5. How the United States grew. "Who gained and who lost as the United States grew?"

Days 80 to 103, weeks 16 to 21. Codes: MA.4.T4.1 to 5, MA.4.T4d.2, MA.4.T4e.2; D2.His.14.

Time order inside a map. The overview map (81), the Purchase and Oregon (82), how people and goods moved (83 to 84), why people went west, two reasons compared, with trail and settlement life (85), and what it meant for the nations already there (86); then Mexico's northern lands, Texas and the war from several sides (87 to 91); then choice and force and the division over slavery's spread into the land just won (92 to 94), which led to the Civil War and which grade 5 picks up; then Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico (95 to 97, 1867 to 1898) and the finished map and timeline (98), so the unit stays in time order.

- End-of-unit assessment: day 102, every owned code: canals, roads and railroads, three gains dated and mapped with a cause and effects for one, two reasons for moving west and trail or settlement life, Mexico's northern lands and two accounts of the war, choice and force, the division over slavery, and a claim. Respond: day 103.
- Candidates: New York grade 4 (the Erie Canal): kept, day 83, using ELA grade 4 unit 3's canal set, no second canal text; social studies may open the set before ELA does (day 83 is early in ELA's unit 3). California grade 5 (the overland trails): kept, inside day 85.

## Unit 6. The regions: states, land and resources. "Why do people in each region make and do what they do?"

Days 107 to 132, weeks 22 to 27. Codes: MA.4.T4a.1 to 4, MA.4.T4b.1, MA.4.T4b.4, MA.4.T4b.5, MA.4.T4c.1, MA.4.T4c.4, MA.4.T4d.1, MA.4.T4d.5, MA.4.T4e.1, MA.4.T4e.5; D2.Geo.1, 8; D2.Eco.4.

States and statehood first (108 to 113), because the maps need the states placed, with the capitals spread by region over days 108 to 110 and retrieved on days 119 and 130; then the five maps (114 to 118), each from a supplied base map and a bounded atlas source set, the Long Walk taught briefly on day 117 before it is mapped; the mid-unit assessment; then adaptation, resources and specialization (121 to 125), the Northeast as the worked case before the others, run as a short question-led investigation of two supplied sources (122); the capital last (126 to 127), beside statehood and Congress.

- Mid-unit assessment: day 120 (the unit is 26 days), every part of the first-half codes: states, capitals and territories located, admission dates and a span, why statehood beat being a colony, a small state map made (not only read), and one item for each map theme; its response opens day 121.
- End-of-unit assessment: day 131, every owned code: the first-half codes as one short prompt each, a question about a region answered from two sources on adaptation, resources, settlement, industry and trade, specialization, Washington, D.C.'s buildings and one other civic resource, and a claim. Respond: day 132.
- Candidate (California grade 5, fifty states and capitals): kept, days 108 to 110, retrieved on days 119 and 130.
- Leans: math 4.NBT.B.4 (math unit 5, days 58 to 76) and 4.MD.A.2 (math unit 6, days 77 to 95) and science 4-ESS3-1 (science unit 2) are all taught before this unit; no bridge needed.

## Unit 7. The regions: peoples and hazards. "How do the people of each region live with each other and with their land?"

Days 137 to 160, weeks 28 to 32. Codes: MA.4.T4a.5, MA.4.T4b.2, MA.4.T4b.3, MA.4.T4c.2, MA.4.T4c.3, MA.4.T4d.3, MA.4.T4d.4, MA.4.T4e.3, MA.4.T4e.4; D2.Geo.7, 9, 12; D2.Civ.13; D4.6.

Peoples region by region, each day naming the standard's groups and one thing each built or gave (138 to 143: the West takes two days, the Gold Rush, Chinese Americans and the Exclusion Act on 142, Japanese Americans and wartime incarceration from their own accounts on 143), our own region (144) and movement of people, goods and ideas compared (145), then hazards region by region (146 to 148), the human-made case (149), who was displaced (150), the far reach of a disaster (151), responses (152), the traced policy (153) and the international comparison (154). The action comes last (155 to 157) because it is judged against the policy the class traced; the claim follows (158), and the end-of-unit assessment opens with a short review, the review day having gone to day 143.

- End-of-unit assessment: day 159, every owned code: one people and one contribution for each region, movement of people, goods and ideas, one disaster and its response for each of four regions, a catastrophe's effect near and far, one response as a policy and one other country's, and a claim; each student's proposal also scored with the unit rubric. Respond: day 160.
- Candidate (California grade 4, the Gold Rush): kept, day 142; Ellis Island and Angel Island only retrieved.
- Leans: science 4-ESS3-2 (science unit 5, ends near school day 117) is taught before.
