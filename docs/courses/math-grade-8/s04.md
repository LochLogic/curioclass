## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Does knowing one thing about a group help predict another?"
**Hook, day 56:** In eight minutes, compare ramp height and toy travel distance: (2,18), (4,29), (4,33), (6,42), in centimetres, invented for practice. What must stay together to predict distance? Ask the compelling question before retrieval.
**Questions:** What does one point record (day 59)? Which line stays near the observations (day 60)? Can a curved pattern still show association (day 61)? Which denominator answers our question (day 67)?
**Closing claim:** Measurements can support approximate predictions; categorical comparisons need proportions. Day 68 joins the Pattern panels, Fitted-line record, Ribbon model and Sign poll evidence below.
**Teaching:** Model pairing on day 57 and scales on day 58. Days 58 and 59 each include the five-minute growth-data explanation. Model informal fitting on day 60. Day 61 explicitly contrasts both directions, curves and clusters before assessment. Day 64 deepens these contrasts. Teach slope on day 63 and intercept on day 65; day 64 practises both. Teach both row and column denominators on day 66; step back on days 65 and 67.

<!-- skel -->
- **8.SP.A.1:** Construct and interpret scatter plots for bivariate measurement data to investigate patterns of association between two quantities. Describe patterns such as clustering, outliers, positive or negative association, linear association, and nonlinear association. (The "such as" list gives examples, not required parts.) Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **8.SP.A.2:** Know that straight lines are widely used to model relationships between two quantitative variables. For scatter plots that suggest a linear association, informally fit a straight line, and informally assess the model fit by judging the closeness of the data points to the line. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **8.SP.A.3:** Use the equation of a linear model to solve problems in the context of bivariate measurement data, interpreting the slope and intercept. For example, in a linear model for a biology experiment, interpret a slope of 1.5 cm/hr as meaning that an additional hour of sunlight each day is associated with an additional 1.5 cm in mature plant height. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **8.SP.A.4:** Understand that patterns of association can also be seen in bivariate categorical data by displaying frequencies and relative frequencies in a two-way table. Construct and interpret a two-way table summarizing data on two categorical variables collected from the same subjects. Use relative frequencies calculated for rows or columns to describe possible association between the two variables. For example, collect data from students in your class on whether or not they have a curfew on school nights and whether or not they have assigned chores at home. Is there evidence that those who have a curfew also tend to have chores? Parts, by verb: whole standard.
