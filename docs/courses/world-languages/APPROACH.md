## What a good world language lesson does

- **Names a task in the language the learner can do.** "Ask a partner what they do after school and tell the class one
  thing you learned." A word list or a verb table is not a lesson goal.
- **Gives language the learner can understand, almost all the time.** The teacher, the page and the tool use the
  target language nearly all of the time (ACTFL's target-language statement says 90 percent or more; ACTFL, 2010,
  read by search summary 2026-11-20), with pictures, gesture, a model and a frame making it clear. Instructions in
  English are a fail (bar part 6). Learner-facing instructions are in the target language with a picture, a gesture
  or a model. The adult's note (the "before you teach" block and the family note) is in English and Spanish and says
  what to say or sign. English for the learner is allowed only to name a word's meaning on first use, said so on the
  page. Pending(docs/agents/decisions/DESK-ANSWERS.md#q-prework-open-2)
- **Teaches the three modes in every unit.** Interpretive (understand a text), interpersonal (a real two-way exchange
  with a gap in information) and presentational (say or sign something to an audience). Every unit ends with one linked
  task across all three (bar part 6, item 6). A scripted dialogue does not count as interpersonal.
- **Teaches words and grammar to serve the task,** in chunks first, rules later (bar part 6, item 3).
- **Brings real language in.** Authentic texts and talk from several communities that speak the language (bar part 6,
  item 5), chosen for the level, with help, not rewritten into textbook language.
- **Withdraws help on a named day.** Frames, word banks and pictures fade; the unit says which day (C7).
- **Brings earlier work back.** Each unit reuses words and structures from the unit before.
- **Treats culture as practices, products and perspectives,** never a list of food and holidays.

## Kinds of day in world languages (the GLOSSARY kinds)

| Kind | What it is in this subject |
|---|---|
| Understanding | First teaching of anything new: a chunk, a structure, a handshape, a sound, a cultural practice. Heard or seen in a short text or scene, then named. |
| Fluency | Practise what earlier days taught until it comes easily: partner rounds, chant, song, sign sets, matching, a quick information-gap game. It teaches nothing new and writes no new piece. |
| Application | Use taught language on a new task: an interpersonal exchange with a gap, a presentation, a reply to a text. Any day that drafts, revises or edits a written or signed piece is Application. |
| Check | The end-of-unit assessment: an understand task, an exchange task and a presentation task at the level, scored against look-fors. |
| Respond | Reteach the structures and words the checks showed were not yet secure; rehearse again. |

## Daily evidence

The day's evidence is **a short sample of the language the day taught, in the mode the day used**: one or two spoken or
signed turns in a partner exchange, a written line on an exit ticket, or a one-item show-you-understood task, seen
against two or three look-fors (did the learner use the structure; was the message clear; did the partner get the
information). The teacher's paper sheet marks "seen / not yet" for each learner to plan the Respond day. A learner
self-check against our own can-do paraphrases is kept by the learner. Nothing is recorded as voice or video, and no
scores are stored (promise 2).

## What world languages add to the unit and section plan fields

Proposed, to be tried on the first unit plans after the pass opens:

- Header: days by kind; minutes; the level and its target sublevel; the language (Spanish or ASL).
- Unit field 2: the World-Readiness standards the unit teaches, in our own words; the theme and its real-world task;
  the structures, words and (ASL) parameters taught; what is carried (frame, word cards, sign set).
- Unit field 3: builds on (the same mode one unit earlier, strong or weak); leads to; comes back; one cross-subject link
  by row id (STANDARD section 6).
- Unit field 4: a short understand-and-reply task on the words and structures the unit builds on; the reteach is the
  earlier structure modelled again.
- Unit field 5: an understand task, a two-way task with a real information gap, a presentation task, with look-fors and
  two sample answers each (one meets, one does not), and the linked end task across the three modes.
- Unit field 6: the error register (see below).
- Unit field 7: the exchange and presentation tasks are judged by the teacher against look-fors, so nothing is marked by
  hands up. Groups come from the sheet's "not yet" cells by structure; at most four groups; the rest rehearse in pairs
  from the frame. Marking time stated in minutes, about one minute a learner for each judged task. The withdrawal day
  for a frame is the unit's second Application day at Level 1 and 2, its first at Level 3 and 4.
- Unit field 8: word cards, frames, the model text by title and record, or the sign chart; no product or program
  names; the room (a class, a small group, a home table).
- Unit field 9: the family note in English and Spanish (decided): what the class learned, one phrase to use at home, and
  one thing to try in any language the family chooses. A signing unit's note shows signs as drawings (see "ASL" below).
- Unit field 10: sources, what was read and how much, from memory marked, no claim of effect, a pointer to the course
  logic model (C16).
- Section field 1: before you teach: the structure explained for the teacher; the sound or parameter at issue; any
  sensitive ground.
- Section field 2: the task goal; what comes just before and after; readiness items used.
- Section field 3: the day's evidence as above, with look-fors per level.
- Section field 4: expected errors, a wrong turn beside a right one, the day shown, a five to ten minute response.
- Section field 5: the model, who holds it, the day the frame is withdrawn.
- Section field 6: days: goal, kind, opening (see "How a day opens" below), work by hand, tool by part, evidence.
- Section field 7: two or three planned questions, one exchange task and one presentation, with look-fors.
- Section field 8: practice that returns: one earlier structure used again for three minutes on new content.
- Section field 9: help for a learner who needs more (frame, picture, partner), extension on the same standard
  (longer text, less help), access notes (see "ASL" below), and a heritage speaker note; see "Supports" below.
- Section field 10 (two rooms): a parent and one child at the table (read the model together, say or sign it back, do the
  exchange together even if the parent does not know the language, using the page's model; or a mixed class);
  one projector (model on screen, frame on the board, partner talk). In ASL home-table days the adult and child share
  the printed chart and sign together; no exchange is claimed when no one in the room signs.
- Section field 11: what is left out, and what is still to make.

## How a day opens

A day opens with a short retrieval task in the target language on one earlier structure (five minutes at most), then
the day's model.

## Supports

Six to eight words a unit are taught in depth with a picture. Reading load: a text of at most the level's stated
length, with a picture for every line at Level 1. Check accommodations: a point-or-sign or oral answer in place of
writing, extra time, a partner to read the prompt. A learner with no home-language support in English or Spanish gets
the picture and model route, never a translation. Extension on the same standard: a longer text, less help, a second
person's view; never the next level's structure.

## What a world language day never does

It never uses a word list or conjugation drill as the whole lesson, has the teacher or tool talk most of the time,
withdraws a frame on no named day, or shows an English gloss as if it were the sign.

## Heritage speakers

A learner who speaks Spanish at home often understands and speaks far above Level 1 and reads and writes far below.
They are not drilled on what they already know. The path, inside each Spanish level (not a separate course; question 6 in
STANDARD.md): more reading and writing, the register (home talk and school talk) named as two registers, not a right
and a wrong, and a regional variety is a variety, never an error. ACTFL's heritage statement says heritage learners do
best when instruction builds on existing home language skills (ACTFL position, 2010, search summary). A learner is
never asked to translate for the class or a family member.

The default Spanish is Latin American; **vosotros is off by default and the setting stays** (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-04).
Peninsular forms appear as a noted variety in cultural units. Accent marks, sounds and spelling are taught directly.

## American Sign Language specifics, inside the graphics-only rule

**No video and no photos of people (Cory, 2026-10-27; do not re-pitch video).** So signs, handshape, location, movement,
palm orientation, non-manual markers, use of space and classifiers are shown as drawings and labelled parameter charts,
storyboard panels showing movement with arrows and numbered frames, and rendered figures (not photographs of a person).
Every drawing is checked by a Deaf signer for correct form, because a drawing can mislead about movement or face
(question 4). Paper path: a printable sign chart. Each sign set is taught with its parameters named.

- **Parameters taught directly** in every unit and practised (bar part 6, item 9).
- **No spoken English during signing practice.** Voice-off is the Deaf community norm for the class (Massachusetts ASL
  guidance recommends it, per search summary); the printed page uses English glosses only as labels, marked as glosses.
- **Interpersonal practice is between people.** A signed exchange needs two signers; a tool can show the model and a
  self-check of handshapes, not watch the learner (no camera; promise 2).
- **Fingerspelling and numbers** are taught as part of Level 1 with chart and drill by hand.
- **Deaf culture and people.** Deaf community, history and the Deaf perspective are part of the course, taught by what the
  community says about itself (the community's own voice first, see the sensitive-read brief). Hearing and Deaf learners
  are not assumed.
- **Access.** A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing is a signer first; a learner who cannot see well needs tactile
  alternatives (a tactile chart of the handshape set). A learner with limited hand use is offered an adapted way
  to produce the sign, never a lowered goal.

## What a tool may and may not do

A tool may show a model sentence in text and audio (Spanish), let a learner hear and replay a phrase (Spanish), give
picture-backed word and structure practice, show a sign as a labelled drawing or storyboard (ASL), and give a private
self-check. It may not record or store a learner's voice, signing or writing; score pronunciation or sign form; place a
learner at a proficiency level; provide the two-way exchange itself (that is people); translate for a family; or hold an
account. Spanish audio uses a stated variety and is checked by a fresh agent read and a person listening to a short sample
(DESK-ANSWERS, "Ten Spanish tools"). Screens never replace a partner.

## Error register in this subject

Errors are unit field 6. Typical kinds: a false friend (a word that looks the same and means differently), word order
carried over from English, a gender or number mismatch, a verb form missing, a sound the learner maps to English, in
ASL a handshape or movement swap that changes the sign, a face left out where grammar needs it. Each has why (often
transfer from the home language) and a remedy. Transfer is a reason, never a fault. Each is "from memory" until a source
is read.

## Sensitive ground and which reads it needs

- **Whose language and whose culture.** The Spanish-speaking world is many places and peoples; examples must not make one
  country or one group carry them all, and must not reduce a culture to food and festivals. The ASL community is a
  linguistic and cultural minority; the pages must not treat deafness as a lack or Deaf people as a lesson for others.
- **No learner is asked about immigration status, how the family came, a parent's work, or whether the family speaks the
  language at home.** Prompts about family and home are offered as one of several and may be invented.
- **A learner's accent, variety or home dialect** is not corrected as an error of meaning.
- **Names for people** use the term the community uses for itself and say that choices differ (Hispanic, Latino,
  Latine).
- **Colonial history and Indigenous languages** are named where they bear on the language, with the community's own
  words first.
- **Religious holidays** are described, never observed.
- **Contested Deaf history** (the oralism era, cochlear implants) is shown as the Deaf community tells it, with both
  views stated and no verdict; no taboo sign is taught, and name signs are explained, not assigned by the page.
- **A learner's hearing status** is never asked or assumed.
- **Reads** (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md): a `sensitivity` read for each unit's cultural texts (community:
  the speakers of the variety or the Deaf community); a `spanish` read for all Spanish text and the family notes; a
  `text` read for authentic texts. A person (a Deaf ASL teacher; a Spanish-speaking teacher) reads before anything
  ASL becomes public (question 4), as the brief itself can only stand in for outside readers. ASL drawings and Deaf
  cultural text: a Deaf signer reads before use; the agent `sensitivity` read checks wording only.

## Pacing and dose

Settled by no one yet (question 7 in STANDARD.md). Published figures, read 2026-11-20:

- ACTFL, *Early Language Learning*, position statement, dated 29 July 2012 (https://www.actfl.org/news/early-language-learning):
  benefits come from instruction that is continuous through the year, connected from grade to grade, **more than twice a
  week and at least 90 minutes a week**, at elementary and middle school levels.
- Other figures turned up by search (an ASCD article saying ACTFL recommends 30 to 40 minutes, three to five days a week
  in elementary, and daily 40 to 50 minutes in middle school; a 105 and 200 minutes a week figure in an unattributed
  document) were not opened and are **not used** (not checked).
- A hours-to-proficiency lead (a University of Oregon CASLS page, https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQHoursToReachIH.pdf)
  was found by search and not opened (not checked).

**Dose by level** (draft, Pending(docs/agents/decisions/DESK-ANSWERS.md#q-prework-open-2)). Default: 36 weeks a year, three sessions a week of 35 minutes, about 63 hours a
level (Levels 1 to 4); a K to 5 learner has the same course at the same dose. This clears ACTFL's floor for elementary
and middle school (more than twice a week, at least 90 minutes a week; ACTFL, 29 July 2012); no national figure was
found for high school, and none is claimed. The course page says the hours are not shown to reach the exit sublevels,
and the exit targets are ours until the hours page is read. A frame may change the dose with a reason, as health does;
the course page says what a school may combine or leave out.

## Where the evidence is thin

The research supports teaching in the target language, task-based work, interaction and feedback, and attention to
form inside meaningful tasks (from memory, not cited yet). No study has tried this course, and no national rubric scores
it. Curio Class says so on the course page. No claim of effect is made.
