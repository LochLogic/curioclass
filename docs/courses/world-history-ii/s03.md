## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why did a few states come to rule most of the world by 1914, and how did the people they ruled answer?"
**Hook:** Compare Europe's borders before and after unification with Sambourne's imperial giant over Africa. Ask whether national independence and ruling others could grow together.
**Supporting questions:** Co-write: How did unification change power (27)? What do maps hide (28)? Why expand (30)? Who gained and lost (33)? Who told the story (35)? How did people answer conquest (36)?
**Closing claim:** Day 37 answers the compelling question using dated maps, the base accounts, Witbooi, Kipling and Tagore. Explain interacting causes, compare regional responses and answer a counterclaim.
**Teaching:** Model sourcing and causation on 27, scale on 28, context on 33 and writers' selection on 35. Revisit questions after each source; withdraw the argument frame on 37. Teach nationalism, imperialism, capital, protectorate, resistance and provenance in context. Name slavery, forced labour, mutilation and genocide plainly. Use no graphic images, slurs or victim or perpetrator role play. Offer private source discussion without personal disclosure. Explain missionary aims without requiring religious assent; distinguish Christianity from colonial government.

**Unseen packet, text to print:**

- **P1, original paraphrase of E. D. Morel, The Black Man's Burden, chapter 1, 1920:** A British journalist and campaigner asks his readers to reconsider pride in imperial adventure. He turns their attention to suffering imposed on conquered peoples. His chosen central image is an African held in chains, appealing for help (checked against the Wikisource text of chapter 1, 2026-11-14: the book's own image is a manacled slave with hands stretched out in supplication). He argues that military equipment helped Europeans dominate others. This is a retrospective argument addressed mainly to European readers, not an African witness's account. It neither describes every African society nor specifically narrates the Nama genocide.
- **P2, original summary of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association's statement, 22 April 2025:** The association recalls the 1905 extermination order and the killing, imprisonment and dispossession of Nama people. It demands renewed negotiations over the genocide and a direct place for descendants in decisions. It challenges accounts that treat the community only as an object of history. This source represents the association's stated position, not every Nama person's views. Its claims about the order's present legal force are outside this task and are not reproduced as settled law.
- **P3, factual context, original secondary briefing:** European imperial expansion used military force, diplomacy and economic pressure. British authority in India changed to Crown government after the 1857 uprising. Wars imposed treaty concessions on Qing China; the Taiping conflict also had powerful domestic causes. Japan modernized and acquired Taiwan in 1895 and annexed Korea in 1910. The Congo Free State's forced extraction involved killings and mutilation; Belgium assumed control in 1908. Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896. German colonial forces committed genocide against the Ovaherero and Nama between 1904 and 1908. Descendants live in Namibia and elsewhere today. Do not generalize one community's position to all descendants.
- **P4, constructed Latin American case:** An independent republic exports wheat through a foreign-financed port. Its elected government must service overseas debts, and most export receipts depend on two foreign markets. A farmer benefits from improved transport but is vulnerable to prices set abroad. This invented case illustrates economic dependence; it is not a historical measurement or a claim about all republics.
- **P5, original secondary cultural records:** (1) Asian workshops produced furniture, porcelain and textiles for overseas customers, sometimes adapting designs. (2) American maize and cacao entered European and United States foodways through Atlantic exchange beginning before 1800. (3) European scholars used overseas sites and collections to build archaeology and anthropology; colonial authority often marginalized local expertise. (4) Museums and international exhibitions displayed objects to tell stories about nations and empire; acquisition histories varied. (5) Japanese prints influenced impressionist compositions; African sculpture influenced cubist approaches to form. Many European artists misunderstood the original functions of objects. These are summaries to analyze, not transcripts of makers' explanations.
