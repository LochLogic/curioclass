## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Did the revolutions of 1776 to 1830 keep the promises they made, and for whom?"
**Hook:** First compare France's 1789 promise of equality with de Gouges's demand that women share it. Ask who could claim the promise.

**Supporting questions:** Co-write and revise these: Who held power (day 3)? Why did France revolt (4)? Which promises survived (7)? What changed in each Atlantic revolution (9)? Did independence change ordinary lives (11)? How do experts define success (12)?

**Closing claim:** On day 13, students answer the compelling question using the three founding declarations, de Gouges, Bolívar and community accounts. They qualify their answer by group and date and answer a counterclaim.

**Teaching:** Model sourcing and a claim on day 1, causal reasoning on 4, and comparing interpretations on 7. Revisit questions after each source. Withdraw the argument frame on 12. Teach sovereignty, serfdom, abolition and independence in context. Name executions and slavery plainly; use no graphic images or victim or perpetrator role play. Students may discuss sources privately without disclosing family experiences.

- **P1:** The French National Assembly's Declaration of 1789, articles 7 to 9, addresses lawful punishment: arrest must follow law; punishments must have a legal basis; an accused person is presumed innocent. This declares a rule, not evidence that authorities always followed it.
- **P2:** Haiti's Constitution of 1805, government articles 30 to 32, assigns the emperor control over making laws, appointing officials and judges, public revenues and foreign relations. Read beside the already taught fact that Haiti had defeated slavery and colonial rule.
- **P3:** Original factual comparison card: Mexico became independent in 1821; Central American provinces formed a federation in 1823 which later broke apart; Brazil ended slavery in 1888. France abolished colonial slavery in 1848. Latin American economies often depended on agricultural and mineral exports; European industrialization was uneven. This card is a secondary summary, not a contemporary witness. Use Rankin and Weise only after the source corrections in field 10.
- **P4:** Summary of historian Lynn Hunt's 2008 lecture synopsis: the American and French declarations introduced a new guarantee of rights in the eighteenth century. Her question is why that change occurred then. This concerns the emergence of a rights concept; it does not establish universal enjoyment of those rights.
- **P5:** Summary of historian Samuel Moyn's 2010 interview, opening answer: earlier rights sources existed, but he resists making them the secure foundation of the later movement. International human rights became widespread as a language and cause much later. His claim concerns historical connections, not whether injustice mattered earlier.
