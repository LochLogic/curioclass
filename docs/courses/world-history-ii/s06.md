## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What did the world decide it owed to the victims of this war, and did it keep that promise?"

**Hook:** On 73, compare a dated occupation map with Atlantic Charter promises. Whose freedom could these protect?
**Supporting questions:** Co-write: How did battles and diplomacy shape victory (76)? What did persecution destroy, and how did people resist (80)? Why do accounts of the bombs differ (82)? What could postwar institutions enforce (86)?
**Closing claim:** On 86, use the Atlantic Charter, survivor testimony and Nuremberg Charter to judge accountability and protection. Answer a counterclaim; distinguish results through 1950 from unresolved later promises.
**Teaching:** Model map causation (73), testimony corroboration (80), interpretation (82) and civic argument (85); withdraw frames on 86. Partners research one battle on 74; survey pages support one child. Explain antisemitism, dehumanization, genocide, hibakusha, sovereignty and jurisdiction in context. No reenactments, shock images, slurs or family disclosure. Name sexual slavery without graphic detail. Distinguish persecution policies across groups, including differing treatment of lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender people. Denial is false; present peoples inherit no collective guilt.


- **D2.His.7.9-12:** Explain how present perspectives influence accounts of earlier events. Parts, by verb: explain that influence, not merely identify disagreement.
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12:** Analyze how personal interests and perspectives affect applying civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights and human rights, and what influence is appropriate. Parts, by verb: analyze effects and appropriate roles across all four categories. Wording was checked in the [official framework](https://www.socialstudies.org/sites/default/files/c3/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.pdf), pages 33 and 47.

**Limits:** Deepen one battle. Days 84 to 85 reach 1950. Later Cold War crises, independence cases and genocide law belong to units 7, 8 and 10. Day 81 introduces fission with a paragraph and diagram, without calculations. Informed action remains in unit 11.
**Carried:** Retain unit 5's nine models: timeline, cause-effect chain, map-scale, coordinates and region return on 73; sourcing, perspective and corroboration on 77 to 82. Supply-demand rests. Claim-evidence-reasoning returns on 85; withdraw its frame on 86, retaining source labels.

**Answer-key cautions:** Wannsee coordinated an existing murder policy; Hitler was not at the meeting. Distinguish concentration camps from killing centers and differing persecutory policies. Christian antisemitism was a historical root, not grounds for blaming all Christians or treating genocide as inevitable. Casualty card for days 81 and 83 and item 4b: [Hiroshima city, Number of deaths](https://www.city.hiroshima.lg.jp/english/peace/1029920/1009857.html) estimates about 140,000 deaths by December 1945, not immediate deaths; the precise total is unknown. Both count deaths following the bombings, not just deaths at impact. [National Museum of the Pacific War, The Enduring Impact of World War II, March 5, 2021](https://www.pacificwarmuseum.org/learn/articles/the-enduring-impact-of-world-war-ii), opening section, gives the worldwide 70 to 85 million military and civilian estimate for the war. It supplies no underlying count series or consistent national cutoffs: label it a broad retrospective estimate, not a census or a confidence interval. Displaced survivors are not added to the death total. These passages were read; the Nagasaki record is Japanese, summarized here in English. Surrender followed both bombings and Soviet entry. Do not require one verdict on necessity, only one motive or an inevitable Cold War.
