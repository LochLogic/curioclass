## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What did the world decide it owed to the victims of this war, and did it keep that promise?"
**Hook:** Day 73 opens with the Atlantic Charter's promises beside a dated map of occupied Europe and Asia. Ask whose freedom those promises could protect.
**Supporting questions:** Co-write: How did battles and diplomacy shape victory (76)? What did persecution destroy, and how did people resist (80)? Why do accounts of the bombs differ (82)? What could postwar institutions enforce (86)?
**Closing claim:** On 86, argue from the Atlantic Charter, survivor testimony and the Nuremberg Charter whether accountability and protection matched promises. Answer a counterclaim; distinguish evidence through 1950 from promises whose later record remains open.
**Teaching:** Model map causation on 73, testimony corroboration on 80 and present-day interpretation on 82. Partners research one battle on 74; complete survey pages support one child. Teach antisemitism, dehumanization, genocide, hibakusha, sovereignty and jurisdiction in context. Teach the four civic and rights categories and model argument on 85; withdraw sentence frames on 86. No reenactments, shock images, slurs or family disclosure. Name sexual slavery without graphic detail. Distinguish persecution policies across groups, including differing treatment of lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender people. Denial is false; present peoples inherit no collective guilt.

- **P:** [Obama, Hiroshima remarks, May 27, 2016](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/2016/05/27/remarks-President-obama-and-prime-minister-abe-japan-hiroshima-peace/), public-domain government speech, read. Print two fragments in separate boxes: "Technological progress without an equivalent progress in human institutions can doom us." Later, he calls for nuclear-armed states to "pursue a world without them." Explain the second fragment's reference to nuclear weapons. Add this labelled original paraphrase: He commemorates the bombing, describes the later American-Japanese alliance and argues that nuclear-armed states still need defense while pursuing disarmament. This is a president's commemorative argument in 2016, not a 1945 decision record or a view shared by all Americans. Students analyze how the contemporary concerns shape his emphasis; they need not agree.
- **Q:** Print this original factual jurisdiction card based on the Nuremberg history linked in field 10: "Four Allied powers created a tribunal for leading German defendants. Each supplied judges and prosecutors. The Charter let defendants present evidence and question prosecution witnesses. Its remit did not put Allied leaders on trial." Beside it, identify chief prosecutor Robert Jackson and his November 21, 1945 opening statement: he wanted the trial to "commend itself to posterity" through detachment and integrity. This fragment comes from the Nuremberg article; distinguish his stated concern about later judgment from proof of a private motive. Add an original comparison card, taught through different examples on 85: civic virtues concern conduct such as impartiality; democratic principles include accountable power; constitutional rights derive from a constitution; human rights concern every person's dignity. The original cards are not historical quotations. They supply categories, not the student's evaluation.

**Answer-key cautions:** Wannsee coordinated an existing murder policy; Hitler was not at the meeting. Distinguish concentration camps from killing centers and differing persecutory policies. Christian antisemitism was a historical root, not grounds for blaming all Christians or treating genocide as inevitable. Deaths by the end of 1945 were about 140,000 in Hiroshima and about 74,000 in Nagasaki (city estimates; ranges vary by source); date and qualify estimates. Surrender followed both bombings and Soviet entry. Do not require one verdict on necessity, only one motive or an inevitable Cold War.
