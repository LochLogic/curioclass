## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Did industrialization make life better for the people who did the work?"

**Hook:** Compare the Rochester petition's account of children's hours with the base text's account of increased production. Ask whether more cloth means better lives.
**Supporting questions:** Co-write and revise these: Why Britain (day 17)? How did industry spread (18)? Who gained (20)? How did decisions reshape places (21)? Which ideas and reforms answered hardship (23)?
**Closing claim:** On day 24, answer the compelling question using population and wage evidence, the Rochester petition, reform records and Taylor Mill. Qualify by group and date; answer a counterclaim.
**Teaching:** Model sourcing and claim-evidence-reasoning on day 16, causal weighting on 17, and data limits on 19. Revisit questions after each source; withdraw the writing frame on 24. Teach enclosure, capital, real wages, class, socialism and suffrage in context. Explain supporters' and critics' reasoning before judgment. Feminism has earlier roots; industry changed its arguments. Name slavery, child labour, injury and pollution plainly. Use no graphic images, coercive simulations or personal disclosures.


- **D2.Geo.8.9-12:** Evaluate how economic activity and political decisions change where people live and work, within and between cities, suburbs and rural areas. Parts: evaluate economic effects; evaluate political effects; address patterns within and among all three regions. The local file lists only the code; the [published indicator](https://www.socialstudies.org/sites/default/files/c3/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.pdf), page 43, supplies the verb.

**Limits:** Focus on Britain, then compare continental Europe, the United States and Japan. Full market theory belongs to Economics; imperial conquest to unit 3; later communist governments to units 5 and 7. Industrial power does not establish cultural superiority.
**Carried:** Keep unit 1's timeline, region, map-scale, perspective, cause-effect-chain and claim-evidence-reasoning. Return to chronology and maps on 18 and perspective on 22. Weight the cause chain on 17. Tie source-sourcing to author, date, audience, purpose and context on 16; remove its checklist on 24. **Unseen packet, printed in full:**

- **P1, invented data for an English industrial-town scenario:** These figures are constructed for reasoning, not historical measurements. Between 1825 and 1855 the town's population changes from 24,000 to 48,000. The weekly wage index for labourers changes from 100 to 112; the household price index from 100 to 125; the managers' wage index from 100 to 160. The number of managers changes from 80 to 240. Every index starts at 100 in 1825. Ownership row, also invented: in 1855 the town's richest tenth of households own about eight in ten pounds of its property (land, buildings and machinery, minus debts), and the poorest half own almost none. Different wage growth shows unequal income gains; the ownership row shows unequal wealth. Neither row describes England's actual distribution, and neither shows why people moved.
- **P2, historical testimony:** Inspector's exact words: "Believing the case scarcely possible, I asked every boy the same questions, and from each received the same answers." Gloss: scarcely possible means hard to believe. This is one continuous extract; the surrounding text and employer name are omitted. Context, paraphrased: boys reported working from six on Friday morning until four on Saturday afternoon, except meals and an extra midnight hour. The inspector checked the time book and threatened punishment. His account mediates the boys' words. British Parliamentary Papers (1836), p. 353; [transcript, PDF p. 9](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/education/factory-actdoc.pdf).
- **P3, original secondary briefing from Rankin and Weise, chapter 7:** More productive farming helped support nonfarm employment, while enclosure deprived some rural people of customary access. Industrial employment attracted migrants. Merchants, managers and professionals formed a growing middle class; gains were unevenly distributed. Many poor workers faced long hours, crowded housing and polluted surroundings. Conditions changed over the century and differed between places. This summary makes general claims, not a count of affected households. Historical ownership evidence, from the [1878 summary of the 1873 land return](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica,_Ninth_Edition,_v._8.djvu/235): in England and Wales, excluding London, 5,408 owner entries of 1,000 acres or more held 18,695,528 of the 33,013,515 returned acres. Dividing these acreages gives about 56.6%. Owners may recur across counties; the return omits some land. This measures land area, not land value or total net wealth, and cannot show that industry caused concentration.
- **P4, invented spatial record for the same scenario:** Before: an urban market stands beside a river; a suburban village has scattered homes; nearby rural families farm strips and use common pasture. After parliamentary enclosure and authorization of a railway, rural land becomes fenced farms, factories gather at the urban station, and homes cluster around new suburban stations. Rural coal and food travel to the city; suburban residents commute there; urban smoke and waste reach surrounding land and water. Factory laws restrict children's hours; a council funds sewers in the urban centre but leaves suburban streets unserved. This is a constructed case, not a map of a real town.
