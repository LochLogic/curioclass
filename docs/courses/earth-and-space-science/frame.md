# Course frame: Earth and Space Science

Course `earth-and-space-science` (catalogue id `earth-space`), grade 12 elective (typical; open to grades 10 to 12 for a student who has finished, or is taking alongside, Biology, Chemistry and Physics: data/courses.csv, Cory, 2026-11-17), draft frame. The scope and sequence sets the days later (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3); this frame sets the units, their order and their day counts.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 9 units, each driven by one anchor phenomenon or question a class can answer from the unit's own evidence (maps, rocks, gauge and buoy records, ice cores, spacecraft images, starlight).
- **Days.** 160 course days: 149 in units and 11 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year: one lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week, 32 weeks. Each investigation's active sessions fit 50-minute lessons; a school with double periods may join two for field days. The course owns 26 outcomes in 19 rows, about 6 days an outcome. Days follow load: unit 1 carries the most outcomes and builds the year's field and lab routines (18 days for 4 outcomes); unit 3 joins three lines of evidence and a volcano record (18 for 4); unit 9 runs the observing log to its end (18 for 3); units 4 and 5 carry stream and lab work and a running weather log (17 each); unit 7 steps a model (17 for 2); units 2 and 6 carry the fewest outcomes (15 each); unit 8 reads records and fits one model (14 for 2). Readiness response is counted inside the receiving units: one-lesson reteach in units 3, 4, 5, 7 and 9 when a check is missed. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment (and, in units over 25 days, its mid-unit assessments; no unit here runs over 25). The 50 minutes hold everything: set-up, clean-up and collecting work come out of the lesson, so each lab or field day names its clean-up time in the last Units column (about 5 minutes for a bench lab, about 10 for field or stream work, inside the active session); a readiness check takes 10 minutes; a fold-in on the Shorter path never takes a feedback or revision day.
- **Day kinds.** The first explicit teaching of anything (a mineral test, contour reading, the decay relation, a lapse rate, the energy balance, a term set) is an Understanding day. A Fluency day practices only what an earlier day taught: in this course, short sets of map, section, lag-time, decay, sounding and light-curve readings on new data, and the course has few of them (about one a unit, none scored). Investigating, field and lab work, drafting, revising and using taught ideas on a new case are Application days. Respond and Check days are as docs/GLOSSARY.md sets them. No score comes from the day a topic is first taught.
- **Grade 12 calendar.** The last weeks hold teaching: unit 9 runs weeks 28 to 31 (its sky log since week 25) and the year-end check, course-level check 3, sits in week 32 (3 check days, 2 respond days). If seniors leave early, the year-end check moves forward and days fold in this order, up to 5: the second respond day after check 3; one Fluency day of unit 9; one Fluency day of unit 8; the third check day (the check keeps its dossiers on units 6 to 8 and drops its oldest item); one Fluency day of unit 7. No fold takes a feedback or revision day, an end-of-unit sitting or the sky log's summary; a school ending seniors more than five days early also ends the sky log at five weeks.
- **Standard.** Curio Class's own outcomes CUR-ESS.1 to CUR-ESS.26 (data/standards/curio-science-electives.txt), in our own words and in teaching order. Every high school Next Generation Science Standards performance expectation is owned by Biology, Chemistry or Physics (Desk card q-hs-science-core), so this course owns no NGSS code: it goes deeper on the Earth and space expectations those courses own, and each unit's What students learn names the expectation it deepens with its core owner. Nothing is taught twice: each unit says what the core course taught and what is new here. The bar is docs/RUBRIC-SCIENCE.md (high school Gateway 2). In the Units table the **Practices and crosscutting concepts** column holds the practices and crosscutting concepts the unit develops. **Check set adds** keeps the frame gate's Candidate prefix for practice, crosscutting concept and nature of science elements (NGSS appendices F, G and H) settled in the scope and sequence. "NGSS" never appears in student, teacher or family copy. Values rule (b): facts and backed sources decide claims; climate is taught as the evidence shows, with the uncertainty the records and models themselves state, as the Chemistry frame treats it (BASELINE rule 5). Owned codes are the course's own outcomes alone. Each "Deepens" line names a core course's code as a revisit, taught as review and named so in the text; the core course that owns it checks it, and this course checks only its own outcomes.
- **Base text.** A combination of open books, all CC BY 4.0 or public domain: Physical Geology, 2nd Edition (Steven Earle, BCcampus, 2019, CC BY 4.0) for units 1 to 4 and part of 7; Astronomy, 1st edition (Andrew Fraknoi, David Morrison, Sidney C. Wolff et al., OpenStax, retired edition, archive revision 2022-01-28, version faae451, CC BY 4.0) for units 8 and 9; JetStream, An Online School for Weather (NOAA National Weather Service, public domain) for units 5 to 7; and, optionally, Introduction to Oceanography (Paul Webb, Roger Williams University, CC BY 4.0) for unit 6. The Source map at the end places every chapter and section. Credit line on adapted days, in the form docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md sets: "Adapted from Physical Geology, 2nd Edition (Steven Earle, BCcampus, 2019), chapter <n>, section <n>, CC BY 4.0, https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/. Changed by Curio Class." (the same shape for Astronomy 1st edition, OpenStax, and for Introduction to Oceanography; JetStream days say "Adapted from JetStream, NOAA National Weather Service, public domain. Changed by Curio Class."). Rules each source carries: Physical Geology's text is CC BY but a long list of its figures is not (all rights reserved, non-commercial, share-alike and one no-derivatives figure, listed on the book's own exceptions page), so no figure from that list is reused; Astronomy is credited as the 1st edition only (the 2nd edition is CC BY-NC-SA and is reference only); JetStream material is never presented as official or as endorsed, its third-party maps and images keep their owners' licences, and the agency's name and logo appear only in the credit line. Physical Geology 2e (with its exceptions page), Astronomy 1st edition and the JetStream lessons are in the source store (S:\CurioClass-sources\physical-geology-2e\, openstax-astronomy-1e\, noaa-jetstream\, each with NOTES.md and MANIFEST.csv; licences read at source on 2026-11-17) and recorded in docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md; Introduction to Oceanography (optional) was not loaded. This frame's Source map was built from the books' published tables of contents; the scope writer checks each placement against the loaded copies. **How phenomena are chosen:** something a class can see, measure or read as data; puzzling at grade 12 and explainable with the unit's ideas; not an anchor, design problem or check case any other science course uses, kindergarten to Physics (Physics's Atlantic sea floor and meteorites, Chemistry's Arctic sea ice and three river deltas, grade 6's 2011 Japan earthquake among them), nor Environmental Science's; a local swap is allowed if it needs the same ideas. **Sources beyond the base:** public-domain data from federal science agencies (geological, seismic, stream gauge, weather, ocean buoy, ice core and spacecraft records), published numbers redrawn as our own tables and graphs, and data the class collects. No paid text, no library trip, no student account.
- **Labs and materials.** Every investigation has three paths: hands-on with classroom-cheap materials (local rocks and a small mineral set, a steel nail, a glass plate with taped edges, streak plates, vinegar, a magnifier, a tape measure, a compass and a protractor-and-string clinometer, a gutter or tray of sand for a stream table, clear cups and sand for porosity, two thermometers for wet and dry bulb, a clear plastic bottle, salt and food colouring, a clamp lamp with an incandescent bulb of 60 watts or less, a printed star chart); a no-kit path from supplied data, photographs or a recorded run; and, where no class could do it (an earthquake, an ice core, a planet), a Curio Class simulation tool (listed per unit, built later through the tool pipeline, DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-13). Every simulation runs with no account and has a paper path (printed maps, graph paper, a step table worked with a calculator). Each no-kit or simulation path names the student decisions, measurements and revisions it keeps; reading supplied results alone is partial coverage where an outcome asks students to map, measure or observe (CUR-ESS.2, CUR-ESS.12, CUR-ESS.24, rubric row S6), and the unit plan names the practical step still owed. Course rules: eye protection whenever glass is scratched or vinegar is used; no rock hammers and no hydrochloric acid; mineral and rock sets hold no asbestiform, lead, mercury, arsenic or radioactive specimens, and hands are washed after handling rocks and minerals; field work on school grounds or a site the school has approved, in pairs inside a group with an adult, never alone; road cuts and cliffs are used from photographs only, never visited; stream work only in shallow, slow water below the knee, from the bank where possible, never after rain, with gloves, cuts covered and hands washed after; outdoor work stops at the first thunder; the cloud bottle is squeezed by hand, with no alcohol, pump, matches or smoke; lamps are incandescent clamp lamps of 60 watts or less, never halogen; the Sun is never looked at directly, with or without any lens; night sky observing is done at home with a family member or at a school evening event, never alone. Provisional class-of-30 and one-child costs are stated in the scope and sequence, with shared equipment counted once; each hands-on day carries a Safety and set-up section (rubric row S5).
- **Storyline and models.** Each unit opens on its anchor, builds a question board, and ends on a transfer task with a phenomenon the class has not seen. Every unit names its initial class model and the points where evidence revises it. The course runs from the ground under the school (unit 1) out through deep time, the moving plates, water, air and ocean, past climate, to other planets and other stars, so each unit uses the tools the one before built (the map in units 1 to 5 and 8, the dated record in units 2, 3 and 7, the energy balance in units 5, 7 and 8).
- **Nature of science, taught on purpose.** Unit 2 (deep time argued from one rock contact before any number existed), unit 3 (independent lines of evidence, geological, tree-ring, written and oral, agreeing on one date) and unit 7 (a model tested against the record, its limits stated).
- **Engineering.** The course owns no engineering outcome; the design practices are owned by Biology (units 9 and 10) and Chemistry (unit 7). Unit 3 uses them again when students turn a hazard estimate into advice for a coastal town, with criteria, constraints and trade-offs.
- **Math readiness.** Every math lean falls on an earlier year (grade 7 and 8 math, Algebra I, Geometry, Algebra II), so each is retrieval by default (DESK 2026-11-15). Logarithms (magnitude, decay, brightness) and rational exponents (the fourth root in an energy balance, Kepler's third law with an exponent of two thirds) come from Algebra II units 3 and 4 in grade 11; the Physics form of Kepler's third law (squares, cubes and roots) is replaced here by the exponent form.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 band; adapted base text and our own readings, with words taught before they are needed.
- **Literacy standards.** The science and technical reading and writing standards for grades 11 and 12 (RST.11-12, WHST.11-12; data/standards/ccss-literacy-6-12.txt) are practiced, not owned (Cory, Desk 2026-11-14: "Load them", no extra days). Each unit's "What students learn" cell ends with the codes its work practices. No unit teaches and checks a lettered part on purpose, so none is claimed in full, none is an owned standard and none adds days.
- **Paper path.** Every simulation, map tool and data set has a paper path: printed topographic and geologic maps, compass and ruler work, graph paper profiles and plots, and step tables worked with a calculator, so no unit needs a screen.
- **Devices.** The course assumes one shared teacher display and student devices in pairs where a school has them; no student needs a device of their own. No owned outcome requires technology: CUR-ESS.21's model is stepped "in a spreadsheet or by hand", and a step table with a calculator meets it in full; every simulation tool named in a unit has its paper path beside it, so a paper-path student makes every measurement, plot and decision in the same order. A scientific calculator (logarithms and roots) per pair is assumed from unit 2.
- **Safety read.** Every unit gets the safety agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md) before the course is called finished, above all unit 1 (glass plates, the vinegar test, mineral specimens, field mapping), unit 4 (stream work and the stream table), unit 5 (the cloud bottle, hot water), unit 8 (a hot lamp) and unit 9 (night observing, never looking at the Sun). The agent reads against these guides by title, each free to read: the School Chemistry Laboratory Safety Guide (Consumer Product Safety Commission and National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, 2006, https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2007-107/); the position statement Safety and School Science Instruction (National Science Teaching Association, https://www.nsta.org/nstas-official-positions/safety-and-school-science-instruction), which covers field work; the National Weather Service's lightning safety pages (https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning); the eye safety page for viewing the Sun (National Aeronautics and Space Administration, https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/safety/); and the safety data sheet of every material named. A student sign-off or conduct form is never cited as guidance. The last column of the Units table gives each hands-on or field unit's hazards and controls in a "Safety (days.csv words)" sentence that days.csv copies; the only teacher demonstration (hot water for the cloud bottle, unit 5) is one these guides pass. The course's checks get the answer-key agent's read. No outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14).
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md). Unit 1 (a mountain's Native name): the agent first studies a published place-name source for Monadnock and the W8banaki Nation's (Odanak and Wôlinak) own published material; the unit says the name comes from an Algonquian language, usually given as Abenaki, that its meaning is uncertain, and takes no side between groups who speak for the Abenaki today. Unit 2 (deep time and families' beliefs about Earth's age): the agent first studies how the Physics frame's units 9 and 10 handle deep time, and published guidance for science teachers on teaching Earth's age respectfully. Unit 3 (a Native nations' history of a great earthquake, and quake and tsunami worry): it first studies the Huu-ay-aht First Nations', Makah Tribe's and Quileute Tribe's own published accounts of the January 1700 earthquake and tsunami, a published collection of Cascadia coastal oral traditions made with the nations, the Quinault Indian Nation's and the Chehalis Tribe's own published material on the homeland the Copalis River runs through (the unit names those nations in one line, checked before writing), and published guidance for schools on talking about disasters; Mount St. Helens killed 57 people in 1980, and the same guidance covers it. Unit 4 (people died in both Ellicott City floods): the city's and county's own published flood reports, residents' published accounts, and guidance for schools on loss. Unit 5 (people died in the 2014 storm, and hurricanes kill and displace people): the same disaster guidance, and published accounts from communities that lived through a recent hurricane. Unit 6 (livelihoods lost in Peru's anchovy collapse): the agent first studies the published records of Peru's own marine research institute and published accounts from Peruvian fishing communities, and the unit describes the fishers through those sources, never only as victims of the sea. Unit 7 (climate worry): a psychological association's climate and mental health guidance and a climate journalism style guide, as the Chemistry frame's units 9 to 11 do. Unit 8: no community content; claims about water or life on Mars are kept to what the data show. Unit 9: no nation's star knowledge is used, so no nation's source is needed. Units 1 to 3 and 9 touch no other community than those named.

### Beside this course: Environmental Science

Earth and Space Science and Environmental Science are both grade 12 electives. Neither assumes the other, and a student may take them in either order or together, so any link between them is by course order, in prose, never in assumes or hands_on. Five ideas sit in both. For each, one course owns it; the other teaches only the part it uses, in one part-lesson in its own terms, so a student taking only that course is covered, and a student taking both meets the second as retrieval. A sixth touch (this course's unit 7, past climate and a model test, against Environmental Science unit 4, an energy transition) shares only Chemistry's climate rows, one looking back and one at mitigation.

## Units

The order runs outward from the ground under the school. Unit 1 builds the map and the rock reading every later Earth unit uses; unit 2 turns rocks into a dated history; unit 3 uses both to read a hazard written in a drowned forest. Water comes next (unit 4), then the air it rises into (unit 5), then the ocean that couples them (unit 6), because each needs the energy and circulation ideas of the one before. Unit 7 gathers the dated records and the energy budget into past climate and a tested model. Units 8 and 9 carry the same energy balance and the same reading of landforms and light to other planets and other stars, ending the year on worlds no one has seen.
