**Model, Curio Class, 2026, ours.** What changed when food concerns became federal law? Comparing a scientific concern with a legal provision helps distinguish a reason for reform from the protection the law actually supplied. Wiley's bulletin introduces an investigation of preservatives' effects on digestion and health. It states a purpose, not a guarantee that every later conclusion is correct. The Act's section 7 defines several forms of food adulteration, including concealed damage. Section 2 specifies covered commerce. Therefore an article should explain both the concern and the reach of the provision, rather than claiming that every unsafe practice disappeared. The federal enforcement history also describes labelling as the law's basis, rather than approval before sale. That distinction limits an explanation of what reform accomplished. **Cold article, day 86: Opening an archive. Curio Class, 2026, ours, invented for practice.** This whole informational case has no computed measure. Its difficulty lies in complementary responses, a revised approach and archival vocabulary. The archive and events are invented, not historical claims about the 1906 reform. Withhold this article, its target words and references until 86; give directions only.

1. A local archive wanted more people to study its collection about the 1906 food and drug reform. Staff scanned boxes of records and placed the images online, expecting distance to become the only remaining barrier. Yet readers could not tell which pages belonged together or who had written an unsigned note. One image had legible headings but illegible lines where the ink had faded. Uploading an image had neither explained the record nor made all its writing readable. A larger collection of scans could therefore leave readers with more material but no clearer path through it.

2. Staff next linked related pages and described their provenance: who created the records and which people or offices later held them. An unsigned note stayed labelled as unsigned rather than receiving a guessed author. During a prearranged visit, a specialist examined fragile papers and advised on conservation, including protection from further light damage. That care could keep the originals available for future study, although it could not bring back ink already lost. Descriptions helped readers understand what they could see; physical care protected what later readers might need. Neither task could substitute for the other.

3. The revised catalogue also let readers suggest corrections with evidence. Each uncertain description was provisional: staff would revise it when a reader supplied a matching dated record. A correction log retained the earlier description and explained each change, so readers could inspect the reasoning rather than trust a silent replacement. Access had become continuing work shared between staff and readers, supported by care for the originals. Even this approach could not recover a letter that nobody had kept. A useful archive made surviving evidence easier to study while keeping its uncertainties and absences visible.
