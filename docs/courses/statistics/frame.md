# Course frame: Statistics

Course `statistics` (catalogue id `statistics`), grade 12 (data/courses.csv lists grades 11 to 12, typical 12), draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame sets the course allocations.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 10 units: nine in the order of the rows (describe data, produce data, probability, inference), and a tenth, the investigation, whose days are spread through the year.
- **Days.** 160 course days: 151 in units and 9 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year of 32 weeks, one lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week: a 5-minute opening of spaced, mixed practice on earlier work, then the lesson. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment and one respond day after it; no unit is over 25 days, so none holds a mid-unit assessment. Days follow the rows' load notes (22 rows, 151 days), not equal shares: unit 7 (24 days) owns four inference outcomes; unit 6 (11 days) owns one outcome but is the hinge of the course, with two sampling distributions and the central limit theorem simulated on paper as well as by technology; unit 4 (9 days, one outcome and one (+) code) has about 4 new days after its review. About 12 of the 151 unit days are review, all in units 1 to 4, where the rows name it (about 5.5 in unit 1: Algebra I displays and summaries, Algebra II normal areas; 2 in unit 2; 1 in unit 3; 3 in unit 4, Geometry probability), each raised to first-year college vocabulary; no day only reteaches an earlier row. Inside the period: the 50 minutes hold the opening, handing out and collecting papers and calculators, and on a hands-on day (dice, cards, coins, the scale) about 5 minutes at the end to count kits back in; a readiness check takes ten minutes; no plan puts more than 50 minutes in a day, and a Shorter path fold-in never takes a feedback or revision day.
- **Day kinds.** Understanding is the first explicit teaching of anything: a new display, summary, rule, distribution, design or inference procedure, including a simulation that first shows an idea (a sampling distribution built from many samples, a p-value by re-randomising). Fluency practises only what an earlier day taught, and is small in this course: computing summaries and z-scores with the table, the probability rules on trees and tables, and the four-move inference write-up (question and parameter, conditions, calculation, conclusion in context) on a procedure already taught; a Fluency day never introduces a new procedure and is never the source of a unit score. Application is using taught ideas on a new task: analysing a new data set, a lab, the investigation's planning, gathering, analysis, drafting and revision days. Check and Respond days are named as such.
- **End-of-unit evidence.** Units 1 to 9 each end with one sitting of unseen items on the unit's second-last day, before its respond day; an end day holds that one performance only, and every data set on it is new to the student. Unit 5, which owns 11 codes, sits twice: a random-variable sitting (S-MD.A codes, CUR-ST.9, CUR-ST.10) at the close of stat-binomial-geometric's days and a decisions sitting (S-MD.B codes) at the unit's end, both inside its 19 days. Unit 10's evidence is a product: the written report, drafted, given written feedback and revised before its score, and the short presentation (The investigation). No score comes from a day a topic is first taught.
- **Standard.** Two lists, both owned in full by the course's rows. The 10 Common Core (+) codes the core courses deferred (data/standards/ccss-math-standards.txt): S-CP.B.9 (unit 4) and all nine S-MD codes (unit 5). Curio Class's own outcome list for what the course teaches beyond them (data/standards/curio-math-electives.txt, CUR-ST.1 to CUR-ST.21, in teaching order, our own words). Every code a row owns sits in one unit below, and S-MD.B.5's lettered parts (S-MD.B.5a, S-MD.B.5b) are named in unit 5. Math has no check set: the C3 column names the unit's practices, and "Check set adds" names candidate checks and extensions. The course is written at first-year college level with Algebra II as its only prerequisite, and assumes neither Precalculus nor Calculus. How the high school rubric applies is the shared L49 answer: an owned-outcome account in place of focus 1a to 1c, plus the minutes line (Focus bullet).
- **Focus, coherence and rigor (how much of docs/RUBRIC-MATH-HS.md applies).** The rubric's focus criteria (1a to 1c) score the standards without a (+) mark, and this course owns none, so they are replaced by the method docs/RUBRIC-MATH-HS.md sets once for Precalculus, Statistics and Calculus (The grade 12 courses; Cory, 2026-11-17, L49), applied here in the same words as the other two. (1) The owned-outcome account: every owned code is taught in full in the unit named below and assessed on that unit's end-of-unit assessment; the course-level checks sample codes again (Course-level checks) and never claim all of them. (2) The minutes line: the minutes spent on earlier courses' rows are counted and capped at 15 percent of unit minutes (151 unit days of 50 minutes, 7,550 minutes, so about 1,130). What counts: the review days and part-days in the rows' load notes, retrieval tasks outside them, readiness checks at ten minutes each, and the daily-opening minutes spent on earlier courses' rows; each minute counts once; check days, respond days and time on this course's own earlier units do not count. A unit reports no share: each unit plan lists its minutes on earlier courses' rows as a count, and the scope and sequence sums them and states the course share against the cap. (3) The modeling cycle stays whole: no step is cut to save time (in the Common Core all of statistics and probability is modeling, and gap 2's modeling record shows the full cycle, from question to report, at least in unit 10 and in each unit's data work). Planned here: about 12 review days in the load notes (about 600 minutes), two readiness sittings (20 minutes), and at most 75 of the 151 openings (375 minutes) on earlier courses' rows: at most about 995 minutes, about 13 percent. 1c reads as work at the course's stated level, first-year college, with no day that only reteaches an earlier course's row. Coherence (1d, 1e), assessment (1f, 1g), rigor (2a to 2d, with direct fluency), the eight practices (2e to 2l), supports (3a to 3u) and the multilingual learner criteria apply in full, as do gaps 2, 4 to 6, 8 and 9. Rigor in this course: understanding (what a sampling distribution is, what a confidence level means, what a p-value does and does not say), procedure (carrying out each inference method), and application (every unit works on a real, public data set, named below). Every read of this course also takes the data-teaching lens, docs/agents/lenses/statistics.md (Cory, 2026-11-17, Desk card q-electives-frames-1): the GAISE recommendations read in our own words as a check on the investigative cycle, variability, real data, concepts before recipes, active learning, technology and assessment; no text copied, never cited as an endorsement.

- **Real data, picks named, sources given.** Every unit works on real, public data, each set with its free source and, where it changes, an "as of" date (the year read); a value made up for a task is labelled "invented for practice" and never passed off as a measurement. Every inference procedure (units 7 to 9, and unit 10) runs on data that really is a random sample or a randomised experiment, so its conditions can be met honestly: published survey tables and full-population tables are read for their own published margins, or serve as the known value an interval is checked against, never fed into a simple-random-sample formula. Picks are chosen not to repeat Algebra II unit 7's contexts (its dated polls, the supplied anonymous health-study outcomes, the neutral counting experiment) or the function contexts of Algebra II (daylight hours, tides, earthquake and acidity scales, the planets). Unit 1: the US Census Bureau's yearly community survey tables of how workers get to work and how long the commute takes, by state (data.census.gov, as of the survey year); yearly recreation visits to each national park (National Park Service visitor use statistics, as of the year); one station's daily high temperatures for one year (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Centers for Environmental Information, daily summaries, as of the year); Old Faithful's eruption intervals as published with the study by Azzalini and Bowman (1990), a two-humped distribution where a normal model fails. Unit 2: vehicle weight against miles per gallon from the US Department of Energy and Environmental Protection Agency fuel economy data (one model year, as of that year; makes and models removed); the US census population counts 1790 to 1900 (US Census Bureau historical counts), straightened by a logarithm; speeds and stopping distances of cars measured in the 1920s, as published by Ezekiel (1930), straightened by a square root; the yearly mean atmospheric carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, Global Monitoring Laboratory, as of the year read) as Chemistry's climate series, its residual plot showing the curve. Unit 3: the 3,000-plus US counties as a printed population to sample (US Census Bureau county population estimates, one vintage, as of that year; one copy per group), stratified by the four census regions; the 1954 field trial of the polio vaccine, from its published summary report (Francis and colleagues, 1955) and later public accounts, as a randomised, blinded, placebo-controlled design set beside its observed-control half. Unit 4: Mendel-style crosses (Biology) with ratios from the theory, labelled as expected values, not data; the shared-birthday problem; cards and dice rolled in class. Unit 5: households by number of vehicles available (US Census Bureau community survey table, as of the year) for S-MD.A.4; one state lottery's published odds and prize table (the state lottery agency's page, as of the date read); two car insurance deductibles with accident rates invented for practice; a screening test with rates invented for practice; a hockey team pulling its goalie late, with scoring rates invented for practice. Unit 6: a flat (uniform) parent from a fair die or random digits and Old Faithful's two-humped intervals (unit 1's source), whose sample means of 10 to 30 turn normal; then the populations of all US counties (unit 3's source) as the counter-case, where samples of 30 are not yet large enough. Unit 7: a simple random sample of about 200 person records for one state, drawn by Curio Class from the US Census Bureau's public-use microdata (as of the survey year; place of birth), with the published state percent kept as the known value each class interval is checked against; the national highway safety agency's yearly seat-belt use survey estimate and margin (as of the year), read beside a supplied simple random sample of observations from one site list, with why a clustered survey's margin is wider than the simple formula gives (where no such sample can be supplied, the survey is read for its published margin only and the procedures run on the microdata sample). Unit 8: the masses of US one-cent coins minted before and after the 1982 change of metal, weighed in class on a 0.01 gram scale (1982 itself excluded; a school without a scale uses a set invented for practice from the mint's published specified masses, labelled so), used first for intervals and as the case where a test's answer is known by design; each station's annual mean temperature in the 1961 to 1990 and 1991 to 2020 climate normals, at stations drawn at random from the national station list (National Centers for Environmental Information climate normals; paired); and two random samples of station annual means from two regions, a two-sample case whose answer is not known in advance. Unit 9: Mendel's own pea counts from his 1866 paper (public domain in English translation) for goodness of fit; a simple random sample of about 400 worker records from the public-use microdata (unit 7's source), commuting mode by region (homogeneity, one sample per region) and commuting mode by a second variable in one sample (independence); Old Faithful's eruption length against the wait to the next eruption (unit 1's source) as the main slope case (successive eruptions are not fully independent, a limit the class names); and a reaction-rate table invented for practice from Chemistry's rate pattern, labelled so, with the rate logged as in unit 2, as a conditions case where a straight-line test on the raw data would mislead.
- **The investigation.** Unit 10 (stat-investigation-project, 13 days) is the course's full modeling cycle and is spread, not taught as a block: 2 days held after Check 1 (days 47 and 48: the question and a data collection plan drafted, the teacher's written feedback, the plan revised and approved), 2 days held in unit 5 (gathering and cleaning the data), and 9 days at the end of the year, days 152 to 160: day 152 the data described, day 153 the inference procedure chosen and carried out, day 154 the written report drafted and handed in, day 155 the presentation visual made while the teacher writes feedback, day 156 the report revised with that written feedback and handed in for its score, days 157 to 159 short presentations (about 3 minutes and one question each, a third of the class a day, one performance per student), and day 160 the respond day (feedback on the presentations and the year's record). All are Application days except the respond day; the revised report and the presentation are the end-of-unit evidence. The Units table keeps it as one unit at the end; the scope and sequence places the held days on their own school days (days.csv). An approved plan is one of two kinds: a randomised experiment within the class (for example, two ways to memorise a list, assigned at random; the conclusion is about these students and the treatments), or a random sample from a public data set; the report states its scope of inference. Questions avoid sensitive personal data; any class data is anonymous, voluntary and harmless (recall of a list after one of two study methods, estimating a 30-second interval, letters in a chosen word), and students never measure or share their bodies.
- **Practices.** All eight are placed (C3 column): MP1 (units 4, 5, 10), MP2 (units 1, 8), MP3 (units 3, 7, 9, 10), MP4 (units 2, 3, 5, 10), MP5 (units 6, 8, 10), MP6 (units 1, 7), MP7 (units 2, 4, 9), MP8 (units 5, 6).
- **Fluency without a timer.** The procedures this course expects to be fluent: summarising a distribution (five-number summary, mean and standard deviation with a calculator) and locating a value with a z-score and the normal table (CUR-ST.2, CUR-ST.3, unit 1); the probability rules on trees and tables (CUR-ST.8, unit 4); and carrying out and writing up an inference procedure in four moves, the question and parameter, the conditions, the calculation, the conclusion in context (CUR-ST.12, CUR-ST.13, unit 7; CUR-ST.16, CUR-ST.18, unit 8). Each is built in its unit and kept up in the daily opening for the rest of the year. The year-end check holds an untimed, independent fluency station that samples each. No countdown timer is ever shown to a student.
- **Technology and the paper path.** Statistics uses technology for large data sets, simulation, regression and inference. Every use has a paper path: simulation with cards, coins, dice and a printed table of random digits; printed normal, t, chi-square and binomial tables; printed data sets of a size worked by hand; dot plots, box plots and scatter plots drawn on grid paper; regression output read from a printed computer printout; a scientific calculator with a statistics mode (mean, standard deviation, linear regression). Any electronic tool is a free one that needs no student account (a browser statistics applet or a spreadsheet), named in no student page.
- **Devices.** One teacher display every day; a class set of 30 scientific calculators with a statistics mode (school-owned, kept in the room); on the days the scope marks for simulation, large data sets or regression, one device for each pair of students (15 for a class of 30), with no student account. No owned code needs a device to be met in full: CUR-ST.3 allows tables, CUR-ST.11 is simulated with cards and dice as well, and CUR-ST.20 reads a printed regression output. A student on the paper path meets every code with printed tables, printed data sets of a size worked by hand, and printed computer output; for the investigation's public data option, the teacher prints a random sample of at most 100 records.
- **Cost ceiling.** About $450 a year for a class of 30 (Cory, 2026-11-17, Desk card q-electives-frames-1): printing (about $360) within it, with the dice and card kit (about $45), 3 scales (about $45), coins and poster paper; a class set of statistics-mode calculators (about $360) bought once where the school has none. No unit's provisional cost passes $150; a unit plan that does names a cheaper route.
- **Grade 12 calendar.** The last weeks hold teaching: Check 3, the year-end check, takes days 149 to 151, and the investigation's 9 end days (152 to 160) close the year, so its report and presentations are the last work. If seniors leave early, Check 3 and the investigation keep their order and move up, and the course folds review time and one presentation day, never a check, an end-of-unit assessment, a feedback or a revision day, one day each in this order: unit 1's categorical review (2 days to 1), unit 1's displays and summaries review (2.5 to 1.5), unit 2's review (2 to 1), unit 4's review of Geometry's rules (3 to 2), and the presentations (3 days to 2, half the class a day): up to 5 days. A school that knows its seniors' last day plans these folds from the start; more than 5 days is For the lead 8.
- **Side standards.** None is claimed. The inference write-ups and the investigation report practise explanatory writing and a short research project (WHST.11-12.2, WHST.11-12.7), practised, not owned.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md). Data sets about health, income, crime or identity name what the agent studies first. Health (unit 3: the vaccine trial and Biology's feedback investigation redesigned on paper; unit 5: a screening test): a national public health body's plain-language pages on clinical trials and on how accurate screening tests are, a health journalists' association guide on reporting studies and risk, and a disability and illness language guide. Income and money (unit 5: the lottery, insurance; the census microdata samples of units 7 and 9, whose income and household fields are dropped before students see them): a national problem-gambling council's guidance for teaching young people about odds, and the census bureau's own notes on what its household measures mean; games of chance are analysed, never played for money, and no task asks about a student's family money. Identity (unit 7: place of birth; unit 9: commuting by region, never by group): the census bureau's explanation of how it asks these questions and a journalists' diversity style guide; groups are never ranked and no student is asked where they or their family were born. Road safety (unit 7: seat-belt use): the national highway safety agency's plain-language seat-belt pages; use rates only, no crash or injury figures. Crime: the course uses no crime data. Students never share their own sensitive data (health, income, family, immigration, location of home); any survey of classmates is anonymous and harmless. The answer-key agent reads every check, assessment, rubric and key, solving each item cold. Word problems use a range of names and settings; the unit plan lists the people and places its problems show.
- **Readiness after a gap.** Count by grade number (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-14): a row used in grade 11 opens as a retrieval task inside a day, a row last used in grade 10 or earlier gets a readiness check, and a day that teaches a row counts as using the rows it assumes (one hop, 2026-11-15). Two units hold readiness checks: unit 1 (alg1-two-way-tables, last used in Geometry, grade 10) in the last ten minutes of day 1, after the hook and the compelling question; unit 4 (geo-conditional-independence, geo-addition-rule, grade 10) in the last ten minutes of Check 1's respond day. Rows taught earlier this year (alg1-two-way-tables in unit 9, alg1-scatter-linear-models in unit 9, alg2-experiments-significance in units 7 and 8) are retrieved. Day 1 of every other unit stays clean for the hook. Results target reminders with the earlier model and parallel support; they never replace the next hook. The last use of each assumed row (grade, core course, unit and day) is in the Seams table, read from the core courses' scopes (data/plans/algebra-i, geometry and algebra-ii days.csv, 2026-11-16; unit days only, a day that teaches a row which assumes it counting as one hop). The scope gate's rule 2 count governs: where it differs, the scope fix pass updates this frame. Grades: data/courses.csv opens this course to grades 11 and 12; the count starts from grade 12 with Algebra II in grade 11. Taken in grade 11, it needs Algebra II in grade 10 (the parked accelerated path, L15), so every core course sits one grade earlier and the gaps and treatments stay the same; a grade 11 student beside Algebra II is not a route this frame plans (Algebra II is the prerequisite).

### Beside it in grade 12: Precalculus and Calculus (by course order)

Precalculus and Calculus are the other grade 12 math electives. Statistics stands beside them and assumes neither: no stat- row names a pc- or calc- row, and none of theirs names a stat- row. A student who takes Calculus as well meets normal-curve areas again as a definite integral (calc-riemann-sums assumes alg2-normal-distributions, as stat-position-normal does), and Precalculus shows combinations behind the binomial coefficients (pc-sequences-series-binomial) without owning S-CP.B.9, which unit 4 owns here. Neither course is needed: unit 4 teaches counting from the start, and nothing here uses a derivative or an integral. On the core route a student has one grade 12 math year, so Statistics is one of three choices (For the lead 7).

## Source map

A section is not a lesson: the days are ours, set by the scope and sequence, each adapting what it uses and crediting the base once a day in the credit line; a section may feed several days or part of one. Check and assessment days are always `ours`, never a kept section, and a kept section or lab is never turned into a test. Goals and notes are in our words. Students read our adapted text, never the base, at most about 600 words a day (about ten minutes at the course's reading level), worked examples, tables and figures beside it.

The base text's order (describe, probability, distributions, inference) is kept, with four moves for teaching: producing data (1.2, 1.4, 1.6) comes in unit 3 after both describing units; regression (chapter 12) moves up to unit 2 so the descriptive half is whole before probability, its inference section joining unit 9; density curves (5.1) join the normal model in unit 1 and the uniform distribution (5.2, 5.4) serves unit 6; and proportions (8.3, 9.1 to 9.5, 10.3) come before means (8.1, 8.2, 9.6, 10.1, 10.4) because their sampling distribution is simpler and the logic of interval and test is built once (unit 7).
