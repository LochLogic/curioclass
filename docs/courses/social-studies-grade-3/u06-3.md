# Section 6.3: Whose voices counted, and what symbols promise

Read this plan with its facts from data (days, codes, minutes): [the Front Office](https://curioclass.org/front-office/#social-studies/social-studies-grade-3/6/s/6.3), or `python -X utf8 scripts/plan_facts.py social-studies-grade-3 u06-3.md`. <!-- facts link -->

## Header


Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain, no sentence over 15 words; the teacher reads every card, source and stem aloud once. Answers may be a sentence, a labeled drawing, a sorted card or words said at the side table. Each day runs 40 minutes. Exits and records are marked shown, shown with help or not yet seen (one scale for each record). Every made-up card is said aloud to be made up and printed "invented for practice". These days state plainly who was left out. Native nations are named by their own names and govern themselves today. Enslaved people are named as enslaved people, with the words from grade 2. No role play, no simulation, no exclusion games; no child is asked about home, family, money, body, religion or status, or to speak for a group they may belong to. The stop card and the right to pass hold (unit 5).

## 1. Standards and practices


- **MA.3.T6.4, day 130.** "...full citizenship was then limited, with voting set by each state and in most states held by white male property owners over 21 (New Jersey's voters included some women and free Black men until 1807)." This section carries the last part, and the difference between citizenship, voting and equal rights. Section 6.2 carries the Constitution and the Bill of Rights.
- **D2.Civ.8.3-5, days 130 to 132.** "Identify civic virtues and democratic principles guiding governments and communities." Day 130: a virtue shown by Elizabeth Freeman (perseverance) and the principle of equal rights. Day 131: the principle of consent applied to the sort. Day 132: what a symbol stands for, as a principle (liberty, remembrance).
- **Limits that bite here.** Voting is taught as set by each state, never as one national rule. The New Jersey exception is a fact with limits: a few women (mostly unmarried or widowed, because property law) and free Black men who met the property and residence rules, ended in 1807 (source to check). Enslaved people were not voters and were enslaved, said plainly. Native nations governed themselves; whether a member voted in a state election is a separate question, and no claim is made for every member. The war itself is grade 5's. Freeman's account omits graphic abuse and the uncertain story of what she overheard.
- **Connections.** Grade 1 citizen and national symbols (readiness, 126). Unit 5's petition and "legal is not right" (131 promise and practice). Unit 4's sovereignty and tribal government (130, 131). Unit 6.1's Declaration and the ten-year strip (130 bands). Unit 6.2's Bill of Rights as limit.
- **What the teacher page must explain.** Citizenship, voting and equal rights are three questions with three answers in 1790; no vote did not mean no citizenship (D). New Jersey's 1776 rules and the 1807 change. What the Oneida Indian Nation says of itself (its own pages, first), and why Native nations are not a "past people". How Freeman's case worked in Massachusetts in 1781 (source to check). A symbol's later meaning can differ from its first purpose. The Barnette decision (1943): no child is made to salute or recite; the teacher states it.

## 3. Checks, written first


**Unit assessment items owned:** Q2b and the distinctions from the day 131 sort (MA.3.T6.4), and Q3 (D2.Civ.8.3-5), asked on day 135 from packet C and packet D. Teaching uses an opening account of the Oneida Indian Nation, invented New Jersey cases and Elizabeth Freeman; packet C's persons, dates and its New Jersey account are withheld, and the petition's comparison is withheld from every day.

**Exit questions.** If many miss one, the next day's repair part opens with that error's wrong example.
- **E130 (Oneida Card, an original summary of a different sentence of the Oneida Indian Nation's own First Allies page, sources.csv U6H, and a New Jersey case, invented for practice; two asks, E130 and E130b).** E130: "Oneida Card. Does the message say Oneida people voted in state elections?" Card: an original summary of the December 1777 message from Congress promising to protect the Oneidas. Key: no; it is a promise between Congress and a nation and says nothing about state elections. Wrong: "Yes, friends of Congress voted in state elections" (U). E130b: "Could this woman vote in 1790?" Key: yes; she meets the property and residence rules, and New Jersey's law named property, not sex, until 1807. Wrong: "No woman ever voted" (J).
- **E131 (New Voting Law Card, invented for practice; two asks, E131 and E131b).** Card: "In 1805 a state law says only men who own land may vote. Reza was born in that state and owns none." E131: "Is Reza still a citizen of the state?" Key: yes; it says who may vote, not who is a citizen. Wrong: "No, he cannot vote, so he is not a citizen" (D). E131b: "What does this law decide?" Key: who may vote. Wrong: "Who is a citizen" (D).
- **E132 (New Monument Caption, invented for practice; two asks, E132 and E132b).** Card: a town council ordered a statue in 1890 "to remind residents of fair courts". E132: "Why was the statue made?" Key: to remind people of fair courts. Wrong: "It is a statue of a person" (B); "So everyone would salute it" (L). E132b: "Does the caption prove everyone was treated equally?" Key: no; it shows an ideal, not equal treatment. Wrong: "The statue proves fair treatment" (F).

**Records (not exits).** Day 130: the three-question card with one line for each. Day 131: the sort of four cases, revised on day 134. Day 132: the five-row symbol card.

## 6. Days

## Day 130


- **5 min. Return and repair (O and E from E129).** One repair: a fresh clause beside the limit it puts on government (O), or three fresh details sorted to the three documents (E). If few missed it, those children use the side table.
- **3 min. Retrieval.** Grade 2 country reports: how does a report name its sources? Now each card gets maker, date, audience and purpose.
- **6 min. Voting was set by each state.** Each state set its own rules. In most, voters were white men over 21 who owned property (the standard's wording; source to check). Enslaved people were not voters, and they were enslaved. The teacher says plainly that many people were left out. Native nations governed themselves under their own laws; the Oneida Indian Nation's opening account is read aloud (its own page; source to check; present tense where it lives on).
- **8 min. New Jersey.** For a time New Jersey's rules named property, not sex or color (source to check). On the strip a band for 1776 to 1807 is drawn under 1787 and 1791. Two invented cards (invented for practice): a free Black man in 1802 with the property, and a woman in 1810. Who could vote? The class says why each, and what changed in 1807 (limited to white male citizens).
- **6 min. Elizabeth Freeman.** A short biography (NPS; source to check): enslaved in Massachusetts, she asked a court for freedom in 1781 and won, and later worked for pay. The class names what she did (perseverance is a civic virtue: something a person does). The overheard-story and any abuse are not told.
- **8 min. Three questions.** Citizenship (is a person counted a member), voting (who may vote) and equal rights (who is treated the same under the law), one invented worked case each (invented for practice). A line for each on the card; the teacher hears a rotating six, one line each.
- **4 min. Exit.** E130 and E130b (Oneida Card and New Jersey Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the strip; each child the card. Tool: None. By hand: the three lines. Evidence: E130; the card. This day carries the sensitivity read. Hand-off: tomorrow, sorting fresh cases into the three questions.

## Day 131


- **5 min. Return and repair (U or J from E130, whichever more missed).** One repair, five minutes. The other letter goes to a small group at the side table during the sort.
- **4 min. Recall.** The three questions, said back from the card.
- **5 min. One together.** A fresh invented case (invented for practice) sorted by the class, with the reason said in the frame "This answers the ___ question because ___."
- **14 min. The sort.** Each child sorts four fresh cases (invented for practice, except the Oneida card, which is an original summary of the Oneida Indian Nation's own account and is the card that answers none of the three: a nation's own decision, not a state election). The record: a pocket and one reason each. The teacher hears a rotating six; the others write or point.
- **8 min. Say why.** Pairs explain one card; the class hears three. The teacher says that a vote or its denial is not a citizenship answer (D). The unit's question comes back: whose agreement counted?
- **4 min. Exit.** E131 and E131b (Voting Law Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the pockets; each child the sort. Tool: None. By hand: the four-card sort. Evidence: E131; the record, revised on day 134. This day carries the sensitivity read. Hand-off: tomorrow, symbols and what they promise.

## Day 132

Shape note: E132 is a one-caption "why was it made" card; no practice or model may use that shape (the five-row card orders captions by year instead).


- **5 min. Return and repair, with retrieval (D or S from E131 and the record).** One repair of at most five minutes; the other letter goes to the six heard during the record. If neither was missed: retrieval of grade 1 symbols (a picture or thing that stands for our country) and unit 6's three questions.
- **17 min. Five cards.** For each of the Liberty Bell, the Statue of Liberty, the national anthem, Independence Day and the 1777 flag resolution: a caption read aloud, why it was made, what it stands for, and its date (4, 4, 3, 3 and 3 minutes). The Liberty Bell's later use by people opposing slavery and the Statue's connection to abolition (NPS; source to check) are named as later meanings. The 1777 resolution is checked in the Journals of the Continental Congress before it is read. Later symbols are dated explicitly. The teacher says plainly that no child has to sing, salute or recite (Barnette, 1943), and that learning about a symbol is enough.
- **8 min. Supported discussion.** The class asks: does a symbol prove everyone was free? Five minutes of this compare a symbol's meaning with a dated rights record (F), and three the L idea: two ways to study a symbol, with the choice to decline kept. Sentence frame: "The symbol says ___; the record says ___."
- **7 min. Record.** Each child completes a five-row card from fresh captions: why it was made (from the caption) and one idea it stands for, with a word bank; for one symbol, how a later meaning differs from the first purpose. Pointing and dictation count. A row left unfinished is finished at day 133's side table, not for a mark. The teacher hears a rotating six.
- **3 min. Exit.** E132 and E132b (Monument Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the captions; each child the card. Tool: None. By hand: the five-row card. Evidence: E132; the record. This day carries the sensitivity read. Hand-off: tomorrow, writing a claim that answers the unit's question.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: citizen, vote, rights, equal, symbol, nation, perseverance. Frames: "This answers the ___ question because ___." "The symbol says ___; the record says ___." "She ___ to get ___." A home-language word beside each is welcome.
- **Access.** Large-print cards, a color for each pocket, speech or a scribe for the sort, the sort done by pointing, a word bank for the symbol card.
- **Check accommodations.** Read each stem aloud; accept a spoken or pointed answer; the six heard may include any child who needs to say it.
- **Reading load.** Source lines are one or two short sentences read aloud; the five captions are read aloud by the teacher, two or three sentences each.
- **Content notes.** These days name unequal law, enslavement, women and free Black men losing a vote, and the denial of rights to Native nations as nations, plainly and without graphic detail. No slur is read. Children may use the stop card or pass at any point. No child is asked about their own group or to be an example.
- **Extension, same standard.** A fifth case written by the child and sorted by a partner, or a second symbol's later meaning.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** The adult reads each card slowly, lets the child stop at any point, and writes the child's words. The three pockets are four boxes drawn on paper. No song is sung and no one salutes; the adult says so. Nothing in this section is the last day of the unit.
- **One projector.** Project the strip with its bands and the captions; each child holds paper copies of the sort cards.
