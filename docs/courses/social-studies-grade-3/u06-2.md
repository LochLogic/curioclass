# Section 6.2: The national plan and the Bill of Rights

## Header


Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain, no sentence over 15 words; the teacher reads every card, source and stem aloud once. Answers may be a sentence, a labelled drawing or a filled table. Each day runs 40 minutes. Exits and records are marked shown, shown with help or not yet seen (one scale for each record). Every made-up card is said aloud to be made up and printed "invented for practice". No child is asked about home, family, money or religion.

## 1. Standards and practices


- **MA.3.T6.4, days 128 and 129.** "Explain that the new nation wrote the Constitution as a plan of government, that the Bill of Rights spells out citizens' rights, and that full citizenship was then limited, with voting set by each state..." This section carries the first two parts: the Constitution as the plan (128) and the Bill of Rights as the list of rights (129). The limits on citizenship and voting are section 6.3's, from day 130.
- **D2.Civ.5.3-5, day 128.** "Explain different government systems' beginnings, purposes and organization, including constitutional systems." Beginnings: a convention drafted the national plan in 1787 and the states approved it. Purposes: a plan sets up government, says what it may and may not do, and lists rights (unit 2's three-job strip, review). Organization: three parts, each with one job (unit 2's job baskets, review).
- **Limits that bite here.** Grade 5 owns the debates, the details of each branch's powers and the amendment process. Three articles are read by their opening sentences only. The Bill of Rights is taught by one clause (speech and the press); other clauses are for the exit and the assessment. The teacher separates a promised right from one actually protected for everyone.
- **Connections.** Unit 2's constitution chart and job baskets (day 128). Unit 6.1's Declaration (day 128 contrast: the Declaration gives reasons, the Constitution sets up a government). Grade 2 countries, retrieval on 128: how a country differs from a state within it.
- **What the teacher page must explain.** The Declaration (1776), the Constitution (1787) and the Bill of Rights (1791; source to check) are three documents with three jobs (E). What a convention was and what ratification meant (the states approved it; source to check). Why the first ten amendments were added. That a right listed in a document is a limit on government (O).

## 3. Checks, written first


**Unit assessment items owned:** Q4 (D2.Civ.5.3-5), asked on day 135 from packet B and the fresh own-state card; Q2a (the Constitution, the first ten amendments, one right) is listed in the unit plan against section 6.2 in the owner cell and is also owned here. Teaching uses only the opening sentence of each article and the speech clause. Packet B's crops are later sentences of Articles I to III, the voter-qualification clause and the petition protection; none is read in teaching, and the religious-exercise phrase is the exit.

**Exit questions.** If many miss one, the next day's repair part opens with that error's wrong example.
- **E128 (Kestrel Isle plan card, invented for practice; the unit plan makes day 128 a record, this exit is added; two asks, E128 and E128b).** Card: "On Kestrel Isle, judges decide whether a person broke a law." E128: "Which job is it: makes laws, carries out laws or decides cases?" Key: decides cases. Wrong: "makes laws, because the judge writes the law" (K). E128b: "In our national plan, who does that job?" Key: the courts. Wrong: "The President" (K). P is covered by the origins record and by Q4b and R2.
- **E129 (the religious-exercise phrase from the First Amendment, "prohibiting the free exercise thereof", with a gloss; unused in teaching; two asks, E129 and E129b).** E129: "What does this phrase protect?" Key: people may practice their religion without the government stopping them; no belief is asked of any child. Wrong: "The government can pick one faith to give rights to" (O). E129b: "Does it set up Congress, list a right or explain independence?" Key: it lists a right. Wrong: "It sets up Congress" or "It explains independence" (E).

**Records (not exits).** Day 128: the origins record, a two-row table filled from a new official state caption: who drafted and who approved our state's plan, and the same for the national plan. Day 129: one right matched to the limit it puts on government.

## 5. What is carried, and instruments


- **Carried.** (1) Unit 2's three-job strip and job baskets (makes laws, carries out laws, decides cases): used again on 128 beside the national plan, then set against the state's. (2) The three-card order strip from unit 2 (proposed, approved by the states, part of the Constitution): reused on 128 for the origin of both plans. (3) The strip with 1776 from 126: 1787 and 1791 are added on 128 and 129. (4) The question board: "What makes a government plan?" and "What rights were listed?" get answers.

- **Key moment 2, day 129: right and limit.** What must be seen: the speech clause paired with what the government may not do. Ideal instrument: a two-column card, right and limit. Spec: print fits.
- **A room with nothing.** The baskets drawn on paper; the sentences copied by hand.

## 6. Days

## Day 128


- **5 min. Return and repair (T and O from E127).** One repair: a fresh invented ruler who asks and one who only commands (T), or a fresh clause beside the limit it puts on a ruler (O). If few missed it, those children use the side table.
- **5 min. Retrieval.** Grade 2 countries: how a country differs from a state within it, on a small map. Unit 2's three-job strip and constitution chart: what a constitution is for.
- **8 min. Where the national plan came from.** The three-card order strip for 1787: delegates met in Philadelphia and drafted a plan; the states approved it (convention account, archives.gov; source to check). Beside it, the Declaration (1776) from 6.1 gives reasons; the Constitution sets up a government (P). Two minutes of the E match: three new details sorted to independence, a government plan or a list of rights.
- **8 min. The three parts.** The opening sentence of Article I, Article II and Article III with job labels (archives.gov transcript; source to check): Congress, a President, courts. The class sorts each into the job baskets from unit 2. The teacher says plainly that the plan splits the work so one person does not do it all (K).
- **5 min. Beside our state.** The class's own state plan from unit 2's chart set beside the national plan: same three baskets, one for the state and one for the country. Pairs name one alike and one different.
- **5 min. Origins record.** From a new official state caption (to choose; source to check), each child fills two rows: who drafted and who approved our state's plan, and the same for the national plan. The teacher hears a rotating six, about 50 seconds each; the record names them.
- **4 min. Exit.** E128 and E128b (Kestrel Isle Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the baskets; each child a record. Tool: None. By hand: the sort, the two-row table. Evidence: E128; the origins record, revised on day 134. Hand-off: tomorrow, the list of rights added to the plan.

## Day 129


- **10 min. Return and repair (K, and P if carried, from E128).** If many missed K, ten minutes: fresh provisions on cards sorted to the three baskets. If few, those children take it at the side table while the rest start the next part, and P goes to the small-group table with the order strip for five minutes.
- **8 min. Why a list.** The Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments, added to the Constitution after it began (1791; source to check). Amendment is a word met in unit 2; here it is taught. The teacher shows the Bill of Rights' short introduction in plain words: the list was added to keep the government from overreaching (source to check). 1791 goes on the strip, four years after 1787.
- **8 min. One right, closely.** The First Amendment's speech and press clause (archives.gov; source to check), read aloud with a gloss: the government may not stop people from speaking or printing their ideas. Pairs say one thing the clause lets a person do.
- **5 min. A right is a limit.** A right listed in the Bill of Rights limits what government may do (O). The class completes a two-column card, right and limit, for the speech clause. The teacher says plainly that a right on paper and a right protected for everyone were not always the same, and that day 130 asks who.
- **6 min. Three documents.** New details sorted to independence, a government plan or a list of rights. Each child sorts three; the teacher hears a rotating six, one sorted card each.
- **3 min. Exit.** E129 and E129b (Phrase Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the strip and the two columns; each child the sort. Tool: None. By hand: the card. Evidence: E129; the right and limit card. Hand-off: tomorrow, who could vote and who was left out.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: constitution, convention, amendment, right, speech, press, limit. Frames: "The ___ makes laws." "The Bill of Rights lists ___." "A right limits ___." A home-language word beside each is welcome.
- **Access.** Large-print cards, a colour for each basket, speech or a scribe for the origins record, sorting by pointing.
- **Check accommodations.** Read each stem aloud; accept a spoken or pointed answer; the six heard may include any child who needs to say it.
- **Reading load.** Articles and clauses are read aloud by the teacher, one sentence at a time; children read labels and the job words only.
- **Extension, same standard.** Match a second article opening to its basket, or add a second state's date to the strip.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** The adult reads each sentence aloud and slowly; the child puts the cards into the three baskets and says the right and its limit. The adult writes the child's words in the origins table. Nothing in this section is the last day of the unit.
- **One projector.** Project the baskets and the strip; each child holds paper copies of the cards.
