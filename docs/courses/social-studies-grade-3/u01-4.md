Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain; the teacher reads every card and exit aloud once. An answer may be a sentence, a labelled map or a filled card. - **MA.3.T2.1, this section's share.** "Use a physical map of North America to locate the Northeast region and identify its important rivers, lakes, shoreline, capes, bays, and mountains." Day 11 locates the class's own region on a US map (the verb "locate"); day 14 names its rivers, lakes, shoreline, capes, bays and mountains on a physical map (the verb "identify"). General form: the class's own region. Where the region has no coast, "no coast" is the right answer.
- **MA.3.T2.2, this section's share.** "Locate the six New England states on a political map of the United States." Day 12 teaches New England's six as the worked panel, then every state of the class's own region; day 13 practises both and adds one more region.
- **D2.Geo.2.3-5, this section's share.** "Use maps, satellite images and photographs to explain how the location of places and regions relates to their environmental features." Day 11 sets a physical map over a political one; day 14 names what a feature could do for a town; day 15 uses a map, a satellite view and a photograph to explain how a feature could help place a town.
- **Limits that bite here.** "Region" means the five-region classroom key in the unit plan (field 5, packet R); the six New England states are the standard's own list and sit inside the key's Northeast. "Important" features are the ones the printed map labels; no one lists every river. A feature is never said to be why a town stands unless a source says so (shows, suggests, cannot tell, from day 9). No local history beyond the town's own page. Q4 to Q6 use L and R; teaching uses other maps.
- **Connections.** The grid cell, letter first (day 5), returns in the day 11 repair. The legend and north arrow (days 7 and 10) are read again on the physical map (day 14). Supporting question 3, "How did land and water help place towns?", is answered on day 15. The satellite view is used on day 15 only.
- **Review, not new (the ledger's words).** State (a part of our country, kindergarten); region (an area that shares something, grade 2); physical map and political map, border (grade 2); river, lake, mountain, harbor (grade 1); settle (to make a home in a place and stay; people have reasons, such as water, food or a way to travel); shows, suggests, cannot tell (day 9); grid cell. New: New England, the five-region key, shoreline, cape, bay, pass, mountain range.
- **What the teacher page must explain.** A state is a part of our country; a region is a group of states placed together by a choice, and the five-region key is a classroom convention, not the only grouping (other maps group states differently; source to check: a Census Bureau region map). Living in one region does not make people alike. A political map shows states and borders; a physical map shows land and water; the colours on a political map only tell neighbours apart. A cape is land that sticks out into water; a bay is water that curves into land; a pass is a low way through mountains. Seeing a river beside a town does not tell why the town began. Feature names come from the printed map's own labels (source to check: USGS or National Park Service pages for each).

**Exit questions.** If many miss one, the next day opens with that error's wrong example. Each exit is a new map or card, read aloud once.
- **E11 (Exit Map 1, new).** A new US political map marks one state: Missouri. If the class's region or the day's model is the Midwest, mark Tennessee. "Which region is the marked state in? Show how you found it." Answer: Midwest (Southeast for Tennessee), by finding the state's name in the region key's list. Wrong: gives the state's own name, or "the Plains", as the region (F); says it is in our region because it is the same colour as our state (B). The data asks it as E11, E11b, one ask each; items.csv governs.
- **E12 (new outline of our region, our state shaded).** "Name the states of our region that touch our state." Key: every touching state on the overlay, each in place; where none touch (an island state), the child names two states of the region and says none touch. Wrong: names a region or a landform instead of a state, such as "the Great Lakes" (F); names one touching state and stops when more touch (S).
- **E13 (new political outline of the Southwest, with a name bank of eight).** The bank holds Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico and Arizona, two states of other regions, the word "Southwest" and "Rocky Mountains". "Label the Southwest's states. Use the key." Answer: the four states, each in place. Wrong: writes "Southwest" or "Rocky Mountains" on a state (F); labels only some of the four (S). A class living in the Southwest labels the West instead, with six of its eleven states already labelled.
- **E14 (Fenmark Panel, invented for practice).** A made-up physical panel with its own key; four places are marked A to D. "What kind of feature is each marked place? Use the key." The bank holds river, lake, mountains, bay, cape, shoreline. Answer: the kinds on the overlay. Wrong: names a state where the mountains are (F); calls a cape a bay or a bay a cape (F); decides by colour alone (B). The data asks it as E14, E14b, E14c, E14d, one ask each; items.csv governs.
- **E15 (Hillford Map and Bridge Photo, invented for practice).** Hillford sits at a river bend; its main road crosses the river on a bridge. "What could help explain why Hillford is where it is? Use the map or the photograph." Answer: the bridge lets the road cross the river, so people could meet and cross here; or the river could help travel. Wrong: "Everyone came for the water" (G); "There is a river, a bridge and a hill" (P).

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: state, region, border, river, lake, mountains, shoreline, cape, bay, pass, harbor, shows, might help, cannot tell. Frames: "Our state is ___. It is in the ___ region." and "The map shows ___. It might help because ___." Accept pointing, labelling and dictation.
- **Access.** Raised-line or tactile maps, large-print name banks, movable name tiles, and an enlarged region.
- **Check accommodations.** Exits read aloud once; spoken answers, pointing and a scribe are fine; spelling is not judged; the name bank stays beside the child.
- **Reading load.** Cards at most 350 words, no sentence over 15 words; the town page paragraph is read aloud.
- **Extension, same standard.** Label a second region's states with no bank, or find a second feature on the Our Town Card and say what it might do.
