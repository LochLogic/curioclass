# Section 6.1: Why independence, and what the writers believed

Read this plan with its facts from data (days, codes, minutes): [the Front Office](https://curioclass.org/front-office/#social-studies/social-studies-grade-3/6/s/6.1), or `python -X utf8 scripts/plan_facts.py social-studies-grade-3 u06-1.md`. <!-- facts link -->

## Header


Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain, no sentence over 15 words; the teacher reads every card, source and stem aloud once. Answers may be a sentence, a labeled drawing or a filled table. Each day runs 40 minutes. Exits and records are marked shown, shown with help or not yet seen (one scale for each record). Every made-up card is said aloud to be made up and printed "invented for practice". Native nations are named by their own names and live today. No role play and no simulation; no child is asked about home, family, money or religion.

## 1. Standards and practices


- **D1.1.3-5, day 125.** "Explain why the compelling question matters to other people." The whole standard: a named other person and a reason the question reaches that person's life. Scored from the day 125 record as revised on day 134.
- **MA.3.T6.3, days 126 and 127.** "Analyze how colonists' sense of injustice led them to declare independence, and what the Declaration of Independence shows about its writers' beliefs." Day 126 carries the injustice and the declaring; day 127 carries the beliefs (equal, rights, consent). The whole standard is covered across the two days.
- **D2.His.1.3-5, day 126.** "Build and use a time sequence." This section creates and uses the strip (extended to 1810, ten-year marks) and places a dated source. Comparing developments that happen together begins on day 130.
- **D2.Civ.8.3-5, day 127.** "Identify civic virtues and democratic principles." Day 127 teaches the two terms with equality and consent as principles and one virtue as something a person does. Day 130 onward uses both.
- **Limits that bite here.** The war, its battles and its people belong to grade 5. The Declaration is read in short pieces, never whole. Grievances are taught from taxation (126) and trade (exit); the grievance about people on the frontier is never read, because it uses a slur against Native peoples. The words "all men are created equal" are read with the plain fact that in 1776 many people were not treated as equal (day 130 asks who). The sentence about changing a harmful government is withheld for the assessment (packet A); day 127 stops at consent.
- **Connections.** Unit 2's fair rule-making read two ways, day 127. Unit 1's matters to others and two readings, day 125. Unit 5's timeline strip and source-sourcing, day 126. Grade 2 interviews and dates, day 126 retrieval. Grade 1 national symbols, day 126 repair.
- **What the teacher page must explain.** The Declaration is a letter of reasons to the world, written in 1776 by a committee of colonists' leaders (maker and date to check). Consent means people agree to how decisions are made, by voting or by choosing representatives; it does not mean everyone likes every law (I). Who the writers meant by "all" and who they left out. Why the room does not sing, salute or recite.

## 3. Checks, written first


**Unit assessment items owned:** Q1a and Q1b (MA.3.T6.3), asked on day 135 from packet A; D1.1 from the day 125 record. Packet A's grievance (dissolved assemblies) and its sentence on changing a harmful government are used on no teaching day, exit or record; teaching uses the taxation grievance (126) and the equality and consent lines (127).

**Exit questions.** If many miss one, the next day's repair part opens with that error's wrong example. Each is read aloud once.
- **E125 (New Worker Exit Case, invented for practice).** "A bus driver is told not to speak at the town meeting. Why might the driver care whose agreement the town government needs?" Key: laws and rules reach the driver's own life and work, so whose agreement counts matters to the driver. Wrong: "Only the mayor cares" (N); "Because the government should be kind to the driver" (Q).
- **E126 (Harbor Card, 1774: an original summary of the First Continental Congress's Declaration and Resolves, sources.csv U6Q; two asks, E126 and E126b).** E126: "How might this help explain why they declared independence?" Key: it adds to their sense of unfair treatment and wanting a government of their own with a say. Wrong: "They wanted no government at all" (C). E126b: "Mark 1774 where it belongs on your strip." Key: between 1770 and 1780, nearer 1770, two years before the 1776 card. Wrong: the card placed one equal step after the last card whatever the years (H).
- **E127 (New Rights Case, invented for practice; two asks, E127 and E127b).** Card: "A town leader says everyone must obey him because he is in charge. No one chose him." E127: "Using the Declaration's idea about power, what is missing?" Key: the people's agreement (consent). Wrong: "Nothing; whoever is in charge decides" (T). E127b: "Why does that matter to the people there?" Key: government is meant to protect their rights and they should have a say in it. Wrong: "The leader owns people's rights" (O).

**Records (not exits).** Day 125: the supporting-question card (a question from the co-written list, a fact it needs, one idea in it people read two ways, and how it connects to the big question). Day 126: the strip with the Declaration card and a source label. Day 127: one claim line.

## 5. What is carried, and instruments


- **Carried.** (1) The question board: the big question with the six co-written supporting questions, answers added after each day (a review of unit 2's routine). (2) The timeline strip from unit 5, extended from 1700 to 1810 with ten-year marks and a fixed scale: it returns on 126 with equal gaps for equal years, and is filled with the Declaration card and the dated trade card; filled examples are withdrawn on 134. (3) Source-sourcing from unit 5 (maker, date, why), now with audience (who it was written for) on 126. (4) The claim frame: kept blank in the folder; a line of it is used on 127.
- **Key moment 1, day 126: the strip.** What must be seen: a card placed by its year with the right gap from its neighbors. Ideal instrument: a ruled strip, one centimetre a year, large enough to draw on. Spec: print fits; no tool is named.
- **Key moment 2, day 127: the two readings of "agreement".** What must be seen: a child separating "everyone voted" from "they chose people to speak for them." Ideal instrument: a two-column chart. Spec: print fits.
- **A room with nothing.** The strip ruled on a long paper strip; the cards copied large by hand.

## 6. Days

## Day 125


- **6 min. Hook.** The Declaration's title and its consent phrase, "the consent of the governed" (archives.gov transcript, public domain; source to check), beside an original two-line explanation: people agree to be governed. The teacher asks "whose agreement does a government need?" Pairs say a name to each other for one minute; the teacher takes three answers. No one is wrong yet.
- **9 min. Consent, taught.** The worked case is Room 5's reading corner (invented for practice): the teacher announces who sits where, then the same corner is set by the class talking and agreeing. Which one had the children's agreement? The term consent is put on the wall: agreeing to how decisions are made. The teacher says plainly that consent is not liking every rule (I). A short reminder card of unit 2's two ways to be fair (everyone votes on each rule, or chosen people decide) is read aloud.
- **6 min. Who else cares?** Unit 1's "matters to others": a shop owner (invented for practice) and a new rule about opening hours. Who cares, and why? The class says the person and the reason in the frame "___ cares because ___."
- **6 min. Co-write.** The class helps write the six supporting questions on the question board: why independence (126), what did the writers believe (127), what makes a government plan (128), what rights were listed (129), whose voices counted (130, 131), what do symbols promise (132). The teacher reads them aloud and points to each day.
- **9 min. Supporting-question record.** Each child fills the four-box card: a question from the board; a fact it needs; one idea in it people read two ways (a voice by voting, or a voice by choosing someone to speak); how it connects to the big question. Words, a drawing or a scribe are fine. The teacher hears a rotating six, one minute each; the record names them.
- **4 min. Exit.** E125.

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the board; each child a record. Tool: None. By hand: the four-box card. Evidence: E125; the record, revised on day 134. Hand-off: tomorrow, why the colonists felt treated unfairly, and the timeline.

## Day 126


- **10 min. Return and repair (A and B from readiness; N and Q from E125).** The readiness block: a flag, a national monument and a chair; a fresh pair to sort; an eagle picture connected to its caption. Whole class if more than a third missed A or B; otherwise a small group while the rest read the trade card for the exit. If many also missed N or Q, the whole class takes five minutes of N or Q first (a fresh law card and an affected person) and the A and B block becomes the small group. If both are many, N or Q moves to day 127's repair part.
- **3 min. Retrieval.** Grade 2 interviews and dates: what can a dated recollection tell us, and what does a date help us know?
- **8 min. Maker, date, audience, purpose.** The Declaration's label, filled by the teacher: who made it (a committee of colonists' leaders; to check), when (1776), who it was for (the king and the wider world; to check), why (to give reasons for separating). Audience is the new word: who a source is written for. Unit 5's source-sourcing becomes four boxes.
- **9 min. A grievance.** The taxation grievance (the Declaration's words about taxes without consent; source to check) beside a plain explanation. Why did this feel unfair? The class links it to consent. The teacher says plainly that the colonists did not want no rules; they wanted a say. The war is named only as "later, in grade 5".
- **7 min. The strip.** Return to the timeline with equal gaps for equal years. Plymouth, 1620, is too far left to draw (80 centimetres before the strip begins), so the teacher marks it with an arrow; the class places 1776 six centimetres after the 1770 mark and checks the gap with a ruler. Each child draws and labels one card for 1776 and fills the four-box label (record P126c).
- **3 min. Exit.** E126 and E126b (Harbor Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the strip and the four-box label; each child a card. Tool: None. By hand: the strip card. Evidence: E126; the strip card. Hand-off: tomorrow, what the Declaration says about rights and consent.

## Day 127


- **5 min. Return and repair (H from E126, or N and Q if carried).** One repair: a fresh dated card placed by its year on the strip. If few missed it, those children use the side table.
- **3 min. Retrieval.** Day 125's consent word and the Room 5 corner, said again by two pairs.
- **10 min. From complaint to a government they chose.** The taxation card from 126 and the colonists' hope for a government with their voice. The class sorts four lines into "wanted no rules" or "wanted a voice" (all four lines are ours, invented for practice). This is the C part; it runs for the whole class every time.
- **10 min. The words.** The equality and consent lines read aloud twice, with a plain gloss: all are created equal, everyone has rights, governments get their power from the people's agreement (public domain; source to check). The teacher says what the writers left out: in 1776 many people, including enslaved people and women, were not treated as equal, and day 130 asks who. The worked case is Arden Lake's boat rule (invented for practice): neighbors chose the lake council; the council wrote the rule.
- **8 min. Principle and virtue.** Two words on the wall: democratic principle (an idea about how a people's government should work, such as equality or consent) and civic virtue (a good habit shown by what a person does, such as fairness). A chart sorts six phrases ours into the two columns. Pairs say one example each.
- **4 min. Exit.** E127 and E127b (Town Leader Card, half slip, two paper items).

Who holds what is carried: the teacher the chart; each child a claim line. Tool: None. By hand: the sort, the claim line. Evidence: E127. Hand-off: tomorrow, how a government gets its plan written down.

## 7. Talk and writing

- Planned questions: day 125, "Whose agreement does a government need?"; day 126, "What did the colonists want instead?"; day 127, "What is missing when no one chose the leader?"
- Written task, day 125: the four-box supporting-question card; it is kept and revised on day 134.
- Judged by the teacher with look-fors: (1) a named other person; (2) a reason that reaches that person's life; (3) the idea of agreement or voice in the connection to the big question. Shown, shown with help or not yet seen.

## 8. Practice that returns

- **Warm-up.** Day 125: none (the hook is the first part). Day 126: the readiness block. Day 127: the strip with the day 126 card.
- **Fluency target.** None; the standard sets none.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: consent, agree, independence, complain, right, fair, strip. Frames: "___ cares because ___." "The colonists wanted ___ because ___." "___ is missing." A home-language word beside each is welcome.
- **Access.** Large-print cards, a color for each box of the label, speech or a scribe for any answer, the strip marked by pointing or with a stamp.
- **Check accommodations.** Read each stem aloud; accept a spoken or pointed answer; the six heard on each day may include any child who needs to say it.
- **Reading load.** Source lines are one or two short sentences read aloud; children read only captions and labels on their cards.
- **Extension, same standard.** A second grievance to link to what the colonists wanted, or the claim line written with a source name.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** The adult reads each card aloud, asks the day's question, and writes the child's words if asked. The adult draws the strip with a ruler and marks the years together. Nothing is acted out or sung. Nothing in this section is the last day of the unit.
- **One projector.** Project the strip, the label and the chart; each child holds paper copies of the cards.
