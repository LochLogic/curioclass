# Section 2.1: Why we have rules and governments

<!-- skel -->
Grade 3: Communities Then and Now. Unit 2, Who decides here (days 25 to 48): see [s02.md](s02.md). **Days 25 to 27.** Days by kind: understanding 3 (25, 26, 27). A plan, not a lesson: nothing here is built or tried with children. Fields follow UNIT-PLAN.md.

- Compelling question (frame): "Who should make the rules in our town?"
- Section 1 of 6 in the unit.
- Day length (frames.csv): 40 minutes.
<!-- /skel -->

Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain, no sentence over 15 words; the teacher reads every source and exit aloud once. An answer may be a sentence, a labelled drawing, a filled table, or spoken at the side table. Each day runs 40 minutes. Every person in a case is a public person from a credited page, or invented and said aloud to be made up; the made-up places in this section are Oakridge (teaching) and Larkfield (exit only). Unit 1's question board, Return and repair, exit ticket, wonder card and "who made it, when and why" carry on as review.

- **D1.2.3-5, this section's share.** "Name the subject concepts tied to a big question that people can interpret in more than one way." Day 26 teaches fair rule-making read two ways (everyone votes on each rule; or chosen people study the choices and decide). Box C of the same record.
- **D2.Civ.3.3-5, this section's share.** "Examine where rules, laws and key parts of the U.S. Constitution came from and what they are for." Here only why groups make rules (safe, settle a disagreement, protect a freedom), day 26. A school rule and a town law traced to their problems: 2.3, day 33. Constitution parts: 2.5.
- **D2.Civ.1.3-5, this section's share.** "Tell apart what different government officials, levels and branches may do and must do, across times and places." Day 27: four levels and one leader at each, with power (may do) and duty (must do). Branches: 2.2.
- **MA.3.T1.3, this section's share.** "Explain why schools, towns, and cities have governments, what they do". Day 27 only: why, and what each level does. How local governments are organized and how people take part: 2.2 and 2.3.
- **Limits that bite here.** No child reports on home, family make-up, money or religion; families are many kinds. A child may pass in talk. Wrong ideas come from "someone might say" cards, never a named child. Cases name offices, never a real officeholder, party or campaign. The class is a group with rules, not a government. Not here: branches, funding, how our town is organized.
- **Connections.** Readiness items from day 24 (ss1-leaders, ss1-voting, ss1-group-roles), used day 26. Retrieval: ss1-elected-leaders and ss1-citizens (day 26); ss2-countries (day 27). D3.4 and D4.1 return on day 44.
- **Review, not new (the ledger's words).** Leader; big question, supporting question, question board; matters to others; two readings; government (leaders and workers who make and carry out a community's rules and provide what everyone uses); law (a rule for everyone in a place); tax; mayor, governor, President; citizen; responsibility; vote; election; right; country's government (grade 2).
- **New here.** Level of government (school, town, state, country); power and duty (what an official may do, what an official must do); freedom (something a person is free to do, such as speak, read or walk safely); representative (a person others choose to decide for them).
- **What the teacher page must explain.** The compelling question has no single answer; the unit builds evidence for the day 45 claim. A "government" is leaders and workers, not a building. Each level looks after needs of its own size; no level is higher in worth. Fair rule-making in two ways is a question of what each way costs, not a right and wrong pair. A rule that harms someone is still a rule; what makes it fair is argued from evidence from day 34.

**Exit questions.** If many miss one, the next day opens with that error's wrong example (field 4).
- **E25 (Larkfield Card, invented for practice).** "Larkfield Card, invented for practice. Why might Oskar care who decides bus 4's route?" Answer: Bus 4's route decides how Oskar gets to school, so a rule about it reaches him. A child who rides is affected too. Wrong: "I like buses." (W); "I do not ride a bus." (W); "Only grown-ups who vote care." (D).
- **E26 (Tanner Street Card, invented for practice).** "Tanner Street Card, invented. Name one group, besides shoppers, who might want a say." Answer: Any group the plan reaches, such as people who live on the street, drivers, or the shops on the street. "Tanner Street Card. Why might that group want a say?" Answer: A reason tied to the plan, such as cars cannot drive through, the street will be noisy, or more customers will come. "Park Card, invented. Bikes must stop for walkers. Why might the park make this rule?" Answer: To keep walkers safe, so no one is hit; a rule can keep people safe, settle a problem or protect a freedom. Wrong: "Nobody else." (W); "Only people old enough to vote." (D); "I like markets." (W); "Because they like markets." (W); "Nobody made it; rules were always there." (S); "Because the park likes being strict." (x).
- **E27 (State Card, invented for practice).** "State Card, invented. Why might the state, not one town, test the lake's water?" Answer: Ten towns share the lake, so the need is shared and no one town should test it for all. Each level looks after needs of its own size. Wrong: "The mayor tests it." (A); "Governments are only for people who vote." (D); "The state is more important." (A).

**Day 26 record (practice, kept for written feedback before day 46).** Copy one supporting question from the board. Box A: "This matters to ___ because ___." Box B: one fact the question needs. ## Day 25

- **7 min. Hook: our turn-taking rule.** Show the class's own rule for taking turns to speak, worded on a chart. Ask: "Who made it? Who can change it? Whose needs does it help?" Whole class by voice; a child may pass. Post the compelling question on the question board.
- **13 min. The town's record.** Read the Local Government Record, teaching edition, aloud (about 60 words). Before reading, say who made it, when and why (review: who made it, when and why). Pairs fill a two-column table, our class rule and our town's rules, with rows: who makes it, who can change it, who it reaches. Seven minutes writing or labelling; the teacher visits 8 pairs for about 1 minute each; two pairs share.
- **9 min. Whose needs count?** Six picture cards of invented Oakridge people near the market crossing: a runner, a shop owner, a crossing guard, a person pushing a stroller, a person using a wheelchair, a delivery driver. The teacher models one ("A crossing rule matters to the guard because ..."). Each pair picks a card and says one need a crossing rule should count. Two pairs share.
- **8 min. What do we wonder?** Each child writes one wonder about who decides, on a card (review: wonder card), kept for day 26. The teacher reads six aloud and groups them; a child may pass.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: rule, government, level, town, state, country, fair, power, duty. Frames: "A rule keeps ___ safe." "Fair could mean ___ or ___." "The ___ looks after ___." Accept home language, drawing with dictation or a pointed card.
- **Access.** Large print and tactile outlines; nested circles can be raised cutouts; pointing or a communication aid is an answer.
- **Check accommodations.** Read aloud once; a scribe writes the child's words without adding; spelling and handwriting are not judged.
- **Reading load.** The teacher reads the town record. Printed pages are at most 350 words, no sentence over 15 words.
- **Extension, same standard.** A second level's need that no other level could meet alone, or a third meaning of "fair".

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Read the town record together and fill the two columns. Sort the need cards onto four paper circles. Ask "Who else needs the answer to our question?" and write it down. The adult takes the second view in the fair-rule-making case.
- **One projector.** Show the record, the circles and the board; children mark paper copies, and the screen never replaces their own mark.
