# Section 2.5: Constitutions, our state's and the nation's

<!-- skel -->
Grade 3: Communities Then and Now. Unit 2, Who decides here (days 25 to 48): see [s02.md](s02.md). **Days 41 to 43.** Days by kind: understanding 2 (41, 43), application 1 (42). A plan, not a lesson: nothing here is built or tried with children. Fields follow UNIT-PLAN.md.

- Compelling question (frame): "Who should make the rules in our town?"
- Section 5 of 6 in the unit.
- Day length (frames.csv): 40 minutes.
<!-- /skel -->

Grade 3 children are early readers and writers. Printed stems are short and plain, no sentence over 15 words; the teacher reads every clause, source and exit aloud once and explains words without giving answers. An answer may be a sentence, a labelled drawing, a filled table or spoken at the side table. Each day runs 40 minutes. Real facts (a state's constitution, the US Constitution, the Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin's constitution) rest on the unit plan's field 10 pages and are marked "source to check" until sources.csv rows exist. The one invented place for teaching is Willowmere; the council in E41 is unnamed.

- **D2.Civ.5.3-5, this section's share.** "Explain where different systems of government came from, what they do and how they are built, including those in the U.S. and state constitutions." Parts: origins (day 43: delegates wrote the US plan in 1787 and the states ratified it; our state's adoption year from day 42); functions and structure (day 41: a plan that sets up a government and limits it; day 43: lawmakers, a leader, judges, and one other system). The US Constitution is a preview here; unit 6 completes it.
- **MA.3.T6.5, this section's share.** "Explain that states have governments too, and that the 1780 Massachusetts Constitution, mainly written by John Adams, is the oldest working constitution in the world and grants basic rights." General form: the class's own state. Day 41 states have plans; day 42 finds the year, one part and one right in our state's plan; Massachusetts 1780 or Colorado 1876 is the worked example (whichever is not our state). The "oldest" claim is said as "often called" (Wikipedia, as a last resort: among the oldest functioning written constitutions; no rival claim is named).
- **Limits that bite here.** The speech safeguard is introductory: Congress proposed it, the states ratified it, it protects speaking freely; no court cases, no detail on other amendments. A constitution is read from the official page; no legal advice. A nation is named by its own name and described in the present tense as it sets itself up; no three-branch template assumed. No costume, sacred material, identity simulation or child as spokesperson; no child is asked their heritage. Constitutions change; historic promises did not give equal rights in practice (unit 6). Not here: how a bill becomes a law, national powers in detail, informed action.
- **Connections.** The three jobs and branch labels from day 28 (2.2), and the state level from day 27. Day 41 reviews grade 1's Constitution (a written plan for the government, 1787). Rights come with responsibilities (day 34): a right is not a prize (M).
- **Review, not new (the ledger's words).** Government; law; branch (one part of a government with one kind of job); legislature; right; responsibility; level of government (review of social-studies-grade-3 u02-1 and u02-2); the Constitution as a written plan for the government (review of social-studies-grade-1 u06-2); nation (a people with their own government; review of social-studies-grade-3 u02-2); Return and repair, exit ticket, the question board, written cue on a record then a revision (review of social-studies-grade-3 u02-3).
- **New here.** Constitution (a basic plan that sets up a government, says what it may and may not do, and may list rights); ratify (the states say yes so the plan counts); Congress (the national legislature); amendment (a part added later; named only, taught in unit 6); delegate (a person chosen to speak and act for a group); judiciary (the part of a government with judges and courts).
- **What the teacher page must explain.** A constitution is a plan, not a list of rules; it sets up who does what and what may not be done. "It was always there" removes the makers (AD). States differ; ours is read from its own official page. Menominee is a nation with its own constitution, not a town (I) and not a copy of a state (U); its government governs today (T). If restoration comes up, say plainly: federal policy tried to end recognition and members worked to restore it; the people and their governance did not begin with recognition. A sensitivity read must be finished before day 43 runs.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: constitution, plan, government, right, ratify, delegate, Congress, judge, court. Frames: "A constitution says ___ may and may not ___." "___ proposed it. The states ___ it." "In their plan the ___ makes laws." Accept home language, drawing or a pointed card.
- **Access.** Large print; strips and baskets as raised or cut shapes; pointing, speech or dictation is an answer.
- **Check accommodations.** Read aloud once; a scribe writes the child's words; spelling is not judged.
- **Reading load.** The teacher reads every clause and summary; no child reads alone more than a card of 40 words, no sentence over 15 words.
- **Extension, same standard.** Compare a second state's adoption note, or find a second right in our state's plan.

## 10. Two rooms
