## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why is our town where it is?"
- **Hook, day 1:** a public-landmark photograph beside its map symbol; read the short local biography. Boston worked case: Frederick Law Olmsted and the Back Bay Fens. Ask why people changed this place.
- **Co-write on day 2:** Where are we on Earth (day 6)? What does our town map reveal (10)? How did land and water help place towns (15)? How have people changed this place (18)? Whose needs does a good location serve (20)?
- **Closing, day 21:** answer the compelling question from Local Current Map and Local Historical Map, with a feature, a human choice and evidence from both. Answers may emphasize travel, resources or later changes; maps alone do not prove motives.
- **Teaching:** model maker/date/purpose before source use (7), compare map and photograph (19), rehearse claims orally. Students choose evidence (20 to 21). Track supporting answers and unanswered questions publicly.

## 5. End-of-unit assessment, whole

Day 23 (End-of-unit assessment): End-of-unit assessment, from a town map and a source not seen before: locate and describe the town, one physical feature and one landmark using title, legend, directions and whole-bar scale; draw a small labelled map of a given place; give a place's location by continent, hemisphere and grid and say why it is warm or cold, wet or dry; name our region's states and one river, coast or mountain range and how it placed a town; explain one land change and how one group's culture shaped it, from an early and a modern map; say why the unit question matters to others, write one supporting question with the facts it needs and one idea people read two ways, and say how it helps answer the big one; a short claim with evidence from a named source
Note (days.csv): every part of each listed code asked once; the D1 prompts may be judged from the unit's question work (days 2 and 20) with the unit rubric

**Administration:** day 23: instructions/source orientation 5 minutes, unaided items 30, short-item marking 5. Q9 to Q12 are collected individually during days 2 and 20, then judged here, not repeated in the thirty minutes. Map labels may be pointed to. Extended responses total about six short sentences, with Q13/Q14 one shared argument, scored for different parts.

**Withheld packet, specified for production.** L: an unseen current official map of the student's own town, with title, north, preserved scale, a named physical feature and historic public landmark; pair with an unseen historical map of the same extent and a dated landmark caption. Before use, teacher prepares a factual answer overlay. No invented local history. Where a local historical map is unavailable, retain the actual current-town task and use a sourced worked map pair for change, rather than a fictional town.

G: an original instruction card, explicitly a model, not an authentic source: north up, columns A to C west to east, rows 1 to 3 south to north, one square = 10 metres; entrance A1, pond A3, pavilion C3. Supply a blank grid. W: world outline, equator/prime meridian and grid, Nairobi marked C2 (Africa, south/east), Stockholm B1 (Europe, north/east). C: invented climate cards, lowland P near equator warm/wet, lowland Q near equator warm/dry, R near North Pole cold. These are simplified models, not measured climate records.

R: fresh US political, North America physical and satellite-view panels, state-name bank and five-region key. Classroom convention: Northeast, ME NH VT MA RI CT NY NJ PA DE MD; Southeast, VA WV KY TN NC SC GA FL AL MS AR LA; Midwest, OH MI IN IL WI MN IA MO ND SD NE KS; Southwest, OK TX NM AZ; West, MT WY CO ID UT NV WA OR CA AK HI. New England is ME NH VT MA RI CT, a subset of the Northeast. R's worked Northeast panel labels Connecticut River, Lake Champlain, Atlantic shoreline, Cape Cod, Massachusetts Bay and Appalachian Mountains. Include corresponding features, or absence of coast, in the child's region. Different layouts and feature questions are used in teaching. L/R images must be agency-owned or openly licensed, with credits preserved, not unlicensed basemap images.

P1: Penobscot Nation Tribal Timeline's 2004 restoration-partnership entry, with maker/title/date. P2: the unseen NOAA Fisheries report, *Building Bridges to Restore Connectivity*, its contemporary project map/photograph and this original read-aloud summary: The Penobscot Nation values the river as a relative and a source of food. Road pipes can block fish. The Nation replaced pipes with bridges at two stream crossings so fish could pass and roads could still cross. P1 is the Nation's published record; P2 supplies project details and the attributed cultural relationship. Hold back these records; teaching uses other examples. No sacred narrative. Read sources without explaining the answers.
