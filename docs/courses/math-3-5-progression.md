# Mathematics, grades 3 to 5: the progression around grade 4

**What this is.** Layer 4 of the Curio Class foundation: how grade 4 mathematics joins grade 3 behind it and grade 5 ahead. Unit plans take field 2 ("Builds on, leads to") and field 4 (the readiness check) from this page.

**Status.** Second draft, 2026-11-11, revised after a cold read by the curriculum expert (docs/reviews/math-3-5-progression-2026-11-11.md). Four points wait for Cory. Each is marked "Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes": stretch 7 (decimals), stretch 8 (rounding), stretch 11 (numbers in the pilot) and section 3 (facts check).

**How to read it.**

- "Prog. p. 149 (143)" means page 149 of the Progressions PDF, printed page 143. "Std. p. 24" is a page of the official standards PDF. Words in quotation marks beside a code are the standard's own.
- **Strong** or **Weak** after "Grade 3 hands it" says how much grade 3 really gives; test a weak handoff hardest. Tags on stretches 2, 4, 5, 6, 14 and 17 are the writer's; the rest are the expert's.
- Readiness items: **W** is written and can be marked; **T** means the teacher listens or watches. At most four items and one T per check. "This year" marks an item on an earlier grade 4 stretch, so the reteach is that unit, not a grade 3 tool.
- A "Practice" line appears only where the Progressions name one.

## 1. Stretch by stretch

### Stretch 1. Factors and Multiples (days 1 to 6; 4.OA.B.4)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** all one-digit products from memory and division within 100 (3.OA.C.7); division as an unknown-factor problem (3.OA.B.6); arrays and rectangles with whole-number sides (3.OA.A.3, 3.MD.C.7). Limit: products within 100.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes ten one-digit products and ten matching quotients (3.OA.C.7). (2) W: rewrites 24 ÷ 3 = ? as 3 × ? = 24 and solves it (3.OA.B.6). (3) W: draws every rectangle with area 12 on grid paper (3.MD.C.7).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** multiples and factors to find common denominators (5.NF.A.1); more thinly, multiples language for powers of 10 (5.NBT.A.2; Prog. p. 74 (68)).
- **Limits in grade 4:** numbers 1 to 100. No greatest common factor or least common multiple (6.NS.B.4). No prime factorization.
- **Hard here:** finding all pairs needs a system, searching until the pairs reverse. Students "need not be fluent" in it (Prog. p. 45 (39)).

### Stretch 2. Number Patterns (days 7 to 9; 4.OA.C.5)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** patterns in the addition and multiplication tables, explained with properties (3.OA.D.9); skip counting from grade 2 (2.NBT.A.2).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes the next four terms of 6, 12, 18 (2.NBT.A.2, 3.OA.D.9). (2) T: says why 4 times a number is always even (3.OA.D.9). (3) W: circles the even numbers in a list up to 20 (2.OA.C.3).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** generating terms from a rule, to compare two patterns (5.OA.B.3).
- **Limits:** the rule is given. The standards "do not require students to infer or guess the underlying rule" (Prog. p. 46 (40)). No ordered pairs, no graphing.
- **The work here:** finding features the rule did not state, and saying why (Prog. pp. 45 to 46 (39 to 40)). The "100th shape" kind of question needs remainders; hold it for stretch 13.
- **Practice:** MP.7 and MP.8, structure and what repeats.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 1, for patterns in multiples.

### Stretch 3. Equivalent Fractions and Comparing (days 10 to 24; 4.NF.A.1, A.2)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** a/b as a parts of size 1/b (3.NF.A.1); fractions on a number line (3.NF.A.2); simple equivalents such as 1/2 = 2/4, whole numbers as fractions, comparing with the same numerator or same denominator (3.NF.A.3a to d). Limit: denominators 2, 3, 4, 6, 8. From grades 1 and 2: equal shares and the words halves, thirds, fourths (1.G.A.3, 2.G.A.3); the number line as a measurement model (2.MD.B.6); unit size against count (2.MD.A.2). Grade 3 leans on length models and number lines more than circles (Prog. p. 146 (140)), so draw readiness items as strips and lines.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: places 3/4 and 5/4 on a number line marked 0, 1, 2 (3.NF.A.2). (2) W: circles the larger of 1/3 and 1/8 and shades a strip for each (3.NF.A.3d). (3) T: two pizzas of different sizes are drawn; is half of one equal to half of the other? Why? (3.NF.A.3d). (4) W: writes 3 as 3/1 and 1 as 4/4 (3.NF.A.3c).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** equivalent fractions made on purpose, to add unlike denominators (5.NF.A.1); benchmark comparing, to judge answers (5.NF.A.2). Unit-fraction language, "3 fifths is 3 pieces of size one fifth", read "two thirds", not "two over three" or "two out of three" (Prog. p. 140 (134)): 5.NF.B.4 and B.7 are built on it. The terms proper and improper are not needed (Prog. p. 146 (140)). The number line, because 5.G.A.1 puts two at right angles (Prog. p. 134 (128)).
- **Limits:** grade 4 denominators only. No simplest form and no least common denominator required (Prog. pp. 149 to 150 (143 to 144)). Explain equivalence by cutting each part into n smaller parts, not by "multiplying by 1" as n/n; that argument needs grade 5 (Prog. p. 150 (144)). The denominator list limits the fractions given in tasks; a common denominator found while working may be outside it (same page).
- **Hard here:** numerator and denominator both grow while the pieces get smaller (Prog. p. 149 (143)). On number lines, students count tick marks, not lengths (Prog. p. 141 (135)). Comparing to 1 by how far each fraction is from 1 (Prog. p. 150 (144), margin).
- **Practice:** MP.3, explaining why two fractions are equivalent.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 1 (multiples).

### Stretch 4. Adding and Subtracting Fractions (days 25 to 38; 4.NF.B.3a to d)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** fractions built from unit fractions (3.NF.A.1); number line (3.NF.A.2); whole numbers as fractions, which mixed numbers rest on (3.NF.A.3c; Prog. p. 151 (145)). Grade 3 does not add fractions.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes the next three terms of 1/4, 2/4, 3/4 (3.NF.A.1, A.2). (2) W: writes 2 as a number of fourths (3.NF.A.3c). (3) W, this year: writes a fraction equal to 2/3 (4.NF.A.1, stretch 3). (4) W: ten sums and differences within 20 (2.OA.B.2).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** like-denominator adding and mixed numbers, the last step of every unlike-denominator sum (5.NF.A.1, A.2).
- **Limits:** like denominators only. The one exception is tenths plus hundredths, in stretch 7 (4.NF.C.5); unlike denominators in general are "not a requirement at this grade" (Std. p. 31, footnote 4, attached to 4.NF.C.5). Turning a mixed number into a fraction is addition, not a separate rule; write the plus sign early, as 2 + 3/4 (Prog. p. 151 (145), margin).
- **Hard here:** adding tops and adding bottoms; seeing a fraction as a sum of unit fractions prevents it (Prog. p. 150 (144), margin). Subtracting from a mixed number needs a one broken into parts (Prog. p. 152 (146)). Problems where the two fractions refer to different wholes cannot be added; include one (Prog. p. 153 (147)).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 3.

### Stretch 5. Line Plots (days 39 to 41; 4.MD.B.4)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** measuring to halves and fourths of an inch and plotting on a line plot (3.MD.B.4); the number line as a measurement model (2.MD.B.6).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: reads three lengths off a pictured ruler to the nearest quarter inch (3.MD.B.4). (2) W: reads a line plot: how many, most common (3.MD.B.4). (3) W, this year: finds 5/8 + 2/8 and 7/8 − 3/8 (4.NF.B.3, stretch 4). (4) W, this year: writes 1/2 and 3/4 as eighths (4.NF.A.1, stretch 3).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** the same plots for all fraction operations (5.MD.B.2); the number line, as in stretch 3.
- **Limits:** scale in halves, fourths, eighths. Adding and subtracting only. Where a problem mixes them, label the scale in eighths (Prog. p. 89 (83)).
- **Hard here:** the scale is a number line, so the tick-mark error from stretch 3 returns.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretches 3 and 4.

### Stretch 6. Multiplying a Fraction by a Whole Number (days 44 to 51; 4.NF.B.4a to c)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** 5 × 3 as five groups of three (3.OA.A.1); unit fractions (3.NF.A.1).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: draws 4 × 6 as 4 groups of 6 (3.OA.A.1). (2) W, this year: writes 3/5 as a sum of unit fractions (4.NF.B.3b, stretch 4). (3) W, this year: writes 7/4 as a mixed number (4.NF.B.3c, stretch 4).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** equal groups of a fraction, extended to fraction times fraction (5.NF.B.4) and scaling (5.NF.B.5); the unit-fraction language of stretch 3.
- **Limits:** whole number times fraction only. Multiplying by a fraction ("1/3 of 12") is grade 5 (Prog. p. 44 (38)).
- **Hard here:** seeing 3 × 2/5 as 6 × 1/5 (4.NF.B.4b); a whole number times a mixed number, done with the distributive property (Prog. p. 154 (148)); "times as much" when the quantity is not a count (Prog. p. 48 (42) and p. 144 (138)). Teaching note, not from the Progressions: watch for students multiplying both top and bottom, a carry-over from stretch 3.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 4.

### Stretch 7. Decimals (days 52 to 61; 4.NF.C.5 to C.7)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** very little directly. Tenths and hundredths are outside grade 3's denominators. The number line (3.NF.A.2) and money (2.MD.C.8) carry over.
- **Readiness.** (1) W, this year: writes 3/10 as hundredths (4.NF.A.1, stretch 3). (2) W, this year: finds 3/10 + 4/10 (4.NF.B.3, stretch 4). (3) W: circles the larger of 3/8 and 5/8 (3.NF.A.3d). (4) W: writes how many dimes and how many cents make a dollar (2.MD.C.8).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** decimals as fractions over 10 or 100, to reach thousandths (5.NBT.A.3) and explain decimal arithmetic (5.NBT.B.7); the number line, as in stretch 3.
- **Limits:** tenths and hundredths. Comparing, not computing, in decimal notation. Adding is done as fractions (4.NF.C.5), with dimes and cents (Prog. p. 154 (148)).
- **The decimals rule. Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes.** For this stretch and stretch 15. Decimal notation may be used; computing in it waits for grade 5 (Prog. p. 89 (83); 5.NBT.B.7).
  1. **May appear:** decimals to hundredths as given quantities (2.5 km, $3.75, 0.4 L).
  2. **May be done with them:** read, write, compare, place on a number line; rename as a fraction with denominator 10 or 100; rename in a smaller unit as a whole number ($3.75 is 375 cents; 2.5 km is 2,500 m).
  3. **Computing:** every sum, difference, product or quotient is done on whole numbers in the smaller unit, or on fractions with denominators 10 and 100. The answer may then be written back as a decimal.
  4. **Never in grade 4:** lining up decimal points in a written algorithm; a decimal times or divided by anything; converting a smaller unit to a larger one where the answer is a decimal (that is 5.MD.A.1).
  5. **Test for a task:** if a student could only get the answer by computing in decimal notation, the task is grade 5.
- **Hard here:** 0.2 against 0.09, read as "2 against 9"; rewrite as 20/100 and 9/100 (Prog. p. 155 (149)). Students expect a "oneths" place, as if the point were the centre (Prog. pp. 69 to 70 (63 to 64)).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretches 3 and 4.

### Stretch 8. Place Value to a Million (days 62 to 71; 4.NBT.A.1 to A.3)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** rounding to the nearest 10 or 100 (3.NBT.A.1); one-digit times multiples of 10 (3.NBT.A.3). The base is grade 2: reading, writing and comparing three-digit numbers (2.NBT.A.1 to A.4).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes how many hundreds, tens and ones are in 706 (2.NBT.A.1). (2) W: compares two three-digit numbers with >, =, < (2.NBT.A.4). (3) W: rounds 368 to the nearest 10 and 100 (3.NBT.A.1). (4) W: fills in "10 tens make ___; 10 hundreds make ___" (2.NBT.A.1a).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** "ten times the place to its right", to add "1/10 of the place to its left" and reach decimals (5.NBT.A.1, A.2).
- **Limits:** whole numbers to 1,000,000.
- **Rounding, unit 8.3. Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes.** Keep three days and re-aim the third. Day 1: rounding on the number line to the leftmost place. Day 2: any place, including a middle place and the "round up into a new place" case. Day 3: rounding used to estimate and check sums and differences, which is what 4.OA.A.3 asks for and what stretch 9 begins. Rounding beyond the leftmost one or two places "need not be practiced" (Prog. p. 69 (63), margin); a middle place is the harder case (Prog. p. 68 (62)).
- **Hard here:** reading large numbers by their comma groups (Prog. p. 69 (63)).
- **Practice:** MP.7 and MP.8, in the tenfold pattern.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 7, for the tenfold pattern.

### Stretch 9. Adding and Subtracting Multi-Digit Numbers (days 72 to 77; 4.NBT.B.4)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** fluent adding and subtracting within 1,000 by any sound method (3.NBT.A.2).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: adds and subtracts three-digit numbers with regrouping, without drawings (3.NBT.A.2). (2) W: finds 402 − 178 (3.NBT.A.2). (3) W, this year: writes a six-digit number from its name in words (4.NBT.A.2, stretch 8).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** the same algorithm for decimals (5.NBT.B.7).
- **Limits:** to 1,000,000. This is the one place grade 4 requires the standard algorithm.
- **Hard here:** little is new. "This extension does not have to take a long time" (Prog. p. 70 (64)).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 8.

### Stretch 10. Multiplicative Comparison (days 80 to 88; 4.OA.A.1, A.2, part of A.3)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** equal groups, arrays and measurement problems (3.OA.A.1, A.3); two-step problems with a letter (3.OA.D.8). Comparison language is new: grade 3 has no "times as many" problems (Prog. p. 37 (31); Std. p. 25, footnote 7, for measurement), and the base is grade 2 additive comparison (2.OA.A.1). Order of operations without parentheses is expected from grade 3 (Std. p. 23, footnote 3); evaluating expressions with grouping symbols is grade 5 (5.OA.A.1).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: solves an equal-groups problem with the group size unknown (3.OA.A.3, A.4). (2) W: solves a "how many more" problem (2.OA.A.1). (3) W: writes an equation with a letter for a two-step problem (3.OA.D.8).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** "times as much", to read multiplication as scaling (5.NF.B.5) and to write expressions (5.OA.A.2).
- **Limits:** whole numbers. "One third as much" is grade 5 (Prog. p. 38 (32), Table 3 note; p. 44 (38)). No remainders in this stretch's two-step problems.
- **Hard here:** the language, "times as many" against "more than". Use "times as much" and "times as many" in tasks and checks; talk with students about why "times more than" is unclear (Prog. p. 44 (38)).
- **Practice:** MP.4, writing an equation for the situation (Prog. p. 112 (106), margin).

### Stretch 11. Area and Perimeter Formulas, pilot (days 89 to 93; 4.MD.A.3)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** area as rows times columns, rectilinear figures (3.MD.C.7a to d); perimeter with an unknown side (3.MD.D.8); unknown-factor equations (3.OA.A.4). From grade 2: rows and columns of squares in a rectangle (2.G.A.2).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: finds the area of a 6 by 7 rectangle and writes the multiplication (3.MD.C.7a). (2) W: finds the perimeter of a rectangle with two sides labelled (3.MD.D.8). (3) W: solves 8 × ? = 48 (3.OA.A.4). (4) W: draws two rectangles with perimeter 12 and different areas (3.MD.D.8).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** area of the base, for V = b × h (5.MD.C.5); fractional side lengths (5.NF.B.4b).
- **Limits:** rectangles. To "apply the formula" does not mean to plug into a memorized one (Prog. p. 112 (106)).
- **Numbers on these days. Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes.** Keep day 89, before multi-digit multiplication and division. Days 89 to 93: areas are products within 100, or a one-digit number times a multiple of 10. Unknown-side area problems are factor-pair problems (4.OA.B.4). Perimeter may use multi-digit addition (4.NBT.B.4) and like-denominator fractions (4.NF.B.3d). Area with larger sides returns in units 12 and 13 as the context for partial products and for division, and in stretch 14 problems. Without that return 4.MD.A.3 is half taught.
- **Hard here:** adding one length and one width for perimeter (Prog. pp. 106 (100) and 111 (105)).

### Stretch 12. Multi-Digit Multiplication (days 94 to 105; 4.NBT.B.5)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** facts (3.OA.C.7); the distributive property, used (3.OA.B.5); one-digit times multiples of 10 (3.NBT.A.3); area of a rectangle with a split side (3.MD.C.7c).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes ten one-digit products (3.OA.C.7). (2) W: writes 6 × 70 as a number of tens and as a product (3.NBT.A.3). (3) W: splits 7 × 14 into 7 × 10 and 7 × 4 on an area drawing (3.MD.C.7c). (4) W, this year: writes 4,385 in expanded form (4.NBT.A.2, stretch 8).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** partial products understood well enough to shorten into the standard algorithm (5.NBT.B.5). The area model itself, which 5.NBT.B.5, 5.NBT.B.7 and 5.NF.B.4b reuse (Prog. p. 39 (33) and p. 143 (137)); keep one area picture from stretch 11 through stretch 12.
- **Limits:** up to four digits by one digit, and two digits by two digits. No "fluently" in the standard: build efficiency, do not time it (section 3).
- **Hard here:** two-digit by two-digit has four partial products and students can lose track of them. Short methods hide what the digits mean (Prog. p. 71 (65)). Products like 5 × 60 carry an "extra" zero (same page).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretches 8, 9, 11.

### Stretch 13. Division with Remainders (days 106 to 117; 4.NBT.B.6)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** division as unknown factor (3.OA.B.6); sharing and grouping (3.OA.A.2); facts (3.OA.C.7). Grade 3 has no remainders.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: writes ten division facts within 100 (3.OA.C.7). (2) W: finds the largest multiple of 7 that is not more than 45 (3.OA.C.7; Prog. p. 72 (66)). (3) W: computes 4 × 8 + 3 (3.OA.D.8; Std. p. 23, footnote 3). (4) W, this year: finds 6 × 300 (4.NBT.B.5, stretch 12).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** place-by-place division, for two-digit divisors (5.NBT.B.6). Writing 84 = 8 × 10 + 4, because 5.NF.B.3 turns the remainder into a fraction (Prog. p. 157 (151)).
- **Limits:** one-digit divisors, dividends to four digits. Fluency with the standard division algorithm is grade 6 (6.NS.B.2). What a remainder means is 4.OA.A.3, carried by unit 13.3.
- **Hard here:** zeros in the dividend or the quotient, and a zero in a remainder part way through (Prog. p. 72 (66), margin). Writing "84 ÷ 10 = 8 R 4" misuses the equal sign; write 84 = 8 × 10 + 4 (Prog. p. 45 (39); also p. 72 (66), margin).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 12.

### Stretch 14. Multistep Problems (days 120 to 123; 4.OA.A.3 in full)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** two-step problems, an equation with a letter, and checking by estimating (3.OA.D.8). Order of operations without parentheses is expected from grade 3 (Std. p. 23, footnote 3); grouping symbols are grade 5 (5.OA.A.1).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: solves a two-step problem and writes its equation (3.OA.D.8). (2) W: computes 20 − 3 × 4 correctly (Std. p. 23, footnote 3). (3) W: rounds to estimate a sum (3.NBT.A.1; 4.NBT.A.3, stretch 8). (4) W, this year: divides with a remainder (4.NBT.B.6, stretch 13).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** the same habits with fractions and decimals (5.NF.A.2, B.6; 5.NBT.B.7).
- **Limits:** whole numbers and whole-number answers. No more than three steps, with easy or medium addition and subtraction (Prog. p. 44 (38)).
- **Hard here:** deciding what a remainder means in the story: drop it, round up, or it is the answer (Prog. p. 45 (39)).
- **Practice:** MP.4, writing an equation for the situation (Prog. p. 112 (106), margin).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretches 9, 10, 12, 13.

### Stretch 15. Measurement Conversions (days 124 to 131; 4.MD.A.1, A.2)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** time to the minute; grams, kilograms, liters; one-step problems (3.MD.A.1, A.2). The base for length is grade 2: inches, feet, centimeters, meters (2.MD.A.1 to A.4), with unit size against count (2.MD.A.2), and money (2.MD.C.8). New in grade 4: km, lb, oz, ml, seconds, and every conversion.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: circles the better unit: a paperclip weighs about 1 gram or 1 kilogram (3.MD.A.2). (2) W: finds the minutes from 2:40 to 3:15 (3.MD.A.1). (3) W, this year: fills in "a meter is ___ times as long as a centimeter" (4.OA.A.1, stretch 10). (4) W, this year: finds 7 × 1,000 and 6 × 12 (4.NBT.B.5, stretch 12).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** unit sizes and the times-as-much link, to convert both ways with decimals (5.MD.A.1).
- **Limits:** larger unit to smaller unit only, within one system. 4.MD.A.2 allows "simple fractions or decimals": the decimals rule in stretch 7 governs every money and metric problem here (Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes).
- **Hard here:** the smaller the unit, the more of them it takes, first met in grade 2 (2.MD.A.2; Prog. p. 103 (97)). Two-column tables show the "times as much" link between units (Prog. p. 110 (104)).
- **Practice:** MP.7 and MP.8, in the two-column tables (Prog. p. 110 (104)).
- **Earlier in this year:** stretches 6, 7, 10, 12, 13.

### Stretch 16. Lines and Angles (days 132 to 143; 4.G.A.1; 4.MD.C.5 to C.7)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Weak):** shapes sorted by their sides and angles, informally (3.G.A.1 and the grade 3 opening, Std. p. 21); "square corner" is grade 1 and 2 language (Prog. p. 125 (119)), and "right angle" may be new as a term. A fraction of a circle is grade 2 (1.G.A.3, 2.G.A.3). Angle measure is new. This is a weak handoff: test it.
- **Readiness.** (1) W: marks the square corners on a drawn rectangle and a tilted square (informal; no grade 3 code). (2) W: shades a quarter of one circle and a half of another (2.G.A.3). (3) W: finds the length from 3 to 8 on a number line (2.MD.B.6). (4) W: finds 360 − 125 and 90 − 35 (3.NBT.A.2).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** perpendicular lines for the coordinate axes (5.G.A.1); angle size and parallel sides for sorting figures (5.G.B.3, B.4).
- **Limits:** whole-number degrees. Unknown angles are found by adding and subtracting parts, including the parts of a right angle (90), a straight line (180) and a full turn (360), and the angles where two lines cross (4.MD.C.7; Prog. pp. 113 to 115 (107 to 109)). The words complementary and supplementary may be used but are not assessed. Using these facts as rules in multi-step equations is grade 7 (7.G.B.5).
- **Hard here:** judging an angle by the length between the endpoints of its arms; not seeing a right angle when it is tilted; taking any slanted line as 45 degrees; believing a protractor must sit flat (Prog. pp. 113 to 114 (107 to 108)). Teaching note, not from the Progressions: reading the wrong protractor scale.
- **Practice:** MP.5 and MP.6, with the protractor.

### Stretch 17. Shapes and Symmetry (days 144 to 147; 4.G.A.2, A.3)

- **Grade 3 hands it (Strong):** categories and subcategories of quadrilaterals by attributes (3.G.A.1).
- **Readiness.** (1) W: sorts six drawn quadrilaterals and writes one attribute a group shares (3.G.A.1). (2) W: draws a quadrilateral that is not a rectangle, rhombus or square (3.G.A.1). (3) W, this year: marks parallel sides, perpendicular sides and a right, an acute and an obtuse angle on drawn figures (4.G.A.1, stretch 16).
- **Grade 5 needs from it:** classifying by properties, for the hierarchy (5.G.B.3, B.4).
- **Limits:** the standard names only right triangles as a category. The Progressions also discuss sorting triangles by sides and angles (Prog. p. 132 (126)); optional; keep it out of checks. No hierarchy statements yet.
- **Hard here (teaching note, not from the Progressions):** a slanted parallelogram looks symmetric but fails the fold test in 4.G.A.3; have students fold or use a mirror before they answer.
- **Earlier in this year:** stretch 16.

### Stretch 18. The Year Together (day 148)

No new standard. What grade 5 needs most from the year, inferred from the grade 5 critical areas (Std. p. 33): fraction equivalence and like-denominator arithmetic, multi-digit multiplication and division by place value, and tenths and hundredths.

## 2. Limits by grade, strand by strand

## 3. Fluency and facts across the three grades

The spine's fluency plan covers 3.OA.C.7, 3.NBT.A.2 and 4.NBT.B.4. On either side of it:

| Grade | The standard says | Code |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "Fluently add and subtract within 20" | 2.OA.B.2 |
| 5 | "Fluently multiply multi-digit whole numbers using the standard algorithm" | 5.NBT.B.5 |
| 6 | "Fluently divide multi-digit numbers using the standard algorithm" | 6.NS.B.2 |

Grade 4 has one fluency standard, 4.NBT.B.4. Multiplication (4.NBT.B.5) has no "fluently" in the standard, but the grade opening asks for growing fluency and efficient procedures (Std. p. 27): build efficiency, do not time it. Factor pairs and division carry no fluency requirement.

**What the grade 4 daily warm-up should carry:**

1. **Multiplication and division facts, all year.** Everything from stretch 1 to stretch 15 leans on them. Start with a check, not an assumption (see below).
2. **Adding and subtracting within 1,000, days 1 to 71**, by any method; **the standard algorithm with larger numbers from day 72** (4.NBT.B.4).
3. **One-digit times multiples of 10, 100, 1,000** from about day 62 (3.NBT.A.3, then 4.NBT.B.5). It feeds stretches 12, 13, 15.
4. **Fraction and decimal sense** after stretch 7: equivalents, comparing to 1/2 and 1, tenths and hundredths. This is upkeep, not a fluency standard.
5. **Multi-digit multiplication and division** after day 118, as practice. Do not time it; fluency is grades 5 and 6.

This matches the spine's fluency plan. The only additions are items 1 (check first) and 3.

**The facts check. Proposed, waiting for Cory's yes.** Keep the spine's label "grade 3 maintenance" for times tables, and add: days 1 to 6 include a facts check; students below the mark get the grade 3 fact tools and print during warm-ups, and the check repeats before day 94. The help lives in warm-ups, not in lesson days. Reaching fact fluency may take much time, and extra time and support are essential where needed (Prog. p. 37 (31)).
