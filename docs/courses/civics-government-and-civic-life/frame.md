# Course frame: Civics: Government and Civic Life

Course id us-hist-8 (id kept from the old list), grade 8, draft frame (2026-11-13). The scope and sequence sets the
days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 10 units, in the anchor's topic order (8.T1 to 8.T7) with two moves named below, ending with the
  year's civic action.
- **Days.** 160 teaching days: 148 in units, 12 for course-level checks and respond days (three checks of 2 days,
  each followed by 2 respond days). A full year, one period a day, five days a week; the scope and sequence states
  the minutes (the pick in "For the lead" is 45).
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 grade 8 (MA.8.T1 to MA.8.T7) is the content anchor: every one of its 56 codes
  sits with one of the 27 grade 8 progression rows, so every code sits in one unit below. Parent codes carry their
  lettered parts where the rows cite only the parent: MA.8.T5.6 carries 6a to 6c (unit 6: each student researches
  one area, as the standard asks, and the class covers all three), and MA.8.T6.8 carries 8a to 8c (unit 7, taught
  for the student's own state). C3 6-8 civics indicators carry the skills; D2.Civ.9.6-8 is owned in grade 7 (one
  procedure) and here (ss8-civic-action compares procedures), each with its depth line. The check set: California,
  Tennessee and New York teach civics as a high school course, so each unit plan reads their government courses
  at grade 8 depth and records any addition with its source; the "Check set adds" column names the candidates this
  frame found, from the writer's reading, each to be confirmed in that unit's comparison table. Check-set names
  never appear in student, teacher or family copy. The values rule governs: (a) where a higher court has ruled, the
  course sides with it (the later ruling stands where the Court overruled itself); (b) backed sources decide claims;
  (c) dignity and openness, LGBTQ people where history includes them (the 2015 marriage ruling in unit 6); (d) the
  age of the student.
- **"Your state" (bar item 13).** The anchor's Massachusetts government topic (8.T6) is taught as a method for the
  student's own state: its constitution, declaration of rights, government, local bodies and budget, with worked
  examples from several states (Massachusetts one of them, never the subject) and a link to each state's own pages.
  Unit 7 carries it; units 8 and 9 use the state constitution's press and petition protections the same way.
- **Base text.** None (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md: every day is Curio Class's own). Partial bases that may serve a
  unit, checked item by item: the retired OpenStax American Government 3e (CC BY, college level, so rewritten for
  grade 8) for background in units 3, 4, 7 and 8; Washington OSPI's Since Time Immemorial middle school unit 1C
  (CC BY; the Medicine Creek Treaty of 1854), after the sensitivity read and keeping its content design, as the
  worked example of a Native nation's government and treaty rights in unit 7 (and background in unit 1); National Archives (DocsTeach,
  CC0 activities) and the founding documents themselves (public domain) in units 2, 3 and 6; court opinions (public
  domain) in units 3 and 6. The frame maps no base lesson.
- **The inquiry arc.** Every unit has a compelling question (the table) with supporting questions set by the unit
  plan, runs the C3 arc, and ends with an end-of-unit assessment that includes a task from an unseen source.
  Content is taught directly: foundations, founding, structure, then participation, rights, the levels of
  government, change and the press.
- **Founding documents lens.** The Declaration, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights are read seven times across
  Curio Class (ELA 8, this course, ELA 9, ELA 11, US History I, ELA 12, Government). Here the lens is what each
  document does: its claims, its structure and powers, and what it left out; rhetoric belongs to ELA.
- **Informed action.** Unit 10, the year's last: the class deliberates on a school or community problem, compares
  deliberative procedures, chooses an action itself and presents to an audience beyond the class. Action is the
  student's choice informed by evidence, never a cause the course picks. The teacher books the audience by the end
  of unit 8 (about day 130); the presentation falls by about day 157; the last day is the look-back, which records
  the answer received or what is still pending and what the class would do next.
- **Writing from sources.** A claim weighed against counterclaims in unit 1; sourced short pieces in units 2 to 5;
  the year's longest research argument in unit 6 (court opinions); an editorial analysis in unit 9; a public
  proposal in unit 10.
- **Party and family neutrality.** Examples come from several parties and eras; students never say whom they or
  their families support. Classes include non-citizens and mixed-status families: no question asks about a
  student's or family's status.
- **Reading level.** Curio Class's own texts at grade 8 level; founding documents and opinions as short excerpts
  with plain-word glosses, and a stated difficulty; each source has a rights line.
- **Sensitive content.** How the Constitution protected slavery, and slavery as the central cause of secession,
  named plainly; Dred Scott's language summarized, not printed; Jehovah's Witness families' persecution named
  plainly; Native nations in the present tense with sacred or restricted knowledge left out; civil rights and the
  2015 marriage ruling taught from the record. Every unit gets the fresh-agent sensitivity read (bar item 12). A
  reader from the community concerned reads units 1 and 7 (Native nations) and units 2 and 6 (slavery and its law)
  (FOUNDATION section 7).
