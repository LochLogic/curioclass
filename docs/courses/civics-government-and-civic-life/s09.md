## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can a citizen tell which claims about one public issue hold up in the news?"

**Hook:** Day 131 opens with two original headlines about bus fares and a fictional official demanding approval before publication. Who checks the claims if officials control what appears?
**Supporting questions:** Write together: Why protect competing accounts (132)? What is each form for (133)? What does digital circulation change (134)? Who made this claim (135)? What do independent sources establish (137)? Which arguments hold up (140)?
**Closing claim:** On 142, answer the compelling question through Boston's evaluation, Baxandall's fare argument and Delano's counterargument. Explain which claims survive checking, answer a counterclaim and name an uncertainty.
Model constitutional reasoning on 131, checking outside a page on 135 and a sourced critique on 138. Ask who, when, for whom and why before using sources. Withdraw completed models on 139.


- **D4.4.6-8:** Judge how credible an argument is. Parts: critique its credibility using claims, evidence and reasoning. The local file lists the code only; the publisher's indicator supplies the wording.

**Parts by verb:** T7.1 explains protection and freedom from censorship; T7.2 gives competing examples; T7.3 explains four purposes; T7.4 evaluates benefits and challenges; T7.5 explains credibility checks, messages and evidence; T7.6 analyzes perspective and evaluates claims. Field 5 covers each. Detailed media law waits for Government. Press freedom has legal limits; it does not guarantee accuracy. Never label an outlet trustworthy by identity. No family politics, income or disability disclosures.

## U3. Exit questions

- **131, MA.8.T7.1:** "A county requires approval before a report on its road repairs appears. Explain one constitutional concern." Answer: official control before publication prevents independent scrutiny (G).
- **132, MA.8.T7.2:** "A reporter records a proposed playground closure; a parent commentary questions its timing. What competing information reaches readers?" Answer: the proposal and its consequences for users; both can inform evaluation (none; an answer naming only one account needs a follow-up prompt).
- **133, MA.8.T7.3:** "A signed guest column recommends later pool hours. What is its purpose, and how does its authorship differ from an editorial?" Answer: an outside author persuades; an editorial states the board's position (B).
- **134, MA.8.T7.4:** "A flood-road update spreads quickly online, but some residents have no connection. Evaluate this medium." Answer: speed helps safety and coordination; access gaps require another channel (D).
- **135, MA.8.T7.5:** "An unsigned post says tomorrow's town meeting has been canceled. Name two checks outside the post." Answer: investigate the publisher through an independent page, and check the dated notice from the responsible town office (C). Reserve this cancellation case from the model and paired practice.
- **136, MA.8.T7.5:** "Two pages repeat a parking-charge figure and both credit one campaign. Have they corroborated it independently?" Answer: no; find an original budget record or independent investigation (C).
- **137, MA.8.T7.5:** "Nine volunteers in a park poll all support more lights. A post says the whole town agrees. Evaluate." Answer: voluntary responses do not establish town-wide views (E).
- **138, MA.8.T7.6:** "A cartoon makes a 'fees' wall block a swimming pool. Analyze its view and name evidence needed to judge it." Answer: it criticizes fees as a barrier; attendance and access evidence would test the claim (B: "a cartoon only shows a fact").

- **142, MA.8.T7.5; D4.4.6-8:** "A school-meal editorial links a budget but omits its staffing costs. What must a credibility judgment explain?"
