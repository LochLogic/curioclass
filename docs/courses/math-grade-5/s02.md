## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "When we share, how can a fraction be the answer to a division?"

**Hook, day 15:** Share paper sandwiches equally among four people, first a whole-number amount, then three sandwiches. Can everyone receive an equal share? The eight-minute invitation includes field 4's retrieval before fractional sharing. Ask the compelling question, then teach with a labeled tape diagram. Adapt the sharing invitation named in field 10; print "invented for practice".
**Supporting questions:** What do numerator and denominator count (day 16)? Where does the quotient lie (day 17)? How does a remainder become part of each share (day 18)? Why does the relationship hold for any whole-number numerator and positive whole-number denominator (day 19)?
**Closing claim:** I can explain why a fraction is a division and solve sharing problems with labeled models, equations and whole-number bounds. Field 5's items 1 to 3 check this on day 20.
**Teaching:** Model equal partitions on day 15; compare two valid drawings on day 16. Connect each quantity to its symbol on day 17. Step back for independent model choice on days 18 and 19. Field 5 gives fresh daily exits.

- **5.NF.B.3, parts by verb:** (a) interpret a fraction as numerator divided by denominator, explaining equal shares and the inverse multiplication; (b) solve whole-number division problems with fractional and mixed-number answers, models or equations, and whole-number bounds. Teach (a) on days 15 to 17 and generalize on day 19; teach (b) on days 15 to 18. Check both on day 20.

**Practices:** On day 19, students explain why five paper sandwiches shared among three people give 5/3 each, then interpret 5 ÷ n = 5/3. MP2 requires identifying n as three people and restoring units; MP4 requires a chosen diagram, an equation and a check that three shares recover five wholes. Print "invented for practice".
**Rigor:** The five teaching days give understanding 180 minutes (60 percent), application 120 (40 percent), and dedicated fluency zero. 5.NF.B.3 sets no fluency expectation. Use untimed retrieval with feedback. All five lessons and this one unit center major work. Excluding check and respond days, then up to 25 minutes of retrieval and prerequisite repair, leaves at least 275 of 300 teaching minutes, 91.7 percent, on major work.
**Limits:** Whole numbers divided by positive whole numbers only. General fraction division waits for grade 6; unit 4 treats the grade 5 unit-fraction cases. Unlike-denominator addition waits for unit 9. No supporting cluster is assigned here.
**Carried:** Keep unit 1's habit of labeling quantities; replace its volume arrays with sharing models. Tie equal paper strips and tape diagrams to division on day 15, fractions on day 16. Tie number-line positions to equations on day 17. Students choose representations on days 18 and 19. Withdraw supplied strips on day 19 and supplied diagrams and lines on day 20; students may draw their own. Restore only the needed model on day 21. Carry tape diagrams and number lines into unit 3.

## 5. End-of-unit assessment, whole

Day 20 (End-of-unit assessment): Explain a fraction as a quotient with an independent sharing model, equation and bounded answer.

| Day | Code | Part (own words) | The item, answer and wrong answers (error letter) | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 5.NF.B.3 | Interpret a fraction as division of its numerator by its denominator. | **1, eight minutes.** Explain why a/b = a ÷ b for a whole-number a and positive whole-number b. Explain how b equal shares recover a wholes. Answer: each person receives a piece of size 1/b from each of a wholes, making a/b; b copies recover all a wholes. "It is b/a" is A. | section 2.1 |
| 20 | 5.NF.B.3 | Solve a division problem with a fractional answer using a model and equation. | **2, twelve minutes.** Five meters of ribbon are shared equally among eight people, invented for practice. Draw equal shares, label one person's amount, write an equation and justify two consecutive whole-number bounds. Answer: 5 ÷ 8 = 5/8 meter; 0 < 5/8 < 1. Divide each of five equal one-meter strips into eighths; each person gets five eighths of one meter. "5/8 of the five-meter collection" is C; a drawing giving people unequal total lengths is D; "between 1 and 2" is F. | section 2.2 |
| 20 | 5.NF.B.3 | Solve a division problem with a mixed-number answer and bound it. | **3, fourteen minutes.** Seventeen kilograms of clay are shared equally among six groups, invented for practice. Give each share as a mixed number with an equation, check by multiplication and justify consecutive whole-number bounds. Answer: 17 ÷ 6 = 17/6 = 2 5/6 kilograms; six shares give 12 + 5 = 17 kilograms; 12 < 17 < 18 gives bounds 2 and 3. "2 5/17" is B; "between 3 and 4" is F. | section 2.2 |

**Look-fors and samples:** Item 1 requires the correct order and a general equal-sharing argument, not just an example. Meets: "Divide each of a wholes into b equal pieces. Each person gets one piece from every whole, so a pieces of size 1/b, or a/b. The b shares recover a wholes." Does not meet: "It is b/a because b people share" (A).

Item 2 requires equal total lengths per person, a labeled individual share, an equation and justified bounds. Accept unequal component pieces when each person receives the same total length. Meets: "Each of five equal strips has eight equal pieces. One person gets one eighth from each strip: 5 ÷ 8 = 5/8 meter, between 0 and 1 because five pieces are fewer than eight eighths." Does not meet: "5/8 of all five meters is one person's share" (C).

Item 3 requires a mixed number, a matching equation and inverse check, and justified bounds. Meets: "17 ÷ 6 = 17/6 = 2 5/6 kilograms. Six groups of two use twelve kilograms; five kilograms remain to share in sixths. Six shares recover seventeen. It lies between 2 and 3 because 12 < 17 < 18." Does not meet: "2 5/17 kilograms" (B).

Score each requested component separately as demonstrated or needing response. ## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Reverses numerator and denominator when translating division. | Loses the roles of total and group count. Repair: label both quantities before dividing. | From memory. |
| B | Uses the original dividend as the denominator of the fractional remainder. | Does not share the remaining wholes among the original groups. Repair: partition each remaining whole by the group count. | From memory. |
| C | Treats one share's fraction as a fraction of the entire collection. | Changes the reference whole. Repair: label one whole strip with its unit before counting fractional pieces. | From memory. |
| D | Accepts unequal total amounts as equal shares. | Counts pieces without comparing each person's total amount. Repair: assemble each person's pieces and compare total lengths. | From memory. |
| E | Identifies the numerator as the unknown group count. | Does not interpret the denominator in the equation. Repair: label the denominator with the people receiving equal shares. | From memory. |
| F | Gives whole-number bounds that exclude the quotient. | Does not connect the share to consecutive whole-number multiples. Repair: bracket the dividend between two consecutive multiples of the divisor. | From memory. |
