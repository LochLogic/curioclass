## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do powers of ten move digits, and how do we change units?"

**Hook, day 110:** Ask the compelling question. Compare 4.6, 46 and 0.46: what changed, and what stayed? After eight minutes including retrieval, model tenfold changes on a fixed chart. This original adaptation follows the base lesson's comparison routine.
**Supporting questions:** What does an exponent count (day 111)? Why does a smaller unit need a larger count (day 112)? When must we divide (day 113)? Why are customary factors different (day 116)?
**Closing claim:** I can explain tenfold digit shifts and solve measurement problems with units at every step. P1, P2, M1, C1 and S1 supply evidence on day 117.
**Teaching:** Model shifts before practice; keep the ones boundary fixed. On day 113 model converting 1.8 meters to centimeters, subtracting 45 centimeters, then reporting 1.35 meters. Step back during independent applications; compare unit choices before correcting.


**Practices:** MP7 develops on day 111 through repeated factors and existing zeros. Divide powers successively by ten to reach 10^0 = 1; practice with 82 x 10^0 = 82. MP6 develops on day 114 through M1's labeled equations and explanation of changing counts. P1 and S1 check both.
**Rigor:** Four understanding days and three application days give 240 and 180 minutes respectively; there are no fluency days. Required 5.NBT.B.5 fluency continues in five-minute mixed warm-ups on days 112 and 115, varying zeros and factor lengths with feedback and no countdown. Supporting conversion practices major powers on days 112 to 115. Preserve the scope and sequence's 240 major minutes of 420 (57.1 percent), including those warm-ups: 60 each on days 110 and 111, 30 each on days 112 to 115. This is one mixed unit; major codes occur on six of seven teaching days.
**Limits:** Convert within one system, never between systems. State customary relationships; they are not powers of ten. Exponents have base ten and nonnegative whole-number values; other bases and exponent laws wait. Decimal computation uses unit 7 strategies, not required algorithm fluency.
**Carried:** Keep unit 7's chart and restore unit 6's number line. Tie chart columns to equations on day 110; fade labels on day 113 and withdraw supplied charts on day 116. Tie paired number-line scales to equivalent lengths on day 112; fade marks on day 114 and withdraw supplied lines on day 117. Students may draw their own. Restore only a needed model on day 118.

**Look-fors and samples:** Judge components separately, including explanations rather than answers alone.
- **P1:** Repeated factors, exponent notation including zero, correct products and an explanation distinguishing existing from added zeros. Meets: "10^2 = 10 x 10. Each digit gains two place values: 230 x 10^2 = 23,000, three trailing zeros because 230 already has one; 23 x 10^2 = 2,300 has two. 640 x 10^0 = 640 because 10^0 = 1, so no places change." Does not: "The exponent tells all the zeros, so both products have two" (B).
- **P2:** Correct results, direction, number of shifts and place values. Meets: "6.38 x 10^2 = 638: six ones become six hundreds. 52.7 divided by 10^2 = 0.527: five tens become five tenths. Each digit changes two places; the ones boundary stays fixed." Does not: "Multiplying adds zeros: 6.3800" (C).
- **M1:** Both conversion directions, a valid multi-step difference, units and a magnitude check. Meets: "0.84 meters = 84 centimeters. 84 - 37.5 = 46.5 centimeters = 0.465 meters. A smaller unit counts more pieces of the same length. The remainder, 0.465 meters, is less than the original 0.84 meters." Does not: "84 - 37.5 = 46.5 meters" (D).
- **C1, formative:** Stated factor, both directions and remaining length. Meets: "4.25 feet x 12 = 51 inches; 51 - 19.5 = 31.5 inches; 31.5 divided by 12 = 2.625 feet." Does not: "4.25 feet = 42.5 inches because all conversions multiply by ten" (E).
- **S1:** Four correct labeled quantities and explanations of larger and smaller units. Meets: "0.73 meters = 73 centimeters; 286 centimeters = 2.86 meters; 3.75 feet = 45 inches; 9.6 inches = 0.8 feet. Smaller units give larger counts; reverse the factor for larger units." Does not: "286 centimeters = 28,600 meters because conversion always multiplies" (F).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows and one repair | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Reads an exponent as a multiplier of ten. | Repeated factors are not distinguished from repeated addition. Repair: expand the power as a product before evaluating. | From memory. |
| B | Treats the exponent as the total number of trailing zeros in every product. | Existing zeros are ignored. Repair: mark existing zeros before filling new placeholder places. | From memory. |
| C | Multiplies a decimal by appending zeros. | Whole-number notation is overgeneralized. Repair: move digit cards on a fixed chart and read their new values. | From memory. |
| D | Labels a computed result with the unit used before conversion. | The computed count is detached from its unit. Repair: write units on every intermediate quantity. | From memory. |
| E | Uses factor ten for feet and inches. | Metric structure is applied to a different relationship. Repair: draw one foot partitioned into twelve inches. | From memory. |
| F | Always multiplies during conversion to a larger unit. | Unit size is not connected to count. Repair: group smaller units into one larger unit before predicting the quotient. | From memory. |
| G | Records the tens partial product as ones. | The algorithm loses the multiplier's place value. Repair: label the tens row with the full multiplier. | From memory. |
| H | Claims a one-place digit shift doubles its value. | Place distance is mistaken for an additive change. Repair: compare the same digit's values in two adjacent columns. | From memory. |
| I | Uses factor 100 where the given metric relationship is 1,000. | Familiar factors replace the stated relationship. Repair: annotate the conversion arrow with the given equality before calculating. | From memory. |
| J | Evaluates 10^0 as zero. | The exponent is mistaken for the power's value. Repair: divide successive powers by ten through 10^1 to 10^0 = 1. | From memory. |

Letters A to J are used throughout; classify only the misconception actually shown.
