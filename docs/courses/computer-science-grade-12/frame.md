# Course frame: Computer Science, Grade 12

Course `computer-science-grade-12` (catalog id `cs-k12`, key `cs-k12-12`), grade 12, one year, the last course of the subject. Draft frame, 2026-11-25, written on the shape of the kindergarten pilot (docs/courses/computer-science-kindergarten/frame.md and its fix report); the scope and sequence sets the days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 5, in the order the year's work builds: judging what a machine says (algorithms and computing and
  society: generated output, the biases of the systems that make it, and a debate on human and artificial
  intelligence), who sets the rules (algorithms, systems and data: the values built into a design, a law that governs
  design and use, and a debate on a data policy), building it as a team (programming: the capstone, built to a
  defined workflow with a design document and review), crediting it and testing it with users (programming:
  attribution, a heuristic review and two rounds of user feedback on the capstone), and what our team taught us and
  where I go next (computing and society: diverse teams from published accounts and the team's own log, then a
  personal plan). Every concept area is taught this year: ALG in units 1 and 2, DAT and SYS in unit 2, PRO in units 3
  and 4, SOC in units 1 and 5.
- **Days.** 64 course days: 58 in units, 4 for course-level checks (2 after unit 2, 2 after unit 4) and 2 respond
  days (one after each check). Two lessons a week of about 45 minutes, 32 weeks: the computer science default for
  grades 6 to 12 (docs/courses/computer-science/STANDARD.md section 2, Cory, Desk q-fix-cs-1), a real elective, so
  course days 2w minus 1 and 2w fall in week w. The 45 minutes hold set-up, clean-up and collecting work: every
  lesson opens with a retrieval trace or predict task of about five minutes (APPROACH.md, "How a day opens"), device
  lessons keep three minutes at the end to save work to a file and shut down, and paper work is collected in the
  last two minutes, so a lesson teaches for about 35 to 37 minutes. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit
  assessment (and, in units over 25 days, its mid-unit assessments; no unit here runs over 25 days). As in the
  pilot, a course-level check that directly follows a unit stands for that unit's end-of-unit assessment, and the
  check's respond day is the unit's respond day: the check after unit 2 stands for unit 2's, the check after unit 4
  for unit 4's (with unit 4's scored refinement product). Each check takes two lessons, since a 45-minute lesson
  holds one full performance (frame-write.md rule 19): the first scores the unit just ended in full, the second
  samples the earlier units. Units 1, 3 and 5 end in their own end-of-unit assessment and respond day. Days follow
  the load of the rows: unit 3 holds the capstone (two rows written "several weeks", with a design, a review step and
  a build in pairs) and gets 21; unit 2 owns three Foundational ids and one S1 id, each with its own case, and gets
  11, all teaching; unit 1 owns two Foundational ids and one S1 id with a debate, and gets 11 with its assessment and
  respond day; unit 4 owns two Foundational ids and one S1 id on a program already built, and gets 7; unit 5 owns two
  society ids met on published accounts and a personal plan, and gets 8 with a draft, written feedback and revision.
  The two check lessons came from unit 3 (one build day folded into the other seven) and unit 4 (its closing
  hand-in and mixed retrieval day, which the check's second lesson now does). So units 1 to 5 run 11, 11, 21, 7 and 8
  days, of which 9, 11, 19, 7 and 6 teach: 52 teaching days, with 3 end-of-unit assessment days (unit 1 day 10, unit
  3 day 20, unit 5 day 7), 4 course-level check days and 5 respond days. 64 lessons for 10 Foundational and 4 S1 ids
  is the progression read's capstone year (docs/reviews/progression-computer-science-2026-11-19.md, finding 1: grade
  12 owns 10 so the capstone has room).
- **Check weeks.** Grades 9 to 12 have no stagger rule but finals weeks (docs/courses/CALENDAR.md, rule 4: week 32 is
  a finals week). This course's checks sit in week 12 (course days 23 and 24, after unit 2; respond day 25 in week
  13) and weeks 27 and 28 (course days 54 and 55, the year-end check after unit 4; respond day 56 in week 28), off
  week 32. The year-end lessons cannot share one week without moving the respond day past week 28 or cutting unit 5
  below its 8 days, so they straddle weeks 27 and 28. Unit 5 runs in weeks 29 to 32 after the year-end check (Grade
  12 calendar, below), as kindergarten's unit 5 does. Overlaps with other after-core subjects' check weeks are the
  lead's calendar pass across the after-core frames.
- **Grade 12 calendar.** The course teaches to its last week. Its year-end check sits in week 28 so a senior who
  leaves early has sat it: units 1 to 4 and both course-level checks end by week 28. Unit 5 (weeks 29 to 32, 8 days)
  owns HS-SOC-CE-45 and HS-SOC-CE-46 and ends in its own end-of-unit product. If seniors leave before week 31, unit 5
  folds to its short form of 4 days in weeks 29 and 30, each named part kept: day 1, the hook (about 3 minutes), the
  team-log study (about 7), the two published accounts read in short excerpts (about 20) and the analysis drafted
  (about 7); day 2, the portfolio read for two skills with evidence (about 7), two supplied field cards (about 12)
  and the plan drafted from interests, skills, fields, a pathway and a next step (about 18); day 3, the
  written-feedback and revision day for both pieces (kept whole, never folded); day 4, the scored hand-in with its
  short unseen log item; the respond day goes. A school whose seniors leave before week 29 names CE-45 and CE-46 as
  not claimed for those students in the course record.
- **Course order.** Computer science in grades 6 to 12 is an elective, so this course never assumes the grade 11
  course was taken (frame-write.md rule 32). By course order, grade 12 follows grade 11: every row assumes a grade 11
  row, the year before, so for a student who took grade 11 each opens as a retrieval task inside a day (frame-write.md
  rule 16; Seams). For a student who did not, the same items are given as a readiness check of ten minutes before the
  unit opens (unit 1: the last ten minutes of day 1, after the hook; unit 2: the last ten minutes of unit 1's respond
  day, course day 11; unit 3: the last ten minutes of the mid-year respond day, course day 25; unit 4: the last ten
  minutes of unit 3's respond day, course day 46; unit 5: the last ten minutes of the year-end respond day, course day
  56), and the unit's first understanding day carries a short worked example of the grade 11 idea for those students
  inside its own minutes, never an added day. Two things come from further back for everyone: the flowchart (last
  made in grade 9, g9-det-vs-prob) and the modules and data structures the capstone uses again (g9-modular,
  HS-PRO-PD-12; g9-design-with-structures, HS-ALG-PS-01; both grade 9, three grades back). They get a readiness check
  in the last ten minutes of course day 22, unit 2's last day, so course day 25 holds only the grade 11 items; its
  results shape unit 3's day 3. Ids that earlier electives begin and this course owns (HS-SOC-HU-44 and HS-SOC-CE-45
  from grade 11, HS-PRO-PD-14 from grades 10 and 11, HS-SOC-CE-46 from grades 9 and 10) are taught here from the
  start; the earlier use is a head start, not a prerequisite.
- **Standard.** The 10 HS Foundational ids grade 12 owns of the 2026 CSTA PK-12 Computer Science Standards
  (STANDARD.md section 2, "Ownership of MS and HS ids": grades 9 to 11 own 12 each): HS-ALG-PS-05, HS-ALG-IM-11,
  HS-DAT-IM-28, HS-PRO-PD-14, HS-PRO-PD-15, HS-PRO-TR-20, HS-SOC-CE-45, HS-SOC-CE-46, HS-SOC-HU-44, HS-SYS-IM-36, and
  grade 12's four Specialty I ids (Cory, 2026-11-20, Desk q-prework-cs-1): S1-AIN-HR-09, S1-DSC-PP-13, S1-SWD-PP-08,
  S1-SWD-UX-05, each taught inside the row that already teaches its topic and checked in full. Every HS id is owned by
  grade 12 at the latest, so this course closes the HS set. Each id is taught at the depth its owner row's notes set
  in our own words, checked against its boundary statement in the source store
  (S:\CurioClass-sources\computer-science\csta-2026-11-21, read for this frame, not copied or quoted), and "What
  students learn" says what is out. Plans cite ids and state each action in a short phrase of our own (STANDARD.md
  "Quoting"); the credit line "Standards: 2026 CSTA PK-12 Computer Science Standards (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)" goes on any
  day that leans on a statement. Check set: each unit plan checks itself against the computer science bar
  (docs/ADOPTION-BAR-SUBJECTS.md part 5, items 1 to 9) and the lens (docs/agents/lenses/computer-science.md); the
  "Check set adds" column names what the writer expects that to add. Values rule (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-12) holds. No
  S2 id and no X + CS id is claimed.
- **Days outside the standards (bar C1).** S1 days count as days outside the course's own standards (STANDARD.md
  section 2). The S1 parts take 4.5 day-equivalents, each split named: unit 1 day 5, whole, and half of day 4 (the
  error counts by group; the other half reads the test-table for HS-ALG-PS-05) (S1-AIN-HR-09); unit 2 half of day 7
  (how the data choices widened or narrowed gaps; the other half judges the policy's efficacy for HS-DAT-IM-28) and
  half of day 10 (the effect on an under-represented group and a fairer dataset; the other half is the written
  judgment of efficacy for HS-DAT-IM-28) (S1-DSC-PP-13); unit 3 day 6, whole (S1-SWD-PP-08, version history on paper);
  unit 4 day 3, whole (S1-SWD-UX-05). That is 4.5 of its 52 teaching days (about 8.7 percent, counting course days
  minus the 12 end-of-unit, check and respond days), under bar C1's 10 percent with about 0.7 of a day of room. The
  capstone build days revisit grade 9's HS-PRO-PD-12 and HS-ALG-PS-01 at full size and so sit inside the standards.
  The scope and sequence counts every outside-the-standards minute by day, shared lessons included, against the same
  teaching-day count, and adds no S1 time.
- **Practices and concept areas.** The Units table's Practices and concept areas column (the C3 column of the core frames) names the
  concept area each unit teaches and the CSTA practices it teaches inside the content, by code and our label
  (STANDARD.md section 3), as bar items 1 and 2 ask. Each unit plan names the practice's day and task. Across the year:
  ESR1 weigh who gains and who is harmed (units 1, 2, 5), ESR2 credit others' work and protect users' data (units 2,
  4), IC3 explain, give and take feedback (units 1, 2, 4, 5), IC4 run a project (unit 3), IC5 be a reliable teammate
  and reflect on the team's work (units 3, 5), CT6 define the problem (units 1, 2, 3, 5), CT7 abstract (unit 3), CT8
  plan, then build (unit 3), CT9 test, debug and refine (units 1, 3, 4), HCD10 learn from and include different users
  (units 2, 4, 5), HCD11 prototype, test and refine (unit 4), HCD12 keep users informed and in control (units 1, 2,
  4). Dispositions are named in the unit plans, not here.
- **Base text.** None. The partial base (the archived Teach Computing Curriculum) covers grades K to 8 only, and high
  school is our own (STANDARD.md section 2; scout, docs/reviews/computer-science-open-material-2026-11-16.md). Sources
  are chosen by STANDARD.md "Real cases in society days": what happened, never a company or product, two views and one
  dated source for each, government bodies and courts named, the source's title and publisher in the teacher's source
  line only. Curio Class writes every reading, case card, code sample, saved-output set, license card and template;
  the saved outputs of generated-text systems are collected by Curio Class, dated and labeled as such, with no product
  named. Each unit names its cases, with a free source and an "as of" date; facts given from memory are marked so and
  checked at source before the unit plan uses them. No Source map section, since there is no base text.
- **Day kinds.** As docs/GLOSSARY.md (row "teaching day") defines them, in computer science's words (APPROACH.md):
  Understanding is an unplugged or trace day, the first teaching of an idea (a supplied set of outputs judged with a
  worked example first, a values trace from a supplied description, a worked team project read whole). Fluency is a
  modify day: change a working program or a working method in small checked steps; this course has some (unit 1 day 6,
  a mixed set judged with the class's own checklist; unit 3 day 7, a starter module changed to fit the design; the
  retrieval openings), since most of grade 12 is new judgment and the capstone. Application is a make, design or
  society day: write a design document, build in pairs, run a user round, audit a case with evidence, hold a debate,
  write an evaluation or a plan. Check is the end-of-unit assessment or a course-level check; Respond acts on it by
  error letter. Inside every unit: understanding before modify before make, and no make day before a trace or modify
  day on the same construct or method.
- **Evidence.** Daily evidence follows APPROACH.md "The form of daily evidence" (exit tickets on trace and judge days,
  the program with look-fors on build days, a claim and two views on society days). Every end-of-unit assessment and
  course-level check is either a 45-minute sitting with unseen items, never the unit's own case, or a product whose
  draft, written feedback and revision days are counted in the unit's days before it is scored (frame-write.md rule
  19). Unit 1: a sitting (day 10). Unit 2: the check after it (course days 23 and 24). Unit 3: the team program scored
  after its written-feedback and revision days (days 17 and 18), a presentation (day 19) and an individual sitting
  (day 20), so each student has a score of their own. Unit 4: the refinement the team made, scored as a product from
  the refinement record handed in on day 7 (each student's own contribution to one implemented refinement, with the
  user-feedback note, before-and-after evidence, test results and the design value it serves, HS-PRO-TR-20), plus
  the year-end check (course days 54 and 55) for transfer. Unit 5: the revised analysis and plan, scored on day 7
  after feedback and revision on day 6, with a short unseen item. Each owned id has its own look-for: a "debate" id
  (HS-DAT-IM-28, HS-SOC-HU-44) is scored on the student's written judgment of the evidence, never on which side won;
  an "apply" or "collaborate" id (HS-PRO-PD-14, HS-PRO-PD-15) on the student's own work and an unseen item; a
  "connect" id (HS-SOC-CE-46) on the student's plan, read privately. No score comes from a day that first teaches a
  row. Project rubrics are written in the unit plan with a sample that meets them and one that does not (bar item 8).
- **Devices.** One device per pair is the default on a device day, never needed per student. Device days: unit 1
  day 4 (the error-count table), unit 3 days 7 to 18 (the build, its reviews and its revision), unit 4 days 2 and 4
  to 7 (credits in the program, user rounds and changes); about 18 of the 64 days, so the course keeps at least one
  unplugged day in every unit (APPROACH.md, Pacing) and its society days unplugged. Every device day is written four
  ways (STANDARD.md "Devices and rooms"): one device per pair; a few devices at stations with the other pairs on the
  paper version of the same step; one screen with the class predicting and a student at the device; or no device.
  No grade 12 owned id is on STANDARD.md's device-only list, so none is marked part met for lack of a device: the
  workflow, the review step, attribution, user rounds and the heuristic review all run in full on paper, and the
  error counts of S1-AIN-HR-09 are tallied by hand from a printed test-table. Three things are part met: S1-AIN-HR-09
  asks students to analyze a model they helped create as well as one others made, so a student with no grade 11
  model of their own uses the supplied saved model on unit 1 day 4 and S1-AIN-HR-09 is recorded part met for that
  student (HS-ALG-PS-05 stays fully claimed; grade 12 adds no model build day, decided by Cory 2026-11-26, For the lead
  6); the version
  control part of S1-SWD-PP-08 (branch, review, merge) is part met for every class until our editor's offline
  version history exists (Cory, 2026-11-21, Desk q-fix-cs-2), taught on paper branch cards meanwhile; and on the
  no-device path the capstone's revisit of HS-PRO-PD-12 is written on paper and marked "part met without a device"
  in the record (it is grade 9's id, not claimed here).
- **Paper path.** Every day has a paper version: saved outputs printed with a margin for notes, the error-count
  table on paper, case and summary cards, debate cards and scoring sheets, the design document, task list and log on
  paper, code printed for the review step, license cards, the heuristic checklist on a printed screen, and the program
  walked through on paper by a user. No tool asks for a student account, stores a student's name or sends anything off
  the device; work is saved to a file the student downloads or printed (STANDARD.md "Privacy and safety"). No student
  uses a live generated-text tool (STANDARD.md "Generated-text tools").
- **Tools.** By generic name, with their jobs, for TOOL-ASKS.md: a saved-output viewer (outputs side by side with a
  note margin; unit 1); an error-count table (tallies a model's errors by group from a test-table and shows the gaps;
  unit 1); our own browser editor in Python with its step-through trace (STANDARD.md "Our own editor"; units 3 and 4)
  and, when built, its offline version history with branch, review and merge (unit 3); a design-document and log
  template (a page that saves to a file; units 3 to 5); a heuristic review checklist (unit 4). Every tool runs in a
  common browser, one file, nothing installed, no account, nothing sent off the device.
- **Reading level and vocabulary.** Code read alone runs to twenty lines at most (APPROACH.md, Supports); a team's
  program is read by module. Case cards and plain-language summaries are written at about a grade 11 to 12 reading
  level, a page at most, with the technical words glossed. Each unit names five to eight words taught in depth (from
  the rows' words), with words that have an everyday meaning taught as two meanings (bias, model, branch, merge,
  license). Writing and speaking are practiced, not owned: the written evaluations, debates and plans practice ELA
  W.11-12.1, W.11-12.2 and SL.11-12.1, which English language arts grade 12 owns (Cross-subject).
- **Family note and students 18 or over.** Each unit's family note says what the unit studies and names any unit a
  school may leave out (below). A student 18 or over gets the family note themselves and makes their own leave-out or
  consent choice; the school's own rules still apply (AGENTS.md, Cory 2026-11-22).
- **Swap or leave out.** STANDARD.md section 3 lets a school swap or leave out a named unit for its community's
  reasons. Unit 5 may be left out (published accounts of people's backgrounds and barriers, and a personal plan, are
  its ground): HS-SOC-CE-45 and HS-SOC-CE-46 are then not claimed that year, and the course still teaches computing and
  society through HS-SOC-HU-44 in unit 1 and the society cases of unit 2; a school may also teach CE-46's plan in its
  own advisory program and name that in the record. Unit 2's cases may be swapped for others of the same kind (a
  different law, a different data policy), not left out, so the course always claims HS-DAT-IM-28, the year's only data
  id (bar part 5 fails a course with nothing on data). Units 1, 3 and 4 are not swapped or left out: unit 1 carries
  the year's algorithms work under AI, and units 3 and 4 are the capstone the rest of the year reads.
- **Safety.** No lab or field work, so no laboratory or field safety guide applies. Device lessons keep cords behind
  the desks and devices carried with two hands; build days in pairs set a stretch break at the driver and navigator
  switch. Security is not taught this year; no day probes, scans or tests a real system (STANDARD.md "Privacy and
  safety"). The scope and sequence copies these lines into each device day.
- **Costs.** Provisional, for a class of 30, beyond the school's own devices, printing only: unit 1 $5 (saved-output
  sets, the printed test-tables, debate cards and scoring sheets), unit 2 $6 (case and summary cards, debate packs),
  unit 3 $8 (the worked team project, design-document and log templates, printed code for two review steps, paper
  branch cards), unit 4 $4 (license cards, heuristic checklists, user-round note sheets), unit 5 $3 (the two published
  accounts, pathway and field cards, plan sheets), about $26 in all.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md);
  no outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Unit 1 has bias content (generated text and images that treat
  groups by race, gender, disability or language unequally) and beliefs about mind and consciousness. Starting pack:
  the National Institute of Standards and Technology's "Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative
  Artificial Intelligence Profile" (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024, doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1), as background on the
  kinds of harm only; the National Center on Disability and Journalism's Disability Language Style Guide
  (ncdj.org/style-guide/); and the Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Position Paper (Indigenous Protocol
  and Artificial Intelligence Working Group, 2020, indigenous-ai.net/position-paper), one set of communities' own

  health, immigration status or money. The course's checks get the answer-key agent's read. No unit is hands-on in
  the laboratory sense, so no safety agent read is planned beyond the Safety lines above.

### Where the threads end

This is the subject's last course; no row hands on past grade 12, and no row's hands_on cell is filled. The threads
end in the student's own work: judging what a machine says and the systems behind it (unit 1); weighing laws,
policies and the values in a design as a citizen (unit 2); building software as a team to a workflow, crediting
others' work and refining it for real users (units 3 and 4); and a personal plan that links what the student has
shown in computing to a goal of their own (unit 5). The student keeps the portfolio and the plan.
