## 1. Standards and practices


- **MA.1.T3.2, days 62 to 65, the whole standard.** Children "explain the meaning of symbols, phrases, and songs that show pride in belonging to the United States". Explain is: what it stands for, and one detail that shows it.
- **MA.1.T3.2a, days 62 and 63.** "Explain the meaning of national symbols such as the flag, the bald eagle, the White House, and the Statue of Liberty." 62: the flag (a star for each state, review), the bald eagle on the Great Seal (the olive branch), the White House. 63: the Statue of Liberty. All four are taught in this section, so all four can be scored on day 67.
- **MA.1.T3.2b, day 64.** "Explain the meaning of national words and mottoes, such as 'E pluribus unum' ('Out of Many, One') and the 'Pledge of Allegiance.'" The motto and the whole Pledge, with its meaning.
- **MA.1.T3.2c, day 65.** "Recognize the tunes and words of patriotic songs such as 'America the Beautiful' and 'The Star-Spangled Banner,' and explain what they mean." Hear both tunes; link a phrase's words to a detail and a meaning.
- **Limits that bite here.** Assess understanding, never patriotism, and never singing, saying or standing. The Pledge is read as a text, including its religious wording, without asking anyone to agree. Saying it is a choice (West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 1943). A symbol's recorded adoption is kept apart from the meanings people give it later. The seal's color meanings are never given to the original flag. Shared ideals do not prove equal treatment: people were enslaved, and Native people were forced from homelands. The Statue of Liberty is welcome as an ideal; not everyone was welcomed the same (K). No child is asked about their own or their family's arrival, status or beliefs, and nobody acts out an arrival.
- **Connections.** Kindergarten's flag, eagle, anthem and Pledge pages (review, 62 to 65). The timeline (review, 63). 6.2's united and diverse (64). Perspective (6.1, 63).
- **What the teacher page must explain.**
  - Flag: thirteen stripes for the first thirteen states and a star for each state, fifty today (review; the law gave no reason for the colors). Eagle: chosen for the Great Seal in 1782; the page names the olive branch (peace) and the arrows (the power to defend) (S: the State Department's Great Seal page, read; wording to check). The bald eagle has been the national bird by law since December 2024 (S: confirm on congress.gov).
  - White House: the home and office of the president, who leads the part of the government that carries out the laws (S: source card to make; the National Park Service or White House Historical page, to read).
  - Statue of Liberty: a gift from France, raised in 1886; the torch means liberty; the poem on its base welcomes tired people who want to be free (S: National Park Service statue-facts and poem pages, read). Immigrants' own Ellis Island accounts are unread and owed before production: nothing here goes beyond the unit plan's "welcome for many; not equal for all".
  - Motto: "E pluribus unum", Latin for "Out of many, one", is on the seal; the record says many states, one country; people say many people, one country (S: Great Seal page).
  - Pledge: current text, read once by the teacher (S: text to make). Pledge, a promise of loyalty; indivisible, one country together; liberty and justice for all, freedom and fair treatment. "Under God" is part of the text, not a belief asked of any listener.
  - Songs: "America the Beautiful" (words by Katharine Lee Bates, written after a trip in 1893; S: the National Park Service page, read) and "The Star-Spangled Banner" (Francis Scott Key, 1814; the 1931 law, review). Public-domain words and scores (S: score to check). Teaching phrases: "purple mountain majesties" and "land of the free and the home of the brave". Four phrases are held out of teaching: "spacious skies" and "broad stripes and bright stars" (exit, 65), and "amber waves of grain" and "our flag was still there" (day 67).

  - 6.2's united and diverse words and the frame "The source shows ___. This supports ___ because ___": returned 62 to 65 as the way to give a meaning with a detail.

- **A room with nothing.** Hand-drawn symbols, scrap-paper bins, an adult humming the two tunes.

## 6. Days

Every day is 30 minutes (frame). Parts are the lines in bold; the day skeleton reads them. No timer is shown to children. Everything is read aloud, hummed or shown; children point, sort, place a token, draw or say. One marking scale: shown, shown with help, not yet seen. The rotating six are heard 40 seconds each (about 4 minutes) during supported work; the record sheet names who was heard. Practice cases are invented and the teacher says so aloud. Views and phrases are kept apart: teaching, exit and the end packet (day 67) each have their own picture or phrase and none is shown twice. Lyric cards show only the phrase of the day. Nobody is asked to say the Pledge, stand or sing; any child may listen quietly.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: symbol, flag, eagle, statue, motto, liberty, justice, belong, song, tune. Frames: "It stands for ___." "The detail is ___." "The song says ___, so it expresses ___." Home-language words welcome.
- **Access.** Large print, raised-line seal and statue, spoken descriptions, tokens in place of drawing. A child who cannot hear a tune: title and lyric-card matching, with meaning; auditory recognition is recorded as inaccessible, not incorrect. A child may listen quietly during every humming.
- **Check accommodations.** Read everything aloud; accept a point, a token, a drawing, familiar-language speech or a private turn. A child not heard stays "not yet seen", never guessed. Never mark silence, standing, saying or singing.
- **Reading load.** None; labels and phrase cards are read aloud.
- **Extension, same standard.** Two readings of one symbol, each with its own detail.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Look at one picture or phrase card, say what it stands for and what shows it, and keep the record apart from what people say. Hum the tune from the printed music; never ask the child to recite, stand or sing.
- **One projector.** Show the seal, statue or phrase card large; children mark paper; the adult hums from the printed sheet; the spoken part is heard at a table.
