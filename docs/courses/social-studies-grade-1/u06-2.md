- **MA.1.T3.1, days 59 to 61, the whole standard.** Children "use evidence to explain how people in the United States are united, such as by a shared national history, and how they are diverse in backgrounds, beliefs, and traditions". 59: united (the Constitution, shared laws, a shared holiday). 60: diverse (languages, birthplaces, beliefs, traditions; Native nations here first and here now). 61: a claim, with evidence for each side. Use evidence is a card, a line of a source or a data strip pointed to and named, never "I like it".
- **Limits that bite here.** Shared ideals do not prove equal treatment: people were enslaved, and Native people were forced from their homelands. Native nations are named by their own names and governed now: the Cherokee Nation, and (review) the Wampanoag. Beliefs are described, never practised, and no child is asked about their own or their family's beliefs, languages, birthplace or money. Local data is a count for the whole town, never a household and never a person; statistics never show anyone's religion or citizenship (error L). Strips have ten squares and the words "some" and "many" (many means more than three shaded), with no percent sign. Assess understanding, never patriotism or belief.
- **Connections.** Kindergarten civic holidays and the timeline (retrieval and review, 59). Kindergarten's Wampanoag, named by their own name and living today (review, 60). Unit 3's claim with a reason, and unit 5's source routine and blank claim frame (the unit plan says they are carried; they are not in the ledger yet), and kindergarten's "I think ___ because ___" (review, 61). 6.1's perspective (return, 60).
- **What the teacher page must explain.**
  - The Constitution is a written plan for the government, written in 1787 (S: National Archives transcript, read); its opening words begin "We the People of the United States". A law is a rule for everyone in a place; the Constitution is the rule paper the government's rules come under. Date cards are supplied; no date is memorized.
  - Independence Day is a shared holiday (review of kindergarten). Constitution Day appears only in the exit; its date and what it remembers are source to check before the exit card is printed.
  - The Cherokee Nation governs itself today, teaches the Cherokee language, including an immersion school, and was forced from its homelands by United States soldiers long ago (S: the Cherokee Nation's about, history and language pages, named in the unit plan, read; exact wording and the year to check). The history page also tells of death on the journey; the teacher says "by force" plainly in two sentences and asks no child to picture it. Cherokee Nation is one Native nation among many.
  - Local data: Census QuickFacts counts for the town (language spoken at home; birthplace), a county count labelled "county" if no town figure exists (S: coverage read). A count tells nothing about any person. A community's own account of a tradition (the Cherokee Nation's pages serve; the local community's own account is study owed) shows what a number cannot.
  - Ancestry and belief are different things; one never fixes the other (L).

## 6. Days

Every day is 30 minutes (frame). Parts are the lines in bold; the day skeleton reads them. No timer is shown to children. Stems, captions and passages are read aloud; children point, sort, place a token, draw or say. One marking scale: shown, shown with help, not yet seen. The rotating six are heard 40 seconds each (about 4 minutes) during supported work, and the record sheet has a column naming who was heard. Every invented town card is printed "invented for practice" and the teacher says aloud that it is made up; one town, one neighbor at a time, in each scene. Teaching towns are Birch Town (59), a local strip and the Cherokee Nation's pages (60), and Oak Town and Maple Town (61); the exits use other towns.

## 7. Talk and writing

- Planned questions: 59, "What do these neighbors share? Must they celebrate alike?"; 60, "What does the strip show? What does the nation's account add?"; 61, "What does the source show? What does that support?"

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: united, law, diverse, nation, language, belief, evidence, claim, source. Frames: "The source shows ___." "This supports ___ because ___." "They share ___, and they are different in ___." Home-language words welcome; nobody is asked which languages or beliefs are theirs.
- **Access.** Large print, raised ten-square strips, a spoken description of every card, tokens in place of drawing. The nation's pages are read aloud in our wording.
- **Check accommodations.** Read everything aloud; accept a point, a token, a drawing, familiar-language speech or a private turn. A child not heard stays "not yet seen", never guessed.
- **Reading load.** None; labels read aloud.
- **Extension, same standard.** A second source detail for the same claim, and one thing the claim leaves out.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Read each card, say what is shared and what differs, and say "The source shows ..., this supports ... because ...". Never ask the child about their family's beliefs, languages or where they were born; a made-up town does the asking.
- **One projector.** Show the card or the strip large; children mark paper cards; the spoken part is heard at a table.
