All assessment items are teacher judged. Score each named component 0, absent or inaccurate; 1, partial; 2, accurate and supported. Accept alternative readings with evidence. Missing evidence is an omission, not an inferred misconception.

For the day 114 argument, use the prompt: Who is punished in the novel, and by whom? Defend a significant claim about how private guilt and community judgment interact. Use evidence from the beginning, middle and end, compare The Minister's Black Veil, and answer a fair counterreading for classmates who know the works. The formative writing guide gives 0 to 2 each for W.11-12.1a's significant claim and organization, 1b's fair development and audience awareness, 1c's cohesion, 1d's formal objective style, and 1e's warranted conclusion. W.11-12.4 asks whether development and style fit this purpose; W.11-12.5 uses the plan, written feedback and revision; W.11-12.6 uses the saved digital versions. Only W.11-12.9a is scored on day 114: relevant and sufficient located evidence, application of literary analysis, and a supported comparison, each 0 to 2. Meets: Public signs expose some guilt while concealing other suffering. In chapter II, Hester faces the crowd wearing her embroidered letter, making her punishment visible. In chapter XII, Dimmesdale stands on the scaffold at night but cannot acknowledge Pearl publicly: concealed guilt punishes him without a legal sentence. In chapter XXIV, Hester voluntarily resumes the letter and becomes a counselor, so the sign no longer means only imposed disgrace. In the tale's final speech, Hooper sees veils on every face, extending concealed guilt beyond himself; the final burial paragraph keeps his veil in place. Unlike Hester's imposed letter, his chosen sign isolates him even in death. These details challenge the claim that visible punishment identifies everyone who suffers. Does not: The claim rests on one chapter, quotes the letter with no explanation and leaves the tale unused, so evidence is partial or absent (add A only if the student states that only Hester suffers).

Day 115's theme components are two defensible themes, their development across the poem, their interaction, an objective summary, and accurate whole-novel breadth, each 0 to 2. Meets: Each and All links failed attempts to possess beauty with the discovery of interdependence. The opening connection between lives, the removed bird and shells, and the final sensory return make beauty depend on relationship. Objectively, the speaker describes connected lives, finds removed objects disappointing, rejects beauty, then experiences it within a larger whole. Does not: Beauty and nature are its themes; the speaker is foolish (B for the disconnected theme list; C for the evaluative summary).

Breadth meets, scored from the retained tracker and summary, not from this answer: the scaffold scenes alter the relation between private knowledge and public interpretation, although the outcomes differ.

Day 115's period components are a shared topic, a supported distinction using both cold poems, and an accurate eighteenth-century comparison from the retained record, each 0 to 2. Meets: Both nineteenth-century poems connect an individual to an unseen larger life. Emerson changes his understanding of beauty through surroundings; Longfellow discovers a song sustained in a friend's memory. Wheatley's eighteenth-century address challenges exclusion within Christianity, while Occom's sermon names his listener "a despised creature" yet puts Negroes, Indians and English under one divine judgment. Does not: All three centuries say the same thing, and publication date proves the comparison (E).
