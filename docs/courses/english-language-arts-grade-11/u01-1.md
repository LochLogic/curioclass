Shares of the standards. Each is begun here and owned later. Nothing from days 1 to 5 is scored (s01 field 5), so every task here is first teaching.

- **~RL.11-12.1, "cite".** Days 1 and 2: mark what the story says (S) apart from what a reader guesses (Q, an open question), then defend two readings of one open question with short exact phrases. Day 5 carries the move into a seminar.

- **~W.11-12.10, "write routinely".** Day 2: a short claim, evidence and reasoning in one sitting. Day 5: the weekly paragraph, written for a peer.
- **~L.11-12.3 and L.11-12.3a, "vary syntax for effect".** Day 2 only: unpack one long sentence and recombine it two ways. Day 7 returns to it.
- **~RI.11-12.1, "cite", and ~RI.11-12.10, "read and comprehend".** Day 3: sort an account into what it claims, implies and cannot prove. Day 5 uses the sort against Brown.
- **~L.11-12.4 with 4a, 4b, 4c and 4d, and ~L.11-12.6, "acquire and use accurately".** Day 4: infer, test a related form, consult an 1828 entry, verify. Each student adds one word they needed.
- **~SL.11-12.1 with 1a to 1d.** Day 5: prepared passages (1a), roles, goal and deadline chosen together (1b), probing questions and a hearing for every position (1c), a synthesis that names what more is needed (1d). Instruction only here; scored in section 1.3.

**Limits that bite here.** The Salem trials, accusations of witchcraft and executions are named plainly; the story's period word "Indian" is read as written in 1835 and said to be of its time; no one acts out a trial, an accusation or a hanging. Brown is an allegory: a symbol is not proof. Mather is read for what he reports and assumes, not for a verdict on Bridget Bishop. Only print 1828 entries are consulted, copied unchanged, with no modern site commentary.

**Connections.** RL.11-12.1 with RI.11-12.1, day 5 (one question put to a story and to an account). L.11-12.4b with the conceive family, day 4. W.11-12.10 with SL.11-12.1, day 5 (the talk feeds the paragraph).

**What the teacher page must explain.** Spectral evidence: the court admitted a victim's report that a person's spirit tormented her; no one else could see it, so it could not be tested. A covenant, for Puritans, was an agreement with God that defined the town; Faith is a wife and also a signal that the story is an allegory. The dream frame lets Brown be read two ways, and neither is the answer. Mather defended the court and printed this account in 1693, so the class reads it as a source with an interest, not as news (from memory). Webster's 1828 dictionary records how words were used then; it does not settle modern meaning. The sensitivity reads in unit field 10 are background for the teacher only.

## 3. Checks, written first

<!-- skel -->
<!-- /skel -->

**Unit assessment items owned:** none. A1 to A8 (day 14) and B1 to B9 (day 15) belong to section 1.3 (s01 field 5). No work from days 1 to 5 is scored.

**Exit questions.** Read after class by the teacher against the answer, about a minute each (ours, untested). If many (more than a third) miss one, the next day's warm-up or Return and repair part (5 minutes) opens with that error's wrong example. Each exit asks about a passage held aside from the day's teaching and reading marks (rule 4), and each is a new case that needs the day's idea (rule 5).

- **E1.** "In the paragraph that describes the traveller's staff, say what the story shows directly and what stays a guess." Answer: a carved staff is described that seems to twist like a living snake; whether it really moved or was a trick of sight is left open, so neither reading is proved. Wrong: the staff was a live snake, so the traveller is the devil (O); it was only a stick, so nothing here is uncertain (O).
- **E2.** "In the paragraph where Brown walks back into the village the next morning, defend two readings of his distrust. Then rewrite the paragraph's last sentence so that the greeting he withholds comes first, keeping every fact." Answer: the forest meeting really happened, or he dreamed it, each with a short exact phrase, and either explains his distrust; the rewrite puts the missing greeting first and keeps the stern and sad look and the passing on. Wrong: states one reading as the true one (O); cuts the look or the greeting and says the meaning is unchanged (G).
- **E3.** "Read the last two sentences of the Mather paragraph labelled II. State one thing the report claims and one thing it cannot prove." Answer: it claims that a witness testified that Bishop said she could not be troubled to see the afflicted thus tormented (and that many such accidents fell out while she was at the bar); it cannot prove that she caused the torments, since it reports words and effects, not their cause. Wrong: she said she was not troubled, so she is the witch who caused it (A).
- **E4.** "In the paragraph that introduces the seated traveller, infer what grave means from the sentence about his clothes, then check it against the printed 1828 entry and say which sense fits." Answer: sober or plain, because it describes his attire; the entry's adjective sense, not the burial-place noun. Wrong: grave means a burial place, since the word is familiar (C); writes a meaning from memory and calls it the entry (E); keeps the first guess without rereading the sentence (F).
- **E5.** "Beside Brown's last tombstone sentence put Mather's single opening sentence. Write a synthesis of two positions your group raised: attribute each, cite a phrase from both texts, and say what evidence is still needed." Answer: attributes one position (the tombstone with no hopeful verse shows Brown stayed corrupt) and another (an indictment is a charge drawn up in form, not a finding); the relation is stated (Brown's sentence gives a verdict on a life, Mather's gives only a charge; neither settles guilt or corruption) and independent testimony is named as the missing evidence. Wrong: both writers sound sure, so both are right (A); two sentences, one for each speaker, with no link (L).

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: testimony, spectre, indicted, covenant, evidence, uncertain, claim. Frame: "The text says __, so I think __, but it does not show __." A bilingual glossary is allowed except on cold passages.
