# Scope and sequence: English Language Arts Grade 11 (American literature)

Course `english-language-arts-grade-11`, grade 11, draft, 2026-11-15.

The 160 course days are 160 school days: five meetings of about 50 minutes a week for 32 weeks. There are 148 days in the frame's nine units and 12 course-level check and respond days. The day-by-day authority is `data/plans/english-language-arts-grade-11/days.csv`; its blank school_day cells mean course day equals school day. Sections divide work into runs of at most six days, without changing unit ownership. W.11-12.9a is taught in unit 6 as the frame requires, the second performance of the argument row owned by unit 3.

## Reading, writing and talk through the year

The text record is `data/plans/ela-texts.csv`. Every required anchor and paired text has a reading placement in the CSV, including the complete prefatory materials and Appendix of Douglass, The Custom-House, all six Dickinson poems, the full Whitman elegy and the entire one-act play. Song of Myself and the longer founding and tenement sources use the selections specified in the record. Texts are never replaced by a simpler summary. Unseen assessment selections and withheld interpretations are chosen in the unit plans and kept out of instruction; their identity, length, rights and demands must be recorded before use.

Demand moves from short but difficult older stories, through aphoristic essays and founding argument, to independently managed research and whole long prose works, then condensed poetry and cross-format judgment. This is a rise in the reader's work and decreasing routine scaffolding, not a claim that every successive text has a higher numerical measure. Poems and drama need qualitative judgments; Douglass's lower reported measure does not remove the demand of narrative framing, argument and historical language. Before reading, supply necessary context briefly; during reading, guide the hardest sentence or passage; after reading, reread for a purpose. Begin fading routine supports in unit 6 according to evidence. Keep access supports and record supported proficiency separately from any independent extension.

The long works use about 20 to 30 minutes of home reading on school nights as the frame authorizes. Douglass is distributed across days 82 to 89. The novel's chapters run across days 96 to 111, with The Custom-House spread over days 96 to 100 and the paired tale over days 106 to 108. The chapter assignments are reading endpoints, not instructions to read everything aloud in class. The teacher checks actual load in the unit plan before adopting the frame's period assumption.

Each teaching day has partner evidence exchange and a written exit ticket about the text. A weekly one-sitting analytic paragraph occurs on the fifth available teaching meeting, using that day's text and folding into an already scheduled response or draft when appropriate. Every unit has both an explicit seminar and a shorter teacher-led text discussion in its day notes. Students prepare evidence, choose or create roles, set a goal and deadline, practise civil decisions and a hearing for all positions, probe reasoning, and synthesize contradictions and further research. Those routines begin in unit 1 and recur without becoming new owned rows in every unit.

Teach about 8 to 10 words per week from the current texts before, during and after reading, with later reuse and historical-reference checks. This follows the frame's weekly rate rather than treating the progression's approximate fifteen words per unit as a cap on a long unit. Students also add three words per unit from independent reading, with sentence and source. Unit plans select the complete weekly lists. The CSV names initial examples and recurring word work, not a fabricated complete list. Unpack and recombine one anchor sentence weekly and apply a choice to the student's writing.

The five process pieces are due on days 31, 51, 75, 114 and 150. Each has a course-written model, a rubric for its type and a named peer response plus teacher conference. Three cold one-sitting essays fall on days 80, 118 and 157. The independent American book is chosen from the frame's shelf on day 1, with progress in reading logs throughout, a conference on day 13 and the year-end conference on day 158. No single book is silently made compulsory in place of that shelf.

Assessment rows list parent codes and their taught lettered parts. Every listed part needs a short prompt or the product or observed performance explicitly named as its evidence. Full-length writing and individual observations are collected once in a window. End days and nearby course-level checks reuse the record, rather than assigning another full piece or conference. Unit-plan task specifications must keep the bounded components within the period. Grade 12 retains Shakespeare in RL.4 and RL.7, legal reasoning in RI.8, early twentieth-century completion of RL.9 and independent high-band proficiency. The grade 11 marks are partial-band teaching, not claims to finish those grade 12 expectations.

## Unit 1. Stories built on doubt, 1693 to 1890

16 days, course days 1 to 16, weeks 1 to 4. Sections 1.1 to 1.3 move from Hawthorne and Mather, to Bierce and the start of Poe, to completed Poe, synthesis and assessment. The compelling question is what these stories refuse to settle and how we know. Mather establishes the nonfiction counterpart: uncertainty must belong to the account, not to a reader who lacks background. Evidence and the year's reading, discussion, word, syntax and writing routines need more time here than any one short story alone would require.

Retrieve the grade 10 prerequisites on days 1 to 6 as inventoried. No older assumed ELA row calls for readiness. Day 14 assesses literary evidence, uncertainty, structure and aesthetic effect, word strategies a to d, syntax for reading and writing, and academic-word use. Day 15 assesses nonfiction evidence and uncertainty and discussion a to d; partner preparation and the teacher-led discussion record from day 8 supply the other discussion settings. The day 13 conference and accumulated short and extended writing stand for reading-band and writing-routine evidence; they are not collected again. Day 16 is the single response allocation. No mid-unit assessment is required in a 16-day unit.

Candidate decisions: keep Gothic as a mode and narrator reliability for 15 minutes of day 12's evidence discussion, with no added day. Drop the second Salem examination record because Mather already supplies the nonfiction uncertainty task and the year routines need the available time. Trials, executions, hanging and premature entombment are flagged before the relevant readings, without accusation or trial role play.

## Unit 2. Trust thyself: Emerson and Thoreau, 1841 to 1873

18 days, course days 17 to 34, weeks 4 to 7. Sections 2.1 to 2.4 read Emerson, complete Thoreau and begin Alcott, build the informative draft, then publish, update and assess. The question is what these writers ask a person to trust and what it costs. Central ideas must be traced before students define the movement; the later Alcott account tests the definition rather than merely illustrating it. Term refinement and figurative nuance feed the explanatory writing.

Retrieval occurs on days 17 to 25. Day 21 includes a five-minute history bridge because the reform context comes later. Day 31 is the informative essay deadline; day 32 assesses that same product, its full a to f requirements and the process and technology-update record. W.6 is demonstrated through an individual shared-device contribution during the school day, with no student account; paper publication alone leaves that part unfinished. Day 33 is a cold essay assessment of interacting central ideas and objective summary, term refinement and senses, figures in context and near-synonym nuance. Day 34 responds once to both assessments. No mid-unit assessment is required.

Drop the Candidate additional Emerson essay, The American Scholar. The two complete essays and Alcott already give the informative piece three sources; another long essay would displace the feedback and update cycle inside these 18 days. Slavery and the war with Mexico are named before Thoreau, without asking students to break rules.

## Unit 3. Covenant and consent: founding words, 1620 to 1865

21 days, course days 35 to 55, weeks 7 to 11. Sections 3.1 to 3.4 establish covenant and advocacy, read promises beside excluded voices, connect Lincoln and changing conventions to the process argument, then assess. The question asks what each text promises, to whom, and how well it argues. Paine precedes evaluation of the Declaration; the Native-authored and Black-authored accounts then test the promises. Lincoln answers the set after a five-minute Civil War bridge. Wheatley and Occom are first read here, with their formal period comparison deliberately saved for unit 6.

Grade 10 document, reasoning, structure, usage and argument rows are retrieved before the respective new work. History's revolutionary and constitutional context is available by days 19 and 34 respectively; the founding documents appear here after those boundaries. Day 51 assesses the existing Thoreau process argument, a to e and nonfiction evidence application, due in week 11. Day 52 assesses advocacy premises, purposes and reasoning plus structural clarity, persuasion and engagement. Day 53 separately assesses themes, purposes and rhetorical features across two cold foundational documents. Day 54 assesses convention change, reference-based resolution, hyphenation and spelling. Day 55 is the one response day for this window. No mid-unit assessment is required.

Drop the Candidate Henry speech. Its reconstructed textual history is worthwhile but would add another advocacy source and source-history lesson at the cost of writing feedback or the Native-authored readings. Also drop the optional short King quotation for this sequence: Thoreau, Paine and the Declaration already supply the required argument evidence; adding a recent quotation can be reconsidered only with its unit-plan rights record and counted time. The Declaration's slur is omitted and disclosed, never spoken. Occom, Wheatley and Apess remain authors with arguments, not background illustrations.

## Unit 4. Reform, 1830 to 1860: a research project

23 days, course days 56 to 78, weeks 12 to 16. Sections 4.1 to 4.5 cover short inquiry and question formation, source gathering, drafting, revision and talks, then assessment and response. The question asks what one reform changed and left undone, with each student narrowing a question within the topic area. Three days on a short three-source project recover the whole cycle before the sustained five-source project. Source judgment and synthesis precede drafting; opposing perspectives and digital-media choices precede rehearsal.

The shared calendar is question by day 60, week 12; annotated source list by day 65, week 13; draft by day 70, week 14; single essay and talk by day 75, week 15. History supplies the question area and source set, ELA the process, citation and talk, with separate scoring of one product. If unpaired, this unit supplies context and the full cycle. The history frame's unit 4 ends on day 71, so the day 75 milestone does not assume history still has unit 4 days: the shared-project handoff remains a coordination item for the lead.

Research retrieval starts day 56, presentation retrieval day 59. Day 76 assesses research transfer on a cold bounded packet and reuses the short and sustained project records for full W.7 and W.8 performance. Day 77 assesses the presentation record, including informal and formal tasks, distinct perspective, opposing views, reasoning, style, strategic digital media and register, with only a brief cold audience-adaptation rationale. Talks occur once, in parallel small-group panels on days 73 to 75, with teacher observation rotations and saved plans and artifacts. The informal source introduction from day 59 is also collected once. Day 78 responds once. No mid-unit assessment is required in 23 days.

Keep the Candidate annotated source list. It costs about 30 minutes of day 62 and 15 minutes of day 65, replacing unannotated listing, not adding days or a second product. It makes source strengths and limitations visible before drafting. Topic content notes name slavery, alcohol and institutional mistreatment; questions require teacher approval, student sharing is chosen, and there is no role play. Digital contributions are recorded individually; the paper handout does not complete the technology part.

## Unit 5. Douglass: the Narrative and the Fourth of July, 1845 to 1852

14 days, course days 82 to 95, weeks 17 to 19. Sections 5.1 to 5.3 read the early life and literacy turning point, finish the life and Appendix and begin the speech, then compare transcriptions and assess. The question asks how learning to read changed what Douglass could do and argue. The whole life establishes interactions of people, ideas and events before students evaluate the speech's audience effects. Truth's two transcriptions then distinguish a speaker's argument from a later recorder's representation.

Retrieve unfolding ideas on day 82 and rhetoric and speaker evaluation on day 90. History's slavery unit ended day 71; if later, days 82 and 83 give Maryland context through Douglass's own text within their reading time. Day 93 assesses event-idea-person interactions with a cold memoir and the existing whole-work chart. Day 94 assesses rhetorical power and speaker stance, premises, links, reasoning, evidence, word choice, emphasis and tone on a cold heard-and-read address. Day 95 responds once. No mid-unit assessment is required, and no new process piece falls due in these history writing weeks.

Drop the Candidate Jacobs chapters to protect complete reading of Douglass and avoid adding sexual-violence content to this short unit. Drop the optional recent reading-and-freedom quotation as an additional required text: no text or rights record is settled, and the whole Narrative and two-transcription comparison use the available time. Whippings and sexual violence are named first; difficult passages are guided, agency stays central, slurs are omitted with disclosure and never spoken, and equivalent text-based alternatives use the same history wording.

## Unit 6. The Scarlet Letter: guilt and the community

21 days, course days 96 to 116, weeks 20 to 24. Sections 6.1 to 6.4 begin the novel and frame, track interactions across its middle, finish the reading and period comparisons, then complete the literary argument and assessments. The question is who is punished, and by whom. A chapter-by-chapter two-theme tracker supports the longer reading without substituting for it. Eighteenth-century comparison reuses Wheatley and Occom; nineteenth-century comparison joins the novel to Emerson and Hawthorne's earlier story; Winthrop enables a separate cross-period contrast. Comparison claims made before chapter 24 are explicitly provisional and revised after the ending.

Theme retrieval occurs day 96; source-comparison retrieval day 108. History reform is available before this unit; if later, day 96 supplies five minutes on Hawthorne's 1850 and Salem family history. Day 114 assesses the existing literary argument, due week 23, specifically W.9a, the second piece of unit 3's row. Day 115 assesses cold same-period comparison and interacting themes with an objective summary, using the existing whole-novel tracker and final summary and the day 109 eighteenth-century comparison for breadth. Day 116 is the single response allocation. No mid-unit assessment is required.

Drop the Candidate Endicott and the Red Cross. The whole novel, The Custom-House and The Minister's Black Veil already carry the Puritan setting; another tale would reduce full-novel reading or literary-writing time. Adultery, public shaming and a child born outside marriage are named without family-status questions or shaming role play. No extra hard companion text is introduced during history's weeks 20 to 22.

## Unit 7. The self in American poetry: Dickinson and Whitman

10 days, course days 121 to 130, weeks 25 to 26. Sections 7.1 and 7.2 move from Dickinson's six complete poems to Whitman's selected Song of Myself and complete Lilacs, then comparison and assessment. The question asks how the poets make a self out of words. Precise meanings, two senses in one line and fresh language recur across very short and longer poems; word choices must be connected to meaning and tone. The short unit owns one reading row, so it does not receive a novel unit's share of days.

Retrieve grade 10 word-choice work on day 121. Day 129 assesses figurative and connotative meaning, double meaning, fresh or beautiful language and tone on a cold poem. Day 130 responds once, with its last ten minutes reserved for unit 8's quantitative-reading readiness check. No mid-unit assessment is required. Shakespeare is left to grade 12.

Keep the Candidate Paul Laurence Dunbar poem We Wear the Mask for 15 minutes on day 128, inside the existing comparison day and replacing a familiar-poem reread. It adds a third nineteenth-century voice while retaining all six Dickinson poems, the Whitman selection and whole elegy. Its retained addition needs a text record and edition and rights confirmation in unit planning. Death, grief and racial oppression are named without requiring personal loss or personal disclosure.

## Unit 8. How the other half lived: Riis and the tenement

8 days, course days 131 to 138, weeks 27 to 28. Sections 8.1 and 8.2 introduce the source formats, integrate and debate their claims, then assess and respond. The question is what tenement life looked like and how far Riis can be trusted. Riis and Sui Sin Far establish that source perspective matters; census data and testimony then test claims with different kinds of evidence. Dates, categories and denominators are checked before a discrepancy is called a contradiction.

The readiness check is on day 130, before the hook, and informs a five-minute table model or extension on day 133. Grade 10 medium comparison is retrieved after the day 131 hook. History immigration ended day 122; if later, day 131 supplies the frame's one-lesson context through the sources. Day 137 assesses a cold report, photograph, table and orally read testimony, requiring a bounded integrated answer from three formats, each source's credibility and the data's discrepancies. Day 138 responds once. No mid-unit assessment is required.

Drop the Candidate Hine photographs: the second photographer's later child-labor setting would need a new chronology and question, while the existing image, words, numbers and oral testimony already serve the tenement inquiry. Riis's stereotypes are evidence of his viewpoint, slurs are omitted and disclosed and never spoken, and no student supplies a family immigration account.

## Unit 9. Inside the marriage: Chopin, Gilman and Glaspell, with a satire pair

17 days, course days 139 to 155, weeks 28 to 31. Sections 9.1 to 9.3 read the three complete marriage texts, compare satire and build the narrative, then finish the narrative, interpret performances and assess. The question is what the writers show that the surrounding men miss. Author choices in setting, order and character determine who can know what. The Franklin-Twain pair is a brief separate application of stated versus meant, not another anchor topic. Trifles supplies the model for two viewpoints before students draft.

Retrieve character and cultural-point-of-view work on day 139 and narrative on day 142. The grade 8 dramatic-irony connection is freshly taught, not assumed or mislabelled as recent retrieval. Day 150 assesses the existing narrative and revision record for all a to e parts, due at the end of week 30. Day 153 assesses author choices and stated versus meant through cold prose and satire. Day 154 assesses several interpretations against an American play scene and poem, with short withheld versions, not a new full play. Day 155 is the single response allocation for this window. No mid-unit assessment is required. Interpretations are first explicitly taught on days 151 and 152, before their assessment.

The required Trifles comparison uses the public-domain full-cast reading and a short live in-class reading, with transcript access. The Whitman comparison uses two recorded readings of the same section, with contrasting teacher readings if recordings are unavailable. No paid production or new volunteer recording is assumed. Drop the Candidate The Open Boat: it introduces another story and setting when the three existing authors already require complete reading, narrative feedback and performance comparison.

Death, cruel marriage, domestic abuse, mental illness and the antiwar prayer's harm to civilians are flagged before reading. Mental illness is treated through Gilman's framing, without diagnosis; school support contacts and equivalent textual alternatives are required. No harmful reenactment or personal trauma writing is required. Shakespeare's performance comparison remains grade 12.
