# Course frame: Psychology

Course `psychology` (catalogue id `psychology`), grades 11 to 12 (full year; rows sit at grade 12 in the data), draft frame (2026-11-16). The
scope and sequence sets the days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 16: the science and its methods first, then the biological, cognitive, developmental, social and
  sociocultural areas, then health, disorders and treatment, so the course ends on getting help.
- **Days.** 160 course days (a full year): 156 in units and 4 for two course-level checks, each an assessment day and
  a respond day. One lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week, across 32 weeks. Why a full year and not a half:
  the standard has 141 learning targets across 18 standard areas, and the standard's own planning appendix sets about
  two weeks a standard area as the ideal pace for a year-long course; a half year would give each area four or five
  days and drop the study-reading work that every unit needs. 34 rows in 156 unit days is about 4.6 days a row,
  weighted by load, not shared equally: unit 1 (three rows, 15 targets and 3 themes, and the course's research-design
  model) gets 14; units 5, 6, 11 and 16 (six to eight ids each) get 8, the floor. The standard marks ten areas as
  priorities when time is short (Scientific Inquiry, Biological Bases, Cognition, Memory, Life Span Development,
  Learning, Social, Multiculturalism and Gender, Disorders, Health); every priority unit after unit 1 gets about one
  day a target or more, while the non-priority units with many short targets (unit 3, sensation and perception;
  unit 4, consciousness; unit 10, motivation and emotion) get fewer days a target. Unit 16 is not a priority area but
  gets the floor of 8, since the course ends on it. Each
  unit's days include its readiness check or retrieval task, its end-of-unit assessment (the form each unit uses is
  in its last Units column, with any draft, feedback and revision days counted in the unit's days) and one respond
  day (a unit over 25 days would also hold a mid-unit assessment; none is that long). Readiness checks (counted in
  the bullet below): one before unit 2 on four Biology rows (last ten minutes of unit 1's respond day) and one before
  unit 3 on grade 8's senses row (last ten minutes of unit 2's respond day); every other earlier row opens as a
  retrieval task inside a day. Inside the period: the 50 minutes hold handing out and collecting papers, pooling any
  class data slips (about five minutes, counted in the day's plan) and the exit ticket; a readiness check takes ten
  minutes, never more; the course has no laboratory, so no day needs clean-up beyond collecting papers. If the scope
  and sequence finds a unit over, it brings a day-transfer proposal rather than cutting a target, a practice or a
  respond day, and a Shorter path fold-in never takes a draft, feedback, revision or respond day.
- **Day kinds.** Understanding is the first explicit teaching of anything: a concept, theory, method, term set or a
  part of the research-design model; most days in this course open that way. Application is using what an earlier
  day taught on a new task: reading an unseen study or claim through the research-design or biopsychosocial model,
  running an approved class task, drafting or revising the research proposal or the informed action piece. Fluency
  is little in this course (the standard has no fluency targets): at most one short retrieval-practice set a unit on
  terms and models an earlier day taught (unit 6's strategies used on the course's own content), never new content
  and never scored as end-of-unit evidence. No score comes from a day a topic is first taught.
- **Readiness and retrieval, counted from grade 12.** The calendar places the course in grade 12, so the count starts
  there (a row last used in grade g gets retrieval in grade g+1 and a readiness check in grade g+2 or later; the
  scope gate's rule 2 count governs, and the scope fix pass updates this frame). Last uses, from the core courses'
  scopes (days as in data/plans/<course>/days.csv on 2026-11-16):
  - bio-body-systems and bio-homeostasis-feedback: last used grade 9, Biology unit 1 (days 1 to 11). Readiness check
    before unit 2.
  - bio-dna-chromosomes-traits: last used grade 9, Biology unit 6 (days 79 to 91). Readiness check before unit 2.
  - bio-natural-selection-factors: last used grade 9, Biology unit 7 (days 95 to 110). Readiness check before unit 2.
  - bio-sources-of-variation: last used grade 11, Physics unit 7 (days 93 to 104, through phys-em-radiation-effects,
    which assumes it). Retrieval in unit 2.
  - s8-senses-brain: last used grade 9, Biology unit 1 (days 1 to 11, through bio-homeostasis-feedback, which assumes
    it). Readiness check before unit 3.
  - phys-wave-math: last used grade 11, Physics unit 6 (days 77 to 90). Retrieval in unit 3.
  - alg2-study-design-randomness, alg2-experiments-significance, alg2-normal-distributions: last used grade 11, Algebra
    II unit 7 (days 137 to 155). Retrieval in units 1 and 8.
  - alg1-data-displays, alg1-center-spread, alg1-correlation-causation: last used grade 11, Algebra II units 6 and 7
    (days 136 to 155, through alg2-normal-distributions and alg2-study-design-randomness). Retrieval in unit 1 (and in
    unit 11 for the correlation coefficient), as math leans open by default.
  - us1-research-inquiry, us1-argument-writing, us1-news-reading: last used grade 11, US History I units 1, 5 and 9
    (days 2 to 17, 76 to 92 and 144 to 146). Retrieval in unit 1.
  A school that places the course in grade 11 changes three things, each written in its Seams or Cross-subject row:
  the three US History I rows, the three Algebra II rows and phys-wave-math are taught later the same year, so each
  "if later" line applies (the unit teaches the part it needs in brief); bio-sources-of-variation is then last used
  in grade 9 (Physics unit 7 comes after unit 2) and joins the readiness check before unit 2; the three Algebra I rows
  are last used in grade 9 (Algebra II reaches them after unit 1), so psychology uses them first and gives the
  readiness check, in the last ten minutes of unit 1's day 1, after the hook; the Biology and grade 8 rows keep their
  readiness checks unchanged (grade 9 is two grades back from grade 11).
- **Standard.** The American Psychological Association's National Standards for High School Psychology Curricula
  (2022), loaded as data/standards/apa-hs-psych-2022.txt: all 141 learning targets (ids APA22.<area>.<content
  standard>.<target>) and the seven integrative themes (APA22.IT.A to IT.G), each owned by one row; every row sits in
  one unit. The themes are owned where they are taught explicitly (A and B in unit 1, G in unit 1, C in unit 2, E in
  unit 3, D in unit 13, F in unit 14) and named again in later units. The course owns no C3 indicator: grades 9 to 12
  own them all, and this course practises the inquiry ones (D1.5, D3.1, D3.3, D4.1 and, in unit 14, D4.7 and D4.8 at
  9-12) on psychological sources. Each unit plan compares the unit with the check-set members that have psychology
  standards (Tennessee's psychology course) and with the first-year college level psychology course framework, and
  adds inside its days what a strong set treats as essential ("Check set adds"); check-set names never appear in
  student, teacher or family copy. The values rule governs: claims rest on research with its sample and replication
  record; where studies disagree, both lines of evidence are taught and the student judges.
- **Open to grade 11 or 12** (data/courses.csv: grades 11 to 12, typical 12). The rows sit at grade 12 and assume
  three US History I rows from grade 11. A grade 11 student meets those rows in the same year, so each one has an
  "if later" line in Seams: the unit teaches the part it needs in brief. The same holds for Algebra II and Physics in
  Cross-subject; the bullet above gives every count and what changes in grade 11. Every lean is on a core course
  (Biology, Physics, grade 8 science, Algebra I, Algebra II, US History I); the course assumes no elective, and
  Statistics, taken beside it by some students, is never assumed.
- **Base text.** OpenStax Psychology 1e, retired CC BY 4.0 edition (store folder `openstax-retired-ccby\`,
  Psychology-OP.pdf; the current edition is NC-SA and is reference only). Credit line on every adapted day: "Adapted
  from OpenStax, Psychology, CC BY 4.0." The Source map at the end places every chapter and section. The course order
  follows the standard and what teaches best, not the book's: methods before the brain, memory after learning, and
  disorders and treatment last, after stress and health.
- **Where the base text is dated, and our own content instead.** The 1e text (2014) predates several changes, so these
  parts use our own content, written from the current research and the bodies named in Sensitivity reads. Each is
  pinned to the base section that carries it (checked section by section against the PDF, 2026-11-16), and the
  Source map row repeats the flag:
  - Unit 1: the replication crisis and the field's response (larger samples, preregistration, open data). The 1e text
    has none of it; theme A is taught through it.
  - Unit 2 and unit 16: the base text's account of disorders as neurotransmitter "imbalances" corrected by medication
    (3.2; also 15.3 and 15.7) is replaced; medications are described by what they act on and what trials show, and
    "chemical imbalance" is named as a simplification the evidence does not support.
  - Unit 3: the base text's candle-flame detection distance (5.1) is dropped.
  - Unit 10: the facial-feedback hypothesis (10.4) is taught with the large multi-lab replication attempts that
    followed the book; basic emotions (10.4, "seven universal emotions") are taught with a cross-cultural recognition
    study beside the later evidence that expressions vary by culture and context, not as settled.
  - Unit 11: the delay-of-gratification ("marshmallow") findings (11.4, in the person-situation debate) are taught with
    the later, larger replication that tied the effect to family background. Unit 5's check-set add uses the same
    later study, in our own content.
  - Unit 8, and units 9 and 15: the book uses an old term for intellectual disability (7.5's text and box, chapter 7's
    review questions, and 9.3 on fetal alcohol exposure); our pages use current, people-first terms and keep the old
    term only for the history of the name change (and in the 1963 law's title, 16.1). Unit 15 says "psychomotor
    slowing" for the book's older clinical term (15.7).
  - Unit 12: the prison simulation (12.2) is taught as a study whose method and claims have been seriously
    questioned, from the published critiques; the 1964 bystander case (12.6) is taught with the later reporting that
    corrected the "38 witnesses" account, beside newer bystander research (12.7).
  - Unit 13: almost all of it. The book has little on identity, privilege and gender identity, and its terms are
    dated; our own content follows the standard and the current terms of the major health and psychological bodies.
  - Unit 15: the book follows DSM-5 (2013). Our pages use the current manual (DSM-5-TR, 2022) beside the international
    classification (ICD-11) and current prevalence figures with source and date.
  - Unit 16: help resources are our own (the national 988 crisis line dates from 2022), checked for the school's area.
  - Every unit: the book's social media and technology examples are replaced with current ones.
  - Every unit: each chapter's personal application questions are not used as written, since many ask students about
    their own stress, sleep, medication, memories, personality or bullying; where one is useful it is rewritten for a
    made-up person.
- **Studies and data, with their sources.** Every study a day teaches is named by its authors, year and journal, and
  its numbers come from the study itself (the article, or a free author copy or public summary of it), never from a
  textbook's retelling; the answer-key agent checks each figure against the article. Class data are real and pooled
  anonymously; anything else built for practice (a score set, a decision problem, a case vignette, a profile) says
  "invented for practice" on the page. No constructed value is shown as a measurement. Figures that change (prevalence,
  drug use, help lines) carry an "as of" date and the agency page they came from. The replication record of the
  classic studies the course teaches, as of 2026-11-16 (the unit writer re-checks it on the day the unit is written
  and updates the date):
  - Unit 1: the large replication project of 100 published studies (Open Science Collaboration, 2015, in Science):
    97 percent of the originals reported a significant result, about 36 percent of the replications did.
  - Unit 2: twin-study heritability from the meta-analysis of fifty years of twin studies (Polderman and colleagues,
    2015). Unit 4: the teenage sleep shift and school start times from the national pediatrics academy's 2014 policy
    statement; drug-use figures from the national annual survey of teen drug use and the national drug abuse institute,
    as of their latest release. Unit 9: sensitive periods from the follow-up of children adopted out of Romanian
    institutions (Sonuga-Barke and colleagues, 2017).
  - Unit 5: Little Albert (Watson and Rayner, 1920): the child's identity and health are disputed in later scholarship
    (Beck, Levinson and Irons, 2009; Powell and colleagues, 2014), and the study has never been repeated. The Bobo doll
    studies (Bandura, Ross and Ross, 1961) are read for method and limits beside the field's 2015 task force review on
    violent media and its published critiques, which disagree.
  - Unit 6: misinformation (Loftus and Palmer, 1974) has replicated widely; the study-strategy evidence comes from the
    2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues.
  - Unit 8: stereotype threat (Steele and Aronson, 1995): a large preregistered test (Flore, Mulder and Wicherts, 2018)
    found no effect in its sample; the record is mixed. The rise in scores over generations (Flynn, 1987) is well
    replicated and has slowed or reversed in some countries since.
  - Unit 10: facial feedback (Strack, Martin and Stepper, 1988): a 17-lab registered replication (Wagenmakers and
    colleagues, 2016) found no effect; a later multi-lab study (Coles and colleagues, 2022) found small effects for some
    methods. Basic emotions: the Fore study (Ekman and Friesen, 1971) beside the Himba study (Gendron and colleagues,
    2014) and the 2019 review by Barrett and colleagues. Growth mindset: small average effects in the 2018
    meta-analyses (Sisk and colleagues) and a small effect for lower-achieving students in the 2019 national study
    (Yeager and colleagues).
  - Unit 11: delay of gratification (Mischel, Shoda and Rodriguez, 1989; first met in unit 5): the larger replication
    (Watts, Duncan and Quan, 2018) found an effect about half the size, much reduced once family background and early
    ability were controlled. Personality change across adulthood: the 2006 meta-analysis by Roberts, Walton and
    Viechtbauer.
  - Unit 12: obedience (Milgram, 1963) with the partial replication (Burger, 2009) and the archival reanalyses;
    conformity (Asch, 1956) with the cross-cultural meta-analysis (Bond and Smith, 1996); the prison simulation (Haney,
    Banks and Zimbardo, 1973) with the published critique of its method (Le Texier, 2019); the 1964 bystander case with
    the correction of the "38 witnesses" account (Manning, Levine and Collins, 2007) and the study of real public
    conflicts on camera that found someone intervened in about nine of ten (Philpot and colleagues, 2020); implicit bias
    measures with the meta-analysis on how weakly they predict behaviour (Oswald and colleagues, 2013).
  - Unit 13: minority stress (Meyer, 2003); the 1973 vote that removed homosexuality from the diagnostic manual.
  - Unit 14: positive psychology interventions (Bolier and colleagues, 2013) with the reanalysis that found smaller
    effects (White, Uttl and Holder, 2019).
  - Unit 16: the claim that depression is caused by low serotonin: the 2022 umbrella review (Moncrieff and colleagues)
    found no consistent support, and replies dispute what that means for treatment; antidepressant trials from the
    2018 network meta-analysis (Cipriani and colleagues). Help resources and prevalence: the national mental health
    institute's statistics pages and the national 988 crisis line (since July 2022), each with an "as of" date.
- **Where the base text carries suicide, self-harm or eating-disorder content** (checked section by section): 10.3
  (an infant sex-reassignment case ending in a death by suicide, unit 13), 12.6 (bullying and cyberbullying with
  suicide outcomes, unit 12, and its chapter summary), 15.1 (a hypothetical about a death by suicide), 15.7 (mood
  disorders and suicide), 15.10 (self-harm and suicidal gestures), chapter 15's review questions on suicide, 16.2
  (treatment for suicidal thoughts) and 16.5 (youth suicide attempt figures), all in units 15 and 16; eating
  disorders in 10.2 (moved to unit 15). Each of those units follows safe-messaging guidance, and unit 15 also the
  eating-disorder guidance below.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md)
  before it ships; no outside human reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Three shared sources recur: the national
  recommendations for safe messaging about suicide (no methods, no details of a death, help named on the same page,
  recovery shown as possible); the plain-language pages of the national mental health institute on each disorder and
  treatment named; and a journalism centre's disability language style guide. By unit, the communities its days touch
  and what the agent studies first, from those communities where they publish:
  - Unit 1: people harmed by past research (the men in the Tuskegee syphilis study, 1932 to 1972, and their families;
    the children at Willowbrook): the federal research-ethics report of 1979 and the descendants' and survivors' own published accounts.
  - Unit 2: people with brain injury and their families: published first-person accounts and a brain injury
    association's public pages; families formed by adoption or donor conception: adoptee-written accounts.
  - Unit 3: Deaf and blind people: Deaf community and signed-language scholarship and a national blind people's
    organisation's own pages, so senses and their absence are described from within.
  - Unit 4: people who use drugs and people in recovery: the national drug abuse institute's language guidance and
    published recovery accounts; families affected by addiction (a family note, no student asked).
  - Unit 5: the child in the Little Albert study and his family: the later historical scholarship named above.
  - Unit 6: people with dementia and their families, and people wrongly convicted: a national dementia association's
    pages and the exonerees' own published statements in the public case records.
  - Unit 7: multilingual students, dialect speakers and signers: signed-language and dialect scholarship by members of
    those communities.
  - Unit 8: disabled people, immigrants, and Black, Native and other communities harmed by eugenics and testing:
    histories written by disabled people and by those communities, the disability language guide, and the state
    apologies named in unit 8's row.
  - Unit 9: grieving students and families, adopted children and people who grew up in institutions: safe-messaging
    guidance and grief guidance for schools from a national children's grief centre.
  - Unit 10: people with eating disorders and the peoples studied in emotion research (the Fore of Papua New Guinea,
    the Himba of Namibia): a national eating disorders body's guidance for schools (no weights, body sizes, calorie
    figures, descriptions of behaviours or images of bodies), and the studies' own accounts of consent and community.
  - Unit 11: no community flag; the agent checks the made-up profiles for stereotypes.
  - Unit 12: young people who have been bullied, and the communities named in obedience and prejudice studies:
    safe-messaging guidance and the national bullying-prevention agency pages; communities near recent violence
    (aggression examples avoid them).
  - Unit 13: racial and ethnic communities, immigrants and LGBTQ people: published first-person accounts and the major
    health and psychological bodies' current guidance on terms for race, ethnicity, gender identity and sexual
    orientation; safe-messaging guidance for the infant sex-reassignment case.
  - Unit 14: students under stress and anyone in crisis: safe-messaging guidance and the national crisis line's pages.
  - Unit 15: people living with mental illness, autistic people and people with ADHD, people with eating disorders:
    the national mental health institute's pages, autistic self-advocates' published guidance on terms, a national
    mental illness alliance's peer accounts, the eating disorders guidance and safe-messaging guidance.
  - Unit 16: people in treatment, people who were harmed by forced treatment (including gay people), families seeking
    help: the national mental illness alliance's peer pages, histories of psychiatric survivors in their own words,
    the disability language guide and safe-messaging guidance.
  The units with health content (units 4, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16) also get the health expert agent's accuracy
  read (docs/agents/lenses/health.md).
- **Mental health rules.** No role play or simulation puts a student in a patient's or a victim's place: no acting out
  symptoms, no simulated diagnosis of a classmate, no re-enactment of an obedience, prison or emergency study. No
  student discloses their own mental health, or a family member's, in class or in any graded work; examples are made-up
  people, published studies and published first-person accounts. No student diagnoses themselves, a classmate, a
  public figure or a fictional character. The school counselor is told before the course opens, with the units that
  carry suicide or self-harm content (9, 12, 13 and 14 to 16), and again before each of them; every page in units 14
  to 16, and every page in units 9, 12 and 13 that touches suicide, names where to get help. A student who shares a
  concern is directed privately to the counselor.
- **Research ethics (teacher note).** Students design studies, and run only minimal-risk, anonymous class tasks a
  teacher approves (a reaction-time task, a word-list memory task, a perception illusion, an anonymous sleep log that
  is optional and never graded). Any study that would need consent the school cannot give (on mood, health, family,
  identity or private behaviour, or using deception) stays a design on paper, and no study is run on classmates or
  other students outside the approved tasks. Results are pooled and anonymous. No unit has a physical hazard (no
  laboratory, no field work, no food or equipment beyond paper); the safety read checks each class task against this
  note, and any timed task lets a student sit out without a mark.
- **Paper path.** Every source, data set and task has a printed version; no day needs a device, an internet connection
  or a student account. Class data are collected on paper slips and pooled by the teacher; brain images, sleep records
  and study graphs are printed with source and date; the informed action piece is printed and posted through the
  school.
- **Devices.** None per student; one teacher display is optional for showing a printed figure larger. No owned APA
  target or theme names technology, so a paper-path student meets every owned code in full. Tools below are named by
  their job; each has a printed version.
- **Literacy standards (also met): practised, not owned.** The unit work practises these Common Core literacy
  standards for science and technical subjects (data/standards/ccss-literacy-6-12.txt; the rows name them as leans),
  with no added days: RST.11-12.8 (units 1 and 14), RST.11-12.7 (unit 1), RST.11-12.2 (unit 2), WHST.11-12.7 (unit 1,
  the research proposal) and WHST.11-12.2 (unit 14, the informed action piece). None is claimed in full: the course
  does not teach and check each lettered part of WHST.11-12.2 (a to f), and its scores are for the APA targets, so a
  literacy standard is practised, never owned or reported here. WHST.11-12.6 is not claimed: the paper path keeps
  every product off a device.
- **Owned, begun and revisited, one way.** Every APA22 id is owned by one row and sits in the Owned standards cell of
  one unit, and that unit's end-of-unit evidence checks it; the course-level checks sample, never re-own. Nothing is
  begun here for a later course (this is the only psychology course). A later unit that uses an earlier target names
  it in What students learn as "revisited (review)", never in Owned standards: the themes A to G after their owning
  unit; unit 12's stereotypes and prejudice (psy-social-cognition) in unit 13; unit 13's minority stress in unit 14;
  unit 5's conditioning in unit 16 (exposure therapy); delay of gratification (unit 5's check-set add) in unit 11's person-situation debate.
  C3 inquiry indicators are "practised", owned by the grade 9 to 12 social studies courses.
- **Tools and what Curio Class writes, by unit.** Tools are named by their job (the scope writes TOOL-ASKS.md from

  - Unit 2. Tools: a printed brain and neuron atlas, a twin-study data card reader. Writes: the brain-imaging tools

  - Unit 4. Tools: a sleep-record reader (a typical night's sleep stages, labelled a typical pattern), the optional

  - Unit 5. Tools: a conditioning scenario sorter. Writes: the Little Albert reading with the later scholarship, the

  - Unit 6. Tools: a word-list memory task and a neutral misinformation task (both printed). Writes: wrongful

  No supplies beyond printing and paper slips, so no cost line per unit; printing is the school's.
- **Review roles.** Review roles follow FOUNDATION section 7.3, revised 2026-11-14: the sensitivity, safety and
  answer-key agents do the content reads, each cold and after studying its sources; the sources named here stay
  binding. The course-level checks get the answer-key agent's read before the course is called finished.
- **Inquiry arc and informed action.** Every unit runs on its compelling question, answered from the studies the unit
  reads, with a study or data task the student has not seen at its end. The year's informed action is in unit 14
  (psy-wellness-action): students choose a question their peers face where psychological science has clear evidence
  (study habits, sleep, screen use and attention, a popular wellness claim), check it against research and public
  health sources, and make a short information piece shared through the school. It takes 2 of the unit's days. Never a
  cause the course picks, and never suicide, self-harm, eating disorders or drug use as its topic. Classroom-only
  version: the piece is shared with the class and filed, unsigned, where the school permits no posting; it needs no
  money, travel or public identity.
- **The grade 12 timetable.** Psychology takes its own period all year, beside US History II (all year) and the second
  social studies period that holds US Government and Politics in the fall half and Economics in the spring half
  (docs/courses/us-history-ii/frame.md). School day = course day. A grade 11 student takes it beside US History I.
- **Senior calendar.** The last weeks hold teaching: unit 16 (help-seeking) is the course's last unit and keeps all 8
  of its days, and the year-end check and its respond day are the course's last two days (days 159 and 160, course
  week 32). Unit 14's informed action is finished in course week 28, so it never waits on the last weeks. Grade 12 may
  end early: a school that ends seniors up to a week early folds up to five days, one each from units 9, 10, 11, 12
  and 13, by merging that unit's check-set addition into its owned days and dropping one Application day of study
  reading; a fold never takes a readiness, draft, feedback, revision, end-of-unit or respond day, and never a day of
  units 14 to 16. The year-end check then sits in the seniors' last week, still followed by its respond day. A school
  that ends seniors more than a week early brings the gap to the lead before the year opens. A grade 11 student has
  the full year and folds nothing.

never a kept section, and no base review question is used as a scored item without the answer-key agent's read.

not the book: at most about 800 words of it a day at a grade 11 to 12 reading level, read in class with the day's
key terms glossed, and a printed copy for every student.
