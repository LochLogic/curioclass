# Unit 1.1: Rectangles and factor pairs

Grade 4 math. Stretch 1, Factors and Multiples (days 1 to 6): see [s01.md](s01.md). **Days 1 to 3.** Days by
kind: understanding 2, fluency 1, use 0, check 0. A plan, not a lesson: nothing here is built or tried with
children. Fields follow [UNIT-PLAN.md](../UNIT-PLAN.md).

## 1. Standards and practices

- **4.OA.B.4, this unit's share.** Part (a): students **find** all factor pairs for a whole number from 1 to
  100.
- **Limits that bite here.** No factor trees. Students "need not be fluent" in finding all pairs
  (Progressions), so nothing is done for speed.
- **Connections.** None from grade 4 yet. Uses 3.MD.C.7 (days 1, 2) and 3.OA.B.6 (day 3).
- **Strategies or algorithm.** None set. Taught, from the Progressions: try 1, 2, 3 in order until the
  pairs turn around.
- **Practices.** MP.7: day 2, a rectangle and its turned copy are one pair. MP.8: day 3, after the turn
  every answer repeats.
- **What the teacher page must explain.** Why the search stops at the turn: once the number tried is larger
  than its partner, that partner has already been tried.

## 2. Where it sits

The year's first unit. It starts in the last 25 minutes of day 1, after the start-of-year check (stretch
field 4). E3 results go to unit 1.2. Facts check, part 1: day 3's warm-up.

## 3. Checks, written first

**End-check items owned:** 1 and 6, given on day 6 (stretch field 5).

**Exit questions.**

- **E1.** "On the grid, draw every rectangle with area 10. Write a multiplication equation inside each one.
  Can 10 squares make a rectangle with 4 rows? Show why or why not." Answer: 1 by 10 and 2 by 5, with 1 × 10
  = 10 and 2 × 5 = 10. No: 4 rows of 2 is 8 and 4 rows of 3 is 12. Wrong: only 2 by 5 (A); 2 by 5 and 5 by 2
  counted as two (C); "yes", with a shape whose last row is short (not a rectangle).
- **E2.** "Find all the factor pairs of 75. Start at 1 and go in order. How do you know you have them all?"
  Answer: 1 × 75, 3 × 25, 5 × 15. After 5, nothing from 6 to 8 works, and 9 × 9 is already more than 75, so
  any new pair would be one of these turned. A student may say: "the next numbers that work are 15 and 25,
  and I have them." Wrong: no 3 × 25 or no 5 × 15 (B); no 1 × 75 (A); 15 × 5 as a fourth pair (C); "I just
  know".
- **E3.** No grid. "Find all the factor pairs of 98. Then: is 6 a factor of 98? Show how you know." Answer:
  1 × 98, 2 × 49, 7 × 14. No: 6 × 16 = 96 and 6 × 17 = 102. Wrong: no 7 × 14 (B); "yes, 98 is even" (E,
  met again in unit 1.2). Results go to unit 1.2.

## 5. What is carried, and instruments

- **Carried.** The rectangle of unit squares. Tiles in hand on day 1, each rectangle tied to its equation
  that day. On day 2 the written form grows into the ordered list of pairs. All withdrawn on day 3: lists
  only.
- **Key moment 1, day 1: a number makes only some rectangles, and each is a multiplication.** Ideal: square
  tiles and grid paper in the student's hands. No tool.
- **Key moment 2, day 2: trying the rows in order finds every pair, and after the turn nothing new comes.**
  Ideal: a fixed count of objects that deals itself into any number of rows at once and shows what is left
  over, so 1 to 12 rows can be tried on 36 or 60 in minutes. **Egg Carton Factory's Pack Station fits, and
  it is the key tool.** Gaps: (1) the built code is behind the spec; (2) nothing asks for the rows in order,
  so the paper table carries the system; (3) a fit reads "4 rows of 9 = 36", never 4 × 9 = 36; (4) no model
  is shown before it asks; (5) the seal card says "prime" and "composite" before unit 1.2 teaches them.
- **Key moment 3, day 3: pairs outside the times tables, with no objects.** Ideal: paper, building from ten
  times; then cases with feedback on whether the list is complete. The Rush cards called Every shape
  (shift 1, cards 4a, 4b) partly fit: each Pack still shows the eggs.
- **A room with nothing.** Squares cut from grid paper; the rows table by hand.

## 7. Talk and writing

- Planned questions: "Is a 3 by 4 rectangle a different rectangle from a 4 by 3? Is it a different factor
  pair?" (day 1). "You found 6 × 6 for 36. Why is every fit after that one you already have?" (day 2). "How
  can 3 be a factor of 42 when 42 is not in the 3 times table you learned?" (day 3).
- Written task, day 3: "Write how you know when to stop looking for factor pairs of 64."
- Judged by the teacher with two look-fors: says the numbers were tried in order; says the pairs turn around
  (8 × 8, or "the next pair is one I have"). One short task is honest for this unit.

## 8. Practice that returns

- **Warm-up.** Day 1: none; the check takes its place. Day 2: the readiness reteach for those who need it;
  adding and subtracting within 1,000 for the rest. Day 3: the facts check, part 1.
- **Fluency target.** None; the standard sets none.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: factor, factor pair, row, column, product, on a labelled rectangle. Frame:
  "__ and __ are a factor pair of __, because __ × __ = __."
- **Access.** Large tiles and grid; a student who cannot move tiles directs a partner. A multiplication
  table for a student below the facts mark.
- **Check accommodations.** Exit questions read aloud; lists may be said aloud or scribed.
- **Reading load.** Low. The written task may be dictated.
- **Extension, same standard.** Which numbers to 50 have an odd number of factors, and why? Which number
  under 100 has the most factor pairs?

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Use 24 coins or paper squares. "Make every rectangle you can. Write each one as a
  multiplication." Next day, write the rows table for 36 together. Ask the questions in field 7 and wait.
  If you know a shortcut for when to stop, hold it back until the child has seen the pairs turn.
- **One projector.** Day 2: for each number of rows the class writes "fits, with __" or "no" before the
  teacher drags the edge. Day 3: everyone writes the whole list, then the class ships it.
