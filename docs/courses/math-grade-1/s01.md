## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What can counting tell us about adding, and about our class?"
- **Hook, day 1:** Show four paper squares, cover them after counting, then add three beside the cover. Ask, "How can we find how many without starting again?" Connect answers to the compelling question. This adapts the counting-collections opening named in field 10.
- **Supporting questions:** What stays counted when we add (day 3)? How can a display keep every response (day 8)? Which questions can our display answer (day 13)?
- **Closing claim:** "I can keep a counted group, count on to add or find a difference, and use a display to ask and answer questions." The counting record and imaginary-animal survey support it; day 16 checks it.
- **Teaching:** Model a count with one movement per added word, then compare counting all with counting on. Model sorting before symbols. Step back during days 13 to 15 as children choose questions and defend answers. Teacher pages explain why the starting quantity is already counted and why subtraction counts the steps, not the ending number.

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Counts the starting number as the first step instead of keeping it still. | Off-by-one counting. Day 3: wrong 6 + 2 = 7 beside correct 8. Hold six still and move only for seven and eight. | From memory. |
| B | Gives the ending counting word as the subtraction answer. | Confuses total with counted steps. Day 12: wrong 15 - 11 = 15 beside correct 4. Mark one dot per step and count those dots. | From memory. |

| D | Places one response into two categories. | Categories overlap in the record. Day 8: one square in two groups beside one square in one group. Move each slip onto exactly one category mat. | From memory. |
| E | Leaves a response out of the representation. | Loses one-to-one tracking. Day 9: four marks for five slips beside five marks. Pair each slip with one mark. | From memory. |
| F | Gives the larger category count as the difference. | Confuses how many with how many more. Day 13: counts 9 and 4, wrong difference 9 beside correct 5. Match pairs and count unmatched marks. | From memory. |

Survey groups never exceed 20 responses. For 30 children use two groups of 15; each child makes an individual record. Participation is optional: offer an anonymous pretend slip instead of requiring a personal choice. In days 12 to 15, a category difference is counted up from the smaller count, with each new word recorded as a step. Arithmetic within data tasks supports major work but receives no extra major-minute credit beyond the scope and sequence allocation.

## U7. Talk and writing

Day 3: "What have we already counted?" Day 12: "Does the last number tell the total or the steps?" Day 13: "Can this display answer your question?" Day 15's individual survey joins drawing and questions; judge it using field 5.

## U8. Practice that returns

Use five-minute mixed counting and taught-fact retrieval daily, without a timer. Days 6, 10 and 14 repeat known partner routines; partners check with counters after an attempt. Keep the numerical facts reserved for checks out of these decks.

## U9. Supports and extension

Read all prompts aloud; no independent reading is required. Pair picture labels with group, total, more and fewer. Offer "I kept __ and counted __ more." Use large, high-contrast outlines and tactile paper mats; a child may point while an adult scribes. Say counting words rather than requiring finger movements. Extend by representing the same three-category data another way and explaining why totals and differences agree.

## U10. Two rooms

At home, use a 35-minute path: hook or retrieval 5, model 5, paper practice 15, talk 5, exit 5. Replace partners with an adult and surveys with anonymous pretend slips. Spread the single counting observation across days 14 to 15 as needed, without repeating the child; day 16 uses 5 minutes for revisions, 20 for the data product, 5 for checking and 5 to close. In class, one shared display is optional; every child works on paper.
