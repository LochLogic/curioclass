## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Did the meetings of peoples around the Mediterranean and beyond, 750 to 1500, bring more exchange or more harm?"
- **Hook, day 75:** Compare Perratore's account of artistic exchange with Cohen's warning about idealized tolerance. Can exchange coexist with unequal rights? Open the compelling question.
- **Supporting questions, drafted together:** Who could coexist, on what terms (75)? Why did crusading begin, change direction and end (77)? What shaped accounts (78)? How did slavery vary (82)? Why did plague spread and recur (84)? Which effects lasted (86)?
- **Claims:** Day 80's one-to-two page Crusades argument uses Fulcher, Solomon, Usama and Choniates, answering one counterclaim. Day 87's closing claim answers the compelling question using those accounts, the slavery comparison and plague evidence. Share with a partner or adult; qualify whose gains and losses are compared.
- **Teaching:** Model sourcing on 75, interacting motives on 76, perspective on 78 and a counterclaim on 79. Withdraw completed writing models on 80. Model competing causal pathways on 83; students build independently on 86. Teach coexistence, tolerance, crusade, pilgrimage, jihad, pogrom, hereditary, manumission, pandemic and mortality through evidence.


- **D2.His.4.9-12:** Analyze interacting influences on people's perspectives across historical eras. Parts, by verb: analyze factors; analyze their interaction.
- **D2.Geo.12.9-12:** Evaluate how human-caused and natural catastrophes affect global trade, politics and migration. Parts, by verb: evaluate each catastrophe type across all three consequences.
- **D2.His.14.9-12:** Analyze several interconnected causes and effects of past events. Parts, by verb: analyze causes; analyze effects.
- **Limits:** Slavery deliberately extends from the 400s through the 1700s. No society stands for a continent; prevalence does not mean identical systems or universal participation. Earlier hereditary slavery existed; unit 8 explains Atlantic scale and racial law. No single plague route, uniform death rate or certain Chinese total is taught. Informed action remains in unit 10.

**Reserved assessment sources and answer anchors**

- **S1, comparison key:** Original dated summaries drawn from Barker, Kloss, Schiel, Smith and Equiano, with provenance on each card. Fifth-century Roman enslavement by conquest and birth supplies the starting point from the earlier progression row. From about 500 to 1500, Black Sea raiders and armies captured people and sold them toward Mediterranean and Asian destinations. Late thirteenth-century Yemeni records document people traded from Ethiopia across the Red Sea. In the 1400s, western Mediterranean buyers used enslaved people in households, workshops and agriculture. In fifteenth-century Mexica society, merchants traded enslaved people; war captives and people sold through debt had distinct situations, and bondage was not generally inherited. Eighteenth-century Equiano recalls capture and successive sales. These cases demonstrate persistence and range, not numerical equality or an unbroken institution in every place. For comparison: household and agricultural work occurred in Mediterranean cases; military enslavement occurred in Asian polities. Mediterranean and Islamic legal traditions could transmit maternal status, subject to particular exceptions; Mexica bondage was generally not inherited. Ransom, manumission, redemption or escape could end particular cases, without guaranteeing release. Reserve Barker's Giorgio petition, Siena, 1460, as the new exit evidence; do not teach it earlier. Explain that he petitions for freedom after childhood kidnapping and forced domestic service, not that every petition succeeded.
- **S2, original evidence summary:** Modern genetic study identifies plague in Central Asian burials dated 1338 and 1339. Historical accounts and route evidence place severe epidemics in Mediterranean Europe, western Asia and North Africa during the first wave. Estimates indicate a third or more of Europe's population died, but regional estimates vary. Comparable totals for all of Africa and Asia are not established. Reserve London in 1361, discussed in [Slavin, section V](https://academic.oup.com/past/article/256/1/325/6658530), and Cairo in 1430 and 1460, dated in the [Borsch and Sabraa research record](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/tpnzk-0bx72), for this check; teach with other outbreaks. The recurrence dates are verified; select and check the underlying record passages before issuing the packet. Do not invent local counts or apply a European estimate to another continent.
- **S3, Anna Komnene, Alexiad, account of crusaders at her father's court:** A Frankish noble sits on the emperor's throne. Another crusader tells him to observe local custom; the emperor remains restrained and later offers military advice. Anna presents the noble as arrogant and her father as composed. Context card: a Byzantine princess writing decades after the First Crusade, in the twelfth century. This is her account, not a verbatim court transcript.
- **S4, Equiano, 1789 narrative, reserved escape-planning passage:** While enslaved, he observes sunrise and sunset and asks for directions home. Companionship with free children does not give him their freedom or an equal place at meals. His account links longing for family and liberty to plans to escape. Context card: a formerly enslaved author addressing readers amid eighteenth-century debate about slavery; his account of African childhood has scholarly disputes about birthplace. Treat it as attributed testimony, not a representative census. Teach the earlier family and abduction passage only.
- **S5, two before-and-after map pairs with evidence notes:** Use independently drawn assessment layers, unlike the teaching maps. Pair one locates Constantinople's Byzantine government before 1204, Latin rule afterward and displaced Byzantine leadership at Nicaea; Choniates records stolen sacred wealth and residents losing homes and status. Pair two locates Mediterranean ports before the first plague wave, later outbreaks, disrupted markets, movement restrictions and reported flight. Supporting notes identify trade losses and urban controls, and distinguish observed movement from inferred migration. The first map also locates Venice and eastern Mediterranean connections documented in the [Venice trade essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/venice-and-the-islamic-world-828-1797), whose commercial discussion was read. Trade disruption in 1204 is an inference from violence at a trading hub, not a measured volume or permanent collapse. Base the second pair on the plague reading's market restrictions and flight evidence. Do not draw precise refugee destinations or trade volumes unsupported by a source. Geography is assessed through both maps and notes, with uncertainty part of the judgment.
