# Course frame: World History I (about 500 to 1800)

Course `world-history-i` (catalogue id `world-hist-hs`), grade 9, draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame names no single day.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 10 units, in time order (about 500 to 1800, picking up where grade 7 ends).
- **Days.** 160 course days: 148 in units and 12 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year. The 37 rows are not equal: the five skill rows of unit 1 and the argument and action cycle of unit 10 carry more days than a single content row, and the "one example in depth" rows (wh1-african-kingdoms, wh1-empire-power-culture) need a survey day plus the in-depth case. About 4 days a row on average. The time a day: one lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week, across 32 weeks, so the 160 days fill the year. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment and one respond day (a unit over 25 days also holds a mid-unit assessment); a unit that assumes an earlier grade's row opens with a readiness check inside its days.
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 World History I, topics WHI.T1 to WHI.T6, is the content anchor: every WHI code is owned by one row and every row sits in one unit below, so every anchor standard has a unit. C3 (9 to 12 indicators) carries the skills. Each unit plan is checked against California, Tennessee and New York and the college-level world history course framework; the "Check set adds" column lists candidate additions with the member they come from, confirmed or dropped in the unit plan's comparison table, and no addition pushes a unit past its days. Check set names never appear in student, teacher or family copy. The values rule (court first, then backed sources, dignity, age) governs every contested claim.
- **Base text.** None (BASE-TEXTS.md: every complete open program fails the bar). Partial base: East Tennessee State University, World History Since 1500 (CC BY 4.0, college level), chapters 1 to 5, can serve units 7 to 10 (the world in 1500; empires in the Americas, Asia and the Islamic world 1500 to 1700; the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment) and the 1450 to 1700 parts of unit 8. It is written for college readers, so passages are adapted down to the grade 9 to 10 band; its CC BY licence covers the text and the maps drawn for the book only, and every image is checked on its own terms (several are non-commercial or stock). Units 1 to 6 have no partial base: sources are chosen from public-domain primary sources in translation, open museum and library collections, and our own maps, each with a stated difficulty and reason. The frame does not map the base lesson by lesson; the scope and sequence does.
- **Inquiry arc.** Every unit has a compelling question that more than one answer can defend from the unit's sources, and runs the four C3 moves. Unit 1 teaches how to build an inquiry and how to judge a source with the five source checks (wh1-inquiry-questions, wh1-sources-of-the-past): the five source checks are new at grade 9 and grow from the sourcing questions of grades 6 to 8 (who made it, when, why, and whether other sources agree), retrieved first. Every later unit reuses both as review. Grade 10 then adds students' own source search, classmates' critique and a second audience (wh2-research-argument); grade 11 extends the research cycle. Writing grows in steps: unit 1 retrieves grade 8's claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph with one counterclaim answered (grade 8 taught counterclaims); unit 3's sourced claim paragraph names and answers one counterclaim; unit 6 writes a one-to-two page claim from the Crusade accounts with one counterclaim named and answered, as a rehearsal; unit 10 teaches the multi-page sourced argument and the sourced explanation at the 9 to 12 level (D4.1 and D4.2 owned there). The mid-year check's short argument tests the unit 6 step.
- **One example in depth.** Where a standard asks for one example (one empire, one kingdom), every student meets all options in a short survey, then studies one in depth. A student working alone (at home, or in a class of one) gets a complete route: the survey pages carry the other options, so nothing depends on hearing classmates' reports. The options are never equal-depth mini-units. MA.WHI.T3.2's own task (map one kingdom or empire and explain its politics, economy, culture and links) sits in unit 3, about 2 of its 16 days: each student maps and explains one unit 3 option (Ghana, Mali and Songhai; Axum or the Swahili city-states; the Mongol Empire); the T3.2 options of units 4 and 7 are taught to everyone at their rows' depth.
- **Informed action.** Once in the year, in unit 10 (wh1-historical-argument): each student finds one public telling of world history near them, checks it against the evidence, and decides what the evidence supports: a correction, a contextual addition, or a confirmation with its evidence, and whether to send it to its keeper. Any outside communication goes through the teacher or parent, using adult contact details; a student may instead prepare an evidence-based corrected label or annotated account and share it with a chosen audience through that adult, so a student learning at home has a full route. Contacting the keeper is optional. The course never picks the cause or the side.
- **Reading level.** Grade 9 to 10 band. Primary sources are excerpted and glossed; each source in a unit plan carries a stated difficulty, a reason for its place, and a rights line.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md) at the unit plan and again at the days; no outside human reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Review roles follow FOUNDATION section 7.3, revised 2026-11-14: the sensitivity, safety and answer-key agents do the content reads, each cold and after studying its sources; the cases and sources named here stay binding. Before it reads units 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8 and 9 (Native, African and religious content, and hard history: the Rhineland and plague massacres, slavery, the devshirme, the Banda conquest) the agent studies the community and sources each unit's last column names, the community's own published voice first. The course's checks get the answer-key read before the course is called finished.
- **Paper path.** Every source, map, timeline and data set has a printed version, and no day needs a device, an internet connection or a student account. The unit 1 display made with an offline mapping tool page (supplied data, no account, no network) has a hand-drawn layered version (tracing paper or clear sheets over a base map) beside it, which a student or school without a device makes instead; the paper version is recorded as the map construction only, since only the device route shows the technology part of D2.Geo.1.
- **Literacy standards (also met).** The unit work also meets these Common Core literacy standards for history and social studies (data/standards/ccss-literacy-6-12.txt; each row's notes name them), with no added days: unit 1, WHST.9-10.7, RH.9-10.1, RH.9-10.6 and RH.9-10.7; unit 3, RH.9-10.6 and RH.9-10.9; unit 6, RH.9-10.3, RH.9-10.6 and RH.9-10.7; unit 8, RH.9-10.7; unit 9, WHST.9-10.8; unit 10, RH.9-10.4, RH.9-10.8, WHST.9-10.1, WHST.9-10.2 and WHST.9-10.9. Across the year: RH.9-10.2, RH.9-10.4 and RH.9-10.10 (every unit reads, glosses and summarizes sources in the grade 9 to 10 band) and WHST.9-10.10 (short writing in every unit, longer pieces in units 6 and 10). WHST.9-10.6 is not claimed: the paper path keeps every product off a device.
- **News and media literacy.** No NML codes sit in this course (they are placed in US History I and US Government); the sourcing work of unit 1 keeps the grade 8 news-literacy habit in use.
