# Course frame: World History I (about 500 to 1800)

Course id `world-hist-hs`, grade 9, draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame names no single day.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 10 units, in time order (about 500 to 1800, picking up where grade 7 ends).
- **Days.** 160 teaching days: 148 in units and 12 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year. The 37 rows are not equal: the five skill rows of unit 1 and the argument and action cycle of unit 10 carry more days than a single content row, and the "one example in depth" rows (wh1-african-kingdoms, wh1-empire-power-culture) need a survey day plus the in-depth case. About 4 days a row on average.
- **Standard.** Massachusetts 2018 World History I, topics WHI.T1 to WHI.T6, is the content anchor: every WHI code is owned by one row and every row sits in one unit below, so every anchor standard has a unit. C3 (9 to 12 indicators) carries the skills. Each unit plan is checked against California, Tennessee and New York and the college-level world history course framework; the "Check set adds" column lists candidate additions with the member they come from, confirmed or dropped in the unit plan's comparison table, and no addition pushes a unit past its days. Check set names never appear in student, teacher or family copy. The values rule (court first, then backed sources, dignity, age) governs every contested claim.
- **Base text.** None (BASE-TEXTS.md: every complete open program fails the bar). Partial base: East Tennessee State University, World History Since 1500 (CC BY 4.0, college level), chapters 1 to 5, can serve units 7 to 10 (the world in 1500; empires in the Americas, Asia and the Islamic world 1500 to 1700; the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment) and the 1450 to 1700 parts of unit 8. It is written for college readers, so passages are adapted down to the grade 9 to 10 band; its CC BY licence covers the text and the maps drawn for the book only, and every image is checked on its own terms (several are non-commercial or stock). Units 1 to 6 have no partial base: sources are chosen from public-domain primary sources in translation, open museum and library collections, and our own maps, each with a stated difficulty and reason. The frame does not map the base lesson by lesson; the scope and sequence does.
- **Inquiry arc.** Every unit has a compelling question that more than one answer can defend from the unit's sources, and runs the four C3 moves. Unit 1 teaches how to build an inquiry and how to judge a source (wh1-inquiry-questions, wh1-sources-of-the-past); every later unit reuses both as review. Writing grows from a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph in unit 1 (the model handed on by grade 8) to the multi-page sourced argument of unit 10.
- **One example in depth.** Where a standard asks for one example (one empire, one kingdom), every student meets all options in a short survey, then studies one in depth. A student working alone (at home, or in a class of one) gets a complete route: the survey pages carry the other options, so nothing depends on hearing classmates' reports. The options are never equal-depth mini-units.
- **Informed action.** Once in the year, in unit 10 (wh1-historical-argument): each student finds one public telling of world history near them, checks it against the evidence, and chooses whether to propose a correction to its keeper through the school. The course never picks the cause or the side.
- **Reading level.** Grade 9 to 10 band. Primary sources are excerpted and glossed; each source in a unit plan carries a stated difficulty, a reason for its place, and a rights line.
- **News and media literacy.** No NML codes sit in this course (they are placed in US History I and US Government); the sourcing work of unit 1 keeps the grade 8 news-literacy habit in use.
