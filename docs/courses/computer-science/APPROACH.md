## The kinds of day, in computer science's words

The `kind` column keeps the shared words; the goal says the activity.

| Shared kind | In computer science | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding | Unplugged or trace day | An idea taught with cards, paper or movement, or a given program traced and predicted before it is run |
| Fluency | Modify day | Students change a working program or algorithm in small, checked steps; short practice with the construct |
| Application | Make day, design day, or society day that applies a row already taught (a society day that first teaches its row is Understanding, as GLOSSARY says) | Students design and build their own program or artifact against criteria, or audit a real system or case with evidence |
| Check | Assessment day | The end-of-unit assessment (unit field 5, below) |
| Respond | Respond day | Fixes from the assessment, by error letter |

Order inside a unit: understanding before modify before make. A make day never comes before a trace or modify day on
the same construct.

## How a day opens (section field 6)

Every day opens in its first five minutes with one of three things. (a) A retrieval trace: a short program or algorithm
from an earlier unit, "what happens?", answered on paper, three minutes. (b) The wrong example from the last exit
ticket, if more than a third missed it. (c) For a new idea, one short concrete moment, unplugged, that the teaching then
names. Teaching first is the default for anything new: no blank editor, and no "try it" on a construct, before one worked
example has been shown and traced. In grades 6 to 12 a day may open with a predict task on a short given program (ten
minutes at most; ours, untested) when students have all they need to attempt it. The day row gives the opening and its
minutes.

## The form of daily evidence

- **Grades K to 2:** the answer is a pointing, sorting or ordering task, or said aloud to a partner and noted by the
  teacher by exception; a program is checked on the screen during the day.
- **Grades 3 to 12, trace or predict days:** an exit ticket with a short program or algorithm and the question "what
  happens?", answered in writing, with wrong answers by error letter (UNIT-PLAN rule 3).
- **Modify and make days:** the program itself, checked against two or three stated look-fors, plus one line from the
  student: what they tested and what they changed. A tool checks the look-fors it can (does the program do these three
  things on the tests?) and shows the student a "yes" or "not yet" for each; the teacher reads the open part for a
  rotating third of the class each session, so every student is read at least once in every three sessions (in K to
  5, which meets once a week, that is once in three weeks), and at the next session as well if the exit ticket shows
  a gap.
- **Unplugged days:** the paper artifact (the sorted cards, the drawn path, the encoded message) with one written
  question (K to 2: said or pointed).
- **Society days:** a short claim and two views, each with one dated source (STANDARD.md "Real cases in society days").
- Student work is read on the device, or on a printout the student hands in.

**Teacher page for a unit with code** carries: the idea at adult level; a finished, tested solution to every program
students write; each common bug as a wrong program beside a right one with what it shows; the exact message the tool
shows for each bug; and "if you are stuck" steps a teacher can follow without writing code. A teacher is never asked to
debug a student's program on the fly.

## Supports (section field 9)

- **English learners.** Each unit names five to eight words taught in depth (for example step, repeat, if, variable,
  bug), each with a picture or a traced example, and two sentence frames: "First the program ___, then it ___" and "I
  predict ___ because line ___ says ___". Words with an everyday meaning (run, loop, bug, sort, cloud, mouse) are taught
  as two meanings. A student may answer a trace in numbers, arrows or the home language. Each key says which wrong
  answers can come from reading the words rather than from the idea, so a language gap is not marked as a content gap.
- **Access.** Every tool works by keyboard alone, needs no color to tell things apart (shape or label as well),
  enlarges text without breaking, has no flashing, and offers a typed or list choice wherever it offers drag and drop;
  every block program has a text view. For a student who cannot see the screen, the unplugged version uses cards and
  objects they can feel, and code is read aloud one line at a time. No tool task is timed.
- **Reading load.** Grades K to 2: no code read alone (icons, read aloud). Grades 3 to 5: up to ten lines. Grades 6 to 12:
  up to twenty lines (ours, untested). Error messages are plain words we write.
- **Check accommodations.** Read aloud, oral explanation, a scribe, extra time at the device, the paper version.
- **Extension.** On the same standard: a second bug, a new case the program must handle, an explanation of why a second
  solution is clearer. Never the next grade's construct.

## Sensitive ground

These rules gather what the computer science pages and progression rows already say. A section that touches one
carries its teacher note in section field 1: what may come up in the room, and how to keep the talk on how a system
was built, tested and used, never on a group or a classmate. Units with cultural, Native or disability content are
read by the sensitivity agent before they go live (docs/FOUNDATION.md section 7; docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md).

- **Bias, exclusion and access.** Who a system serves, leaves out or treats worse (for example E4-ALG-IM-03,
  E5-SOC-HI-16, MS-ALG-IM-10 and 11, MS-SYS-IM-37, MS-SOC-ET-41 and 42, HS-ALG-IM-10, HS-SOC-ET-41). Cases follow
  STANDARD.md "Real cases in society days": what happened, two views, a dated source for each, no company named.
- **Data about people and communities.** Personal data, large-scale collection and data sovereignty (MS-DAT-IM-28,
  HS-DAT-IM-27 and 28). Class data follows STANDARD.md "Privacy and safety"; a case about a Native nation's or other
  community's data gets the sensitivity read above.
- **Online safety and personal information.** Accounts, sharing and online actions (E1-SYS-SE-12, E2-SYS-SE-12,
  E3-SYS-SE-13, MS-SOC-HU-43) are taught as STANDARD.md "Privacy and safety" sets: a pretend page or a paper account.
- **Work and automation.** Jobs made or replaced by automation (MS-SOC-CE-45) can touch a student's family; the
  teacher note says so.
- **If a student tells you something.** Computer science uses health's steps, word for word
  (docs/courses/health/APPROACH.md, "If a student tells you something"); every teacher page of an online safety or
  personal information section carries them, as PE's do (Cory, 2026-11-21, Desk q-fix-pe-1).
- **What a school may swap or leave out:** STANDARD.md section 3 (Cory, 2026-11-21, Desk q-fix-cs-1), the same rule
  as PE and the arts.

## Pacing

Each course frame states weeks, sessions a week and minutes a session; the defaults and the short path are in
STANDARD.md section 2 (Cory, 2026-11-21, Desk q-fix-cs-1). Because K to 5 meet once a week, every teaching and respond
session opens with a three-minute retrieval task, a trace where the earlier row is a program or algorithm, and every unit ends with a respond day. Ours, untested: kindergarten to
grade 2 spend about two thirds of the time away from a device; grades 3 to 5 about half; grades 6 to 12 at least one
unplugged day in every unit. A K to 5 unit lets two or three ids share one anchor program, so one id is not one
session. Which ids a high school year owns is set in STANDARD.md "Ownership of MS and HS ids".

## Honest limits

The order of days (read and predict, then modify, then make) follows PRIMM (predict, run, investigate, modify, make),
read for this page on 2026-11-21. What that evidence supports, and no more: one quasi-experimental study (2018, about
500 students aged 11 to 14 in English schools, 8 to 12 weeks of Python lessons on sequence, selection and iteration,
against classes taught the same topics the school's usual way) found higher post-test scores for the PRIMM classes,
and interviews with teachers found they valued the shared structure and the talk about code. The groups were not
randomised, the evidence is for ages 11 to 14 in text-based Python, and the authors say which parts make the difference
is still unknown. Our use in kindergarten to grade 5 (block and unplugged days) and our session counts are ours and
untested. Two ideas from the same sources are already in this page and are now grounded: reading and tracing code before
writing it, and pair or small-group talk about a given program away from the device. No page claims that learning to
program improves thinking in general or school results. The course has not been tried with children. A one-page logic
model is owed at course level. Sources read for this page are listed in docs/APPROACH-SOURCES.md "Computer science
approach reads, 2026-11-21".
