## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

**Hook, day 72.** Show the Music Here map and read Posadas remembering gatherings in Mexico and California. Ask the compelling question before retrieving grade 1 learning. Can music travel without fitting in a suitcase?
**Supporting questions.** Shape children's questions into: What is culture (72)? What can we ask a teller (73 to 75)? What does a memory show or leave unknown (76)? What travels with people (77)? What changes for whom, and what goal can people share (78)?
**Closing claim, day 79.** Answer the compelling question from Music Here and an interview the teller permits sharing. A child may use Language Today instead. Defend either emphasis or explain why these sources cannot measure which changes more; never rank cultures.
**Teaching.** Model a respectful question on 73, listening and a follow-up on 74, timeline placement on 76 and a claim on 79 using a separate example. Withdraw composed questions on 75 and the completed claim on 79. Source every card before reading. On 77, compare music gatherings with language classes. On 78, teach Posadas and the Cherokee Nation's language school first as worked examples, each in its own place; then the class's own town is the community.

**Limits and teacher background.** Culture includes everyday language, food, music, beliefs, holidays, art and dress, with differences within groups. Do not use ancestry charts, costume activities, tastings, religious observances or immigration-status questions. Learning English need not replace another language. Native nations are living governments, not immigrant groups. Belonging never depends on contributing a tradition. The year's action remains in unit 3.

**Interview prompts, days 73 to 75.** Children write or dictate: "Where did you or someone in your family live before? How did they travel here? Why did they move? When did they arrive in this town or state? What would you like to tell us about that move?" Practise "Could you explain that part?" "We have always lived here" (a Native family, or a family rooted here for generations) is a full answer to the where and when questions; record it as given. The adult chooses a safe move within or between places and may decline any question. Record the teller, interview date and place, event date, exact remembered words where possible, and permission to share. Do not collect recordings or legal documents. Each child asks a question and a follow-up directly, including in a small-group school interview. For the complete school path on 75, the teacher is the teller for five groups of six, five minutes per group, while others sort printed source cards. Reserve five minutes for recording and reflection. Every group covers all topics, and every child records the answers; the teacher notes individual asking and listening. A parent teaching one child serves as the teller, with the same option to decline questions.

**Held-back packet, still to lay out.** Neither source nor its story is used for instruction in this unit. Unit 5 used a different Antin account; these details must remain fresh here.

- **Memory M:** "In Polotzk, I read stories in Yiddish. After we reached Boston, we had to learn English. In Chelsea, my mother ran a store. She learned English while helping customers. Father read Yiddish newspapers at home. At school I wrote a poem and read it to my classmates. Later a Boston newspaper printed my poem." Before layout, each sentence is matched to a page of The Promised Land and the page numbers go in the source record; any sentence not found is cut. Label: "Shortened from Mary Antin's words in The Promised Land, 1912." If fewer than five sentences survive, swap Memory M for an Ellis Island oral history not used in unit 5 (the production writer's choice, recorded with its source). Add an original context caption: her Jewish family moved from Polotzk, then in the Russian Empire, to Massachusetts in 1894. Her mother had run a store before the move. The place of writing is not established here. These are memories, not a complete account of every family member.
- **Photograph P:** Use the school-group photograph opposite printed page 230 of the same 1912 book, captioned for children "Public school children in Chelsea, Massachusetts." Preserve the original caption in the source record. Identify photographer and exposure date as unknown; publication was 1912. Do not identify Mary in it or say it depicts her poem reading. The photograph supports observations about the visible group, not their language, religion, citizenship or feelings. The image still needs visual inspection before layout.

**Look-fors and sample answers.** Score each part as independent, prompted or not yet, keeping parts separate on a paper record.

- Item 1 requires written questions, the child's own asking and listening, answers faithfully recorded, and a sourced timeline entry. Meets: "I asked where, how, why and when. The adult said a train brought her here in 2014 for work. I put her move before the 2019 school event and labeled who told me." Does not: "My partner asked; I guessed the date." Record actual evidence, not the wording of this illustrative sample. Choosing the school path does not lower the score.
- Item 2 requires the child's own question about this source and the historical development, plus a justified use and limit. Meets: "When was P taken? It shows children together at a school, but cannot tell what they said after moving here." Does not: "What is your favorite color? P proves everyone spoke English." Do not require a particular question or an answer the packet cannot establish.
- Item 3 requires two elements, something brought, change in both directions, and a claim linked to a named source. Meets: "Language and poems are culture. M says the family knew Yiddish and shop work. Mother learned English; neighbors had her store and Mary's poem to read. I think the place changed Mary more because she had to learn English, but M also shows she added something." Does not: "Only the suitcase changed places. Their old culture was worse." Accept a different emphasis or "we cannot measure more" with evidence.
- Day 78's community observation is about one community, the class's own town: newcomers and long-time residents (from the interview timeline) and the Native nation whose homeland it is (from that nation's own pages). Meets: "In our town, the cook who came in 2018 teaches a song at the fair, and families who have always lived here run the library garden. Our town is on the homeland of the [named nation], which teaches its language today. Everyone adds something." Does not: "Only newcomers have culture; Native people are gone." This is scored during day 78, not asked again on 80.
- Day 79's closing claim requires an answer, named evidence and reasoning. Meets: "Music Here shows people changed the community by teaching music, but gatherings became shorter there. Both changed, and these examples cannot measure which changed more." Does not: "Moving is good because I say so."
