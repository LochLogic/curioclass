# Section 1.3: Flat maps of a round world

<!-- skel -->
Grade 2: Places and Peoples of the World. Unit 1, Maps tell stories. *Which map shows the world best, and best for what?* ("Can a map ever show the world just as it is?" stays as a supporting question) (days 1 to 16): see [s01.md](s01.md). **Days 9 to 12.** Days by kind: understanding 2 (9, 10), application 2 (11, 12). A plan, not a lesson: nothing here is built or tried with children. Fields follow UNIT-PLAN.md.

- Section 3 of 4 in the unit.
- Day length (frames.csv): 30 to 40 minutes.
<!-- /skel -->

Grade 2 children read short labels; the teacher reads every caption, credit and prompt aloud. Answers are circled, pointed to or a few written words. Each day runs 35 minutes. World Projection Cards are ours, drawn by Curio Class from a public-domain coastline dataset (source to check), and each carries a credit line. Pip the puppet says each wrong idea, one line at a time. No place or people are ranked by how big a map draws them.

Unit plan field 2, the lines naming this section's days:

> **Supporting questions.** Shape the class's questions into: What do map parts tell us (day 5)? Who made this source, and what can we check (day 8)? Can a map show the world just as it is (day 11)? What should our map show (day 13)? Keep students' wording beside these questions.
> **Teaching.** Model reading map parts on days 2 to 4 and sourcing on day 6. Bridge RI.2.6 with "What is this source for?" Model claim, evidence and reason on day 11; partners try a different purpose. Step back during days 13 and 14. Revisit the question board after each answer.
<!-- /skel -->

- **MA.2.T1.2, this section's share (days 9 to 11).** "Compare map projections such as Mercator and Peters and explain how each shows the world differently." Day 9: a round skin cannot lie flat unless it is stretched, folded or opened, so every flat map changes something. Day 10: the Mercator map keeps the shapes of very small areas and draws lands far from the equator too big. Day 11: the Peters map keeps sizes and stretches shapes; each map shows one thing well; choose for a stated purpose. Unit assessment item 2 (day 15) is owned here. Children say "the map that keeps sizes" and "the map that keeps small shapes"; the teacher says Mercator and Peters, and "projection" (a way to draw the round Earth on flat paper).
- **D2.His.11.K-2, this section's share (day 11).** "Identify who made a source, when and where, using clues in the source itself." On day 11 the three questions are asked of each World Projection Card's own credit line. The people who first made each kind of map, and when, are withheld until a source is checked (source to check: the federal projection page named in unit plan field 10, not yet read).
- **MA.2.T1.1, this section's share (day 12).** "Explain what map parts such as the compass rose, scale, key, and title tell us": the title and the key (and the rose) on a labelled outline of North America. Practice only; no unit item.
- **Limits that bite here.** No ruler, ratio or latitude calculation; the word "latitude" is not taught. "Equator" is met as the line round the middle of the globe, not tested. Mercator keeps local angles, not whole continents' shapes or sizes; Peters keeps relative areas, not shapes or all distances. A ship holds one compass direction, which is not always the shortest route. No flat map is "wrong"; no map is the answer for every job. Named continents are supported on the cards; unit 2 teaches where they lie. The day 12 map names three countries and the waters and ranges of the unit's list, not every country.
- **Connections.** Grade 1 and kindergarten: the globe and two world maps (kindergarten u03-2) may come back as a reminder on day 9, never as an exit; "Both ___, but ___" is review. Claim, evidence and because (day 11): grade 1's claim frame. Section 1.1: the rose (day 11's ship) and scale (day 11 revises it).
- **What the teacher page must explain.** Any flat map changes sizes, shapes, directions or distances somewhere. Mercator keeps small shapes and lets a ship hold one compass direction (so it suits sailing a course) but makes lands far from the equator look too big; Greenland looks as big as Africa on it, though Africa is far bigger (from memory; source to check). Peters keeps relative sizes but stretches shapes. Neither keeps everything the globe does. No single scale bar holds across a flat world map. Native nations live across North America today; the day 12 map shows countries and waters only and does not say the land was empty.

## 5. What is carried, and instruments

<!-- skel -->
- days.csv names no tools, supplies or safety lines on these days.

Unit plan field 8 (tools and supplies across the unit):

> **Tools: None.** Paper maps, a globe and equal strips provide the comparisons; a printed globe net and a soft paper sphere substitute for a globe. The teacher opens the paper shell on day 9 by tearing a printed line; no blade or student cutting. Gather pencils, paper, string, counters, equal strips, a north reference, map cards and a self-check strip per child. Everything prints in black and white; one shared display is optional. No student timers.
> **Language support.** Teach title, key, compass rose, scale, maker, date and place with labelled pictures and gestures. Rehearse "The key tells me ___" and "Each scale length stands for ___, so the distance is ___." Accept pointing and home-language answers first. Judge map reasoning separately from English.
<!-- /skel -->

- **Carried.** The globe stays beside every map; "it stands for" (kindergarten) is said of each card. Scale: the strip model of section 1.1 is revised on day 11 (no one scale bar holds across a flat world map: a paper cut-out of Greenland fits the globe but not the Mercator card). The compass rose is carried to day 11's ship. The three questions (section 1.2) are used on each card's credit on day 11. The question board gets "Can a map show the world just as it is?" on days 9 and 11. Teacher modelling of the claim, evidence and because frame ends on day 11; partners use it.
- **Key moment 1, day 9: the shell will not lie flat.** Ideal instrument: a soft ball with land printed on it and a flat sheet, and a real peel as a teacher demonstration. Spec: a printed paper shell torn along a printed line by the teacher, and a paper cup pressed flat by each pair, fit. The teacher alone handles a real peel (citrus is not handled by children). No blade or student cutting. This section has no key tool.
- **Key moment 2, day 10: true-size cut-outs laid on the Mercator card.** Ideal instrument: a transparent overlay of land areas kept true. Spec: partly fits. Paper cut-outs made from the equal-area card keep area and bend shape, which the teacher says aloud ("true size, bent shape").
- **A room with nothing.** A ball of paper and a flat sheet; a drawn globe beside two hand-drawn maps; the pairs flatten a paper cup.

## 7. Talk and writing

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words: round, flat, stretch, fold, tear, globe, projection, Mercator, Peters, size, shape, ocean, country, river, mountains. Frames: "To lie flat, the skin must ___." "Both ___, but ___." "I think the ___ map is best for ___ because ___." Pointing first.
- **Access.** Raised outline cards; a ball or a papier-mache shell for a child who cannot see; neutral description of each map; the teacher hands the cup to a child who cannot press it.
- **Check accommodations.** Prompts read aloud; circle, point, scribe; the word bank on E12 may be read aloud; the writing is shortened to the labels a child can write. Record the support used.
- **Reading load.** A label of one to three words; the word bank is the longest read; at most 150 words a student reads in a day.
- **Extension, same standard.** A child secure on day 11 says what the Peters card bends that the Mercator card keeps.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Day 9: press a paper cup flat and a ball of paper flat; say what had to change. Day 10: compare two pictures of one land. Day 11: pick one of two maps for a job and say why. Day 12: label a printed outline from a word bank. The wrong ideas are said by a toy.
- **One projector.** Show the two cards side by side with the globe on the table; children still circle on their own card; the cup and cut-outs are paper.
