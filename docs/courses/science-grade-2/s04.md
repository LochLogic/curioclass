# Unit 4: Can we change it back?

Course `science-grade-2` (Science, Grade 2). **Days 39 to 47** (9 course days; school days 65 to 78 (the course reserves three meetings a week, within the school-day span), weeks 13 to 16). Sections: 4.1 (days 39 to 42); 4.2 (days 43 to 47). Days by kind: understanding 2, fluency 1, application 3, check 2, respond 1. A plan, not a lesson; unbuilt and untried with children.

- Section 4.1: The same pieces, and changes that return.
- Section 4.2: Changes that remain, and arguments from evidence.

## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Our block castle became a boat, and melted chocolate hardened again, but a cooked egg never runs again: which changes can be undone?"
Day 39 opens with the castle dismantled into a boat beside sealed chocolate before and after melting and cooling, and photographs of the teacher's egg before cooking and after cooling. Ask the compelling question before naming kinds of change.
On day 47, write the class explanation: rearrangement reuses pieces; heating and cooling sometimes restore observed properties, but cooling did not uncook the egg. Cite days 40 to 44, including attempted returns.
Model recording observations on day 40. Thereafter ask which observation supports each claim; let children challenge accounts before showing the return evidence.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


Group the day 39 question board: Where did the castle pieces go (40)? Can another set make different objects (41)? Does changing temperature back restore things (42)? Does that always work (43)? What evidence settles a disagreement (44)? Each answer raises the next question. Transfer uses unfamiliar card rocket and bus builds and new butter, cookie, juice and cucumber records.
Limits: observable changes only, no particles, chemical explanations, measured temperatures or conservation of weight. Reversing a state change need not restore the original outline. A lasting change is supported by attempted return, not merely waiting.
Carried: retain unit 3's property-to-purpose reasoning. Day 39 starts a parts-and-whole sketch and a change-arrow model with claim, observation and reason. Day 41 revises the parts model using two sets: shape and stiffness support different uses. Day 42 adds return arrows from water and chocolate evidence. Day 43 adds lasting branches from egg, leaf and paper records. Day 44's consensus joins the evidence-based account, parts-and-whole system and heating-or-cooling causes. Children use these models on days 45 and 46; day 47 repairs them.

Use one observation record across days 45 and 46. Day 45 totals 38 minutes: three minutes of read-aloud directions, three four-minute observation groups, three minutes for transitions including two to pack up, and twenty for individual accounts and an unscored anchor explanation. Each child has their own five-piece set and record. Watch each dismantle and rearrange; hear one brief reason while others draw. The first fifteen children finish their scored account that day. The waiting half prepare accounts of familiar builds and revisit the anchor; their different, unopened bus task begins on day 46. Keep each observation group's builds and accounts private from waiting groups; show no assessment solutions before both sittings end. No earlier product or teaching-day work is scored.

Day 46 totals 35 minutes: five minutes for read-aloud directions, three four-minute groups plus three minutes for transitions including two to pack up, and fifteen minutes for the argument. Everyone receives the same argument time after observation. During observation, children complete their paper account across the fifteen-minute station block or work on familiar cards. Hear each remaining child's reason in their own window; retain the first fifteen records without another performance or score. No reteaching occurs on either day. The anchor explanation is unscored and never substitutes for transfer.

Reassembly look-fors, one point each: observes the original arrangement; dismantles it; makes a different whole; records its new characteristics; connects matching pieces to the explanation that different objects can use the same set; explains a shape and material property fitting a new use. The teacher checks the real starting arrangement before class. Meeting example with matching drawings: "My rocket had triangles at its sides. I took it apart. Now the same five pieces make a house. The triangle makes a pointed roof; stiff card keeps its shape." This earns six when the actions are observed. Below expectation: "The house is different, so these must be new pieces" (A). Award separately demonstrated components only.

Argument look-fors: each of four records earns one point for a classification justified by its attempted-return result and one for linking the initial before-and-after change to heating or cooling. One further point connects both kinds of result to the general claim that some temperature changes reverse and others remain; one identifies the missing return evidence in the fifth card. Meeting example: "Butter flowed when heated and was solid after cooling. Heating changed soft dough into a firm cookie; cooling left it a cookie. Juice froze when cooled and flowed after warming. The cucumber was firm before freezing and soft after thawing; its original firmness did not return. Heating and cooling can cause either kind of change. We need to see what happens when the second butter sample cools." With all comparisons, this earns ten. Below expectation: "Cooling always fixes it" (C). All sixteen points require observations or argument with an idea on a new case; none reward vocabulary alone.

Section 4.1 closes on day 42 with a three-minute exit, using unseen, unlabelled, out-of-order puddle photographs in a sequence labeled "invented for practice": liquid before freezing, ice and liquid after thawing. Read: "Order the pictures, then mark the two observations that show a return." Answer: liquid, ice, liquid; mark the two liquid observations, showing the original state returning. Accept either liquid photograph at either end. Wrong: the liquid-to-ice pair alone proves return (D). These are paper records only. Every teaching day, children mark model pictures on paper while prompts are read aloud; hear a rotating six reasons. Notebooks retain observations and revisions.

Section 4.2 closes with day 47's fresh retry, reserved until after repair. Read the relevant two-part question, with pictures labeled "invented for practice": "Mark the matching pieces in this card windmill and turtle, then explain how one piece suits its new use". Claiming new pieces are needed is A; saying stiffness cannot affect use is E. Alternatively read: "Mark which changes returned, then use the attempted returns to explain whether cooling always restores the start". Saying a new outline prevents the solid state returning is B; saying cooling always restores is C; deciding without return evidence is D. The second set shows an ice star melting and refreezing as a block, pale bread heated into browned toast that stays browned after cooling, and a melted chocolate sample with its cooled result hidden. Accept the same pieces with a property-linked use; solid water returning despite outline change; toast remaining browned after cooling, refuting the claim that cooling always restores; and insufficient evidence for the chocolate. Hear a rotating six reasons; other omissions receive descriptive feedback.

Access: read every prompt and caption aloud; every child marks pictures on paper. Offer enlarged or tactile pieces, pointing and a scribe without supplying a decision. A parent uses the same sets with one child. No student device is required.

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A different whole needs new pieces. | Identity is confused with arrangement. Repair by matching each old piece to its place in the new whole. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| B | A different outline means the solid state did not return. | Shape substitutes for state evidence. Repair by comparing a solid lump and solid sheet made from the same melted sample. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| C | Cooling always restores the starting properties. | One reversible result becomes a rule for everything. Repair by comparing an egg before cooking and after cooking and cooling. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| D | Before-and-after pictures alone prove the change reverses. | No attempted return is required. Repair by adding a third observation after changing the temperature back before judging. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| E | Stiffness cannot affect a piece's use. | Material properties are disconnected from purpose. Repair by reading stiff and floppy card records for an upright sign. | Anticipated, from memory. |

Each letter describes only its stated error. File omissions descriptively. These anticipated errors are not observed frequencies.
