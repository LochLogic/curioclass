## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did the burrs get all over my sock, and why do bees visit flower after flower?"
Day 60 opens with a fuzzy sock bearing stored burrs beside two flower-visit photographs. Ask the compelling question before explaining. A labelled hook-and-loop stand-in replaces unsafe specimens.
Day 65 records the closing explanation: hooks catch fibers and carry seeds away; bees seek food, and attached pollen can reach another flower. Children cite attachment trials and flower evidence, answering both anchor questions.
Model careful looking first, then ask children to connect each model part to evidence. Demonstrate pollen transfer on day 62; step back during independent designs. **Verb:** "Develop" an evidence-based working model.

Both days total 35 minutes. All children make their own drawing, build and trial record on day 63; groups describe a scheduling arrangement, never a shared scored product. Materials are pre-cut, with five individual sets per group. The teacher hears each child's brief action while reading explanations later, rather than expecting five full oral presentations in four minutes. Record actual participation, not attendance.

Read both new evidence cards on day 63, then let each child choose one. Every card says "invented for practice"; the events are invented, while the mechanisms come from field 10. Dog card: "A dog brushes a burdock plant. Hooked burrs containing seeds catch in its fur. Later some fall beside a path." Bat card: "A nectar-feeding bat reaches into a cactus flower for food. Pollen catches on its hairy head. At another flower, some reaches the receiving part." Original pictures show the sequence, not a finished model to copy.

Read the same task for either card: "Design and build something that does this animal's plant job. Show what your parts represent and try it. Use the result to improve your model and explain its limits." Success means the model carries plant material between places. Limits are the supplied large pieces, one tray, gentle hand movement and no live animals. Children select surfaces and attachment positions, sketch, build, try and improve.

For the dog, accept a felt animal body picking up hooked card burrs from a plant outline, moving them and releasing them at another place. Burrs contain seeds; hooks represent the plant's structure, fibers the animal's fur. For the bat, accept a felt-headed model touching loose paper pollen with its hook patch and carrying it from one flower's source to another flower's receiving patch. Each paper pollen piece has soft loop material on both faces. The head has a small hook patch; the receiver has a larger one. The exposed loop face meets the receiver, and gentle withdrawal releases the smaller contact. Require successful adult pickup and deposition trials before use. The source is a shallow folded-card pocket. Keep source, receiver and nectar spot distinct. Both flowers represent the same kind of plant. Fasteners model attachment, not real pollen hooks or glue. If transfer fails, adjust contact areas before the lesson.

Give written comments after day 63 and a chance to revise on day 64. Score the retained day 64 versions on day 65 before class, with no extra student sitting. Days 60 to 62 are unscored, including first pollen teaching. Day 63 and 64 live observations corroborate each child's work, unscored. Score only the earlier artifacts that received written feedback and later revision; store drawing, working model and trial record together.

Teacher-judged components, one point each, all on the unfamiliar chosen case: the model represents the relevant animal covering; it represents the relevant plant part; it represents the correct transported material; its drawing connects model parts to natural parts; its route starts at the plant source; it carries material on the animal representation; it delivers material to a new place or flower receiver; its explanation connects surface structure with movement, including food-seeking and incidental pollen transfer for the bat (withhold this point for deliberate-help explanations); its recorded trial supports a purposeful revision or a justified decision to retain a working feature; it identifies a specific model limitation. The teacher checks retained models gently against the child's drawing before marking. Each of ten points uses modelling with a plant-animal idea on a new case, exceeding the 80 percent requirement. Unrelated vocabulary and neatness earn no points.

Meeting dog example, with a working build and labelled sequence: "Felt is fur. These hooked cards stand for burrs with seeds. They catch, travel and come off here. My trial carried one; I put more felt where the plant brushes. A dog is not a sock." Meeting bat example, with a working build: "The fuzzy head carries paper pollen from this source to that receiving patch while it visits for nectar. I moved the patch after my trial missed. Paper pieces are much bigger than pollen." Award only components actually present. Separate below-standard answers: "The bat carries seeds between flowers" (A); "The bat visits to help plants" (C); "My pretty bat need not carry anything" (B). Withhold the affected component; assess independently correct components separately.

Accommodations: read all prompts and feedback aloud without adding content; offer large tactile parts, high-contrast pictures, pointing and a scribe. A child may direct an adult's handling while supplying the design, observations and revisions. Record the adaptation; no child needs a device.
