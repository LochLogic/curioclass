## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did the burrs get all over my sock, and why do bees visit flower after flower?"
Day 60 opens with a fuzzy sock bearing stored burrs beside two flower-visit photographs. Ask the compelling question before explaining. A labelled hook-and-loop stand-in replaces unsafe specimens.
Day 65 records the closing explanation: hooks catch fibers and carry seeds away; bees seek food, and attached pollen can reach another flower. Children cite attachment trials and flower evidence, answering both anchor questions.
Model careful looking first, then ask children to connect each model part to evidence. On day 62 children try pollen transfer first and say what they found before it is named; step back during independent designs. **Verb:** "Develop" an evidence-based working model.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


Question board: What caught on the sock, and why visit flowers (60)? Can a model carry seeds (61)? Will every seed stick, and how is carrying pollen different (62)? Can another animal do either job (63 and 64)? What explains both anchors (65)? Each answer raises the next question. Transfer offers a jay and acorns or a nectar-feeding bat and flowers, withheld until day 63.
Limits: observable movement and structure, without reproductive anatomy, fertilization or complete life cycles. Pollen is not a seed. Some plants use animal transport; neither job applies to every plant. Models show movement, not successful seed growth.
Carried: day 60 starts a plant-animal arrow model, recalling unit 5 evidence-based claims. Day 61 revises structure and function from hooked attachment trials and adds design tests. Day 62 revises the seed claim with smooth-seed failures and adds source-to-body-to-receiver arrows from pollen trials. Day 64 independent designs use these relationships; day 65 consensus retains both pathways and their limits. Design cycle, structure and function, and system model remain available.

Both days total 35 minutes. All children make their own drawing, build and trial record on day 63; groups describe a scheduling arrangement, never a shared scored product. Materials are pre-cut, with five individual sets per group. The teacher hears each child's brief action while reading explanations later, rather than expecting five full oral presentations in four minutes. Record actual participation, not attendance.

Read the new seed evidence card on day 63; offer the bat card for unscored practice only. Every card says "invented for practice"; events are invented, while mechanisms come from field 10. Jay pictures show an acorn beneath an oak, the same marked acorn between a jay's beak halves, then that acorn in leaf litter away from the oak with the beak empty. Caption only the places, times and parts; identify the acorn as a seed. Bat pictures show a head before and after reaching a flower's nectar spot, with new yellow marks on its hairs; a second flower's receiver before and after the visit has matching marks while fewer remain on the head. Label pollen, nectar and receiver, without arrows or an explanation. Children infer each route and the structure that makes it possible before designing. Supply no model diagram.

Read the same task for either card: "Design and build something that does this animal's plant job. Show what your parts represent and try it. Use the result to improve your model and explain its limits." Success means the model carries plant material between places. Limits are the supplied large pieces, one tray, gentle hand movement and no live animals. Children select surfaces and attachment positions, sketch, build, try and improve.

For the jay, accept folded-card beak halves that close around a large card acorn at a plant outline, hold it during movement and open over paper leaf litter elsewhere. The card represents an acorn; the closing halves represent the beak, not fur or seed hooks. For bat practice, accept a felt-headed model touching loose paper pollen with its hook patch and carrying it from one flower's source to another flower's receiving patch. Each paper pollen piece has soft loop material on both faces. The head has a small hook patch; the receiver has a larger one. The exposed loop face meets the receiver, and gentle withdrawal releases the smaller contact. Require successful adult pickup and deposition trials before use. The source is a shallow folded-card pocket. Keep source, receiver and nectar spot distinct. Both flowers represent the same kind of plant. Fasteners model attachment, not real pollen hooks or glue. If transfer fails, adjust contact areas before the lesson.

Give written comments after day 63 and a chance to revise on day 64. Score the retained day 64 versions on day 65 before class, with no extra student sitting. Days 60 to 62 are unscored, including first pollen teaching. The day 63 retrieval arrow is scored from the card. Day 63 and 64 live observations corroborate each child's work, unscored; teachers still run them, since they feed the written feedback that the scored version answers. Score only the earlier artifacts that received written feedback and later revision; store drawing, working model and trial record together.

Teacher-judged components, one point each, nine on the unfamiliar jay case and one on the familiar pollen model: the model represents the relevant animal part; it represents the relevant plant part; it represents the correct transported material; its drawing connects model parts to natural parts; its route starts at the plant source; it carries material on the animal representation; it delivers material to a new place; its pollen arrow on the day 63 retrieval card runs from the source flower through the animal body to the receiving patch (the beak hold-and-release explanation stays as unscored practice); its recorded trial supports a purposeful revision or a justified decision to retain a working feature; it identifies a specific model limitation. The teacher checks retained models gently against the child's drawing before marking. Nine of ten points use modelling with a plant-animal idea on a new case, exceeding the 80 percent requirement; the tenth scores pollination, half of 2-LS2-2. Unrelated vocabulary and neatness earn no points.

Meeting jay example, with a working build and labelled sequence: "These card halves are the beak. This card is an acorn from the oak. Closing holds it; opening leaves it here. Bat practice examples: "The fuzzy head carries paper pollen to the receiver during nectar visits"; "The bat carries seeds" (A); "The bat visits to help plants" (C). Give feedback on bat work without points.

Accommodations: read all prompts and feedback aloud without adding content; offer large tactile parts, high-contrast pictures, pointing and a scribe. A child may direct an adult's handling while supplying the design, observations and revisions. Record the adaptation; no child needs a device.
