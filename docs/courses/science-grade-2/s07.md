## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What kinds of plants and animals do we find in each place, and how are the places alike and different?"
Day 66 opens with a doorway look at two schoolyard spots: do they hold the same living things? Ask the compelling question, collect predictions, then survey before explaining. Field 10 names the original observation source.
Day 71's shared written explanation answers the anchor with both dated inventories: which kinds overlapped, which differed and what repeated counts support. Report inconclusive comparisons honestly.
Model recording one observation on day 66 and counting each kind once on day 67. Thereafter ask children to justify comparisons from records. **Verbs:** observe, record and compare.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):

| 2-LS4-1 | Planning and Carrying Out Investigations: Observe directly or through media to gather data for making comparisons. | LS4.D Biodiversity and Humans: Many kinds of living things share any one area, living in many spots on land and in the water. | Patterns: Compare observable similarities and differences as evidence. Locally selected from the Framework and Appendix G; this expectation assigns no crosscutting element. |

Question board: What lives here (66)? Does seeing more individuals mean more kinds (67)? Are other habitats alike (pond and forest, 68)? Can equal counts hide differences (desert and ocean shore, 69)? Would another survey agree (70)? What can we claim (71)? Each answer raises the next question. Day 72 transfers to unseen meadow, stream-edge and marsh scenes.
Limits: observed variety, without scientific names, food webs, adaptation mechanisms or habitat rankings. Describing labels are provisional, not species identifications. An unseen organism is not necessarily absent. Equal effort improves comparisons but cannot reveal every kind.
Carried: day 66 begins a habitat picture model joining living things to observed places and recalled resources. Day 67 revises its graph to count kinds once. Days 68 and 69 revise patterns using shared kinds and equal-count contrasts. Day 70 adds a second dated inventory; day 71's consensus qualifies evidence-based claims with search limits. Systems, graph, patterns and claim-evidence reasoning remain available; day 72 uses this representation on new scenes.

Directions take three minutes; items take 32, totaling 35. Every child works independently on paper; the teacher reads prompts aloud without explaining answers. No reteaching or separate exit occurs on day 72. The teacher can observe paper work for thirty children without thirty oral conferences.

All three original scenes say "invented for practice" and are withheld until day 72. Draw meadow: three identical grass tufts, two clover plants, two identical butterflies and three ants. Draw stream edge: two grass tufts, two willow shrubs, two frogs and three identical snails. Draw marsh: three reed clumps, two ducks and two frogs. Keep the same kind visually identical across scenes; distinguish each other kind by shape, not color alone. No hidden, cut-off or ambiguous organisms. Pictures show land, bank and water context. These are constructed examples, not actual survey results or claims about all such habitats.

Item 1 earns one point for observed habitat features, one for a usable observation-and-recording method. Item 2 earns one point each for accurate plant inventories across all scenes, animal inventories across all scenes, counting each kind once despite repeated individuals, and a graph showing 4, 4, 3. Item 3 earns one each for the count comparison, a correct shared-and-different comparison, and explaining the equal-count contrast. Item 4 earns one for an observed comparison with the limit. Accept descriptive drawings and labels; scientific names earn nothing. All ten points require observing, organizing or comparing evidence about living kinds on new cases, exceeding 80 percent. The blank habitat-model layout is familiar; none of its new-case content is supplied.

- **Item 1 samples.** Meets: "Meadow has land; the stream has water and banks; the marsh has shallow water and plants. I will scan each scene from left to right and draw each different plant and animal kind once." Does not: "I will draw a picture." Habitat features and an observation method are missing.
- **Item 2 samples.** Meets: "Meadow plants: grass, clover; animals: butterfly, ant. Stream-edge plants: grass, willow; animals: frog, snail. Marsh plants: reed; animals: duck, frog. Each kind counts once. Graph, one dot per kind: meadow ●●●●; stream edge ●●●●; marsh ●●●." Does not: "Meadow has ten kinds, so its graph has ten dots, one per individual" (A).
- **Item 3 samples.** Meets: "Meadow and stream edge each show four kinds; marsh shows three. The first pair shares grass, but only the meadow shows clover. Equal counts can include different kinds." Does not: "Meadow and stream edge each have four, so their kinds are identical" (B).
- **Item 4 samples.** Meets: "The meadow picture shows more kinds than the marsh picture. Other kinds may be outside these pictures." Does not: "No snail can live in the marsh because none is pictured" (C).

Award independently correct components; a missing comparison is an omission, not a misconception letter.

Day 72 produces the unaided record, unscored. After class, give written component feedback. Day 73 begins with reading that feedback and an individual revision opportunity before targeted teaching. Score the retained revised artifact after day 73, with the original attached; all scored work began on an earlier day than scoring, and no first-teaching work is scored. Keep original performance and response gains distinguishable. Later course checks carry this one record without re-scoring.

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Each individual counts as a different kind. | Abundance replaces variety. Repair by grouping repeated pictures of the same kind before counting groups. | Anticipated, from memory; evidence statement in field 10. |
| B | Equal numbers of kinds mean identical kinds. | A count replaces identity. Repair by pairing equal-sized inventories with visibly different members. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| C | A kind not observed cannot live there. | A limited search becomes proof of absence. Repair by revealing a previously concealed organism in a second view. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| D | Wetter habitats always have more kinds. | A local contrast becomes a universal ranking. Repair with an invented equal-count wet and dry pair. | Anticipated, from memory. |

These are anticipated ideas, not measured frequencies. Record miscounts, missing plants and unsupported comparisons descriptively unless the child's answer states one of these claims.
