- **R1.** Look for dissent as the purpose, two connected moves, passage evidence and their effect on senators. Meets: Repeated assertions that citizens retain rights make dissent legitimate during war. The later claim that suppressing speech obstructs successful war turns that right into a practical necessity. Together they ask senators to protect criticism rather than equate loyalty with silence. Does not: He repeats himself so everyone will enlist.
- **R2.** Look for purpose, audience, linked effects, accurate speech and poster details, and a warranted-conclusion judgment. Meets: Wilson asks Congress to authorize war. Submarine attacks establish a grievance; separating the German people from their government narrows blame; the closing democratic principles turn intervention into an obligation. The attacks support a protective response, but alone do not prove that intervention will secure democracy. Pershing's Crusaders instead puts mounted leadership and marching troops beneath ghostly knights: heroic unity replaces the speech's qualifications. Does not: Wilson wants a good outcome, so every reason is sufficient. The poster gives all the same reasons.
- **L1.** Look for both voices, their contrast and an inference grounded in both. Meets: Paul still wonders whether life might offer him something after the war. The ending leaves his first-person account and reports his death on a militarily quiet day. The contrast removes the future he imagines and shows how the military record can hide one person's loss. Does not: Paul tells us he dies, proving that he knows he will return home safely.
- **L2.** Look for chapters 1, 7 and 12, Tagore evidence, comparison and distinct cultural relationships. Meets: In chapter 1 Kantorek's schoolroom authority has urged the young men toward duty, but Kemmerich's hospital bed exposes the cost. In chapter 7 Paul's leave reveals a distance between soldiers and civilians: civilians presume to explain the front while he struggles to speak at home. Chapter 12 ends with an impersonal report that reduces his death to military quiet, overturning the promised belonging. Tagore's postmaster can leave the Bengali village and console himself with general thoughts about separation, while Ratan keeps waiting. Both works expose unequal experiences of return, but military and school authority constrain Paul; employer and dependent relationships shape the postmaster and Ratan. Neither narrator speaks for an entire nation. Does not: The report erases Paul's hopes, unlike the postmaster's departure. This is partial: it omits chapters 1 and 7, Ratan's evidence and the institutional distinction.
- **L3.** Look for cited sentences from existing work explaining purpose, audience and development across time frames. Meets: My 94 explanation to the teacher says, The wounded horses interrupt the battle's heroic image. My 100 synthesis for seminar readers says, Speech and poster make duty public while Paul shows its private cost. My L2 argument for another class adds, Ratan is the village girl who serves the postmaster and waits after he leaves. The first sentence explains one scene; the synthesis connects accounts; the final argument develops a whole-book comparison and supplies context unfamiliar readers need. Does not: My argument is longer than my explanation, so its purpose and audience have changed.
- **Pope comparison.** Look for accurate injury images from both poems and an explained reversal. Meets: Pope presents returning with a crutch as preferable to missing the action and reassures recruits about return. Owen's gas victim dies before the speaker's eyes and recurs in his dreams. The fatal, continuing suffering challenges the promise that injury will be a manageable price for adventure. Does not: Both poets make injury sound like an enjoyable game.
- **Theme.** Look for a developing claim across chapters 2, 6 and 8 and a separate objective summary. Meets: Comradeship increasingly challenges national duty: chapter 2 replaces civilian ambitions with dependence on fellow soldiers; chapter 6 intensifies that dependence under bombardment; chapter 8 extends recognition toward enemy prisoners. Summary: Paul recalls his earlier life and training, endures the front with his company, and later observes hungry Russian prisoners. Does not: War is the theme. Paul foolishly stays with his friends and should know better.
- **Motives.** Look for conflicting motives, interaction and development of theme. Meets: Fear drives Paul to strike the French soldier in the shell hole, but the wounded man's suffering draws him toward help. Reading the man's name and seeing his family makes the enemy an individual. Compassion conflicts with self-protection and unsettles the national category that permitted the attack. Does not: Paul helps him because compassion leaves his idea of the enemy unchanged.
- **Diction.** Look for contextual meanings, several bodily images and their cumulative effect on tone. Meets: Owen's bent bodies suggest exhaustion rather than ceremonial strength; stumbling and lost boots strip marching of dignity; the drowning image makes gas feel inescapable. These images accumulate into agony that makes the concluding patriotic saying sound cruelly false rather than noble. Does not: One unpleasant word proves the whole poem's tone, so the other images do not matter.
- **Mediums.** Look for one subject, accurate details from both mediums and differing emphasis. Meets: Wilson represents participation through grievances, a distinction between government and people, and democratic principles. Under Four Flags groups four armed Allied soldiers advancing together beneath a victory slogan. The speech explains a decision; the image makes united action visible without explaining those qualifications. Does not: The poster visually includes every qualification in Wilson's argument.
- **Words.** Look for a gathered word, contextual inference, grammatical forms, print and digital consultation, verification and precise reuse. Meets: I gathered autocracy while reading Wilson and inferred concentrated rule from his contrast with self-government. A print general dictionary confirmed the noun's meaning and pronunciation; a digital specialist civics glossary clarified its political sense. Autocrat names a ruler, while autocratic describes concentrated authority. Checking the original context, I wrote, Wilson distinguishes the German people from autocracy; an autocrat holds power that an autocratic system denies the public. Does not: Persuasion means whatever I first guessed; persuade, persuasion and persuasive can replace one another in any sentence.
- **Evidence.** Look for an explicit observation, a distinct inference, citations and connecting reasoning. Meets: In chapter 9 Paul tries to help the wounded French soldier, an explicit action. Later, the name and family materials make the dead man personally knowable. I infer that Paul's enemy category has weakened because he now imagines this individual's relationships rather than only his uniform. Does not: Paul sees a name. That quotation automatically proves my inference without explanation.
- **Discussion.** Look for individual prepared evidence, participation in rules or roles, inclusive questioning and qualified synthesis. Meets: My preparation cites Paul's leave and Ratan's waiting. I proposed that we invite each partner before a second turn and volunteered to track evidence. During discussion I asked, Maya, does Ratan's waiting challenge our claim that leaving ends attachment? After her answer I said, We agree that separation creates unequal losses; we differ about whether Paul chooses his distance. I now qualify my comparison because military authority limits his choices. Does not: I came without citations, ignored our roles and repeated my position after a partner challenged it.
- **Independent book.** Look for a cited Crane episode and an explained change in the reader's view of Henry. Meets: Henry imagines heroic battle before he has fought, but his flight when the line faces renewed attack reveals fear beneath that ideal. This contrast makes his imagined courage unreliable; his self-justifications show that his understanding is still changing. Does not: Henry goes to war and several things happen, which proves that he is always heroic.
