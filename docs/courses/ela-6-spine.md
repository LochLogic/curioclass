## What to check

1. **The shape.** The year is six topic units, each built on a set of texts, with reading, writing, speaking
   and language taught through those texts. This is not the order of data/sequence.csv, which lists modules one
   skill at a time. The five differences are marked **changed** below and listed after the order table.
2. **The text plan.** Every unit now names its texts, with a source, a licence and a status. Only texts marked
   "on file" are in data/library today. Everything marked "to verify" must be checked before a section leans on it.
3. **The reading load.** Each unit says how many words a day are read in class and at home. The year reads
   less inside the lesson than "read whole" promised in the first draft, and says so.
4. **The judgment calls.** They are gathered in "Least sure" at the end: the weekly 20-minute reading block, the
   choice of second hero, six units of 17 to 25 days, the 50-minute period and the 150-day year.

## The year at a glance

- **150 teaching days** (est.): 138 in sections, 12 cumulative check and respond days.
- **Period: 50 minutes** (est.). Four days a week: a 10-minute opener (two on sentences, one on words, one on
  fluency), a 35-minute lesson, a 5-minute written exit question about the day's text. One day a week: a
  20-minute block of sustained independent reading with teacher conferences, a 25-minute lesson, the exit
  question. The year is 125 hours.
- **8 units, 47 sections.** An opening unit, six topic units, a closing unit. A unit is one topic and
  one set of texts, 17 to 25 days. A section is a package of 2 to 5 days inside it. Units 3 and 4 are a pair
  (polar journeys, then the Klondike) with one word list carried through both: a 46-day run on one body of
  knowledge.
- **How the 150 days split by the main work of the day** (est.; most days mix strands, so this counts the lead one):

- **Reading days are 49 percent literature and 51 percent informational** (34 and 36 of 70). The outside criteria
  describe about 45 and 55 in grades 6 to 8, counted by texts. That balance is reported in an outside report, not
  scored, so it is a guide and not a hard line. The count by texts can be made once the text plan is verified.
- **Writing: 13 pieces**, 6 taken through the full process and 7 written in one sitting. By type: 4 argument,
  5 informative, 4 narrative (31, 38 and 31 percent). The process pieces are 2, 2 and 2. The outside criteria

- **Most days are print or discussion lessons, not tool time.** The 23 tools plus one new tool add up to about

- **Reading outside the lesson, said honestly.** At 45 to 60 minutes a lesson, the outside programs the review

  It does some of each: the novel is planned with a home path and a no-homework path, the other anchors are

- **What exists today:** 23 tools, all at spec stage and none built (9 grade 6 specs fixed, 14 band specs not yet

## Text plan

This section comes before any section. No section is written until its unit's text set is named, verified and
licensed, and an ELA section counts as live only when its unit's text set is live.

**Two corrections to the first draft.** It said every text in the year was written before 1930. That is not so:
data/library/speeches.csv holds Franklin Roosevelt, Kennedy, Johnson, Reagan and the Apollo 11 transcript, several
tagged for grade 6, and data/library/stories-pd.csv holds 1950s stories by Bradbury and Asimov. It also said three
of the six topic units needed texts. With second texts counted, all six do.

**What the shelf holds today.** 157 curated pieces in data/library/stories-pd.csv have a grade range that includes
grade 6. By the review's count none is informational except four memoirs, 13 are Hardy Boys and Nancy Drew books,
106 are from the United States or England, and about 19 are by authors of color. The "short story" kind on that
list also covers whole novels in chapter sets, so no count by kind is quoted here until the kinds are fixed. None
of the pieces has word help, questions, a teacher note or a text record yet: the Enrich step has not started for
this band. Units 3, 5 and 6 (66 of the 138 section days) have no anchor on file.

**What each status means.**

- **On file**: I found the text in data/library (stories-pd.csv, mythology.csv or speeches.csv). It is curated,
  not yet ready to teach.
- **Verified, not on file**: the review confirmed the text exists and is public domain, and names where. It is
  not in data/library.
- **To verify**: the review named it from memory, or named a source without checking the item. Nothing may be
  built on it until someone checks the text, the edition and the licence, page by page for government sites,
  which can carry non-federal authors and third-party images.

### Rules for linked texts

- A linked text may never be an anchor. Links break, and some sites want accounts.
- Every linked text needs a hosted stand-in, so a class with no connection that day loses nothing.
- Link, do not host: Poetry Foundation, poets.org, Library of Congress collections, museum sites.

### The informational shelf: a piece the course owes

The Reading Room has no informational shelf. Without one, grade 6 to 8 ELA cannot be built: three of the six
topic units stand on informational text. The shelf belongs in the Reading Room plan (the us-gov licence basis
already exists in story.json) and comes before any section in units 3, 5 and 6. It also answers a fair worry about
range: the public domain shelf alone gives a year that is largely one era and one tradition, and nothing written in
a sixth grader's lifetime. Government works, open licences and a few commissioned pieces are how this course
widens that.

### How hard the texts are (rough measures)

The first draft said the texts climb through the grade band across the year. That claim is dropped. Order
inside the band does not need to climb. What each anchor needs is a text record that says where it sits in the
band and why it is placed where it is.

**The pilot passage sits below the band by computed measure.** It measures 5.9 on Flesch-Kincaid, against a floor
of 6.51 for grades 6 to 8, and inside the grades 4 to 5 band (4.51 to 7.73). The whole book measures 7.0, low in
the grade 6 to 8 band. The passage is placed in grade 6 on a person's judgment of its old language and the
knowledge it assumes. It is the easiest anchor passage of the year, placed first on purpose, so that the first
teaching of theme and summary is not blocked by the text. Its text record must say all of this plainly. The climb
has to be shown by later anchors.

## The sections inside each unit

Sections are 2 to 5 days. ELA differs from math in one way that matters: a section here reads part of a unit's text
set and cannot be lifted out and taught alone. The unit is the real teaching package: one topic, one set of
texts, one word list, one main piece of writing.

**The section of record.** The question the first draft left open is settled this way.

- The small sections stay as the rows of record in data/units.csv, so "section days" means the same thing in every
  subject and work ships in small pieces. The first draft's 55 sections are merged to 47.
- Each row carries a unit column. A small unit record owns the text set, the word list, the culminating
  task and the checks.
- An ELA section's days count as live only when its unit's text set is live.
- The public course page offers the unit, not the section, as the thing a teacher picks up. An outside reader
  will read "section" as the unit.
- No rows go into data/units.csv until Cory's final yes.

### Unit 5, reworked

The first draft had students argue and debate "the argument over child labor, about 1900 to 1920". That question is
settled. A debate needs two sides a person could defend, and a written argument needs a claim a reader could
reject. So:

- **The topic stays.** It is right for this age and gives the year its history.
- **The 1900s texts are for reading and tracing, not for taking sides.** Spargo and the modern government history
  lead. Mother Jones is read as a partisan voice, beside a newspaper's telling of the same march. One text from
  the other side, a mill owner's statement, is read so that students meet claims with support and claims without
  it, which RI.6.8 needs.
- **The speech is a recording**, so SL.6.3 is real listening: a Roosevelt fireside chat on the 1938 wages and hours
  law if it can be verified, or Kennedy's moon speech from the shelf today.
- **The written argument and the debate move to a live question** the same knowledge serves, with current
  government sources. A working form of it: "Should twelve-year-olds be allowed to work for pay, and at what?"
  (farm work rules, child performers, recent changes in state law). No student is asked to defend child labor
  as it was in 1900.
- **The fallback, if the texts cannot be verified:** pair units 5 and 6 on one topic. The class researches
  the earth's hazards, then argues "should people rebuild where disaster strikes?" The standards and the day
  counts stay. This would also give the year a second 44-day knowledge run, like units 3 and 4.

## The checks

Checking is 16 of 150 days, 11 percent (est.): 2 at the start of the year, 2 end-of-unit assessment days (units 2
and 5), and 4 cumulative checks of two periods each, each followed by a respond day.

**No class is tested twice in a week.** In the first draft three cumulative checks fell straight after an
end-of-unit assessment on overlapping standards. Now, where a cumulative check follows a unit, it is that
unit's own assessment. That is true of units 3, 4, 6 and 7. Units 2 and 5 keep their own end-of-unit assessment: a short
new text on the unit's topic with questions, about half a period, and the other half spent going over it.

**At the unit, not in every section.** Each of the six topic units has a 10-minute readiness check on
the standards in its "Builds on" row. It sits in the last ten minutes of the previous unit's last day, as
field 4 of docs/courses/UNIT-PLAN.md says for every subject, not on the unit's own first day. The unit's process piece, scored with a rubric, is the other half of the
evidence. Every day ends with a written exit question about the text.

**A one-minute oral reading check for every student in the first two weeks.** It is done during independent
reading and takes no extra day. It is there to flag students who need more help with reading words than this
course can give. The flag: a student below about 112 words correct a minute gets a closer look (ours, untested).
That figure is the 25th percentile for the fall of grade 6 in published norms (Hasbrouck and Tindal, 2017, read
in part by the expert). A low score is a reason to look closer, not a diagnosis. This is a core course, not an intervention, and the course page must say so plainly.

**Four cumulative checks, two periods each.** Period one: a new text or pair of texts, read cold, with questions.
Period two: writing in one sitting from the same texts. The first draft's single period (20 minutes of reading,
30 of writing) did not fit a period that also has to be handed out and collected. The third day is for responding:
the class goes over the check, and the key says "if many missed this, do that". The year does not end on a test:
Check 4 is done by day 148, and the last two days belong to the folder.

What each needs, and how ELA differs from the math checks:

- **Not from generators alone.** A reading check needs a fixed new text and questions written for that text.
  Each check needs two forms (A and B) so it can be given again. Only the language and vocabulary items can be
  redrawn from generators.
- **Two cold-read texts are ready on the shelf:** Reagan's Challenger address (647 words, about grade 6 by the
  review's measure) and the Apollo 11 transcript. Zitkala-Sa's memoirs are never used as cold-read texts.

- **Speaking and listening is not on paper.** It is checked with a one-page look-for sheet at the seminars (4.7,

- **Accommodations.** Directions and writing prompts may be read aloud and time extended. Reading the passage
  aloud changes what a reading check tests, so the key says when that is and is not allowed.
- **Results** live on a printable class record sheet, as in math. There are no accounts.

**The long-sentence routine.** On anchor reading days, one long sentence from the day's text is taken apart into
its short sentences and rebuilt. It takes about three minutes at the start of the lesson, or the whole opener on a
sentence day. It serves the long, old-fashioned sentences of the myths and the 1900s texts, it serves English
learners, and it is sentence work done inside the texts. It is not used on the pilot passage of unit 2, where
the sentences are short and the old forms are what is hard.

**Why the language days changed.** Grammar taught apart from writing does little for writing, and sentence
combining does more (Writing Next, 2007, a review of studies in grades 4 to 12, read in part by the expert; the
outcome was the quality of writing, not a test of conventions). So the two pronoun days moved into the
revise days of section 2.8, where students fix pronouns in their own drafts; the lone spelling day moved into the
editing step of the report; and whole language days fell from 10 to 8.

**Sentence openers follow the units:** pronouns in units 2 and 3, punctuation for extra information in
units 3 and 4, shades of meaning in unit 4, sentence variety and formal style in units 5 and 6,
spelling in unit 6, style and tone in unit 7. **Word openers:** roots and figures of speech from unit 2
(odyssey, mentor, titanic, echo, panic, chronic), all four word-solving strategies from unit 3.

**L.6.1e, with care.** The standard asks students to notice variations from standard English in their own and
others' writing. The Call of the Wild writes French-Canadian speech in dialect, and many students speak a home
variety of English. This is taught as choosing language for an audience, never as correcting how a family talks.

**English learner supports, per unit.** They belong to the unit record, not only to each section: the unit's
word list with Word Wall cards; sentence frames for the seminar and for the unit's writing type; the
long-sentence routine; the audio beside the text; a home-language note where StoryWeaver has a parallel text. None
of these is written.
