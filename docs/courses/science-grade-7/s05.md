## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How does changing an ecosystem affect what lives there?"
**Hook, day 97:** Compare original land-cover diagrams and historical orangutan observations. A tree crop occupies former rainforest, yet orangutans still use some retained forest patches. Ask the compelling question before offering mechanisms.
**Closing, day 126:** Explain the anchor using resource trials, interaction records and tested proposals; identify uncertainty about recovery and livelihoods.
**Teaching:** Model controls and evidence critique. Students choose comparisons and challenge mechanisms; revisit questions before investigations and fade sentence frames.

<!-- skel -->
- **MS-LS2-1:** Use data as evidence of how the amount of resources available affects single organisms and whole populations in an ecosystem. Emphasis: cause and effect between resources and the growth of organisms and population size when resources are plentiful or scarce.
- **MS-LS2-4:** Argue from evidence that changes to living or nonliving parts of an ecosystem change its populations. Emphasis: spotting patterns in data, drawing justified conclusions about population change, and judging the evidence behind arguments about ecosystem change.
- **MS-LS2-2:** Explain and predict patterns in how organisms interact, across many different ecosystems. Emphasis: predicting the same patterns (competition, predation, mutual benefit) among organisms and nonliving parts in different ecosystems.
- **MS-LS2-5:** Judge competing designs for protecting biodiversity and the services ecosystems give people.
- **MS-ETS1-1:** State a design problem's criteria and constraints clearly enough to lead to a good solution, considering science ideas and effects on people and nature.

**M1, invented for practice:** Nine matched pond enclosures each begin with 12 grazers. Three repeats at 6 food units per week give individual mass gains in milligrams (1, 2, 1), original survivors (6, 7, 6) and final populations including young (8, 9, 8). At 18 food units these are (4, 5, 4), (10, 11, 10) and (16, 17, 16); at 30 units, (6, 7, 6), (11, 12, 11) and (21, 22, 21). Water, light, temperature, area and duration match. Explain effects of plentiful and scarce resources on individuals and populations using repeats, then predict reducing food from 30 to 6. Key: more food supports greater growth, survival and population size here; reduction likely lowers each. Survivors differ from total population including young. This does not establish unlimited growth.

**M2, invented for practice:** In another pond, an outlet change lowers water depth from 10 to 9.5 centimeters, drying 2 of 40 equal shoreline plots. Plants die in those plots, which held the only shallow nursery habitat. Insect counts fall from 80 to 32. A matched unaltered pond changes from 78 to 76; water temperature is unchanged in both, with equal survey effort. Explain how the nonliving water change and living plant loss could change the population; compare magnitudes and explain how a small component change could have a large effect. Judge 'Temperature caused the decline.' Key: concentrated nursery loss can affect recruitment widely. A 5 percent water-depth reduction and 5 percent habitat-area loss accompany a 60 percent insect decline; percentages are supplied and measure different things. Unchanged temperature weakens that alternative. The comparison supports the habitat mechanism without eliminating every unmeasured cause.
