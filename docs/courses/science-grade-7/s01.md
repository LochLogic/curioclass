## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can we make something new that was not there before?"

**Hook, day 1:** Watch a teacher add a plain bath-bomb blend to water. Record fizzing, disappearing solid and competing gas explanations. Introduce the compelling question in 15 minutes; safety takes 25 minutes, readiness ten.
**Closing explanation, day 23:** Explain the anchor using combination tests, gas-property records and bounded mass records: substances form when atoms regroup; escaping gas lowers measured mass without destroying atoms.
**Teaching:** Separate observations from inferences. Ask which evidence distinguishes explanations. Step back during planning and critique; teach atomic language after days 16 and 17.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation:


**Question board, day 4:** Mass records address "Did matter disappear?" on day 6; gas identity remains unresolved. Ingredient comparisons answer "Which combination?" on day 10. Gas records narrow candidates on day 12, raising "How could new particles form?" Heating and electrolysis comparisons address this on day 17. Atoms answer "What stays?" on day 20; odor records address receptors on day 21. Keep unanswered questions visible. Transfer: unfamiliar stone weathering, field 5.
**Limits:** No atomic masses, symbolic balancing or bonding forces. Molecules and repeating crystals begin MS-PS1-1; unit 3 completes it. Odor sensing continues MS-LS1-8, begun in grade 6 unit 6; grade 8 completes it. No engineering expectation is claimed.
**Carried:** Keep the particle model. Individual drawings begin day 2, the class version day 4. Revise boundaries from mass records on day 6, inputs and outputs from properties on day 14, competing mechanisms on day 15, and atom arrangements from heating and electrolysis evidence on day 18. Day 19 adds repeating crystals; day 20 establishes the conserved-atom consensus used in transfer. Claim-evidence-reasoning begins day 4, adds qualified property claims on day 12, distinguishes evidence from historical authority on day 18, and supports the day 23 explanation. Neither model is withdrawn.

**Prompt, invented for practice:** A supplied record shows a peroxide sample forming water and oxygen. A peroxide molecule contains two hydrogen and two oxygen atoms; water has two hydrogen and one oxygen; oxygen gas has two oxygen atoms. Begin with two peroxide molecules. Develop and use a before-and-after model, counting each kind of atom. A flexible closed apparatus plus contents reads 142.6 g before and after. After gas leaves through an opening, it reads 141.9 g. Explain both readings using your model and a drawn boundary. Unchanged solvent is omitted from the particle drawing but remains inside the mass boundary. This is a paper case, never a classroom procedure.

**Key and scoring:** Show four hydrogen and four oxygen atoms initially (2); rearrange them into two water molecules and one oxygen molecule with unchanged atom counts (2); use conservation of atoms to explain unchanged total mass (2); show gas atoms crossing the boundary and explain the lower apparatus reading (2). "Gas has no mass" (C). "Oxygen atoms disappear" (D). "The whole closed system loses mass" (F). Molecular-size differences do not license equal masses per molecule. No atomic masses or symbolic equation is required. This case is withheld until day 21.

**Formative checkpoint, day 13:** Use 35 minutes on this new case, ten on justification and five on collection. Invented for practice: bubbles appear during the interaction. Isolated input U and output V have these profiles, in the order density, melting point, boiling point, solubility, flammability and odor. U: 0.9 grams per milliliter, -25 degrees Celsius, 75 degrees Celsius, 30 grams per 100 milliliters water, burns, sweet. V: 1.4 grams per milliliter, 35 degrees Celsius, 170 degrees Celsius, 3 grams per 100 milliliters water, does not burn, odorless. Densities and solubilities are at 20 degrees Celsius; phase temperatures are at one atmosphere. Ask: "What do the six property comparisons support, and would bubbles alone establish a reaction?" Key: each property differs, supporting a different substance after interaction; bubbles alone cannot distinguish reaction from a state change. "Bubbles alone prove it" (E). Record property reasoning only, not full MS-PS1-2 attainment. Day 14 responds for 15 minutes once, then uses 35 for model work.

**Section exits:** Each is one question with at most two parts, answered alone from fresh supplied evidence in the last five teaching minutes.

- Day 6, invented for practice: "A vessel sends its gas through a tube to a collector, and the tube has a small leak. The vessel changes from 132.8 g to 132.0 g; the collector changes from 44.5 g to 45.1 g. How many grams left the vessel, how many reached the collector, and how many are in neither? Where could they be? Can these readings identify the gas?" Key: 0.8 g left the vessel, 0.6 g reached the collector, and 0.2 g crossed a boundary into the air through the leak. These readings cannot identify the gas. "Gas has no mass" (C); "Equal mass identifies the gas" (J).
- Day 12, invented for practice: "Unknown gas and candidates K and L all fail to burn; unknown and K are denser than the surrounding gas, while L is less dense. Which candidate remains supported, and what repeat measurement could challenge that identification?" Key: K remains supported, not proved; repeat density under matching conditions. "One matching property proves identity" (H).
- Day 17, invented for practice: "At one atmosphere, heating liquid T produces a vapor whose condensate matches T in density (0.8 grams per milliliter at 20 degrees Celsius) and boiling point (65 degrees Celsius). Electrical treatment of T produces gas G, which stays gaseous at 20 degrees Celsius. T, its condensate and G do not burn. A student claims both outputs are unchanged T because neither burns. Is the claim supported, and which evidence decides?" Key: the condensate profile supports unchanged T after heating. G is gaseous where T is liquid, so it has different boiling behavior; matching flammability cannot establish identity. "Neither burns, so both are T" (H).
- Day 21, invented for practice: "Two supplied molecule diagrams differ in arrangement and their odor records differ. Explain how that difference could change which receptors respond and how information reaches the brain." Key: different molecules can stimulate different receptor combinations, which send signals to the brain. No smell identification or personal sensory performance is required. "Every molecule triggers identical receptors" (K).

Daily notebooks capture observations, comparisons or model revisions. Section plans supply any further daily exits with new evidence.

**Teacher judgment:** For item 1, look for six accurate comparisons and a supported decision. ## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Color alone identifies a substance. | A shared appearance is treated as decisive. Repair: compare solubility of two white powders. | From memory. |
| B | Any mixing makes a new substance. | Interaction is confused with reaction. Repair: separate an unchanged mixture and compare its components. | From memory. |
| C | Gas has no mass. | Invisible matter is excluded. Repair: track mass before and after gas crosses a boundary. | From memory. |
| D | Atoms disappear during a reaction. | Matter is destroyed in the microscopic account. Repair: match every atom card before and after. | From memory. |
| E | Bubbles prove that a chemical reaction occurred. | State change is confused with reaction. Repair: compare condensation evidence for boiled water. | From memory. |
| F | A closed reacting system loses total mass. | The boundary excludes a product. Repair: enclose every product in the model boundary. | From memory. |
| G | Crumbling proves that a chemical reaction occurred. | Fragmentation is treated as new substance formation. Repair: compare original and scraped-fragment properties. | From memory. |
| H | One matching property proves substance identity. | A profile is reduced to one feature. Repair: compare two candidates sharing only that feature. | From memory. |
| I | Conserved matter means unchanged particle arrangements. | Conservation is confused with unchanged substances. Repair: regroup a fixed collection of atom cards. | From memory. |
| J | Equal before-and-after mass identifies the gas. | Boundary evidence is mistaken for identity evidence. Repair: contrast two gases in equal-mass closed cases. | From memory. |
| K | Every molecule triggers identical odor receptors. | Receptor selectivity is omitted. Repair: compare supplied receptor-response patterns. | From memory. |

The letters identify single ideas, not diagnoses of a child. Accept an observation without treating it as proof. Explanations not described by a letter receive a written comment rather than an unrelated error label.

## 8. Tools and supplies across the unit

**Cost:** Provisional allowances from the scope and sequence: $173 initially, including $154 reusable equipment and $19 consumables; $19 with equipment held. One child: $18. These are estimates, not quotations.

**Safety and set-up:** Day 1 is teacher-only mixing. Per group of three, provide an observation sheet; the teacher uses one tray, a 250 milliliter plastic cup, 50 milliliters water and at most one eighth teaspoon each baking soda and citric acid. Everyone wears splash goggles before the demonstration. Its three-minute clean-up sits inside the safety block.

Days 8 and 10: per group, one tray, two 250 milliliter plastic cups, two plastic spoons, 100 milliliters water and separate teacher-portioned powders. Each open-cup trial uses 25 milliliters water and at most one eighth teaspoon of each selected powder. Day 8 tests ingredients separately; day 10 compares water alone, each ingredient alone and both, reusing rinsed cups. Keep water and quantities constant. Reserve five clean-up minutes before each five-minute exit.

The day before mixing, check labels, safety data sheets, sensitivities, washing and eye-flushing access; portion powders and fit goggles. Everyone wears splash goggles. Citric-acid dust and splashes irritate eyes; avoid dust, skin contact, tasting and sniffing. Use spoons, covered stock and open cups on trays. Tie hair, cover feet and wipe spills. Stop for missing protection, irritation or spills. The teacher collects waste for locally permitted disposal, rinses equipment and supervises handwashing. No live closed-container trials.

Day 3 uses one scenario sheet and fitted goggles per student; inspect and clean goggles beforehand, prohibit mixing, and allow two minutes to clean and store them. Card-model days use one printed card set per group; mats are one for each student on day 2, three for each group on day 4 and one for each group on other card days; teacher-cut cards beforehand, avoid loose small counters and allow two minutes to collect paper. Paper work needs no eye protection.

**Three tiers:** Household mixing; supplied records for hazardous tests; paper cards instead of day 20's spec-only simulation. Records replace measurement gathering; students choose comparisons and critique limitations. The simulation (key, ten minutes, practise with feedback) partly fits pending specification; it needs conserved atom labels and movable boundaries.
