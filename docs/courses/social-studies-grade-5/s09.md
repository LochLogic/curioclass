## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **A. Curio Class historian caption, based on the National Park Service's segregation-law and Reconstruction accounts and Chicago Public Library's migration history:** After Reconstruction, governments required racial separation in many schools and public places. Discrimination restricted jobs and educational opportunities. Poll taxes, unfair tests and intimidation kept Black citizens from voting. The Ku Klux Klan and other white supremacist mobs threatened and murdered people. Black churches offered meeting places and mutual help. Black newspapers exposed injustice and connected readers with opportunities. Families moved to seek greater safety and work, but discrimination also existed outside the South.
- **B. Mary Church Terrell, address to a women's suffrage convention, 1898, paraphrase of the education and clubs passages:** Black women had made educational gains despite severe obstacles. Their clubs organized kindergartens, helped families and worked to extend educational opportunity. Their achievements showed what women could accomplish when given a chance. Terrell spoke as a Black organizer seeking recognition and equal opportunity; she did not describe every Black woman's life.
- **C. Curio Class historian context from the Capitol's 1866 petition record and National Archives suffrage history:** Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony and other advocates petitioned Congress for women's voting rights. The 14th Amendment established citizenship and equal protection without securing women's vote; its representation provision referred to male voters. The 15th prohibited race-based voting discrimination without adding protection against exclusion by sex. Advocates divided over accepting Black men's voting protection first or opposing an amendment that excluded women. Black women sought racial and sex equality together.
- **D. National Organization for Women, founding statement, 1966, paraphrase:** Women should participate equally in public life and employment. Federal law already prohibited sex discrimination in employment, but organizers said enforcement was inadequate. They called for organized action to make legal promises effective and identified overlapping race and sex discrimination against Black women.
- **E. 19th Amendment, ratified 1920, paraphrase of section 1:** Neither the United States nor a state may deny or limit a citizen's right to vote because of sex. Pair with this historian context: Southern officials continued using racial barriers against Black women as well as Black men. Voting protection did not itself change citizenship restrictions affecting other women: many Native people were not citizens until 1924, and some states kept Native people from voting until 1948 or later; most Asian immigrants could not become citizens until 1952.
- **F1. Curio Class historian caption from the SNCC Digital Gateway's Greensboro account:** In February 1960, four Black college students sat at a segregated lunch counter and stayed until closing. More students joined on later days. Sit-ins spread to other cities, making resistance to segregation visible. Reserve this example for assessment.
- **F2. Kitty Cone, participant, Short History of the 504 Sit-in, published April 4, 2013:** Selected phrases from her account: "We learned about sit ins from the civil rights movement"; "We set up committees". She reports that a church and the Black Panther party "sent many delicious meals". The occupation "lasted 26 days" and helped secure "regulations almost unchanged". **Historian context:** Section 504 drew on earlier civil rights laws against race and sex discrimination. Delayed rules prompted disabled organizers to demand enforceable protection. Their committees organized outreach and press work. Cone credits protest and a Washington delegation with securing rules. This is her account of the connection, not proof that protest was the only cause. The 1990 law was separate.
