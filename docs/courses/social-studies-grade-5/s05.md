## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did the Constitution try to keep power from growing too strong, and whose rights did it protect?"
- **Hook, day 60:** put the Preamble's promise of liberty beside Benjamin Banneker's letter to Thomas Jefferson of 19 August 1791, which argues from the Declaration's equality claim. Ask who could use government to protect freedom.
- **Co-write questions, day 60:** Why strengthen government (61)? Whose power grew through compromise (62)? How was power limited and shared (63 to 66)? Who lacked equal rights (67)? How could the text change (68)? Why add rights protections (69 to 72)?
- **Closing claim, day 72:** answer the compelling question using the Constitution, Hall's petition and the First Amendment investigation, then defend why that amendment was added in 1791. Require evidence and reasoning; allow different judgments.
- **Teaching:** model reasoning from a provision to its purpose on 63 and a First Amendment argument on 70; guide source comparison on 71. Remove the worked argument on 72; offer a writing frame because ELA W.5.1 comes later. Teach preamble, constitution, representative, branch, federal, compromise, ratify and amendment.

**Teacher explanation:** Congress gained taxing power; states retained powers. The 1791 Bill of Rights constrained federal government; later law extended many protections against states. Branches divide national work; levels divide responsibilities geographically. Native nations govern themselves as sovereign nations within the United States today. On 63 the President's oath (Article II) shows officeholders' responsibilities. Background for the teacher: District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) held the Second Amendment protects an individual right; checked 2026-11-14. Equality was an ideal unevenly protected, not a general equality guarantee in the original text.

**Limits:** no counterclaim, partisan policy debate, reenactment or personal disclosure. Study all ten amendments on 69 through short paired readings; do not promise unlimited freedoms. Detailed litigation waits.
