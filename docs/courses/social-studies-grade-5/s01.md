## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Whose land was it, and who decided?"
- **Hook, day 1:** compare an original New England homeland sketch with the Rhode Island charter's land grant. Who could promise land where other people already governed?
- **Co-write questions on day 1:** How did disease change relationships (2)? What did agreements mean (3)? Why did wars happen (4 to 5)? Who lives and governs here today (6)? Why found colonies (7 to 8)? Why did English institutions persist (9)? How did climate, crops and labor connect (10 to 11)?
- **Closing claim, day 12:** answer the compelling question using the Narragansett Early History and Founding Providence cards. Both establish Native authority in a negotiated settlement; students may emphasize negotiation or the limits of a colonial claim. Name each source and connect its evidence to the answer.
- **Teaching:** model source identity and purpose on day 2, context on 4, and evidence and reasoning on 5. Students select evidence on 8 and 12. Revisit questions daily. Teach colony, charter, treaty, ownership, climate, labor and evidence with examples.


**Content parts:** explain disease, land views and conflict (MA.5.T1.1); compare founding and research (MA.5.T1.2); analyze English influence (MA.5.T1.3); locate colonies and describe regional crops, climate and labor (MA.5.T1.4).
**Limits:** comparison and claims stay at grade 4 depth until unit 3. No counterclaim, modern property ruling, universal Native ownership model or military survey. Slavery's laws and freedom struggles belong to unit 2. Study religion historically. Informed action remains in unit 10.
**Carried:** read the century timeline on day 2, add agreements and wars on 3 to 5, revisit on 9. Read scale on 1 and 10; use it on 12. Build the map key and regions on 10, revise for labor on 11, apply on 12. Perspective connects land views on 2, war accounts on 4 to 5 and present voices on 6. Keep these models; withdraw the worked claim on 12.

**Assessment packet, withheld until day 13.** Cards are numbered; letters name errors only. These are original summaries of facts, not quotations or adaptations of protected community wording. Give source identity and setting with each card. A modern community history is not an eyewitness record of the seventeenth century. Historical names recur necessarily; teaching and exits use different passages, map layouts and evidence combinations. Never rehearse these cards or sample answers.

- **Card 1, Aquinnah history:** European diseases killed people in entire Wampanoag villages. Newcomers introduced private ownership practices that conflicted with Wampanoag communal land practices. Source: Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), Wampanoag History, modern public history for readers learning about the tribe.
- **Card 2, Easton:** In 1675 John Easton met Wampanoag leaders while trying to prevent war. He recorded complaints that English documents claimed more land than leaders agreed to give, and that settlers' cattle damaged corn. Fighting followed. Source: Easton's Relation, contemporary English official's report. These are leaders' complaints through Easton's recording, not a Native-authored transcript.
- **Card 3, founding:** Massachusetts Bay leaders sought a Puritan religious community and punished religious disagreement. Williams opposed government control of belief and was banished. Rhode Island's 1663 charter protected peaceful religious disagreement. Sources: the religious-authority paragraphs of New and Dangerous Opinions and the charter's conscience clause, reserved from teaching.
- **Card 4, government:** Two royal documents written in English: the Rhode Island charter of 1663 provided for town-elected deputies to meet in a general assembly; the Massachusetts Bay charter of 1629 allowed colonial laws only when consistent with English law. Give these concrete provisions without an explanation of later influence. Sources: the assembly clause and lawmaking clause, respectively, reserved from teaching and exits. Explain Britain and England in the context note. Students infer how these arrangements could carry language and political practices forward; exploration dates alone do not answer why.
- **Card 5, original assessment atlas, about 1770:** an outline map has thirteen numbered blank colony footprints and a name bank. Answer overlay: 1 New Hampshire; 2 Massachusetts, including present-day Maine; 3 Rhode Island; 4 Connecticut; 5 New York; 6 New Jersey; 7 Pennsylvania; 8 Delaware; 9 Maryland; 10 Virginia; 11 North Carolina; 12 South Carolina; 13 Georgia. Do not substitute modern state borders or depict claims as uncontested Native land transfers. Pair the map with the following keyed data. Symbols show examples present, not exclusive work or numerical shares.
- **Cards 6 to 8, founders (each student gets the card matching the day 8 choice):** each card gives one detail that confirms a likely finding and one that complicates it, from a passage not used on day 8. Card 6, Penn: the 1682 Frame of Government and Laws Agreed Upon in England protected worship for people who acknowledged God, but limited office to Christians (Avalon Project). Card 7, Williams: his 1644 book The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution argued that government should not force belief, but Rhode Island's laws still had to agree with England's (1663 charter). Card 8, Winthrop: his 1630 sermon A Model of Christian Charity described a community bound together under God, and the colony he governed banished Anne Hutchinson in 1638.
