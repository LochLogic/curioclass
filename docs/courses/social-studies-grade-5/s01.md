## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Whose land was it, and who decided?"
- **Hook, day 1:** compare an original New England homeland sketch with the Rhode Island charter's land grant. Who could promise land where other people already governed?
- **Co-write questions on day 1:** How did disease change relationships (2)? What did agreements mean (3)? Why did wars happen (4 to 5)? Who lives and governs here today (6)? Why found colonies (7 to 8)? Why did English institutions persist (9)? How did climate, crops and labor connect (10 to 11)?
- **Closing claim, day 12:** answer the compelling question using the Narragansett Early History and Founding Providence cards. Both establish Native authority in a negotiated settlement; students may emphasize negotiation or the limits of a colonial claim. Name each source and connect its evidence to the answer.
- **Teaching:** model source identity and purpose on day 2, context on 4, and evidence and reasoning on 5. Students select evidence on 8 and 12. Revisit questions daily. Teach colony, charter, treaty, ownership, climate, labor and evidence with examples.

**Assessment packet, withheld until day 13.** These are original summaries of facts, not quotations or adaptations of protected community wording. Give source identity and setting with each card. A modern community history is not an eyewitness record of the seventeenth century. - **A, Aquinnah history card:** European diseases killed people in entire Wampanoag villages. Newcomers introduced private ownership practices that conflicted with Wampanoag communal land practices. Source: Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah), Wampanoag History, modern public history for readers learning about the tribe.
- **B, Easton card:** In 1675 John Easton met Wampanoag leaders while trying to prevent war. He recorded complaints that English documents claimed more land than leaders agreed to give, and that settlers' cattle damaged corn. Fighting followed. Source: Easton's Relation, contemporary English official's report. These are leaders' complaints through Easton's recording, not a Native-authored transcript.
- **C, founding card:** Massachusetts Bay leaders sought a Puritan religious community and punished religious disagreement. Williams opposed government control of belief and was banished. Rhode Island's 1663 charter protected peaceful religious disagreement. Sources: the religious-authority paragraphs of New and Dangerous Opinions and the charter's conscience clause, reserved from teaching.
- **D, government card:** The Rhode Island charter permitted elected deputies but required laws consistent with English law. The thirteen colonies that became the United States were under British government and used English in government. French, Spanish and Dutch colonization also left lasting influences. Sources: the charter's assembly clause, the anchor's MA.5.T1.3 and New York framework 4.3b. Explain Britain and England in the context note; do not equate first arrival with later influence.
