## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did enslaved and free Africans shape the colonies, and how did they seek freedom?"
- **Hook, day 15:** read Venture Smith's account of working toward freedom beside the Vassall family's later land record. How could someone build a colony yet be denied control over his own life?
- **Co-write questions, day 15:** How did laws create hereditary slavery (16, 18)? What did Atlantic routes mean for people (17)? How did legal status shape daily life (19, 21)? How did people resist (20)? How did people gain freedom and build communities (22 to 23)?
- **Closing claim, day 24:** use Smith's chapter 2 and the Vassalls' 1781 petition; reserve chapter 3 and the 1780 petition for E24. Require a claim, two attributed details and reasoning.
- **Teaching:** model source identity and context on 16, map arrows on 17, comparison on 19 and evidence-to-claim reasoning on 21. Withdraw the worked claim on 24; retain source prompts until unit 3. Teach hereditary, Middle Passage, artisan, hired out, manumission, petition and emancipation. Revisit questions daily.


**Teacher explanation:** 1619 is Virginia's recorded arrival, not slavery's beginning in the Americas. Virginia's 1662 law made status follow the mother; its 1667 law denied freedom through baptism. These laws strengthened racial, lifelong bondage. Georgia barred slavery from 1735 until 1751; from then until northern abolition began, all thirteen colonies permitted it; ownership was widespread, not universal. Hired-out workers remained enslaved even when allowed some earnings. Education restrictions varied. Enslaved people built families despite threatened separation; legal freedom brought greater control, not equality. Massachusetts courts ended slavery by 1783, rather than through a gradual abolition statute.
**Limits:** claims remain at grade 4 depth: what sources both confirm. Unprompted sourcing starts in unit 3; nineteenth-century abolition belongs to units 7 and 8. Informed action stays in unit 10. No reenactment, simulation, auction, graphic image or slur. Never ask children to defend enslavement or disclose family history.
**Carried:** extend unit 1's timeline with laws on 16 and freedom cases on 22 to 23. Return to scale on 17, explaining that mapped routes simplify a network. Retain the labor map's key, regions and perspective prompts on 18 to 19; revise its work categories on 21. Keep these for later units.

## 9. Family note

We will study slavery, Black families, skilled work and efforts to gain freedom through maps and people's words. Children will explain historical claims with evidence while learning about coercion, resistance and lives built under unequal laws.
