## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why did the Civil War happen, and what did it change?"
- **Hook, day 113:** compare original captions showing slavery's legal status in 1860 and the 1865 amendment. Ask what happened between them. Each child explains why the question matters to others.
- **Co-write questions:** Why did states secede (114 to 116)? What changed through war (117 to 118)? How did people change laws and lives (119 to 122)?
- **Closing claim, 124:** answer the compelling question using Mississippi's declaration and the South Carolina Black convention's address; explain causes, changes and remaining barriers.
- **Teaching:** model weighing causes on 114, guide on 115, withdraw filled chains on 116. Model a history's central claim before guided practice on 123. Source every document first. Teach secession, Union, Confederacy, emancipation, ratification, citizenship, equal protection and Reconstruction.

T5.6a and b belong to unit 9, days 131, 132 and 141, as the scope and sequence specifies.
**Limits and teacher explanation:** no campaign catalogue or counterclaim task. Slavery is central; western disputes and party conflict concern its expansion. Lincoln helped build support for the new party, issued emancipation and supported the 13th Amendment; the 14th and 15th followed his death. Congress proposes and states ratify amendments. Emancipation required enforcement; freedom did not guarantee equality. Explain the 13th Amendment's punishment exception plainly. Do not equate all Union soldiers' aims or all Southern people's views. Name deaths and white supremacist violence without graphic detail, slurs or reenactments. Pause distress, offer a private check-in and an equivalent non-graphic source; never require personal disclosure.

- **G, Georgia secession convention, declaration of causes, January 29, 1861, explaining separation to other states and the world:** Convention delegates complained that Northern states threatened slavery and that their people could not take enslaved people into the western territories on equal terms. They described the Republican Party's opposition to slavery as its defining purpose. This records the delegates' justification, not the views of everyone in Georgia or a valid claim to own people.
- **E, Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie and Yates Sampson, representing Edisto Island freed people to the president, October 28, 1865:** They asked for a way to buy land. Landowners would not sell to them. Without land, they faced pressure to work for former enslavers. They wanted secure homes and an independent living. Use this later petition only in the assessment; teach with the committee's October 20 or 21 letter's opening request and the commissioner's reply, omitting violence details.
- **N, Black citizens' Norfolk address to the American public, 1865:** The authors argued that voting was needed to protect their rights and that they had a constitutional claim to it. Teaching uses the South Carolina address instead. Both historical texts are reproduced in the museum's conventions exhibit.
- **Law cards, historical documents in fresh plain-language form:** the Proclamation, January 1, 1863, covered designated areas in rebellion, excluding loyal slave states and specified occupied areas; its enforcement depended on Union power and people securing freedom. The 13th Amendment, 1865, prohibited slavery and forced service except as punishment following conviction. The 14th, 1868, established citizenship for persons born or naturalized in the country and subject to its jurisdiction, barred states from denying due process and required equal protection. The 15th, 1870, barred voting discrimination based on race, color or previous enslavement. It did not prohibit exclusion based on sex. Retain the jurisdiction language; do not imply every Native person became a citizen in 1868.
- **Officeholding card, original historian caption:** John A. Chesnut of Kershaw District served at the 1865 South Carolina Colored People's Convention and later served in the state legislature. Some Black men voted and held office under Reconstruction laws before the 15th Amendment; it added constitutional protection rather than starting all Black voting. Participation also depended on organizing and enforcement. Teach with William Beverly Nash instead.
- **H, Curio Class historian paragraph for grade 5, written for this assessment; the student copy is printed "Written by Curio Class for this task":** "The Civil War changed the country's laws more quickly than it removed injustice from daily life. Union victory made it possible to abolish slavery throughout the country. Congress and the states then changed the Constitution to protect citizenship and voting rights. Black Americans organized conventions and schools to make freedom meaningful. White supremacist violence and weakening federal protection threatened those gains. The legal changes mattered, but they needed people and enforcement to give them lasting force." Teach summary with a separately written history of why the war began, using the declarations and timeline.
