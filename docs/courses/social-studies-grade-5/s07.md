## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did people resist injustice in the early 1800s?"
- **Hook, day 97:** place the Mashpee 1833 self-government resolutions beside the Tribe's present government page. Ask what people can do when others deny their right to govern. Each child writes why the question matters to others (D1.1).
- **Co-write questions:** What did Native nations defend (98 to 104)? How could slavery end in some places and grow elsewhere (105 to 106)? How did abolitionists work for freedom and rights (107 to 109)?
- **Closing claim, 110:** answer the compelling question using the Mashpee resolutions and Douglass's learning-to-read account; explain how each action challenged injustice and what it could not settle alone.
- **Teaching:** source every document first. Model perspective and context on 100, guide comparison on 104, model purpose and evidence on 108, then withdraw the worked argument on 110. Teach sovereignty, syllabary, removal, abolition, internal trade, petition and equality.


- **D1.1.3-5:** Explain why the question matters to others. Part: explain importance.
- **D2.His.4.3-5:** Explain why people in the same period held differing views. Parts: identify differences; explain why.
- **D2.His.5.3-5:** Explain links between historical circumstances and people's views. Part: connect context and perspective.
- **D2.His.6.3-5:** Describe how a creator's perspective shaped a historical source. Part: describe influence.
- **D2.Civ.14.3-5:** Illustrate ways people changed society in the past and today. Parts: illustrate both periods.
- **D2.His.16.3-5:** Use evidence to develop a historical claim. Parts: select; support.

**Content parts:** T4.5 explains land and self-government conflicts through all four named cases; T5.1 traces regional change and distinguishes both trades; T5.3 explains ideas and roles.
**Limits and teacher explanation:** grade 11 develops treaty disputes; unit 8 teaches the Civil War. Native governments predate written constitutions and persist today. The 1830 Act authorized removal treaties; coercive policy forced removal. Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory under Worcester. Some Cherokee slaveholders forced enslaved Black people west on removal; their descendants are the Cherokee Freedmen. Day 105 traces abolition state by state. Jackson's successor carried out the 1838 roundup. Do not present all removals as one march in 1838. No slurs, graphic descriptions, simulations or personal disclosures. Explain a harmful view without endorsing it. If distress arises, pause, offer a private check-in and a non-graphic passage on the same goal; never ask a child to speak for a community.

## 8. Tools and supplies across the unit

**Tools: None.** Supply printed sources, outlined maps, timeline strips, a source log and pencils. A projector enlarges print; an adult partners at home. Limit independent reading to 100 words per source; read originals aloud. Offer large print, a scribe, oral responses, bilingual vocabulary and "Because __, the writer emphasized __." Check understanding orally before identifying a language gap. Every task has a paper path; no student timer.
