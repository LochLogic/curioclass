Course `physics` (Physics). **Days 1 to 18** (18 course days; school days 1 to 18, weeks 1 to 4). Sections: 1.1 (days 1 to 4); 1.2 (days 5 to 9); 1.3 (days 10 to 14); 1.4 (days 15 to 18). Days by kind: understanding 7, fluency 3, application 5, check 1, respond 2. A plan, not a lesson: nothing here is built or tried with children. Fields follow UNIT-PLAN.md.

- Section 1.3 accounts for momentum (days 10 to 14).
- Section 1.4 joins evidence and explanation (days 15 to 18).

## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why does the lighter skater move faster, and what stays the same in every push, crash or bounce?"
Day 1 shows original equal-time skater position strips, labelled "invented for practice": unequal masses start still, push apart, then leave unequal gaps. Students sketch forces before hearing an explanation. Field 10 sources the phenomenon. Nobody reenacts it.

On day 14, students explain why equal interaction forces produce greater acceleration of the smaller mass, while the pair retains zero total momentum. Cart comparisons and collision records support the account.

Model reading axes on day 3; let groups choose controlled comparisons on days 5 and 6. On day 13, contrast system boundaries. Thereafter students select representations independently.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


Group the question board by evidence needed. Section 1.1 answers "What changed besides position?" through velocity-time slopes on day 4. That raises section 1.2's "What causes acceleration?", answered with controlled data on day 8. Unequal speeds then raise section 1.3's "What stays unchanged?", answered through signed momentum and boundaries on days 10 to 14. Section 1.4 asks "How dependable is our explanation?" on day 15 before transfer on day 16. Revisit the board at each answer. Day 17 introduces unfamiliar warehouse sled measurements and a coupling event.

Limits: algebra and signed one-dimensional quantities suffice. Exclude calculus, rotation, relativistic motion and energy-conservation calculations. Engineering design begins in unit 2.

Carried: force-diagram starts on day 1; controlled comparisons revise it on day 8 to distinguish net force from motion. Data-graph starts on day 3; day 4 adds acceleration slopes, day 8 adds controlled-force comparisons, and day 15 adds repeat ranges. System-model starts around the skaters on day 1; day 10 adds signed momentum, day 11 tests totals, and day 13 distinguishes outside exchange. Day 14 joins the three; day 15 qualifies reliability. Students use all three consensus representations on day 17. None is withdrawn.

Work on the floor or a low table with a clear lane, hands outside the track, no riders and no high drops. Teacher-only steps are cutting bumpers and adjusting supports. Everyone wears impact eye protection during set-up, trials and clearing. Stop for a loose mass, unstable board or escaped cart; substitute records. If protection or a secure track is unavailable, use records. Reserve five clean-up minutes each day to retrieve carts, count masses and store equipment.

Day 5 varies pull at fixed mass; day 6 varies mass at measured equal net force. Measure resistance rather than equating pull with net force. Without calibrated force and adequate motion measurements, use matched supplied records. Day 11 compares signed momentum before and after contact. Wheel rotation and friction can limit toy-cart quantitative results; never force agreement with an ideal model.

No-kit records preserve comparison and repetition choices but provide less measurement practice. Day 7 uses falling-object records, without live drops.
