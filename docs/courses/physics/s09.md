## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why is the sea floor youngest in the middle, and what moves the plates?"
Anchor: day 128 displays the Atlantic age map without explanatory captions. Students trace matching age bands, sketch possible causes and post questions.
Day 142's explanation connects new crust at ridges, spreading and recycling to interior heat and surface change, using seismic, laboratory, magnetic, age and motion evidence.
Model one evidence-to-inference link on day 130. Thereafter ask what each observation rules out; students choose evidence and challenge unsupported arrows.

(134 to 137), and "Do ages support movement?" (138 to 141). Revisit unresolved questions daily. Assessment transfers to unfamiliar survey records, a rising coast and a different ridge transect.
Limits: qualitative convection, no mineral chemistry, named-landform recall or logarithms. Distinguish outer-core magnetic evidence from mantle flow. Include retained formation heat beside radioactive heating; a water plume cannot establish mantle state or speed. Sinking slabs belong to convection; plates are not passive rafts.
Carried: retain unit 8's energy and nuclear models and unit 6's wave model. Start matter-cycle, plate-model, rock-cycle and scale-proportion sketches on day 128. Revise plate structure with seismic and pressure evidence (131); matter cycling with three-dimensional convection and heat accounting (133); rock cycling and scales with relief feedback (136); convection limits with water observations (137); plate history with age and motion evidence (141). Day 142 joins the consensus model; assessment uses its mechanisms on new records. Models remain available for revision. Day 133 distinguishes a closed matter boundary from outward heat transfer; energy accounting includes nuclear stores.

| 143 | HS-ESS2-3 | Develop an evidence-based interior model; use energy transfer to explain matter cycling. | Item 1, 30 minutes, 12 points. Invented for practice: Survey R's receiver records show transverse arrivals along paths confined above 2,950 kilometres depth, but none transmitted across the next shell. Compressional echoes return from interfaces at 2,950 and 5,200 kilometres. Another arrival requires conversion to transverse vibration inside the deeper interface. Laboratory comparisons show that solid samples transmit transverse vibration, liquids do not; compressed iron is denser than rock and remains solid at the estimated central conditions. Surface rock samples are least dense. Drilling reaches only 11 kilometres. Build a layered model and a perspective view of convection linking heat to plate motion. Annotate evidence for each inference and explain how matter cycles while energy changes stores or crosses boundaries. Predict whether an added receiver would detect a direct transverse arrival on a path crossing the intermediate shell, and justify it. Answer: solid crust and mostly solid mantle surround liquid outer and solid inner core; echo returns indicate property boundaries, and transverse transmission distinguishes states. Relative density supports differentiation, while pressure helps explain a solid inner core despite high temperature. Predict no direct transverse arrival across the liquid shell. Include rising warmer and sinking cooler mantle material with connections out of the page, moving lithosphere, radioactive and retained heat, and outward energy transfer. Earth does not create matter as new crust forms. Wrong: a liquid mantle ocean (A); energy disappears when rock rises (B); plates contain only crust (C). | section 9.4 |
| 143 | HS-ESS2-3 | Evaluate evidence and model limits; connect scientific questions with engineered instruments. | Item 2, 15 minutes, 6 points. Continue Survey R, invented for practice. Card P records matching vibration behaviour between compressed iron samples and the central seismic arrivals; laboratory and inferred interior densities overlap within uncertainty. Card M reports surface magnetic direction at one station changing from six to eight to eleven degrees east over successive decades; laboratory tests show that moving conducting liquid can sustain a changing field, while heated nonconducting rock cannot in this apparatus. Card D reports only rock in the 11-kilometre borehole. Identify what each evidence type supports and cannot show, explain why converging observations strengthen the model, and describe how an instrument improvement could test a remaining question. Compare with a warm-water plume analogue. Answer: laboratory agreement constrains candidates without directly sampling deep Earth; magnetic change and conductivity tests support a conducting-flow mechanism but cannot locate it alone. Combined with seismic states and density, they support outer-core flow, not mantle liquidity; drilling cannot validate deep layers. Better seismic receivers or pressure equipment can improve a test through scientists and engineers working together. Water shows buoyant flow, not solid-rock timescales. Wrong: the magnetic record proves a liquid mantle (D); the plume proves mantle speed (E). | section 9.4 |
