<!-- skel -->
- **HS-ESS2-3:** Build a model from evidence about Earth's interior to describe how heat-driven convection cycles matter. Emphasis: a model of layers sorted by density and a 3D model driven by mantle convection and plate motion; evidence from seismic maps, magnetic field records and high-pressure lab work.
- **HS-ESS2-1:** Model how Earth's inner and surface processes, working at different sizes and speeds, shape continents and the ocean floor. Emphasis: landforms and seafloor features result from building forces such as volcanoes and uplift and from wearing forces such as weathering, landslides and coastal erosion. Limit: No memorizing how particular named landforms formed.
- **HS-ESS1-5:** Weigh evidence of past and present crust movement and plate tectonics to explain how old crustal rocks are. Emphasis: plate tectonics as the explanation of rock ages; examples: ocean crust grows older away from ridges, and North American crust grows younger away from its ancient core.
- **RST.11-12.1:** Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of science and technical texts, attending to important distinctions the author makes and to any gaps or inconsistencies in the account. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **RST.11-12.7:** Integrate and evaluate multiple sources of information presented in diverse formats and media (e.g., quantitative data, video, multimedia) in order to address a question or solve a problem. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **WHST.11-12.1:** Write arguments focused on discipline-specific content. Parts, by verb: a (WHST.11-12.1a) Introduce precise, knowledgeable claim(s), establish the significance of the claim(s), distinguish the claim(s) from alternate or opposing claims, and create an organization that logically sequences the claim(s), counterclaims, reasons, and evidence.; b (WHST.11-12.1b) Develop claim(s) and counterclaims fairly and thoroughly, supplying the most relevant data and evidence for each while pointing out the strengths and limitations of both claim(s) and counterclaims in a discipline-appropriate form that anticipates the audience’s knowledge level, concerns, values, and possible biases.; c (WHST.11-12.1c) Use words, phrases, and clauses as well as varied syntax to link the major sections of the text, create cohesion, and clarify the relationships between claim(s) and reasons, between reasons and evidence, and between claim(s) and counterclaims.; d (WHST.11-12.1d) Establish and maintain a formal style and objective tone while attending to the norms and conventions of the discipline in which they are writing.; e (WHST.11-12.1e) Provide a concluding statement or section that follows from or supports the argument presented.
