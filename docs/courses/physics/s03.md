## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can people predict where something in space will be, years ahead?"
Day 31 opens with a dated route drawing and an aim-point inset: Galileo passed within one kilometre of its target on December 8, 1992, three years after launch. Students mark what would have to be predicted before seeing any explanation. The archive in field 10 supplies the encounter record.
On day 43, students explain the arrival through gravitational acceleration, orbital patterns and repeatedly corrected predictions, using force comparisons, orbit records and their step tables. Precision depends on measurements and corrections, beyond the classroom model.
Model equation reading on days 33 and 34. Thereafter require predictions before revealing records; ask students which evidence warrants changing a model.

- **2c:** "Four times the radius gives 64 times the squared period, hence eight times the period. The 2-day steps give top, left, bottom, right at days 2, 4, 6, 8, matching the reference. Equal path spacing needs varying time steps for an ellipse."
- **2d:** "The encounter and collision change the craft's motion, so update its starting conditions. Planet, moon and craft require more than one isolated pair. The planet and craft exert equal opposite forces, but the much smaller craft accelerates much more. Its inward acceleration bends its route. Tracking measurements test the scientists' prediction; engineers use the discrepancy to plan a correction and new observations."
