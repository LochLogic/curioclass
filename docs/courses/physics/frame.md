# Course frame: Physics

Course id `physics`, grade 11, draft frame. Its scope and sequence (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3) sets every day in order later; this frame names no single day.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 10 units, each driven by one anchor phenomenon or design problem.
- **Days.** 160 teaching days: 149 in units and 11 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year: 50-minute lessons, five a week, 32 weeks. Every investigation fits one lesson; a school with double periods may join two. Physics owns 20 expectations in 20 rows, fewer than Biology (30) or Chemistry (21), yet keeps the same 149 unit days for two reasons: it is the most quantitative of the three (every unit from 1 to 6 carries measured labs and algebra), and twelve of its handoffs come from grade 6 or 8 rows that have lain idle for three to five years, each needing a readiness check and a one-lesson reteach. About 7 days an expectation; units 1, 8 and 9 carry the most.
- **Standard.** The Next Generation Science Standards high school performance expectations Physics owns: HS-PS2-1 to HS-PS2-5, HS-PS3-2, HS-PS3-5, HS-PS4 (all five), and the Earth and space expectations folded in under the modified science domains model (HS-ESS1-1 to HS-ESS1-6, HS-ESS2-1, HS-ESS2-3; Desk card q-hs-science-core). Each expectation is taught in full: practice, core idea and crosscutting concept together, every clarification example named in the row, and inside its assessment boundary. The bar is docs/RUBRIC-SCIENCE.md (high school Gateway 2). In the Units table the **C3** column, a social studies column, holds for science the practices and crosscutting concepts the unit develops. **Check set adds** holds candidate practice, crosscutting concept and nature of science elements (NGSS appendices F, G and H) that the unit plan confirms or drops. "NGSS" never appears in student, teacher or family copy. Values rule (b): facts and backed sources decide claims; the age of the universe and of the Earth are taught as the evidence shows (BASELINE rule 5).
- **Base text.** None. High school science is Curio Class's own (docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md, "Our own"); the open high school units listed there are reference only, for sequence and ideas, never copied (no shared run of 8 words). **How phenomena are chosen:** something a class can see, measure or read as data; puzzling at grade 11 and explainable with the unit's core ideas; not an anchor grades 6 to 8, Biology or Chemistry already uses (the dropped phone, the truck playing music, the speaker, the Everest rocks, the Japan 2011 tsunami, the Arctic sea ice of Chemistry unit 9 and the others); a local swap is allowed if it needs the same ideas. **Sources:** public-domain data and images from federal science agencies (spacecraft, telescope, earthquake and sea floor records), open-licence (CC BY) material checked on its own terms, published numbers redrawn as our own, and data the class collects, including from a phone's free built-in sensors where a student has one (never required). No paid text, no library trip, no student account.
- **Labs and materials.** Every investigation has three paths: hands-on with classroom-cheap materials (carts or toy cars, ramps from shelving, masking tape, metre sticks, a stopwatch, springs, magnets, a compass, wire, a 1.5-volt cell, a slinky), a safe substitute named where one exists; a no-kit path from a provided data set or a recorded video that students measure frame by frame; and, where no class could run it (orbits, stars, the mantle), a Curio Class simulation tool (listed per unit, built later through the tool pipeline, DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-13). Every simulation runs with no account and has a paper path. Electricity stays at battery voltage; nothing plugs into the wall. A unit's cost to the school is stated in its unit plan; each hands-on day carries a Safety and set-up section (rubric row S5).
- **Storyline and models.** Each unit opens on its anchor, builds a question board, and ends on a transfer task with a phenomenon the class has not seen. Every unit names its initial class model and the points where evidence revises it.
- **Nature of science, taught on purpose.** Unit 3 (Kepler's laws found in data before Newton explained them), unit 7 (two models of light, each useful where the other fails) and unit 8 (the Big Bang theory as the account several lines of evidence agree on).
- **Engineering.** Unit 2 runs the course's design cycle with the practices Biology and Chemistry taught (HS-ETS1-1 to HS-ETS1-4 are owned there). Physics owns no engineering expectation; unit 6 uses the trade-off weighing again for digital information.
- **Math readiness.** Algebra I (grade 9) is done, so slope as a rate, rearranging formulas and reading squared terms are retrieval. Orbits use Kepler's laws, Newton's law of gravitation and a supplied step-by-step spreadsheet, never vectors or trigonometry beyond Geometry; motion stays in one dimension (the progression review's warning that orbits must not become an untaught vector course). Algebra II runs the same year; no unit needs it.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 band; every reading is our own or adapted public-domain text, with words taught before they are needed.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh-agent read. Every hands-on day needs a qualified science teacher's safety and materials read (FOUNDATION 7.3), above all units 1, 2 and 5. Unit 2 (car crashes) and unit 7 (sun exposure and cancer, phone radiation worries) need a teacher reader experienced in handling worry. Units 8 to 10 (the age of the universe and Earth, with students of faith) need a reader experienced in those conversations. Who reads, and any pay, is Cory's.

## Units

The order runs from motion on the ground (forces, momentum, a crash design) to motion in the sky (gravitation and orbits), then to energy, which ties the two together, then to the electric and magnetic side of fields. Waves come next because light carries the evidence for the last three units: stars and the Big Bang are known through light, and Earth's interior is mapped with seismic waves (unit 9 needs unit 6's wave math). The year ends on the age of the Earth, the end of the deep-time thread that started in grade 6.
