## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do we know what stars are made of, how they make elements, and how the universe began?"
Day 110 opens with laboratory element reference lines beside contrasting stellar spectra, sourced in field 10. Students mark matches, differences and unanswered questions before proposing explanations. Distinguish measured lines from calculated reference displays.
Day 123's explanation links spectral signatures to composition, fusion to stellar energy and elements, and redshift, background radiation and abundances to a hot early universe.
Model one spectral inference on day 112. Students then select evidence, challenge missing links and revise explanations with decreasing prompts through day 125.

**Item 1, reserved transfer.** A newly surveyed stellar population contains hydrogen-burning stars with these constructed mass and lifetime pairs: 0.7 solar masses and 30 billion years; one solar mass and ten billion years; four solar masses and 0.16 billion years. These comparisons are invented for practice, not observations. Published background in field 10 supports the direction of the pattern. A supplied solar reference gives age about 4.6 billion years and a core hydrogen-burning duration about ten billion years. Independent evidence cards distinguish a brief magnetic flare, repeated sunspot activity over several decades, and modeled gradual luminosity growth over billions of years. None is a direct movie of a complete solar lifetime. The long-term card, labeled "model prediction", uses Bahcall, Pinsonneault and Basu (2001), table 2, linked in field 10: solar ages 1, 4.6 and 8 billion years have luminosities 0.764, 1.003 and 1.363 times today's luminosity. These are calculated values, not a measured time series.

Build a labeled Sun-to-Earth energy model and use the population comparison to explain its fuel-limited duration. Place the three variability records on suitable timescales and explain which support a lifetime inference. A proposed replacement model powers the Sun by chemical combustion and moves hot gas all the way to Earth. Explain how your model repairs it.

**Answer and scoring:** Award two points per criterion, one for a correct relation and one for its evidence or mechanism: (1) nuclear fusion in the core releases energy (B); (2) radiation carries energy through the radiative region; (3) convection transports energy through the outer interior, followed by radiation across space to Earth (E); (4) absorbed radiation transfers energy into Earth's matter, with later outward radiation; (5) a finite fuel supply bounds the hydrogen-burning phase near ten billion years, including elapsed time; (6) greater mass can accompany shorter life because consumption rises faster than available fuel (D); (7) flares and roughly eleven-year magnetic activity are distinct from fuel exhaustion (C); (8) slow luminosity evolution and mass-lifetime comparisons constrain the long-term model, while a brief flare alone cannot measure it. Local energy arrows track transfers, not creation of energy. Missing links without a named misconception receive component feedback.

Meets: "The more massive star has more fuel but uses it much faster. The Sun's core releases fusion energy; radiation and then convection carry it outward, and radiation crosses space. Absorption transfers energy to Earth, which later radiates energy outward. The approximate ten-billion-year phase includes elapsed time. Flares and magnetic cycles do not reset the fuel clock; slow evolution and comparisons help test the long-term model." Does not: "Four times the mass means four times the life" (D).

**Item 2, reserved transfer.** A different survey finds the same three-line pattern in three galaxies. All survey values, including the background and abundance records below, are invented for practice. Rest wavelengths are 420, 510 and 630 nanometres. Galaxy A shows 424.2, 515.1 and 636.3; B shows 428.4, 520.2 and 642.6; C shows 436.8, 530.4 and 655.2. Supplied independent distance estimates are 140, 280 and 560 million light-years. Equal-luminosity comparison sources have apparent brightnesses 16, 4 and 1 in common units. These are rounded teaching relationships; no redshift calculation is required. Repeated lamp calibrations support the wavelength scale, and distances were not calculated from these redshifts.

An all-sky microwave record, after foreground removal, shows near-uniform radiation with small temperature differences around 2.7 kelvins. Abundance estimates give a lightly processed gas cloud 76 mass units of hydrogen and 24 of helium, and a low-heavy-element stellar envelope 75 and 25; trace elements are omitted. Independent methods give helium mass estimates within two units of these central values; hydrogen supplies the remainder. These constructed uncertainties illustrate checking, not an observed dataset.

Construct and justify a connected explanation of a hot early universe from these survey records. Compare it with the claim that galaxies are simply changing elements and the microwave map is a photograph of present stars. Explain how the evidence would be checked, what a persistent mismatch would require, and how an instrument team could improve a weak observation. Include one local radiation-energy transfer. Do not calculate a total energy for the universe.

**Answer and scoring:** Ten criteria, two points each for a correct relation with supporting evidence: (1) the preserved but stretched pattern identifies corresponding elements, rather than newly invented ones (I); (2) larger redshift at greater independent distance supports expansion, without privileging our galaxy as a centre (J); (3) brightness depends on distance as well as intrinsic luminosity (P); (4) near-uniform microwave radiation is a cooled early-universe relic, with small differences rather than individual star photographs (K); (5) both abundance estimates agree approximately with the prediction by mass, not atom number (L); (6) hydrogen and helium are mainly primordial while stars subsequently make and disperse heavier nuclei (M); (7) independent calibration, foreground checks and abundance uncertainties support reliability, without making a constructed record proof of real history; (8) using the same atomic laws across time and place makes these inferences possible; (9) convergence supports a scientific theory, and reproducible anomalies can require revision rather than dismissal (N); (10) scientific questions guide detector improvements, whose better evidence refines explanations, while a local example traces emitted radiation into absorbed thermal energy without asserting a simple global cosmic energy total (O).

Meets: "The whole line pattern stretches more in the more distant galaxies. Equal intrinsic luminosities explain why apparent brightness can constrain distance here. A pervasive cool background and nearly primordial gas give independent support for an earlier hot dense state, about 13.8 billion years ago. The proportions are by mass. Common atomic laws, independent calibrations and foreground checks make the inference testable. Better spectral resolution could separate blended lines; a persistent mismatch would change the explanation. A nearby absorber gains thermal energy from radiation, but these arrows do not supply a cosmic energy total." Does not: "The microwave spots are individual stars" (K).

**Item 3, reserved transfer.** Invented for practice: a nine-solar-mass star is in a late burning stage; a 0.6-solar-mass star is still fusing core hydrogen. Supplied spectra show oxygen in both outer atmospheres. A supplied simplified nucleus panel combines oxygen-16 and helium-4 into neon-20. Explain the different production possibilities and the limit of the atmospheric evidence; annotate the panel with total nucleons before and after. Contrast fusion of lighter nuclei through the iron group with sources of nuclei beyond iron. Explain where the initial hydrogen and helium originated and how later stellar processing and dispersal enriched matter. No detailed reaction pathway is requested.

**Answer and scoring:** Six criteria, two points each: (1) the massive late-stage star can build heavier nuclei through the iron group, while the smaller hydrogen-burning star chiefly makes helium at this stage (F); (2) the oxygen signature establishes atmospheric presence, not that either core is currently making oxygen (A); (3) two input nuclei become one while 16 + 4 = 20 total nucleons remain (H); (4) energy-releasing fusion of lighter nuclei does not continue indefinitely beyond iron; (5) massive-star explosions, neutron-star collisions and slow neutron capture in ageing stars contribute heavier nuclei, with contributions varying by element (G); (6) the explanation distinguishes initial light elements from later stellar processing and dispersal (M).

Meets: "The massive late-stage star can build nuclei through the iron group; the smaller core-hydrogen-burning star chiefly makes helium now. Atmospheric oxygen shows presence, not current core production, and can be inherited. This panel changes two nuclei into one while keeping twenty nucleons. Energy-releasing lighter-nucleus fusion does not continue indefinitely beyond iron; explosions, collisions and neutron capture in ageing stars contribute heavier nuclei. Hydrogen and helium are mainly primordial; stellar processing and dispersal enrich later clouds." Does not: "Every star eventually fuses its way to iron" (F).
