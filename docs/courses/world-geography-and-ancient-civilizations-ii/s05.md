## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

**India dossier, day 56.** Give a fresh physical map of the four regions and these original factual cards, based on units 1 to 4's references. Label them secondary reconstructions and date each development. Anchor Egyptian comparisons in the Old Kingdom, about 2650 to 2130 BCE, and Mesopotamian city comparisons around 2600 to 1800 BCE. Label later Babylonian astronomy separately. These are comparisons across periods, not claims of simultaneous contact.

- **I-a:** Mature Indus cities, about 2600 to 1900 BCE within the wider 3000 to 1300 BCE sequence, had planned drains, common weights and trade. Mesopotamian cities had canals and temples. Their texts name rulers; the Indus script remains undeciphered.
- **I-b:** Hinduism developed over centuries from Vedic traditions. Hindu traditions discuss dharma, karma, rebirth and liberation, with internal differences. Important texts include Vedas and Upanishads. Egyptian traditions connected divine authority with kingship and life after death.
- **I-c:** Gautama Buddha taught in India in the sixth to fourth centuries BCE. Buddhist teaching links suffering with craving, teaches that its ending is possible and sets out the Eightfold Path. Egyptian funerary traditions concern death and renewed life, a different teaching.
- **I-d:** Ashoka's edicts after Kalinga advocate restraint and moral rule. Rock Edict XIII claims moral influence as far as Hellenistic kingdoms in the Mediterranean and North Africa; the later Sri Lankan chronicle (Mahavamsa) describes missions toward Central and Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka. The four regions the standard names are North Africa, Central Asia, Southeast Asia and Mediterranean Europe. A ruler's claim and a later mission account are different kinds of evidence, and neither proves universal conversion. Later Gupta patronage supported Sanskrit literature, sculpture and painting. Mark Ashoka in the third century BCE and Gupta rule about 320 to 550 CE. Egyptian rulers also sponsored religious monuments and art in different periods.
- **I-e:** Varna describes an ideal hierarchy in texts; numerous jatis developed historically, organizing birth-based status and occupations. Discrimination harmed those treated as outside the hierarchy. Egyptian status also shaped opportunity without being the same system. India's Constitution abolished untouchability in 1950; discrimination persists and is resisted.
- **I-f:** Indian examples include Sanchi's architecture, Ajanta's paintings, ironworking, Aryabhata's astronomy and place-value numerals with zero developing over time. Mesopotamian observers recorded celestial patterns and used place value in a different number system. These developments were neither simultaneous nor identical.
- **I-g:** Oases and passes supported staged Asian exchange; deserts and mountains raised costs. Ports and seasonal winds linked India with East Africa, Western Asia and onward Mediterranean Europe. Mesopotamian rivers supported transport and linked networks too. Profit depended on demand and costs.

**Common I prompt:** "For each card, describe the Indian development and compare it with the named society. Explain a similarity or difference using the card or map. Finish with a judgment across economic, political and religious developments, two named cards, the periods and one source limit." Short sentences and map annotations are sufficient.

**China dossier, day 57.** Give new cards and a fresh map with the same regions:

- **C-a:** Shang and Zhou traditions of kingship and learning spanned about 1600 to 256 BCE. Scribes, divination and later schools supported learning; Zhou control weakened. Egyptian kingship also endured across changing dynasties without uninterrupted political unity.
- **C-b:** Confucian teachings concern ethical government, proper conduct and reciprocal but unequal family and social duties. Taoist teachings emphasize harmony with the natural way and non-forcing. Compare these with Hindu and Buddhist teachings recalled from earlier units, with a factual reference strip available.
- **C-c:** Qin conquest united rival states in 221 BCE. Appointed officials, strict laws, common measures and compulsory state projects strengthened central power. Walls and roads cost workers labor and lives; Qin ended in 206 BCE. Mauryan rulers also governed a large empire; Ashoka's declarations do not prove an absence of coercion.
- **C-d:** Chinese characters usually write meaningful syllables; many combine meaning and sound clues. Egyptian writing combined sound and meaning signs too. Alphabetic letters principally represent speech sounds. Indus signs remain undeciphered, limiting language comparisons.
- **C-e:** Chinese bronze casting and silk production preceded Qin. Han paper preceded gunpowder, documented much later in the ninth century CE. Bronze served vessels and weapons, silk supplied cloth and trade, and later gunpowder served fireworks and weapons. Indian ironworking and textiles illustrate different solutions to material needs.
- **C-f:** Chinese silk moved west through intermediaries; horses and glass moved east. Connected land and sea routes reached Asia, Africa and Europe. Buddhist teachers and texts reached China around the first century CE. India originated Buddhism and participated in the same networks.

**Common C prompt:** "For each card, describe the Chinese development and compare it with the named society, explaining a similarity or difference from evidence. Finish with a judgment across economics, politics and religion, two named cards, periods and a source limit." For C-b and C-f, compare India. Use secondary reconstructions as reconstructions, not eyewitness testimony.
