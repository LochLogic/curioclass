## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why does Europe look the way it does on a map, and why does the world set its clocks there?"
- **Hook, day 78.** Compare Europe's physical map with a longitude grid centered on Greenwich. Ask why mountains have physical locations but the zero line needed agreement; post the compelling question.
- **Supporting questions.** Write together: What do map symbols reveal (78 and 81)? How does a map extend a text (79)? Why settle here (80)? Why Greenwich rather than another meridian (82)? How can clocks locate ships and distinguish dates (83)? How do we compare local times (84)?
- **Closing claim, day 85.** Answer the compelling question using the settlement maps, river evidence and unseen conference record. Distinguish physical influences from conventions; name another explanation and an evidence limit.
- **Teaching moves.** Model map reading on 78, a causal claim on 80 and comparing sources on 82. Partners challenge explanations on 81; withdraw completed examples on 84. Source every document by maker, date and purpose. Teach peninsula, plain, navigable, population size, meridian, time zone, offset and chronometer through examples.

- **P, unseen physical map.** Make a world locator and European map with a title, compass, scale and symbols for water, rivers, mountains and plains. Number Europe, Mediterranean Sea, North Sea, Baltic Sea, Danube, Rhine, Alps, Pyrenees and North European Plain in that order. Label the Black Sea. Mark a schematic segment three scale bars long, each representing 120 kilometers. Label schematic measurements as practice, not travel distances. Add latitude lines at 35, 45, 55 and 65 degrees north and separate landform, climate and population panels from field 10. Mark a Rhine lowland point near Bonn (50.7 degrees north, 7.1 degrees east) and a high Alpine point (47.05 degrees north, 10.75 degrees east). Preserve the source classes and legends. Caption their 2021 population cells: 37,965 and 510 inhabitants respectively, each covering 25 square kilometers, not city totals. The lowland cell has more residents; this comparison does not establish a universal rule.
- **Q, unseen political map and text.** Show Austria and Hungary, capital stars at Vienna and Budapest, and another city dot at Graz. Label Vienna at approximately 48 degrees north, 16 degrees east, and Budapest at approximately 47 degrees north, 19 degrees east. Provide the original text: "Vienna is a city in Europe." Students must add information rather than repeat it.
- **R, unseen settlement dossier.** Write original factual captions beside maps: the Danube basin connects central Europe toward the Black Sea; plains provide farmland, while steep highlands restrict large continuous fields. P's lowland climate cell is temperate, with warm summers and no dry season; its high Alpine cell is tundra, with colder conditions limiting cultivation. These are 1991 to 2020 climate classes, not today's weather. Water supports farming, drinking supplies, energy and transport. The basin management plan reported about 80 million residents in 2021. A separate lower Rhine card describes sea connections, navigable channels and industrial centers. Compare these with the population panel, not with an invented population count. Include the caution that policy, infrastructure and migration also affect population; no single physical factor explains the total. Instruction uses other river-report passages and examples.
**Selected panels, checked November 2026.** P and R use Bonn and high Alps, with captions verified against both datasets. Exit 80 uses Nantes (47.2 north, 1.6 west): temperate, warm summers, no dry season; 194,299 residents in its population cell, not the whole city. Exit 81's supporting panel uses the Pyrenees cell at 42.7 north, 0.5 east: zero residents in 2021. Teaching uses other cells; withhold P's two selected cells until 85. Modern panels compare conditions, not prove past causes.

- **S, unseen primary source card.** Government delegates, International Meridian Conference, Washington, 1884; purpose: coordinate longitude and time. Final Act II proposes "the adoption of the meridian ... at the Observatory of Greenwich as the initial meridian for longitude." IV: "shall not interfere with the use of local or other standard time where desirable." French delegate Janssen, October 6: "Our meridian will be neutral if ... we choose a meridian based only upon geographical considerations". Ellipses mark omissions.

**S, provenance and separate glosses.** *Protocols of the Proceedings*, Washington, 1884, English edition; Final Act II, page 199, and IV, page 201; Janssen, session III, October 6, pages 56 to 57. Public-domain text; credit the conference record, omit distributor material. Words checked in the free transcriptions linked in field 10; [original page scans](https://www.ucolick.org/~sla/leapsecs/scans-meridian.html) also available. Glosses, outside quotations: initial means starting; neutral here means independent of a national observatory; universal day means a shared day count. These are delegates' arguments, not everyone's consent. Day 82 uses other passages; reserve all three excerpts for 85.
- **T, time and navigation card.** Give London reference time as Sunday 22:00. Supply these winter offsets: Tokyo nine hours ahead, Cairo two ahead, Mexico City six behind. Give a separate idealized navigation observation: the local Sun reaches noon while an accurate clock retaining Greenwich time reads 16:00. State that solar-time corrections are already allowed for. A separate Pacific diagram shows the date line near 180 degrees, with bends around political boundaries. These are supplied conditions, not rules inferred from a city's longitude.

**Meets:** "Landforms and water help explain European settlement and trade, but political decisions also shape maps. Greenwich became a shared reference through agreement and established chart use, not because Europe is Earth's natural center.
