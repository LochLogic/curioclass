## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why does Europe look the way it does on a map, and why does the world set its clocks there?"
**Hook, day 78.** Compare Europe's physical map with a longitude grid centered on Greenwich. Ask why mountains have physical locations but the zero line needed agreement; post the compelling question.
**Supporting questions.** Write together: What do map symbols reveal (78 and 81)? How does a map extend a text (79)? Why settle here (80)? Why Greenwich rather than another meridian (82)? How can clocks locate ships and distinguish dates (83)? How do we compare local times (84)?
**Closing claim, day 85.** Answer the compelling question using the settlement maps, river evidence and unseen conference record. Distinguish physical influences from conventions; name another explanation and an evidence limit.
**Teaching moves.** Model map reading on 78, a causal claim on 80 and comparing sources on 82. Partners challenge explanations on 81; withdraw completed examples on 84. Source every document by maker, date and purpose. Teach peninsula, plain, navigable, population size, meridian, time zone, offset and chronometer through examples.

## 5. End-of-unit assessment, whole

Day 85 (End-of-unit assessment): End-of-unit assessment: an unseen keyed map of Europe's seas, rivers and mountains read, a political map used to add to a text, Europe's settlement explained, time zones, longitude by clock, the date line and Greenwich explained and offsets worked out, with the compelling question answered

**Assessment pack and time.** Ten minutes for directions and targeted reteaching, then forty minutes unaided work. Printed directions keep the opening available for a day 84 gap. Allocate seven minutes to item 1, six to item 2, nine to item 3, twelve to item 4 and six to the closing claim. No student timer. Reserve the following map treatments, passages, numerical readings and questions for day 85. Required place names and defining facts recur; instruction and exit tickets use other coordinate readings, segments, passages and time problems.

- **P, unseen physical map.** Make a world locator and European map with a title, compass, scale and symbols for water, rivers, mountains and plains. Number Europe, Mediterranean Sea, North Sea, Baltic Sea, Danube, Rhine, Alps, Pyrenees and North European Plain in that order. Label the Black Sea. Mark a schematic segment three scale bars long, each representing 120 kilometers. Add separate landform, climate and population panels showing settlement concentrated in many lowlands and valleys, with fewer residents on high Alpine slopes. Label schematic measurements as practice, not travel distances.
- **Q, unseen political map and text.** Show Austria and Hungary, capital stars at Vienna and Budapest, and another city dot at Graz. Label Vienna at approximately 48 degrees north, 16 degrees east, and Budapest at approximately 47 degrees north, 19 degrees east. Provide the original text: "Vienna is a city in Europe." Students must add information rather than repeat it.
- **R, unseen settlement dossier.** Write original factual captions beside maps: the Danube basin connects central Europe toward the Black Sea; plains provide farmland, while steep highlands restrict large continuous fields. Climate ranges from colder mountain conditions to warmer lowlands. Water supports farming, drinking supplies, energy and transport. The basin management plan reported about 80 million residents in 2021. A separate lower Rhine card describes sea connections, navigable channels and industrial centers. Compare these with the population panel, not with an invented population count. Include the caution that policy, infrastructure and migration also affect population; no single physical factor explains the total. Instruction uses other river-report passages and examples.
- **S, unseen primary source card.** Identify the International Meridian Conference, Washington, 1884, its government delegates and its coordination purpose. Provide an original paraphrase of Final Act resolutions II and IV: delegates proposed Greenwich as the longitude reference; a universal day need not replace local standard time. Pair it with a short, previously unused paraphrase of the French delegate's argument for a meridian unaffiliated with a national observatory. The official record shows proposals and disagreement, not every resident's consent. Teach from other conference passages on 82.
- **T, time and navigation card.** Give London reference time as Sunday 22:00. Supply these winter offsets: Tokyo nine hours ahead, Cairo two ahead, Mexico City six behind. Give a separate idealized navigation observation: the local Sun reaches noon while an accurate clock retaining Greenwich time reads 16:00. State that solar-time corrections are already allowed for. A separate Pacific diagram shows the date line near 180 degrees, with bends around political boundaries. These are supplied conditions, not rules inferred from a city's longitude.

**Closing claim, items 3 and 4 together.** "Why does Europe look the way it does on a map, and why does the world set its clocks there? Use P, R and S, identify S's maker and purpose, and name a limit or competing explanation."

**Meets:** "Landforms and water help explain European settlement and trade, but political decisions also shape maps. Greenwich became a shared reference through agreement and established chart use, not because Europe is Earth's natural center. The 1884 delegates proposed a common meridian while preserving local time; the French proposal shows another choice was possible. Their record explains their decisions, not what all peoples wanted. Today countries choose their clock rules."

**Does not:** "Europe is the world's center and its mild climate made every country rich; everyone agreed that Greenwich must control all clocks" (C, D, H).

**Judging.** Items 2 and 3 give contrasting samples. For item 1, a complete answer above meets; "The Alps are a river and the Danube reaches the North Sea" does not (A). For item 4, the answer above meets; "Greenwich invented longitude in 1884, is the date line and controls every country's clocks" does not (D, E, F). The closing claim needs sourced evidence, causal reasoning, the physical and conventional distinction, and a limit. Mark knowledge and reasoning separately from spelling. **Accommodations:** read directions and source cards aloud without interpreting them; allow large maps, a scribe or recorded oral responses with the same evidence demands.

## 9. Family note

We will investigate Europe's land and settlement, then explain how people agreed on shared references for time. Ask your child to compare two places using a map and explain why their clocks can show different dates at the same moment.
