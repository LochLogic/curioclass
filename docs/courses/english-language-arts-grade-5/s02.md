## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do the living things of El Yunque depend on each other, and how do people protect it?"
**Hook, day 42.** Read *A Year Above the Forest Floor*'s opening: which living things could affect this observation?
**Inquiry.** Day 44: Which clauses connect organisms? Day 46: What does chronology show that problem and solution hides? Day 57: How do the adventures approach one theme differently? Day 59: How does sensory language shape setting?
**Closing claim.** Day 80's source note explains how the narrative represents interdependence and protection, using *A Year Above the Forest Floor*, the federal selections and *Protecting a Living Forest*.

**Cold information packet A: Studying and Protecting a Plot. Invented for practice.** Provide A1 and A4 in print and A2 and A3 as teacher-managed digital pages for day 49, with identical print copies.

A1, Field log. In January, a team marked a forest plot and recorded its shaded plants. In March, the team measured the light reaching those plants. In June, it returned to the same marked places. Using the same places let the team compare observations over time instead of confusing a change of location with a change of conditions.

A2, Two places. A shaded patch and a canopy gap both contained seedlings. In the shaded patch, leaves above blocked much of the sunlight. In the gap, fewer overhead leaves blocked light. The two places belonged to one forest, but the amount of light reaching their seedlings differed.

A3, Linked changes. When a branch fell, a gap opened in the canopy. More light reached the plants below because fewer leaves intercepted it. The broken branch did not create sunlight; it changed how much light reached that patch. Observing the same patch before and after the fall helped the team describe the change. The observations alone did not show how every plant would grow in the future.

A4, Keeping a record. Walkers sometimes stepped on the small labels marking observation places. Without readable labels, researchers could confuse one place with another. The team moved labels beside the path and drew a location map. It then checked labels against the map before taking notes. This was a proposed solution to a record-keeping problem, not evidence that all damage to the forest had ended.
