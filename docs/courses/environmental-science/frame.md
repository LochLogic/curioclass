# Course frame: Environmental Science

Course `environmental-science` (catalogue id `environmental`), grade 12 elective (the catalogue allows grades 11 to 12), draft frame. The scope and sequence sets the days later (PRODUCTION-PATH step 3); this frame sets the units, their order and their day counts.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 9 units, each driven by one anchor phenomenon or question a class can answer from the unit's own evidence (field counts, soil and water tests, monitoring records, grid and population data, survey records, temperature maps).
- **Days.** 160 course days: 149 in units and 11 for course-level checks and respond days. A full year: one lesson of about 50 minutes a day, five a week, 32 weeks. Each investigation's active sessions fit 50-minute lessons; a school with double periods may join two for field days. The course owns 21 outcomes in 20 rows, about 7 days an outcome. Days follow load: the capstone carries the most (unit 9, 20 days: a local investigation, a design and a policy route); the field units next (unit 1, 18 for the year's first field work, its sampling habits and three outcomes; unit 3, 18 for water quality, water supply and stream work; unit 8, 18 for three outcomes and a heat walk); unit 5, 16, carries mining, town waste and toxicology; the data-reading units carry the fewest (units 2, 4 and 6, 15 each; unit 7, 14). Readiness response is counted inside the receiving units: one-lesson reteach in units 1 to 8 when a check is missed (unit 9 needs none: every row it assumes is checked earlier in the year). Each unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment (and, in units over 25 days, its mid-unit assessments; no unit here runs over 25). The 50 minutes hold everything: set-up, clean-up and collecting work come out of the lesson, so each lab or field day names its clean-up time in the last Units column (about 5 minutes for a bench lab, about 10 for field or stream work, inside the active session); a readiness check takes 10 minutes; a fold-in on the Shorter path never takes a feedback or revision day.
- **Day kinds.** The first explicit teaching of anything (a sampling method, a diversity index, a water test, a dose comparison, a population step table, a term set) is an Understanding day. A Fluency day practices only what an earlier day taught: in this course, short sets of index, tolerance-score, grid-record, population-step and exposure readings on new data, and the course has few of them (about one a unit, none scored). Investigating, field and lab work, drafting, revising, designing and using taught ideas on a new case are Application days. Respond and Check days are as docs/GLOSSARY.md sets them. No score comes from the day a topic is first taught.
- **Grade 12 calendar.** The last weeks hold teaching: unit 9, the capstone, runs weeks 28 to 31 and the year-end check, course-level check 3, sits in week 32 (3 check days, 2 respond days). If seniors leave early, the year-end check moves forward and days fold in this order, up to 5: the second respond day after check 3; one of unit 9's data-collection days (each team samples one site, not two); the third check day (the check keeps its dossiers on units 6 to 8 and drops its oldest item); one Fluency day of unit 8; one Fluency day of unit 7. No fold takes a feedback or revision day, the capstone's scored report or its presentation day.
- **Standard.** Curio Class's own outcomes CUR-ENV.1 to CUR-ENV.21 (data/standards/curio-science-electives.txt), in our own words and in teaching order. Every high school Next Generation Science Standards performance expectation is owned by Biology, Chemistry or Physics (Desk card q-hs-science-core), so this course owns no NGSS code: it goes deeper on the ecosystem, human impact and engineering expectations those courses own, and each unit's What students learn names the expectation it deepens with its core owner. Nothing is taught twice: each unit says what the core course taught and what is new here. The bar is docs/RUBRIC-SCIENCE.md (high school Gateway 2). In the Units table the **Practices and crosscutting concepts** column holds the practices and crosscutting concepts the unit develops. **Check set adds** keeps the frame gate's Candidate prefix for practice, crosscutting concept and nature of science elements (NGSS appendices F, G and H) settled in the scope and sequence. "NGSS" never appears in student, teacher or family copy. Values rule (b): facts and backed sources decide claims; climate is taught as the evidence shows, as the Chemistry frame treats it; where a unit reaches a choice (an energy policy, a cleanup, a local proposal) science says what can happen and values and law decide what should, students keep the two apart, and no student is asked to declare a political view. Owned codes are the course's own outcomes alone. Each "Deepens" line names a core course's code as a revisit, taught as review and named so in the text; the core course that owns it checks it, and this course checks only its own outcomes.
- **Base text.** Introduction to Environmental Sciences and Sustainability (Emily P. Harris, with Chasidy Hobbs and Kwame Owusu-Daaku, University of West Florida, 2023, CC BY 4.0), 14 chapters, every section placed in the Source map at the end. It is built on Environmental Biology (Matthew R. Fisher, Open Oregon Educational Resources, 2017, CC BY 4.0) for chapters 1 to 11 and adds section 11.4 and chapters 12 to 14 (waste, economics and policy, cities). Credit line on adapted days, in the form docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md sets: "Adapted from Introduction to Environmental Sciences and Sustainability (Emily P. Harris, University of West Florida, 2023), chapter <n>, section <n>, CC BY 4.0, https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/. Changed by Curio Class." The book's own sources (open biology and sustainability texts, CC BY) are named in its introduction and carried in its section credits. Rule for the added parts: the source and attribution notes of chapters 12 to 14 and section 11.4 have still to be checked before any day adapts them (the book has no central exceptions page); a part that is not CC BY is not adapted, and the unit writes its own day. For chapters 1 to 11, Fisher's Environmental Biology is the fallback base if a Harris change does not check out. Figures are reused only where their own credit says CC BY or public domain. The copy is in the source store (S:\CurioClass-sources\harris-environmental-sciences-2023\, PDF and EPUB with NOTES.md and MANIFEST.csv; CC BY 4.0 except where noted, read from its copyright page on 2026-11-17) and recorded in docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md; Fisher's Environmental Biology, the fallback, was not loaded. This frame's Source map was built from the book's published table of contents; the scope writer checks each placement against the loaded copy. **How phenomena are chosen:** something a class can see, measure or read as data; puzzling at grade 12 and explainable with the unit's ideas; not an anchor, design problem or check case any other science course uses, kindergarten to Physics (Biology's Isle Royale wolves, Elwha dams and Newfoundland cod, Chemistry's Arctic sea ice and three river deltas, grade 7's rain gardens and US History II's Dust Bowl among them), nor Earth and Space Science's, unless the unit says it returns on purpose and what is new; a local swap is allowed if it needs the same ideas. **Sources beyond the base:** public-domain data from federal science agencies (water, air, energy, wildlife survey and health records), international and national statistics offices' population data, the published long-term records of the field experiments named, a community's own published accounts, published numbers redrawn as our own tables and graphs, and data the class collects. No paid text, no library trip, no student account.
- **Labs and materials.** Every investigation has three paths: hands-on with classroom-cheap materials (string and tent pegs for quadrats, a tape measure, clear jars for soil settling, a tin can for infiltration, strip tests for nitrate and pH, a transparency tube for turbidity, a kick net and white trays, a small solar cell with a multimeter, a shaded thermometer and an infrared surface thermometer, coloured drink powder and sand for a leaching model, dried beans for mark-recapture); a no-kit path from supplied data, photographs or a recorded run; and, where no class could do it (a century-long experiment, a national grid, a population projection), a Curio Class simulation tool (listed per unit, built later through the tool pipeline, DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-13). Every simulation runs with no account and has a paper path (printed maps and data tables, graph paper, step tables worked with a calculator). Each no-kit or simulation path names the student decisions, measurements and revisions it keeps; reading supplied results alone is partial coverage where an outcome asks students to sample, test or measure (CUR-ENV.1, CUR-ENV.4, CUR-ENV.7, CUR-ENV.17, CUR-ENV.19, rubric row S6), and the unit plan names the practical step still owed. Course rules: eye protection for every soil, water and leaching activity; gloves for soil and water work and hands washed after; no soil from roadsides or old building sites (lead); water tests are strips (nitrate, pH) and a transparency tube (turbidity), with no liquid or tablet reagents, and dissolved oxygen is read from published monitoring records or a loaned electronic meter; water with a visible algal scum or after a sewage overflow is never sampled or touched; stream work only in shallow, slow water below the knee, with an adult, never after rain, cuts covered; stream animals are kept in shaded water in white trays and returned where they were found; long trousers, socks over trouser legs and a tick check after field work in grass or woods; field work in groups with an adult, never alone, on school grounds or a site the school has approved; outdoor work stops at the first thunder; heat walks keep to shade and water breaks and are cancelled on dangerous heat days; electricity work at low voltage only, never mains wiring; no real ore or mine waste in class. Provisional class-of-30 and one-child costs are stated in the scope and sequence, with shared equipment counted once; each hands-on day carries a Safety and set-up section (rubric row S5).
- **Storyline and models.** Each unit opens on its anchor, builds a question board, and ends on a transfer task with a case the class has not seen. Every unit names its initial class model and the points where evidence revises it. The course runs from living things and the ground (units 1 and 2) through water, energy and mining (units 3 to 5), to people and wildlife (units 6 and 7), to air and heat in a city (unit 8), and ends with the class's own place (unit 9), which uses every method of the year.
- **Nature of science, taught on purpose.** Unit 1 (a designed experiment running since 1856 set against a correlation in the class's own field counts), unit 7 (one cause weighed against rival causes in survey records) and unit 8 (each link in a chain from an old map to a hot street argued on its own evidence).
- **Engineering.** The course owns one design outcome (CUR-ENV.20) and runs a full design cycle in unit 9, the fifth in high school after Biology units 9 and 10, Chemistry unit 7 and Physics unit 2; it deepens HS-ETS1-1, HS-ETS1-2 and HS-ETS1-3 (owned by Biology unit 9 and Chemistry unit 7). Units 2, 3 and 7 use the criteria, constraints and trade-offs those courses taught to judge real practices and programmes.
- **Math readiness.** For a grade 12 student every math lean falls on an earlier year (grade 7 math, Algebra I, Geometry, Algebra II), so each is retrieval by default (DESK 2026-11-15). A grade 11 taker meets the Algebra II leans the same year, several of them later than this course needs them; each carries an if-later line under Cross-subject, and the scope and sequence writes the short in-unit version for that case.
- **Reading level.** Grade 11 to 12 band; adapted base text and our own readings, with words taught before they are needed.
- **Literacy standards.** The science and technical reading and writing standards for grades 11 and 12 (RST.11-12, WHST.11-12; data/standards/ccss-literacy-6-12.txt) are practiced, not owned (Cory, Desk 2026-11-14: "Load them", no extra days). Each unit's "What students learn" cell ends with the codes its work practices. No unit teaches and checks a lettered part on purpose, so none is claimed in full, none is an owned standard and none adds days.
- **Paper path.** Every simulation, map and data set has a paper path: printed maps and data tables, graph paper plots, tally sheets for field counts, and step tables worked with a calculator, so no unit needs a screen.
- **Devices.** The course assumes one shared teacher display and student devices in pairs where a school has them; no student needs a device of their own. No owned outcome requires technology: CUR-ENV.12's projection is stepped by hand in an age-group table, CUR-ENV.7's dissolved oxygen comes from published records where no meter is loaned, and every simulation tool named in a unit has its paper path beside it, so a paper-path student makes every measurement, plot and decision in the same order. A scientific calculator per pair is assumed.
- **Safety read.** Every unit with a hands-on or field day (1 to 5, 7, 8 and 9) gets the safety agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md) before the course is called finished, above all unit 1 (outdoor sampling, stinging and poisonous plants, insects and ticks), unit 3 (stream work, water testing and stream animals), unit 8 (a heat walk) and unit 9 (team field work). The agent reads against these guides by title, each free to read: the position statement Safety and School Science Instruction (National Science Teaching Association, https://www.nsta.org/nstas-official-positions/safety-and-school-science-instruction), which covers field work; the School Chemistry Laboratory Safety Guide (Consumer Product Safety Commission and National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, 2006, https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2007-107/); the tick bite prevention pages (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/); the harmful algal bloom pages (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, https://www.cdc.gov/harmful-algal-blooms/); the heat safety and lightning safety pages (National Weather Service, https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat and https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning); and the safety data sheet of every material named. A student sign-off or conduct form is never cited as guidance. The last column of the Units table gives each hands-on or field unit's hazards and controls in a "Safety (days.csv words)" sentence that days.csv copies; the course has no teacher demonstration. The course's checks get the answer-key agent's read. No outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14).
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md). Unit 5 (abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation, illness, a nation's sovereignty over its land, its research and its cleanup): the agent first studies the Navajo Nation's own sources (the Navajo Nation Environmental Protection Agency's public pages, the Navajo Nation Human Research Review Board's public rules, the Nation's uranium commission reports, the Navajo Birth Cohort Study's community reports, the 1979 Church Rock tailings spill in the Nation's own accounts, and reporting in the Navajo Times) and the Native American Journalists Association's guide to reporting on Native nations, and checks that the unit shows the Nation's own scientists, leaders and decisions, not only harm. The unit uses only material the Navajo Nation has already released publicly, makes no contact with the Nation, designs no class collection of community health information, and uses "Diné" and "Navajo Nation" as the Nation does. That condition is Cory's (2026-11-17), and a fresh sensitivity agent reads unit 5 against these sources before its unit plan is written (data/plans/later.csv L68). Unit 8 (heat, race and 1930s lending maps in Richmond, Virginia): it first studies the Richmond neighbourhoods' own published accounts and community groups' heat projects, the published heat-mapping studies (Richmond in 2017, and the same team's study of 108 cities), one published study that tests the link against conditions the 1930s grades already recorded (density, industry, rail lines), and US History II unit 6's treatment of redlining. Unit 6 (family size, religion, poverty): published guidance on teaching population without blame, and Japan's and Niger's own statistics offices' publications. Unit 3 (unsafe tap water), unit 4 (coal communities and jobs) and unit 8 (asthma): published guidance for schools on health topics, former coal communities' own published accounts, and a climate journalism style guide. Unit 2 (farming families and the people moved from the Loess Plateau's steepest slopes): Chinese researchers' own published studies and the published accounts of the programme by those who ran it and lived under it. Unit 7 (removal programmes kill animals; families who keep reptiles): the park's and the state wildlife agency's own published removal programme pages. Unit 9 (local disputes): the town's own published plans and meeting records, chosen by the teacher. Unit 1: no community content beyond private allergy plans.

### Beside this course: Earth and Space Science

Earth and Space Science and Environmental Science are both grade 12 electives. Neither assumes the other, and a student may take them in either order or together, so any link between them is by course order, in prose, never in assumes or hands_on. Five ideas sit in both. For each, one course owns it; the other teaches only the part it uses, in one part-lesson in its own terms, so a student taking only that course is covered, and a student taking both meets the second as retrieval. A sixth touch (Earth and Space Science unit 7, past climate and a model test, against this course's unit 4, an energy transition) shares only Chemistry's climate rows, one looking back and one at mitigation.

## Units

The order starts outdoors with living things and the ground under them (units 1 and 2), because the sampling, testing and data habits built there are used all year. Water follows (unit 3), since nutrients wash off the soil of unit 2 into it. Energy (unit 4) and mining (unit 5) follow the materials people take from the Earth, and unit 5's question of who bears a risk opens the human half of the course: population and consumption (unit 6), then the wildlife they press on (unit 7), then the air and heat of the places people live (unit 8). Unit 9 brings every method home to the class's own place and ends the year on a proposal and the route it would take.

## Unit by unit: evidence, tools, writing, data and costs

Each unit's end-of-unit evidence sits inside its days in the Units table; no day count changes here. A product's draft, written feedback and revision come before its score; no score comes from the day a topic is first taught; an end day holds at most one full performance. Tools are named generically with their job and paper path (the scope writer builds TOOL-ASKS.md from this column). Curio Class writes the texts in the fourth column in one writing step before the units run.
