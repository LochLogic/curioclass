## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can facts we know help with bigger numbers and harder problems?"
**Hook, day 78:** Invented for practice: show three groups of four paper tens. What total do they represent, and how does knowing three fours help? Allow eight minutes, then pose the compelling question before retrieval.
**Supporting questions:** Why does splitting a factor preserve the product (day 80)? What quantity is unknown (day 83)? Which operation comes first (day 85)? How can multiplication explain a larger quotient (day 87)?
**Closing claim:** "I can use known facts and place value to solve larger equal-group and two-step problems and explain my equations." Tens Reasoning, Story Grid, Equation Positions and Two-Step Record supply the evidence considered on day 90.
**Teaching:** Model tens on day 78 and matching rectangle parts to partial products on day 80. Step back for strategy choices on day 81 and unknowns on day 83. Model operation order on day 85, including same-priority operations from left to right. Connect both division meanings to decomposed dividends on day 87 before day 88's practice.

- **3.NBT.A.3:** Multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10 in the range 10–90 (e.g., 9 × 80, 5 × 60) using strategies based on place value and properties of operations. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.C.7:** Fluently multiply and divide within 100, using strategies such as the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8) or properties of operations. By the end of Grade 3, know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers. (The "such as" list gives examples, not required parts.) Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.NBT.A.2:** Fluently add and subtract within 1000 using strategies and algorithms based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.A.3:** Use multiplication and division within 100 to solve word problems in situations involving equal groups, arrays, and measurement quantities, e.g., by using drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number to represent the problem. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.A.4:** Determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation relating three whole numbers. For example, determine the unknown number that makes the equation true in each of the equations 8 × ? = 48, 5 = _ ÷ 3, 6 × 6 = ?. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.MD.C.7c:** Use tiling to show in a concrete case that the area of a rectangle with whole-number side lengths a and b + c is the sum of a × b and a × c. Use area models to represent the distributive property in mathematical reasoning. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.B.5:** Apply properties of operations as strategies to multiply and divide. Examples: If 6 × 4 = 24 is known, then 4 × 6 = 24 is also known. (Commutative property of multiplication.) 3 × 5 × 2 can be found by 3 × 5 = 15, then 15 × 2 = 30, or by 5 × 2 = 10, then 3 × 10 = 30. (Associative property of multiplication.) Knowing that 8 × 5 = 40 and 8 × 2 = 16, one can find 8 × 7 as 8 × (5 + 2) = (8 × 5) + (8 × 2) = 40 + 16 = 56. (Distributive property.) Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.D.8:** Solve two-step word problems using the four operations. Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity. Assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation strategies including rounding. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.A.2:** Interpret whole-number quotients of whole numbers, e.g., interpret 56 ÷ 8 as the number of objects in each share when 56 objects are partitioned equally into 8 shares, or as a number of shares when 56 objects are partitioned into equal shares of 8 objects each. For example, describe a context in which a number of shares or a number of groups can be expressed as 56 ÷ 8. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **3.OA.B.6:** Understand division as an unknown-factor problem. For example, find 32 ÷ 8 by finding the number that makes 32 when multiplied by 8. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
