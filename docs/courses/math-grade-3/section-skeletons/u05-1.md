## 1. Standards and practices

<!-- skel -->
- **3.NBT.A.3** (days 78): Multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10 in the range 10–90 (e.g., 9 × 80, 5 × 60) using strategies based on place value and properties of operations.
- **3.OA.C.7** (days 78, 79, 80, 81): Fluently multiply and divide within 100, using strategies such as the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8) or properties of operations. By the end of Grade 3, know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers.
- **3.NBT.A.2** (days 78, 79, 80, 81): Fluently add and subtract within 1000 using strategies and algorithms based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction.
- **3.OA.A.3** (days 79, 80, 81): Use multiplication and division within 100 to solve word problems in situations involving equal groups, arrays, and measurement quantities, e.g., by using drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number to represent the problem.
- **3.OA.A.4** (days 79, 80, 81): Determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation relating three whole numbers. For example, determine the unknown number that makes the equation true in each of the equations 8 × ? = 48, 5 = _ ÷ 3, 6 × 6 = ?.
- **3.MD.C.7c** (days 80): Use tiling to show in a concrete case that the area of a rectangle with whole-number side lengths a and b + c is the sum of a × b and a × c. Use area models to represent the distributive property in mathematical reasoning.
- **3.OA.B.5** (days 80, 81): Apply properties of operations as strategies to multiply and divide. Examples: If 6 × 4 = 24 is known, then 4 × 6 = 24 is also known. (Commutative property of multiplication.) 3 × 5 × 2 can be found by 3 × 5 = 15, then 15 × 2 = 30, or by 5 × 2 = 10, then 3 × 10 = 30. (Associative property of multiplication.) Knowing that 8 × 5 = 40 and 8 × 2 = 16, one can find 8 × 7 as 8 × (5 + 2) = (8 × 5) + (8 × 2) = 40 + 16 = 56. (Distributive property.)

Unit plan field 2, the lines naming this section's days:

> **Hook, day 78:** Invented for practice: show three groups of four paper tens. What total do they represent, and how does knowing three fours help? Allow eight minutes, then pose the compelling question before retrieval.
> **Supporting questions:** Why does splitting a factor preserve the product (day 80)? What quantity is unknown (day 83)? Which operation comes first (day 85)? How can multiplication explain a larger quotient (day 87)?
> **Teaching:** Model tens on day 78 and matching rectangle parts to partial products on day 80. Step back for strategy choices on day 81 and unknowns on day 83. Model operation order on day 85, including same-priority operations from left to right. Connect both division meanings to decomposed dividends on day 87 before day 88's practice.
>
> - **3.NBT.A.3:** Multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10 in the range 10–90 (e.g., 9 × 80, 5 × 60) using strategies based on place value and properties of operations. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
>
> **Fluency:** Maintain 3.OA.C.7 and 3.NBT.A.2 through one shared five-minute rotation of spaced mixed facts and written computation each teaching day, with private feedback and no time pressure. Days 81, 83 and 88 apply established strategies; fact memory remains a year-end goal.
> **Carried:** Restore unit 4's arrays and area model; day 80 ties rectangle partitions to equations, day 81 removes grids, and day 83 withdraws supplied arrays. Base-ten drawings connect tens to products on day 78, fade on day 79, return for partitioning on day 80 and division on day 87, then withdraw on day 88. Tape diagrams tie groups to equations on day 79, carry two-step quantities on day 84 and withdraw as supplied scaffolds on day 89. Student drawings remain available for explanation and repair.
