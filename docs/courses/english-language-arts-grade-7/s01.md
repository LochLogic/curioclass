## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "When the river a family has fished for generations changes, what do they hold on to, and what do they let go?"

Day 1 opens on the first contrasting sibling accounts: what does each notice about the river? Introduce the compelling question and supply river background without explaining the plot.

Supporting questions: What does each sibling notice (day 3)? How does the river constrain a choice (day 5)? Which details complicate their apparent disagreement (day 7)? Where is a theme tested (day 11)? What can federal evidence establish that narration cannot (day 12)? How does the ending revise an earlier interpretation (day 20)?

The day 20 claim explains contrasting views and theme through several novella chapters. Federal evidence checks background, not motives.

Model several citations on day 1, theme development on day 2, contrasting views on day 3 and discussion on day 4. Day 4 also models, in five minutes, an elaboration question and acknowledging new evidence. Model revision, concise language and sentence combining on day 14, then fade frames. Discussion routine: partners annotate first; a facilitator states the question, roles and deadline; speakers cite, paraphrase, request elaboration and return to the question. A recorder checks progress halfway. Each student writes what new evidence changed or qualified a view. Model day 4; practise days 9, 18 and 26; assess day 29. Feedback names one move.

### Day 27 reading text: The Empty Square

Curio Class, 2026. Ours. This story is invented for practice. Its students and exhibition are fictional. Do not distribute it before day 27.

**1. Lin.** By the lunch bell, our poster would explain which school exhibition visitors liked best. I had drawn a square for each display and borrowed a gold pen for the winner. Dev stood beside the survey box in the corridor, smoothing a stack of cards. Twenty visitors had returned cards. Seven chose the model railway, five the painted scenery, and eight left the choice blank. I put the blanks under the box. "No favourite," I said. Dev kept looking at them. Beyond him, the railway display flashed through its window. The other displays were inside rooms whose doors had swung shut when the corridor filled. There were eleven minutes before we had to put up the poster.

**2. Dev.** Lin had made the squares exactly the same size, even measuring them twice. I knew she wanted to be fair. But when I had handed out the cards, people were still asking where the exhibitions were. One visitor had pointed at the shut doors and asked whether those rooms were open. I had answered, then hurried away to collect another card. Now Lin was counting. If I mentioned the question, she might ask why I had not written it down. I took out a blank card and turned it over. On the back someone had drawn a small rectangle with a question mark inside. It might have been a door. It might have been nothing.

**3. Lin.** "Ask them," Dev said. He held out the card as though it were evidence. "Which them?" I asked. We had not taken names. He went into the corridor anyway. I printed MOST POPULAR above the railway square, but stopped before using the gold pen. Beside the railway and scenery squares I drew one more square for the blank cards and left it empty. Through the doorway I watched a visitor glance into the railway room and pass two closed doors without slowing. I had put arrows on the poster to help people find the results. We had put no arrows outside to help them find the displays.

**4. Dev.** I found two people who remembered leaving a blank. One had visited only the railway. The other had not wanted to choose between two displays. I could not find the person who had drawn the rectangle. "So one means 'not enough seen' and one means 'hard to choose'," Lin said when I came back. "And six mean we don't know," I added. She rubbed at MOST POPULAR until the paper grew thin. I wanted to tell her that nobody would notice the heading. Instead I fetched a clean strip of paper. We could cover it without tearing the square.

**5. Lin.** We wrote CHOICES ON TWELVE COMPLETED CARDS. Beside the empty square we explained that eight cards gave no choice, for reasons we had not all established. The gold pen lay between us. "We could ask again, with the doors open," Dev said. The bell rang before I answered. A new survey would have to wait until the next exhibition. I used the gold pen to outline the unanswered square. It was the largest thing on the poster now, though I had measured all the squares the same.

**6. Dev.** At lunch someone asked why there was no winner. Lin pointed to the heading rather than to me. I explained the two different blanks we had traced and the six we had not. "Then what should we do next time?" the visitor asked. Lin turned the poster over. On its plain back she drew the corridor, this time with every doorway marked. I picked up a pencil and wrote the question I should have written down that morning.

**Cold-story text record.** The Empty Square has 602 words, excluding headings; a validated quantitative complexity score is not yet available. Its central problem is an unwarranted public conclusion from incomplete information. Alternating accounts reveal Lin's wish for a fair result and Dev's hesitation to expose his own omission. The equal squares acquire a figurative meaning, and the unresolved cards qualify the ending. Readers need no survey expertise. This whole story changes topic from the novella while testing the same reading skills. Supply accessible print without discussing its conflict. The text agent must check its grade-level demand before use.
