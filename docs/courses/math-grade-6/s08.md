## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What is typical, and how much do the answers vary?"
**Hook, day 142:** Measure assorted teacher-supplied paper strips. Would one length describe them all? Ask the compelling question before sorting questions and gathering the lengths.
**Supporting questions:** What makes a question statistical (142)? What does grouping hide (145)? Why does equal sharing give the mean (147)? Can equal means hide different spreads (150)? Which center fits (152)?
**Closing claim:** We can answer a statistical question with an accurate display and a contextual account of center, variability and shape. The Paper-strip report on day 155 and short prompts on 156 establish this claim.
**Teaching moves:** Model equal intervals on 143, distance from the mean on 149 and quartiles on 153. Step back for distribution comparisons on 146 and center arguments on 152. Teacher pages explain why grouping loses exact values, why distances cannot cancel and why quartiles need ordered data.

<!-- skel -->
- **6.SP.A.1:** Recognize a statistical question as one that anticipates variability in the data related to the question and accounts for it in the answers. For example, “How old am I?” is not a statistical question, but “How old are the students in my school?” is a statistical question because one anticipates variability in students’ ages. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **6.SP.A.2:** Understand that a set of data collected to answer a statistical question has a distribution which can be described by its center, spread, and overall shape. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **6.SP.B.4:** Display numerical data in plots on a number line, including dot plots, histograms, and box plots. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **6.SP.A.3:** Recognize that a measure of center for a numerical data set summarizes all of its values with a single number, while a measure of variation describes how its values vary with a single number. Parts, by verb: whole standard.
- **6.SP.B.5:** Summarize numerical data sets in relation to their context, such as by: (The "such as" list gives examples, not required parts.) Parts, by verb: a (6.SP.B.5a) Reporting the number of observations.; b (6.SP.B.5b) Describing the nature of the attribute under investigation, including how it was measured and its units of measurement.; c (6.SP.B.5c) Giving quantitative measures of center (median and/or mean) and variability (interquartile range and/or mean absolute deviation), as well as describing any overall pattern and any striking deviations from the overall pattern with reference to the context in which the data were gathered.; d (6.SP.B.5d) Relating the choice of measures of center and variability to the shape of the data distribution and the context in which the data were gathered.

## U7. Talk and writing

Ask on 146: "What can this bin tell us, and what has it hidden?" On 152: "Which claim survives changing one extreme value?" Day 154's summary is judged for count, measurement description, correct measures and evidence for their contextual choice.

## U8. Practice that returns

Openings use two minutes of earlier ratio and fraction reasoning and three minutes rotating long division and the four decimal operations across five days. Use feedback and untimed retries. No new fluency target is set.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach observation, frequency, bin, center, variability and quartile with labelled examples. Offer "I chose ___ because the distribution ___." Read contexts aloud; students independently read short labels and numbers. Offer enlarged axes, raised marks and a scribe for precise plotting directions. Extend within these standards by constructing equal-mean sets with different spreads, then proving the difference.

## U10. Two rooms

For one child, use supplied strips and data instead of a class survey, speak both sides of a center argument and retain written evidence. On 157 revise missed components before a fresh retry. With one projector, display models while every student draws on paper.
