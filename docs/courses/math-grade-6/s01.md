## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can we find the area of a shape we have no formula for?"
**Hook, day 1:** Compare two paper mosaics made from identical pieces, one with gaps. Ask which covers more and whether moving pieces changes their area. Then pose the compelling question. The first tiling notice takes five minutes.
**Supporting questions:** What stays unchanged when pieces move (day 2)? Why must height be perpendicular (day 4)? Why halve a triangle product (day 7)? How can a flat pattern account for hidden faces (day 12)?
**Closing claim:** We can split unfamiliar polygons into known shapes and use every face of a net to defend a surface-area estimate. The tent on day 16 and the remaining short prompts on day 17 check this claim.
**Teaching moves:** Model one cut and its matching equation, compare two student methods, then withdraw the worked example. On days 9 and 13, ask students to test an incorrect height or net before explaining the repair. Teacher notes must distinguish perpendicular distance from side length and surface coverage from filling.

## U3. Exit tickets

Each exit is one fresh question with at most two parts, completed independently in five minutes. Reserve these cases for the exit; teaching and practice use different dimensions. Answers include square units unless stated otherwise. A wrong answer names one error from field 6. No separate exit is added to days 16 and 17; T1, T2 and P1 to P5 are their evidence.

- **E1, day 1, 6.G.A.1:** An L-shaped region on grid paper is a column of 6 unit squares with a row of 3 more unit squares attached along the bottom. (a) Find its area. (b) The region is cut into its two bars and re-pasted end to end as one long bar, with no gaps or overlaps. Did the area change? Explain what the cutting and re-pasting each change. Answer: 9 square units; the area stays 9, because the same pieces are only moved and none are added or lost. Wrong: the long bar has a different area (A).
- **E2, day 2, 6.G.A.1, MP7:** A region is a 6-unit by 5-unit rectangle with a 2-unit by 2-unit corner removed. Find its area and explain how your diagram accounts for every piece once. Answer: 30 - 4 = 26 square units, or two rectangles of areas 20 and 6. Wrong: 34 adds the removed corner (I).
- **E3, day 3, 6.G.A.1:** A parallelogram has a horizontal base of 7 units and perpendicular height 3 units. (a) Show a cut and rearrangement into a rectangle, label its dimensions and state the area. (b) Name which opposite sides of the original figure are parallel. Answer: move an end triangle to the other end to obtain a 7 by 3 rectangle with area 21; both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. Wrong: area 10 adds the dimensions (K).
- **E4, day 4, 6.G.A.1 and begun 6.EE.A.2c:** A parallelogram has base 9 cm, sloping side 5 cm and perpendicular distance 4 cm between the base and opposite side. Choose the matching base and height, evaluate A = bh and explain the rectangle it represents. Answer: 9 x 4 = 36 square centimeters, from a rearranged 9 by 4 rectangle. Wrong: 45 uses the sloping side as height (B).
- **E5, day 5, 6.G.A.1 and begun 6.EE.A.2c:** Evaluate A = bh for parallelograms with (a) b = 6 cm and h = 4 cm; (b) b = 3/2 cm and h = 2/3 cm. Answer: 24 and 1 square centimeters. Wrong: 10 and 13/6 add base and height (K).
- **E6, day 6, 6.G.A.1:** Two identical right triangles each have perpendicular legs 5 units and 2 units. Show how to join them into a parallelogram, label its dimensions and state its area. Answer: join along the hypotenuse to form a 5 by 2 rectangle, a parallelogram of area 10 square units. A correct nonrectangular arrangement also qualifies with its matching dimensions. Wrong: 20 counts each triangle as an entire 5 by 2 rectangle (C).
- **E7, day 7, 6.G.A.1:** A triangle has base 9 cm and perpendicular height 6 cm. Find its area and show why a matching parallelogram contains twice that area. Answer: 27 square centimeters; two congruent copies give a parallelogram of area 54. Wrong: 54 for the single triangle (C).
- **E8, day 8, 6.G.A.1 and begun 6.EE.A.2c:** Evaluate A = (1/2)bh for triangles with (a) b = 4 cm and h = 3 cm; (b) b = 3/2 cm and h = 0.8 cm. Answer: 6 and 0.6 square centimeters. Wrong: 12 and 1.2 omit halving (C).
- **E9, day 9, 6.G.A.1:** In the printed obtuse triangle, AB is horizontal and 6 cm long. C is 3 cm beyond B and 4 cm above line AB, so BC is 5 cm. (a) Draw the height for base AB and find the area. (b) The drawing also prints a perpendicular height of 4.8 cm for base BC. Show that base BC with this height gives the same area, and explain why. Answer: exterior height 4 cm and area 12 square centimeters; (1/2) x 5 x 4.8 = 12. Wrong: the exterior height is unusable (E); using BC itself as AB's height gives 15 (B). Students need not derive the supplied side length.
- **E10, day 10, 6.G.A.1 and begun 6.EE.A.2c:** A paper banner, invented for practice, is a trapezoid with parallel bases 2 m and 6 m and perpendicular height 3 m. Find its area by decomposing it and show a numerical expression for your pieces. Answer: a 2 by 3 rectangle and triangles with combined base 4 and height 3 give 6 + (1/2) x 4 x 3 = 12 square meters. Any valid decomposition qualifies. Wrong: 18 counts the triangles as full rectangles (C).
- **E11, day 11, 6.G.A.4:** (a) Describe the faces of a rectangular prism with edges 7 cm, 2 cm and 1 cm, and of a square pyramid. (b) Which units measure covering their faces and filling their interiors? Answer: the prism has pairs of 7 by 2, 7 by 1 and 2 by 1 rectangles; the pyramid has a square and four triangles. Covering uses square centimeters, filling cubic centimeters. Wrong: cubic centimeters for covering (F).
- **E12, day 12, 6.G.A.4:** Draw a labeled net of a closed 3 cm by 2 cm by 1 cm rectangular prism and use it to find surface area. Answer: a strip of rectangles 3 by 2, 3 by 1, 3 by 2, 3 by 1, with a 2 by 1 rectangle attached to each opposite 2 cm end of the first rectangle; 6 + 3 + 6 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 22 square centimeters. Other valid nets qualify. Wrong: 20 omits one 2 by 1 face (D).
- **E13, day 13, 6.G.A.4:** The first printed cube net has four squares in a row and one above and below the second square; the second is a staircase of three pairs of squares, each pair shifted one square along from the pair before. Every square has side 3 cm. A third drawing omits one face. (a) Use the two valid nets to find the surface area. (b) Explain why the third cannot close the cube and how much area it omits. Answer: both valid nets give 6 x 9 = 54 square centimeters; the third has only five faces and omits 9 square centimeters. Wrong: 45 covers the closed cube (D).
- **E14, day 14, begun 6.EE.A.1:** A cube has edge length 5 cm. (a) Write and evaluate a power for one square face's area. (b) Write and evaluate a power for its volume, explaining the units. Answer: 5^2 = 25 square centimeters; 5^3 = 125 cubic centimeters. Wrong: 10 and 15 multiply by the exponent (G).
- **E15, day 15, 6.G.A.4 and begun 6.EE.A.1 and 6.EE.A.2c:** A cube has edge length 1.5 cm. Evaluate A = 6s^2 and explain what each factor counts. Answer: 6 x 2.25 = 13.5 square centimeters; six equal faces, each of area 1.5 x 1.5. Wrong: (6 x 1.5)^2 = 81 squares the entire product (H); 18 interprets the exponent as multiplication by 2 (G).
- **E18, day 18, 6.G.A.1 and 6.G.A.4:** After correcting your earlier error, solve this changed question. (a) A triangle has base 11 cm and exterior perpendicular height 2 cm. Find its area and mark the height on the supplied drawing. (b) Label every face area on the supplied valid net of a closed 2 cm by 5 cm by 6 cm prism and find the total. Answer: 11 square centimeters; face pairs 10, 12 and 30 give 104 square centimeters. Wrong: 22 for the triangle (C); 94 after omitting a 10-square-centimeter face (D). Preserve the original and retry separately.

## U7. Talk and writing

Day 2: "What would have to happen for moving pieces to change covered area?" Day 9: "Does choosing another base change the triangle?" Day 13: "Where is this hidden face in your net?" On day 10, write a short area argument with a labeled decomposition, a matching expression and square units. These are the teacher's three look-fors.

## U8. Practice that returns

Field 4 holds the warm-ups. Mix previously explained cases instead of introducing new procedures on fluency days. A student estimates, substitutes, explains a choice, then checks against a worked answer. Rotate whole-number, fraction and decimal values across days 5, 8 and 15. There is no speed target or countdown.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach base, perpendicular, face and net through labeled diagrams and gestures. Offer: "I chose this height because ___." Read context prompts aloud; independent reading is limited to a short prompt and its labels. Provide large grids, high-contrast outlines and pre-cut pieces. Accept spoken explanations or a scribe; the child still makes mathematical decisions. Partners describe diagrams aloud when needed. Extend within the standard: find another decomposition or net and prove that it gives the same area, rather than adding a new solid.

## U10. Two rooms

At home, use one paper model and replace partner talk with explaining a choice to the adult. Reduce repeated practice, preserving the worked example, independent exit and response. On day 18, revise flagged work after written feedback and keep the retry beside it. With one projector, display one diagram while every child draws, labels and calculates on paper; no device is required.
