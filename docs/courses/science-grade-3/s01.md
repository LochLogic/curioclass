## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What makes a swing start, stop and keep coming back?"
Day 1: watch an empty swing from outside its path, or an original drawing beside the pendulum. The teacher releases the pendulum, then holds it still at the bottom. Students draw an explanation.
On day 13, students explain starting, stopping and returning using box-test evidence and motion records: pushes can change motion; gravity pulls down while the string guides the curved path. Motion carries it past the bottom. The path repeats while swings become smaller.
Model safe observation on day 1, fair comparison on day 3 and recording on day 9. Groups then choose tests and defend conclusions. Read notebooks daily to choose support.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


Group notices and wonders into causes and patterns on the question board. Days 1 to 5 ask, "Can pushes act without movement?" Class controlled tests connect contact forces with cause and effect. Days 6 to 10 ask, "Will our explanation work in another test, and what repeats?" Group tests lead to position records and predictions. Days 11 to 13 ask, "When should we change a prediction?" Bowl observations and a changed pendulum test the pattern's limits. Mark answered questions with evidence; retain unresolved ones. Transfer uses a hanging delivery basket on day 14 and an unfamiliar cardboard delivery sled on day 15.
No engineering expectation is claimed. A turning point is not balanced merely because motion pauses. A failed prediction calls for checking observations as well as changed conditions.
Carried: Cause and effect and force arrows begin on day 1; day 4 adds opposing arrows from box evidence; day 8 distinguishes the string's pull from gravity using pendulum observations. Day 13 reaches consensus: arrows describe forces on one object, not its travel. Patterns and position tables begin on day 1; day 9 adds repeated ordered observations; day 12 checks predictions at a fixed observation beat after changing string length. Day 13 consensus separates repeating order from shrinking travel. Both assessments use these models; none is withdrawn.

Look-fors follow the point components above, each recorded separately. All 16 points require investigating or interpreting observations with a core idea on a case new when produced: 100 percent. The paper basket and the sled differ from every worked case. The motion task uses observed regularity to predict; the force task tests a cause by controlling variables. Approximate hand pushes are not calibrated forces: rest alone does not establish equality of the hands, since the surface can also push. Reward a child who identifies that limitation. The teacher checks that each child's own station trial is recorded; shared observations do not substitute for an individual record.

Motion sample meeting the look-fors: "The basket turned right, left, right, left. It should turn right next because the same order happened twice." The record also shows the intervening center crossings. Non-meeting sample: "Left, because I like left" (G). Force sample meeting the look-fors: "I used the same sled and tray, changed only how strong one push was and tried each way more than once. With about equal opposite pushes it stayed near the start, 1 cm; with one stronger push it moved 9 cm. My arrows show those pushes." Non-meeting sample: "I changed the strength and the tray so it was a fair comparison" (D).

Accommodations: read aloud, enlarge records, accept dictated explanations and let a partner apply the child's directed push while the child chooses conditions and records evidence. Do not supply the prediction or causal explanation. The no-kit route is unavailable for the check days; a supplied record cannot establish actual conducting of the force investigation.

Section exit tickets use cases not worked earlier that day, all labeled "invented for practice". Day 5: fresh drawings label initial motion and push arrows: a resting wagon pushed left, a left-rolling wagon pushed right, a left-rolling wagon pushed toward the top of the page, and a resting package with equal opposing pushes. Vary arrow lengths to compare strengths. Ask, "How do arrow direction and length explain these four changes or nonchanges, and where does gravity point?" Key: the first wagon starts left; the second slows under the opposing push; the third turns toward the top of the page under the sideways push; equal opposing pushes leave the resting package still. Longer arrows mean stronger pushes; gravity points down toward the floor. Errors map to A, B or C only when that claim appears. Ask, "What position comes next, and what repeated observations support it?" Key: center, supported by both earlier right-turn-to-center observations; missing evidence is an incomplete component; assign G only to expressed preference or rejection of observational support. Say why", key no, the pushes cancel; then a released cart's record start, middle, far end, middle twice, "Where is it next?", key start, a little lower is fine, because the order repeated. ## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A still object has no forces acting on it. | Confuses rest with absence of forces. Repair by drawing the pushes on a held box. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| B | Equal opposing pushes make a resting object start moving. | Misses cancellation. Repair by predicting and testing opposing pushes on one box. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| C | A longer force arrow means farther travel. | Confuses force strength with travel distance. Repair by labeling arrow strength beside a separate motion record. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| D | Changing two conditions gives a fair causal comparison. | Cannot isolate a cause. Repair by crossing out one change in a proposed test. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| E | Repeating motion stays forever at its starting side. | Does not use the observed alternation. Repair by arranging two cycles of turning-position cards. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| F | Every swing reaches exactly the same endpoint forever. | Extends a pattern beyond its evidence. Repair by comparing successive measured turning distances. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| G | A prediction needs no observation evidence. | Substitutes preference for evidence. Repair by highlighting two earlier matching transitions. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| H | One trial establishes a dependable result. | Omits checking repeatability. Repair by running the same condition again before concluding. | Anticipated, from memory. |

The letters describe anticipated ideas, not claims about observed children. File an omitted or incomplete answer by its missing component, without assigning a misconception the child has not expressed.
