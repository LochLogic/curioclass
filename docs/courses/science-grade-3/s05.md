## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do we know places have changed, and what happens to living things when they do?"
Day 82: reveal original drawings of fossil tree trunks preserved where they grew on Svalbard, beside a map and a treeless Arctic landscape. Hide explanatory captions. Ask how both landscapes could belong to one place.
Day 93 closes the anchor: fossil trees and the study's habitat interpretation support a formerly warm forest, unlike the comparison landscape. Students cite fossil records and distinguish ancient change from their short weather log.
Model observation versus inference on day 83 and a merit argument on day 88. Then ask children which evidence warrants each revision.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


Group the question board into past evidence, changed needs and useful responses. Days 82 to 86 analyze fossil kinds, sizes and locations to infer past organisms and environments across long times. This raises how change affects living systems. Days 87 to 91 argue solution merit from changed interactions; day 92 asks whether replanting always helps. Days 93 and 94 connect both scales. Transfer uses new fossil sites and water-access problems.
The assessed verbs are "Analyze and interpret data" and "Make a claim". Limits: no species-name recall, absolute dates, plate-motion mechanism or greenhouse-effect assessment. Extinct means absent everywhere, not locally missing. Deeper means older only in undisturbed layers. Size alone cannot establish climate.
Carried: day 82 starts a past-environment drawing and evidence table, retaining patterns and data models. Day 85 adds extinct forms and relative layers, introducing long times and revising stability and change. Day 87 revises unit 4's system with condition-to-organism arrows. Days 88 and 89 revise unit 2's design cycle and evidence argument using crossing and passage records. Day 91 adds plant-food interactions. Day 85 compares extinct early whale forms with living whales; not all whales are extinct. Day 93 joins these in consensus; day 94 tests evidence limits. Both assessment tasks annotate these models. At day 88, define goals and limits, propose a crossing, inspect test records and improve the proposal; research supplies the needed habitat knowledge.

**Fossil product F, day 86, 12 minutes; revise day 93, eight minutes.** An unfamiliar grassland site has undisturbed layers. The lower layer contains complete freshwater-fish fossils 18 centimetres long and water-plant leaves six centimetres long at two mapped locations. An identification guide states that this fish form is extinct worldwide, though other fish exist. The upper layer contains rooted tree-trunk pieces 80 centimetres long. The guide associates the lower finds with lake water and the upper rooted trunks with trees growing on land. Ask children to annotate their past-environment model and explain what the kinds, sizes and locations support, what changed, and which layer is older. Contrast this long history with a week's weather observations. Fill the checklist after day 86. Day 93 revisions earn the repair mark recorded after day 95.

The six components, scored on the day 95 fresh case and marked as repair on the revised product: using fish and plant evidence to infer a former lake; using both mapped locations without claiming the whole region was lake; interpreting sizes as evidence of organism dimensions, with a trunk piece giving only a minimum length; ordering the lower lake before the upper land trees with the undisturbed condition; distinguishing the extinct form from all fish; and revising the model with evidence while distinguishing long environmental history from a week's weather. All require analyzing evidence with past-life ideas and scale. Meets: "Fish and water plants at both places support lake water there before the rooted trees grew. The fish were 18 centimetres long, but the tree could be longer than its 80-centimetre piece. The lower layer is older because these layers were not disturbed. This fish form is gone everywhere, not all fish. I changed my all-grassland model to lake, then trees, then the shown grassland. These fossils concern long ago; a week's log cannot explain that history." Does not: "It is grassland now, so it always was" (A).

Day 86 fits five weather minutes, 20 regional-fossil minutes, 12 product minutes, five exit minutes and three cleanup minutes. Day 93 fits five weather minutes, eight revision minutes, 29 consensus minutes and three cleanup minutes. Do not turn the regional worked example into scored work.

**Solution product S, day 91, 20 minutes; revise day 94, eight minutes.** A new wet-meadow case isolates water loss after a drainage ditch opens. Supplied records show wet-soil plants failing to make seeds, frogs moving to another pond, trapped water insects dying, and dry-ground beetles arriving and reproducing. Goals are restoring wet plants and frog breeding, within six material tokens and a rule keeping the path dry. In matched model sites, a ditch plug costs five tokens, restores eight of ten wet plants and six frog egg clusters, but floods the path. A side pool costs six tokens, restores seven plants and five egg clusters, and keeps the path dry. The goal card requires at least seven plants and five clusters. Ask for a merit claim comparing the solutions, evidence-linked system arrows for plant and animal changes, and one limit of the evidence. The checklist follows day 91; revise on day 94; the repair mark is recorded after day 96. No solution is built in a real habitat.

The six components, scored on the day 96 fresh case and marked as repair on the revised product: explain water loss affecting plant reproduction; use records to distinguish moving, dying and arrival with reproduction; model the water-to-plant and water-to-frog interactions; support the side pool with both goal counts; compare both costs and the path constraint; and qualify the claim because matched model-site results do not guarantee every organism benefits in the field. Meets: "The ditch removes water the wet plants need to make seeds. Frogs leave, trapped insects die, and beetles arrive and breed. My arrows show water supporting plants and frog breeding. The side pool meets seven plants and five egg clusters for six tokens and leaves the path dry. The plug costs five tokens and the pool costs six. Both fit the budget, but the plug floods the path. The pool has merit for these goals; these model results do not prove it helps every organism." Does not: "The plug is best because eight is biggest, even if the path floods" (E).

Day 86, five minutes: a map marks freshwater-snail fossils two centimetres long at a western site and land-animal tracks nine centimetres long at an eastern site. A guide states that these snails lived in freshwater and these tracks were made on land. "What do the kinds, sizes and locations support about past organisms and environments, and what remains uncertain?" Answer: freshwater snails of the recorded length at the west and land animals making the recorded tracks at the east; track length is not body length. The map alone cannot show whether both environments existed together, the full water extent, an exact date or climate from size. Wrong: present dryness proves permanent dryness (A).

Day 91, seven minutes: a new spreading vine replaces seed plants eaten by field mice. Removing strips of vine restores eight seed plants and six feeding visits, costs four tokens and leaves the vine elsewhere. Whole-patch removal restores ten seed plants and eight feeding visits for six tokens. Goal: at least seven seed plants and five visits, within five tokens, without clearing the whole patch. "Which removal has merit, using the changed plant-food interaction and the goals and limits?" Answer: strip removal meets both counts and limits, restoring a food link without proving full recovery; whole-patch removal exceeds the budget and clears the whole patch. Wrong: all organisms respond alike (D), or whole-patch removal is best because its counts are largest despite broken limits (E).

Day 94, seven minutes: a map shows marine-coral fossils at one ridge and rooted tree remains at another, with no matching layers to order them. A guide says this coral lived in seawater. A separate modern record shows lost pond shade warming the water; a shade trial costs four tokens and retains three water plants and two insect larvae, meeting goals of three plants and two larvae within four tokens. "What does each record support about environmental change, and what can neither establish?" Answer: seawater at one sampled place and land trees at the other long ago, without establishing which came first; restoring shade supports temperature-dependent pond life for the stated goals. Neither gives an exact fossil date or guarantees all organisms benefit. Wrong: today's environment always existed (A), or all organisms respond alike (D).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Today's environment must always have existed here. | Treats present conditions as past evidence. Repair by matching fossils to their supported past conditions. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| B | Absence from one place proves worldwide extinction. | Confuses local loss with global absence. Repair by comparing a local map with the supplied worldwide extinction statement. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| C | Deeper fossils are always older. | Drops the undisturbed-layer condition. Repair by comparing an intact stack with a flipped stack of cards. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| D | Environmental change affects every organism in the same way. | Ignores differing needs and responses. Repair by tracing one supplied need for each organism. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| E | The solution with the largest count is best even when it breaks a limit. | Ignores constraints. Repair by checking the goal and limit boxes before judging merit. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| F | A fossil's size alone proves a tropical climate. | Treats a dimension as sufficient environmental evidence. Repair by pairing size records with independently supported habitat information. | Anticipated, from memory. |

Use repairs in teaching slots or day 97, never on assessment days. These are anticipated ideas, not observed errors.
