## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why do some living things survive in a place while others do not?"
Day 59: reveal original drawings of outward-facing musk oxen around calves and a white hare against snow. Give winter conditions without explanatory captions. Ask which animals children notice, which they nearly miss, and whether a rainforest frog could remain there.
Day 74 closes the anchor: group actions can protect members, coloring can reduce detection, and survival depends on needs meeting conditions. Children write with evidence from readings, camouflage counts and habitat comparisons.
Model an evidence-to-cause link on day 60. Thereafter ask children to justify revisions and challenge unsupported claims.

- **3-LS4-2:** Explain, with evidence, how differences among members of one species can help some survive, find mates and reproduce.

Use saguaro for the cactus, red-eyed tree frog for the tropical frog, and coastal tundra with access to seal-bearing sea ice for the polar bear. Do not equate winter dormancy with poor survival.

Written feedback follows day 68. Revise for eight minutes on day 74 before scoring after day 75. Award four points, one each for a root-survival explanation using eight versus three, a plume-mate explanation using seven versus two, a reproduction link using six versus one, and a model showing cause-and-effect links without guaranteeing an outcome. Meets: "More deeper-rooted sunflowers survived the dry plots, eight rather than three. Reaching water could help them survive. Give unfamiliar illustrated records of small meerkat foraging parties with a lookout and diggers, and a much larger emperor-penguin huddle that tightens when wind increases. The meerkat practice record shows eight of ten foraging trips with lookout calls end with food and safe return, compared with four of ten without calls; a separate observation shows a call followed by retreat to shelter. The penguin card shows neighbors sheltering one another as cold wind rises, with individuals changing positions. Ask for an argument about how these group actions help survival, using evidence and a cause for food, protection and handling change, identifying different jobs and relative group sizes. In the large huddle, neighbors shelter one another as wind rises. These are different group sizes and jobs that can help survival." Does not: "All animals must live in groups" (B). These supplied records support an argument but do not prove a controlled cause or that every larger group does better.

**Scored item H, produced day 71, 12 minutes.** Give a new original garden-pond system: permanent fresh water, oxygen, sunlight, waterlogged margins and no dry resting ground. Three invented kinds have explicit needs: waterweed needs light and wet roots, marsh flower needs wet soil but grows poorly when submerged, and burrowing grassland mammal needs dry burrows. Does not: "They all survive equally because they are living things" (F).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Comparing different species establishes an advantage within one species. | The comparison changes kind. Repair by sorting records by species before comparing individual features. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| B | Every animal must live in a group. | A conditional benefit becomes a universal need. Repair by comparing a supported solitary-survival record. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| C | An advantageous feature guarantees success. | A pattern becomes certainty. Repair by locating an exception in the supplied outcomes. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| D | A conspicuous color is always better camouflage. | Ignores the background. Repair by comparing the same paper colors against reversed backgrounds. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| E | Finding a mate proves young were produced. | Treats an opportunity as an observed outcome. Repair by separating pairing records from young records. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| F | All living things survive equally well in one habitat. | Ignores differing needs. Repair by matching each need to the available resource. | Anticipated, from memory. |
| G | A habitat difficult for one kind cannot support any kind. | Generalizes one organism's limits. Repair by comparing a second kind whose needs are met. | Anticipated, from memory. |

These are anticipated ideas, not observations of children. Teach repairs in the next suitable teaching slot or on day 78, never on assessment days.
