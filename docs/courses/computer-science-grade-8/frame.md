# Course frame: Computer Science, Grade 8

Course `computer-science-grade-8` (catalog id `cs-k12`), grade 8, one year, the last of the three middle school courses. Draft frame, 2026-11-25, after the kindergarten pilot; the scope and sequence sets the days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 5, each a concept area at its grade 8 depth, in the order the ideas build: systems that hold up
  (systems and security, so the year's toy security page, device rules and accessibility tests come first), make it,
  credit it, hand it over (programming, the year's text project), data that answers back (data and analysis, after
  math grade 8 teaches scatter plots), AI under test (algorithms and machine learning, using unit 3's data and unit 2's
  project), and new technology, our town (computing and society, the year's closer).
- **Days.** 64 course days: 58 in units, 4 for course-level checks (2 after unit 2, 2 after unit 5) and 2 respond days
  (one after each check). Two lessons a week of about 45 minutes, 32 weeks: the computer science default for grades 6
  to 12 (docs/courses/computer-science/STANDARD.md section 2, Cory, Desk q-fix-cs-1), an elective, so course days 2k-1
  and 2k fall in week k. The 45 minutes hold set-up, clean-up and collecting work: a lesson opens in five minutes with a
  three-minute retrieval trace, a wrong example from the last exit ticket, or a short concrete moment (APPROACH.md, "How a day
  opens"), and device lessons keep three minutes at the start to get devices out and open the page and four at the end to save
  work and put devices away, so a device lesson teaches for about 33 minutes and a paper lesson for about 40. Each unit's days include its end-of-unit
  assessment and end with a respond day, with one shape for a unit that a course-level check follows directly (the
  kindergarten pilot's pattern): in units 2 and 5 the check stands for the unit's own end-of-unit assessment and the
  check's respond day is the unit's respond day, so no week holds an assessment and a respond day just before a check.
  Each course-level check takes two lessons, since a 45-minute lesson holds one full performance (frame-write.md rule
  19): the first scores the unit just ended in full, the second samples earlier units with short new items
  (frame-write.md rule 20). Units 1 to 5 run 12, 13, 13, 12 and 8 days and teach on 10, 13, 11, 10 and 8 of them, 52
  teaching days. Days follow the load of the rows: unit 2 carries the year's multi-week text project (g8-text-project,
  "a multi-week text project") with the attribution and refine-for-users rows inside it, the heaviest load, and gets
  13; unit 3 owns three data ids whose boundaries ask for a prediction judged on unfamiliar input, a full
  investigation summary and both consequences of a biased stage (a misleading chart and a weaker model), and gets 13;
  unit 1 has four systems rows, two of them design-and-test rows, and gets 12; unit 4 owns four ids with device days
  and gets 12; unit 5's four society rows are single unplugged days in the progression, and with a two-lesson debate
  get 8. The two second check lessons and unit 3's added model-consequence day come from unit 2, the largest unit
  (its hook joins the worked trace on day 13, and its two modify lessons become one on day 14), and from unit 1,
  where g8-choose-a-system, a used-again id whose row is one unplugged day, compares and justifies on one day (day 6).
  64 lessons for 18 rows (13 owned ids) is about three and a half a row, against the kindergarten pilot's two, because
  grade 8 rows carry a device version, a written product or a project. No unit runs over 25 days, so there are no
  mid-unit assessments.
- **Check weeks.** Grade 8 checks are kept off core check days (docs/courses/CALENDAR.md, stagger rule 3, grades 6 to 8
  by school day; the grade 8 core check school days are civics 48, 49, 96, 97, 145 and 146, ELA 89, 90, 125 and 126,
  math 70 and 116, science 75, and week 32's finals days). The mid-year check is course days 26 and 27 (school days 65
  and 68, weeks 13 and 14, after unit 2), with its respond day on course day 28, school day 70 (a respond day, not a
  check day, on math's check day). The two lessons cannot share week 14, since a check on course days 27 and 28 would
  put its second lesson on school day 70. The year-end check is course days 62 and 63 (school days 155 and 158, weeks
  31 and 32, week 32 the finals week, stagger rule 3), with its respond day on course day 64, school day 160, which
  closes the year, so the two lessons straddle weeks 31 and 32. Unit 5 runs in weeks 27 to 31 before it. Overlaps with
  other after-core subjects' check days are the lead's calendar pass across the after-core frames (For the lead 1).
- **Course order.** Computer science in grades 6 to 12 is an elective, so this course never assumes the grade 7
  course was taken (frame-write.md rule 32). By course order, grade 8 follows grade 7: every row assumes a grade 7
  row, the year before, so for a student who took grade 7 each opens as a retrieval task inside the day the Seams
  name (frame-write.md rule 16). For a student who did not, the same items are given as a readiness check of ten
  minutes before the unit opens (unit 1: the last ten minutes of day 1, after the hook, on network paths, the CIA
  triangle, requirements and access needs; unit 2: the last ten minutes of unit 1's respond day, course day 12, on
  variable types, procedures, lists, tracing and test-tables; unit 3: of the mid-year respond day, course day 28, on
  table operations and chart choices; unit 4: of unit 3's respond day, course day 41, on training and test data,
  algorithmic harm and choosing an approach; unit 5: of unit 4's respond day, course day 53, on technology
  suitability, historical effects and automation), and the unit's first understanding day carries a short worked
  example of the grade 7 idea for those students inside its own minutes, never an added day (unit 1: day 1's new map
  and day 3's attack cards; unit 2: day 13's worked game, with a list-diagram and a trace-table read line by line;
  unit 3: day 29's hand plot, with one sort and one chart choice shown; unit 4: day 43's test results, with a training
  set and a test set shown apart; unit 5: day 54's video visit case, with a two-arrow timeline shown). Where a school
  left out grade 7's unit 4 (its Swap or leave out bullet), g8-ml-evaluate, g8-modify-for-bias and g8-ai-tool-day
  were taught to no one, and day 41's readiness check covers them for the whole class. No grade 8 owned id is begun
  in an earlier course, and no grade 8 row assumes a row two or more grades back.

  MS-SYS-SE-33, MS-SYS-IM-36, MS-PRO-PD-14, MS-PRO-TR-20, MS-DAT-DI-25, MS-DAT-DI-27, MS-DAT-IM-29, MS-ALG-ML-08,
  MS-ALG-IM-11, MS-ALG-PS-05, MS-PRO-RD-18, MS-SOC-ET-41, MS-SOC-ET-42 (STANDARD.md "Ownership of MS and HS ids", 13 in
  grade 8). Grade 8 is the last middle school owner, so every MS id is owned by the end of this year. Five more rows use
  seven ids owned in grades 6 and 7 in a new setting (~MS-SYS-NT-35, ~MS-SYS-HW-30, ~MS-ALG-PS-01, ~MS-PRO-RD-17,
  ~MS-SOC-HI-39, ~MS-SOC-HU-43, ~MS-SOC-CE-44); they are named in "What students learn" as used again, never in Owned
  standards. Each owned id is taught at the depth its owner row's notes set, in our own words (STANDARD.md "Depth"; the
  boundary statements are never copied or quoted), and "What students learn" says what is out. Plans cite ids and state
  each action in a short phrase of our own (STANDARD.md "Quoting"); the credit line "Standards: 2026 CSTA PK-12
  Computer Science Standards (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)" goes on any day that leans on a statement. Check set: each unit plan
  checks itself against the computer science bar (docs/ADOPTION-BAR-SUBJECTS.md part 5, items 1 to 9) and the lens
  (docs/agents/lenses/computer-science.md), and reads the partial base unit it adapts for order; the "Check set adds"
  column names what the writer expects that to add. Values rule (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-12) holds. No Specialty id, and
  no X + CS id, is claimed. Days outside the standards (bar criterion C1): lists go past the middle school boundary
  (g8-text-project notes, Cory, 2026-11-21, Desk q-fix-cs-2), and the list work in unit 2 is about 4 of its 52
  teaching days (about 8 percent: day 13's list trace, day 14's list changes and the list parts of the build days 16,
  17 and 20), under the 10 percent limit with about 1 day of room; the scope and sequence counts any other such day
  inside the same limit.
- **Practices and concept areas.** The Units table's Practices and concept areas column (the C3 column of the core
  frames) names, for computer science, the concept area each unit teaches and the CSTA practices it teaches inside the
  content, by code and our label (STANDARD.md section 3), as bar items 1 and 2 ask. Each unit plan names the
  practice's day and task. Across the year: CT6 define the problem (units 1, 3), CT7 abstract and find patterns (units
  2, 3), CT8 build (units 2, 4), CT9 test, debug and refine (units 1, 2, 4), IC3 explain and give feedback (units 2, 3,
  4, 5), IC4 run a project (unit 2), IC5 be a reliable teammate (units 1, 2, 5, pair and team roles), HCD10 include
  different users (units 1, 5), HCD11 prototype and test for access (units 1, 2), HCD12 keep users informed (unit 4,
  the model card), ESR1 weigh who gains and who is harmed (units 3, 4, 5), ESR2 credit others' work and protect users'
  data (units 1, 2). Dispositions are named in the unit plans, not here.
- **Base text.** Partial: England's open computing curriculum, the Teach Computing Curriculum, archived edition
  (raspberrypi.org/curriculum/archived, key stage 3, resources dated 2023), which its publisher no longer maintains;
  England's current lessons are on Oak National Academy (as the kindergarten frame records). England's Year 9 is the
  grade 8 age. License: the scout read the Open Government Licence v3.0 "unless expressly stated otherwise" on the
  publisher's terms page; the archive's unit pages carry no license line of their own, so each file's license line is
  read when the store folder is made. Adapted unit by unit and rephrased for an American audience (STANDARD.md section
  2, Cory 2026-11-16): US spelling, school years named as US grades, no money in pounds and no English law, no product
  or company name, our own editor and pages in place of named apps. Three of its six Year 9 units feed units 1, 2 and
  3: lessons 2, 4 and 5 of the cybersecurity unit, lessons 1 and 5 of the Python sequences unit (grade 7 adapts lessons
  2 and 4 and leaves out lesson 3) and the data science unit, with one Year 8 lesson the grade 7 frame offered; its
  animation, audiovisual representation and physical computing units are left out (see Source map). Units 4 and 5 are
  our own: the key stage 3 curriculum has no AI unit and little of the society strand (docs/reviews/
  computer-science-open-material-2026-11-16.md). The source's name appears only in a day's credit line, once a day.
  Pending(docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md#teach-computing-curriculum): the source store holds no copy of this base, so the
  Source map rests on the public unit and lesson pages (lesson titles, unit overviews and lesson descriptions, opened
  2026-11-25), and each adapted lesson's check for third-party images, text and data waits on the store folder (For
  the lead 2).
- **Day kinds.** As docs/GLOSSARY.md (row "teaching day") defines them, in computer science's words (APPROACH.md);
  GLOSSARY wins where the two differ. Understanding is the first explicit teaching of a construct, idea, method or row,
  often an unplugged or trace lesson (a network map cut and traced, a worked twenty-line program traced with a
  trace-table, a dataset followed through its stages on paper); predict-and-watch only is Understanding, and a day that
  first teaches and then tests or drafts is Understanding (unit 2 day 14, where the debugging routine is first named on
  the modify work; day 22, the anchor game's faults named as a worked example before classmates test; unit 5 day 57,
  where the debate roles and frame are taught). Fluency is a modify lesson that teaches nothing new: change a working
  program, rule or chart in small checked steps (unit 4 day 47). Application is a make, design or society lesson:
  design and build a program against criteria, test a system with users' stated needs, trace a partner's program, run
  a data investigation, write a justified verdict or debate with sources (unit 1 days 2, 5 and 8 to 10, unit 2 days 15
  to 17, 19 to 21, 23 and 24, unit 3 days 32, 33, 35, 36, 38 and 39, unit 4 days 45, 50 and 51, unit 5 days 55, 58,
  59 and 61). Unit 2's hand-in, day 25, is a Check day. Check is the end-of-unit assessment or a
  course-level check; Respond acts on it by error letter. Inside a unit: understanding before modify before make, and
  no make day before a trace or modify day on the same construct.
- **Evidence.** Daily: an exit ticket with a short program or case and "what happens?" or "who does this change?",
  answered in writing with wrong answers by error letter; on modify and make days the program itself against two or
  three look-fors and one line on what was tested and changed (APPROACH.md, "The form of daily evidence"). Scored: every
  end-of-unit assessment is a sitting with unseen items, never the unit's own anchor, of about 35 minutes on paper or
  device. Two units also score a product whose draft, written feedback and revision days are counted in the unit:
  unit 2's text project (draft day 20, written feedback days 21 and 22, revision days 23 and 24, hand-in day 25, on a
  rubric) and unit 3's investigation summary (draft day 38, written feedback and revision day 39). Each owned id has
  its own look-for: a "modify" or "refine" id (MS-ALG-IM-11, MS-PRO-TR-20) by a changed rule or program retested, a
  "justify", "summarize" or "evaluate" id (MS-PRO-PD-14, MS-DAT-DI-27, MS-ALG-ML-08, MS-SOC-ET-41) by the student's
  own written reasoning on a new case, a "collaborate" id (MS-SYS-IM-36) by the
  team's observed product (day 10) beside a written item, a "debate" id (MS-SOC-ET-42) by a written claim with its backing and a rebuttal,
  never by recognition of a supplied answer. Units 2 and 5 are assessed in the course-level check that follows them
  (Days). No score comes from a lesson that first teaches a row. The respond day reteaches by error letter and repeats
  the missed item in a new form.
- **Devices.** One device per pair is the default on a device day, with roles (driver and navigator) that switch at a
  stated time and every student typing in every device lesson. Device days: unit 1 days 4 and 7 (a toy inbox and a
  practice sign-up page built into our pages), unit 2 days 14, 16, 17, 19, 20 and 22 to 25 (our editor, Python
  text; day 13 runs on paper with the teacher's one screen for the live-code, so the trace keeps about 40 minutes), unit 3 days 30, 31, 32 and 33 (a table-and-scatter tool), unit 4 days 43, 44, 47, 50 and 51 (a classifier
  test page, our editor), and the year-end check's code items where devices allow. Every unit keeps at least one
  unplugged day (APPROACH.md, Pacing). Every device day is written four ways (STANDARD.md "Devices and rooms"): one
  device per pair, a few devices at stations with the other pairs on the paper version, one screen with a student at
  the device for each step, or no device. Owned codes that need technology: MS-DAT-DI-25 is met only on a device
  (STANDARD.md list); on the no-device path the student plots the points by hand and draws the trend line, and the
  course record marks it "part met without a device". MS-ALG-PS-05 is met only on the teacher's one screen with a tool
  the school has already approved and no student accounts; a school with none uses Curio Class's saved outputs and
  marks the id "part met" (STANDARD.md "Generated-text tools", Cory, Desk q-prework-cs-1; For the lead 7 asks whether
  that rule changes). Every other owned code is met in full on paper: printed test results and a printed model card
  (MS-ALG-ML-08), printed code samples run by hand on a paper test-table (MS-PRO-RD-18), a paper prototype of a
  program's screens tried by two classmates against the stated needs (MS-PRO-TR-20), a printed sign-up page with its
  barrier (MS-SYS-IM-36).
- **Paper path.** Every lesson has a paper version: programs written and traced on paper and checked by a partner who
  is "the computer", trace-tables, list-diagrams, printed tables plotted by hand, printed samples and cards. No tool
  asks for a student account, stores a student's name or sends anything off the device (STANDARD.md "Privacy and
  safety"); security lessons use toy systems built into the page, with no scanning and no attack on any real system.
- **Reading level and vocabulary.** Code read on a page is at most twenty lines (APPROACH.md, Supports). Each unit names
  five to eight words taught in depth, with words that have an everyday meaning taught as two meanings (bug, cloud,
  model, license, feature). Sentence frames: "I predict ___ because line ___ says ___" and "This decision changes the
  experience for ___ because ___". Error messages in our tools are plain words we write. A student may answer a trace
  in numbers, arrows or the home language, and each key says which wrong answers can come from reading the words.
- **Swap or leave out.** STANDARD.md section 3 lets a school swap or leave out a named unit for its community's
  reasons. Unit 5 may be swapped for other emerging technology cases and another local debate topic, or left out:
  MS-SOC-ET-41 and MS-SOC-ET-42 are then not claimed that year and the family note names them; the course still meets
  computing and society through unit 4's harms and bias rows and unit 2's attribution row. Unit 3 may be swapped for
  other supplied data sets, not left out, so the course always claims MS-DAT-DI-25, MS-DAT-DI-27 and MS-DAT-IM-29 (bar
  part 5 fails a course with nothing on data). Units 1, 2 and 4 carry the year's security, programming and AI ids and
  the project the later units use, and are not swapped or left out.
- **Safety.** No lab or field work, so no laboratory or field safety guide applies. Device lessons keep cords behind
  the table and devices carried closed with two hands; security lessons run only on toy systems inside our pages,
  never on a real school or home system or account. The scope and sequence copies these lines into each device and
  security day.
- **Costs.** Provisional, for a class of 30, beyond the school's own devices: unit 1 $4 (printing network maps, attack
  and prevention cards, the printed sign-up page and user-need cards), unit 2 $5 (printing the worked game program,
  trace-tables, test-tables, the credit-case cards and the project rubric), unit 3 $5 (printed tables, graph paper for
  the hand plots, the stage cards), unit 4 $4 (printed test results, model cards and code samples), unit 5 $4 (case
  cards and two debate source packets), about $22 in all; no consumables and no board.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md);
  no outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Unit 1 has disability content (users who work by keyboard alone, by
  screen reader or with enlarged text) and online safety content (fake messages asking for a password). Before it
  reads, the agent studies a starting pack: the W3C Web Accessibility Initiative's "How People with Disabilities Use the
  Web" (w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/), the Autistic Self Advocacy Network's "Identity-First Language" page
  (autisticadvocacy.org/about-asan/identity-first-language/), one community's own account, not taken as every
  community's, and the National Center on Disability and Journalism's Disability Language Style Guide
  (ncdj.org/style-guide/), and widens it to the brief's five sources, two of them owned by disabled people's own
  organizations, and records them. It checks that no task asks a student to act out a disability (needs are test
  conditions, not roles) and that the online safety days carry health's "If a student tells you something" steps word
  for word (docs/courses/health/APPROACH.md; APPROACH.md, Sensitive ground). Unit 5 touches rural communities, older
  adults, families with low incomes and speakers of languages other than English (the video visit case): the agent
  studies the federal report itself (the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation's 2022 report
  on telehealth use in 2021, aspe.hhs.gov), the National Telecommunications and Information Administration's Internet
  Use Survey (ntia.gov) and the National Digital Inclusion Alliance's own pages (digitalinclusion.org), and adds a
  source from a rural or language community's own organization; it checks that the talk stays on how the service was
  designed, never on a classmate's home, health or devices. Unit 5's career day and unit 4's lottery case can touch
  families' work and means (APPROACH.md, "Work and automation"): the agent checks no task asks about a family member's
  job, money or transport. Unit 3's datasets and unit 4's classifier hold no data about real or identifiable people.
  The course's checks get the answer-key agent's read. No unit is hands-on in the laboratory sense, so no safety agent
  read is planned beyond the Safety lines above, which the sensitivity read confirms are on each day.
