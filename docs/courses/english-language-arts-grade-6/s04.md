**P: Clarke and the stone. Curio Class, original assessment draft, 2026; rights: ours.**

P1. In 1801, British and French officers disputed the Rosetta Stone's transfer after the French defeat in Egypt. Edward Daniel Clarke, a traveller from Cambridge, became involved in the dispute.

P2. Clarke obtained a pass to the antiquities' encampment. A letter describes the quarrel and his wish that the stone might go to Cambridge or London. These actions provide evidence of his involvement.

P3. British soldiers took possession of the stone; it reached England in 1802. Four university casts were ordered, including one for Cambridge. Cambridge received a cast rather than the original, which went to London. His letter remains one participant's account of a disputed transfer.

**C: Carrying an inscription beyond the stone. Curio Class, original assessment draft, 2026; rights: ours.**

C1. The Rosetta Stone's inscription records a decree in hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek scripts. Readers who knew Greek could use it to investigate the Egyptian writing. But a stone cannot travel to every reader. Studying its writing elsewhere required a copy that preserved the signs.

C2. Printers found more than one way to carry those signs onto paper. With ink on the raised surface and moisture in the cuts, paper took an impression with light signs against a dark background. Another method left ink in the cuts, producing dark signs against light paper. In both methods, pressure brought the paper into contact with the ink.

C3. Such copies travelled to readers far from the stone. For a transcription, a reader follows a record line by line and writes its signs on another surface; an ink impression instead transfers its image. Comparing copies can help a reader check a doubtful sign. Access to the inscription could spread even while the stone stayed in one place.

The final two general sentences in C3 explain copying as a method, without attributing an undocumented act to a historical person. Field 10's exhibition supports C1 to C2 and the distribution of prints in C3; its wording is not reproduced.

**Reference card, ours, 2026.** Impression: im-PRESH-un, noun; a printed mark or copy made through contact and pressure; also an idea formed about someone or something. Transcription: tran-SKRIP-shun, noun; a written copy of an existing record or speech. These respellings mark the stressed syllable in capitals. Provide the card both in print and on a local screen as a small digital glossary; references are permitted for A5 to A6 only. The same two formats are used with other words on 91. Independent selection of a sense is judged. Pronunciation and word senses can be checked in the free [transcription entry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/transcription) and [impression entry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/impression), consulted as references, not reproduced; their share-alike text is not adapted.

**Editing card, invented for practice.** Three identical copies read: "The Cambridge cast a study copy showed the stone's signs." A second card reads: "The account gives evidense about the stone's possesion." Use P2 and P3 as spelling references. Students create the dash marks on their paper; this plan names that punctuation without printing it.
