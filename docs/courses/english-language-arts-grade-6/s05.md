**Form A story: The arrow.**

From the upstairs window, I watched rainwater collect in the courtyard. I drew its route towards the drain on a chart. My arrow was straight and precise. I liked that word better than fussy, which was what my brother called my tiny marks. A neat chart ought to make the water's next move easy to predict.

But the pool widened sideways into a hollow I had not noticed. The drain remained dry. I checked my drawing against the courtyard twice. A gust rattled the window, and I looked up from the chart. Water had reached a second hollow while I was checking the first.

The arrow on my chart boasted of a journey the water had never made. I crossed it out, drew the two pools and wrote the time beside each. I left the next part blank. The chart was less tidy, but I could now tell which marks recorded something seen and which had only been a guess.

**Form A poem: The chart.** Each numbered group is one complete stanza; line breaks are shown explicitly.

1. I rule a road the rain must take,<br>a precise line from pool to drain.<br>My arrow finishes the trip<br>before the water moves again.

2. The water slips beside my line,<br>and joins a hollow by the wall.<br>My ruler cannot tell me why<br>the smallest pool should change it all.

3. I turn my arrow to a question,<br>leave room for what I cannot know.<br>My chart will follow where I look;<br>it cannot tell the rain to go.

**Form B story: The spaces.**

At the window I began to record the rain tapping on the metal sill. I put a mark for each tap, leaving equal spaces between the marks. I liked my orderly row; my sister called it regimented. I thought its regular pattern would tell someone exactly what I had heard.

Then a long silence came between two taps. My pencil hovered. There was no place for a silence in my system, so I put the next mark in its usual place. A gust rattled the pane, but I waited for the taps. Another silence lasted longer than the first. On my page all the gaps still looked alike.

My tidy row kept marching through a silence that was really there. I started again, leaving a wider space for a longer wait. The new marks wandered across the page. I could no longer predict where the next one would go, but someone following them would know where I had stopped to listen.

**Form B poem: The record.**

1. An orderly parade of marks<br>crosses the page from left to right.<br>I leave a little equal gap<br>for every tap I hear tonight.

2. A long hush shouldered through the beats;<br>my pencil waited in the air.<br>The equal spaces on my page<br>had left no room to put it there.

3. I spread the marks to hold the waits;<br>the page looks rougher than before.<br>Now every gap can mean a pause,<br>not just a step towards the door.

1. [Leena and Sam sit beside a blank notice sheet. Leena holds a pencil.]

4. Leena: Then what do you want me to write?
5. [Sam looks at the blank sheet. Pause for three silent counts.]
6. Sam: First, a space for the message. Then a space for who needs it.
7. Leena: And how will they know when to listen?
8. [Sam places a finger beside the second space, without touching Leena.]
9. Sam: One name, one message, then a question. We wait for an answer.
10. [Leena draws two boxes and holds the sheet upright.]
11. Leena: There. Shall we try yours first?
12. Sam: Yes. Mine is a question, too.
