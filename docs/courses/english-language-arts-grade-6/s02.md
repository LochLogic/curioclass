**M-A, Which baskets qualify?**

1. The invented town of Linton let workers leave small baskets in a shared storehouse. Its rule said only that a basket must be small. A narrow basket could be taller than a broad one, so two clerks sometimes reached different decisions about the same basket. Earlier versions of the rule remained in the town archive.

2. The committee replaced small with two conditions. A basket had to fit inside a painted rectangle and weigh less than the marked limit. The clerk would verify a doubtful weight by weighing the basket again. Workers could now observe the same tests instead of guessing what small meant.

3. One member called the committee stingy for refusing baskets just beyond the mark. Another described the rule as a sieve: it selected cases that met its conditions. Nobody shook baskets through mesh. The committee recorded refusals with the measured size and weight so it could examine how the conditions worked.

4. Wet baskets presented another problem. Water could add weight to an otherwise qualifying basket. The committee therefore set those baskets aside until dry and weighed them again. The final rule included both measurable conditions and an exception for cases needing another test. A short adjective alone had supplied neither.

**M-B, The travelling rule.**

1. In the invented town of Oren, a rule for sharing a covered workbench stood at the central hall. A register listed the approved wording and the date of each change. Workers visiting the hall could compare the notice with that official record. Workers living farther away could not always make the journey before choosing a day to use the bench.

2. The committee sent a reader with a copy to each outlying meeting place. The reader pointed to the sentence explaining when tools had to be returned. A traveller who could not read could listen and ask about the same wording as a traveller at the hall. The journey opened a door to the rule for people whom the fixed notice did not reach.

3. One member called the reader bossy for stopping to compare the travelling copy with the register. Yet the reader had found an old return time on one copy. The committee agreed to revise that copy, changing the outdated line after checking the approved wording. Carrying words farther was useful only if they remained the right words.

4. Copies now travelled with a date and a check sheet. The reader compared them at the hall before leaving, then offered an explanation at each stop. The central notice stayed too. The plan combined a public record with a way to reach people who could not use it at the hall. It did not promise to settle every question.

**R-A article, Keeping the current rule.**

1. The invented town of Fen kept its approved rules in a register. That official record held each version with its date. When the market changed its closing time, the clerk entered the new wording beside the old and marked when the change took effect. Readers could follow the thread of the decision from proposal to approved rule without guessing which notice came first.

2. The clerk asked helpers to revise the displayed notices. They changed the time and printed the new date beneath it. One helper called the comparison meddlesome, as if the clerk were interfering with a finished job. But a notice at the east entrance still carried the old time. Checking caught an error that neat appearance had concealed.

3. The town retained the old version in the register rather than erasing it. Every public copy was compared with the newly approved wording. A lasting record showed what had changed; a dated check helped the current rule reach visitors. Neither step could replace the other.

**R-A editorial, Keep the paper notice.**

The council should keep paper notices as well as its device display. Both show the approved wording, but reach different visitors. In the invented market survey, four of ten visitors had no device with them; all four found the paper notice. Keeping paper therefore gives those visitors another way to read the rule. A notice is a doorway into the decision, not merely decoration. Once every rule is posted on paper, disputes will disappear forever. The council should retain paper and compare it with the signed wording each week.

**R-A recording, A weekly comparison.** Read or record exactly, neutrally:

"I ask the council to compare market notices with the signed rule each week. A weekly check could catch copies that no longer match the approved words. In our invented inspection yesterday, two of five notices had the old closing time. That supports checking, because neat appearance did not reveal their age. Keep a record of each comparison so the next checker knows when it happened. This plan will guarantee that no notice ever contains an error again. Please approve the weekly comparison and its record."

**R-B article, Keeping a rule available.**

1. The invented town of Vale stored the approved park rule in an archive. That collection preserved the signed wording and earlier versions. When workers repaired the noticeboard, the clerk placed a temporary copy by the gate. It carried the same wording and date as the signed copy. The temporary sheet was a window onto the rule while the permanent board was unavailable.

2. Before posting it, a helper was asked to verify each line against the signed version. A member called the helper timid for asking about a missing word, as though checking showed a lack of courage. Yet that word changed when the gate could open. Comparison let the clerk correct the sheet before visitors used it.

3. When the board returned, the clerk checked that copy too. The temporary notice stayed until the repaired board could be read at the entrance. The town had preserved the wording and maintained a way to find it. A record kept safely indoors could not, by itself, fill the gap at the gate.

**R-B editorial, Keep the spoken announcement.**

The council should keep a staffed announcement as well as its wall notice. Read the approved rule aloud and let listeners ask about its words. In our invented park survey, three of nine visitors could not read the notice; all three heard the reader explain the hours. That supports keeping a spoken route alongside writing. An announcement is a doorway into the rule. Once the reader explains it, everyone will agree with every rule. Retain the session and check its script against the signed wording.

**R-B recording, Shorter workbench turns.**

"I ask the council to trial one-hour turns at the shared workbench instead of two-hour turns. In our invented job log yesterday, six of eight jobs finished in under fifty minutes. Most recorded jobs therefore fitted within an hour. The other two needed longer, so retain a way to request an exception. Keep a job log during the trial so we can decide whether the limit fits later work. Shorter turns will guarantee that every tool lasts twice as long. Please approve the trial, exceptions and log."
