## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How does reading poems help someone become a writer?"
Day 1 compares a hesitant notebook entry with its revision: which words help readers see?
Ask which actions reveal feelings (day 3), what spacing emphasizes (day 4), how tellings differ (day 8), which facts support inference (day 19) and which reading changed writing (day 33).
Day 33 links reading to poem revision with textual evidence; day 38 shares it.
Model evidence (days 2 and 4), paragraphs (14), essay revision (22) and presentation (31); then release decisions.


Read the notebook whole on days 2 to 15.
Track changing judgments and poems answering poems. Model inference, then reread conflicting entries. Fade the story map after day 13. The verse measure is unverified.

**Williams essay exemplar, invented for practice.** These four paragraphs illustrate D27a and D36b's separate components. Other poets remain valid choices; judge their claims against their own documented biography and poem. The subject is evidence for an interpretation, not an invented account of why a poet wrote a particular poem.

William Carlos Williams combined writing with another kind of work. His life record and his wheelbarrow poem help a reader see how ordinary things can become a subject for close attention.

Williams chose both medicine and writing while at school. He returned to Rutherford after his medical training and continued both kinds of work. These facts establish two parts of his working life. They do not tell us which event caused the wheelbarrow poem.

The poem itself gives firmer evidence about its effects. For example, short verse lines divide the names of ordinary objects. The red wheelbarrow and white chickens supply contrasting colors. These precise images make the familiar scene easier to picture.

A reader can therefore learn from the attention given to everyday objects in this poem. The life facts supply context; the poem's words support the explanation of its effect.

Locations: Williams biography in field 10, opening two paragraphs, for the career facts; XXII in Spring and All, lines 3 to 4 and 5 to 6, for the divided compounds, and lines 3 and 7 for the colors. Do not treat a speaker as the poet or a plausible influence as a documented cause.
