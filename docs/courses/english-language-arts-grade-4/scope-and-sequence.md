## The daily reading and skills rhythm

Each 90-minute day has a 60-minute unit lesson and a 30-minute skills block. Long-word reading and aligned spelling receive 10 minutes daily. Monday, Wednesday and Friday add 20 minutes of independent reading with logs and teacher conferences; Tuesday and Thursday add a 5-minute grammar warm-up retrieving already taught grammar and 15 minutes of partner rereading with feedback. Reading conferences sample literary and informational band texts. Course-level oral-reading rotations replace the usual conference/rereading work on their two check days. Day rows name twice-weekly rereading, including animal and canal passages in units 2 and 3. For 30 students, cumulative windows allow up to 15 children per day at two teacher minutes each, using the 30-minute skills block; each child reads a passage, explains meaning and reads unfamiliar long words. A printed spelling/reference item occupies ten of the same thirty skills minutes, completed independently while the teacher listens to other children; it adds no extra block or whole-class dictation. Directions fit the unit lesson's launch. Reuse any matching individual record from the same window within ten days. No child repeats it on the respond days. The teacher holds the watch privately.

The first row of each five-day week in days.csv specifies its word-analysis focus and eight vocabulary targets. The focus runs from retrieved syllable types through three- and four-syllable division, schwa and stress, combined affixes, and Greek/Latin roots, then increasingly unfamiliar transfer. Each day reads and spells words in isolation, sentences and the current passage. Short pattern probes sit in that block on days 14, 29, 44, 59, 89, 104, 119 and 134. Days 74 and 149 are regrouping practice, leaving the winter and spring long-word records to days 78 to 79 and 157 to 158. No extra transfer sample is added to a unit assessment. The next day's 10 minutes first corrects the observed pattern, then continues or extends it. Students read letters and word parts before using context to confirm; pictures and context never replace decoding. Fluency attends to purpose, accuracy, expression and self-correction, not speed alone, with brief teacher listening every week and fall, winter and spring records.

The word-part inventory adds roots to grade 3's affixes. Weeks 7 and 8 teach scrib/script and tract; week 9 graph and photo; week 10 tele; weeks 11 and 12 port and rupt; week 13 ject; week 14 retrieves -tion/-sion for unstressed-vowel spelling; week 15 aud and vis; weeks 17 and 18 retrieve port/rupt while adding struct and form. Each named week's five 10-minute word blocks move through meaning and a model, guided decoding, independent word/sentence reading and spelling, application in verified text words, and transfer. Roots recur in later units, without another introductory week. Earlier affixes are retrieved inside these words, never given reteaching weeks. The grade 5 handoff records exactly these roots and which transfers still need support.

Vocabulary targets are commissioning requirements for the original texts, not a claim that unwritten texts have been examined. The text writer must verify each target occurs meaningfully in the named week's reading, or substitute an equally useful word actually there. Introduce meaning before the relevant passage, inspect its use while reading, then require it in talk and writing after reading. Repeated targets such as evidence, source, precise, revise and conclusion deliberately connect units. No new list is added to a cold check; week 16 and week 32 lists retrieve familiar words. Direct word-strategy teaching remains in unit 2 even though grade 3 strategies support unit 1 reading.

Every reading day includes partner talk and a short written exit ticket needing that day's text. W.4.10 is daily; W.4.9a is taught on the literary texts in unit 1 and W.4.9b on informational research in unit 2. Unit 1 biography responses use RI.4.1 and the grade 3 evidence routine without claiming the later W.4.9b step. Formal discussions are placed about every ten course days, including days 10, 20, 26, 33, 40, 50, 60, 68, 90, 99, 104, 114, 128, 134, 140, 145 and 155, with additional assessed discussions. Capitalization is explicitly taught on day 21 before day 27 samples it. Models precede drafting, and each unit includes planning, peer critique, teacher feedback, revision, editing and publication.

## Text demand and availability

The four anchors give a planned balance of two literary whole books and two informational whole books. The verse novel is read in eight installments, completed on day 15; the animal book's overview and all four defense sections are read on days 39 to 43 and returned to throughout research. The canal history book's overview, land and labor sections are completed by day 90. The chapter book is read in twenty **reading installments**, not assumed chapter numbers, completed on day 141. Its last two installments must be short enough to share that day. No reading of a whole anchor is assigned only as optional homework.

Text ids in notes resolve to the title, author, use and status in data/plans/ela-texts.csv. The named poems are read whole; the text owner identifies the exact poem from each permitted collection before its unit is planned. Expert groups read their own animal article whole and share across groups, rather than every child reading all five articles. All three myths are read whole by every student. Coherent memorial, travel and archive excerpts retain maker, date and enough context to interpret them. The unconfirmed optional animal text g4-u2-05 is not needed on this path: original animal-book and expert-article passages carry its reading moves. This is an item-level text choice, not a rights confirmation or a cut of a frame-owned row.

Demand rises in what a reader must coordinate: poems with layered images and a changing first-person voice; explanation with diagrams and unfamiliar animal vocabulary; historical prose with conflicting arguments; then a longer chapter book with two times and letters embedded in a story.
