**P44, Quills and Shelter.** Paragraph 1: A North American porcupine has a coat of modified hairs called quills. Each quill ends in a sharp point. The quills are loosely attached. They can come away when another animal touches them. Protection depends on contact, not on quills flying through the air.

Paragraph 2: In this observation scene, invented for practice, a wary porcupine turns its back toward an approaching animal. Its quills are exposed, or uncovered. The other animal recoils, drawing back. A photograph records the moment when both stand still.

Paragraph 3: Quills are not the only protection. A porcupine can climb a tree and find refuge, a place of safety. Reading about both its body and its climbing helps explain why a slow animal can escape danger.

Use P44 in print and the quill paragraph of the federal Porcupine page digitally for D44. The federal passage supplies details about quill contact and back-turning; its author, title and address remain visible. Students have not worked through P44 before this sitting.

**P48, A Borrowed Shell.** Four numbered paragraphs describe striped hermit crabs; the sequence scene is invented for practice.

1. First a nearby animal touches the crab. Next the crab pulls back into its empty snail shell. Finally, only its claw tips remain visible.
2. The crab's rear body is soft. Because the discarded shell surrounds that body part, it forms a hard barrier between the soft body and a predator.
3. The shell gives a hard covering, while withdrawal moves exposed parts farther inside. Covering and movement protect differently, but work together.
4. A growing crab becomes too large for its shelter. Finding a larger empty shell solves the problem of a shelter that no longer fits.

Display A is a labeled sequence diagram: contact -> crab withdraws -> claw tips at shell opening. Arrows show order. Display B is a horizontal bar graph, invented for practice: Observed responses in two sets of practice scenes. Each block represents three scenes. Partial withdrawal: four blocks, twelve scenes. Deep withdrawal: seven blocks, twenty-one scenes. Include an axis from zero to twenty-one marked by threes. Counts describe responses, not survival or effectiveness. Read paragraph 3 aloud twice; cover its transcript until the listening response is collected.

**P56, A Shell with a Hinge.** Paragraph 1: An adult woodland box turtle has an upper shell and a lower shell. The lower shell has a hinge. A hinge is a joint that allows a part to move. When the turtle draws in its head and legs, the lower shell can close much of the opening. A hard covering and a movable part work together.

Paragraph 2: The shell's markings provide a different kind of help. Patches of yellow and orange can blend with sunlight and shadow on the forest floor. Protection may therefore involve being hard to notice as well as being hard to reach. A photograph can record the markings, but it cannot alone explain how the hinge moves. In a research scene, invented for practice, observers use a telegraph to send a written message to a distant station.

For D56 pair this printed text with the digital Thinking Inside the Box excerpt named in field 10. Investigate body, setting and protection. This turtle is different from every group's five expert animals. The second source adds shell material and markings; do not open its later cultural or health passages. Reserve both P62 texts for day 62; do not reuse either turtle passage then.

**P62A, Colors in the Soil.** A Texas horned lizard can be difficult to notice on bare ground. Its patterned skin may look like the soil around it. Some populations have yellowish coloring; others are reddish or dark brown. Camouflage means blending with the surroundings. A predator may overlook a lizard whose colors resemble the ground. This protection depends on the background, so the same colors need not hide it everywhere.

**P62B, A Shape That Hides.** Color is only part of the Texas horned lizard's protection. Its broad body is flattened. When the lizard lies against the earth, it makes a small shadow. Spines along its sides also interrupt the outline of its body. For this low-shadow position, the lizard must lie close to the ground. Shape and position can make it harder for a predator to notice the animal.

Supply P62A in print and P62B digitally with their titles, Curio Class authorship, 2026 date and paragraph locations. Neither text is taught, used for retrieval or shown during essay practice. Both are complete single-paragraph accounts, not extracts from the teaching manuscripts. Their factual basis is the lizard source in field 10; no source wording or images are reproduced.

**P74, Slime Through Pores.** Paragraph 1: A hagfish has glands along its sides. Glands are body parts that produce and release substances. When a fish predator tries to grasp it, glands can send slime through pores, small openings in the skin. The slime enters the predator's mouth and gill spaces. The hagfish does not swell its stomach to make this defense.

Paragraph 2: Slime in the gill spaces can interfere with water flow. In filmed encounters, predators pulled away after receiving slime, leaving the hagfish free. This does not prove that every encounter ends in escape. A photograph can preserve a moment; a labeled diagram can trace where the slime travels. In an observation scene, invented for practice, a telephoto lens records the distant fish without moving the camera closer.

Display C: side gland -> skin pore -> slime in predator's mouth and gill spaces -> predator pulls away. Label both animals; arrows trace release and the observed response, not movement into the hagfish's stomach. Read paragraph 2 twice without paraphrasing. Print paragraph 1 and the diagram; uncover paragraph 2 only after listening answers.
