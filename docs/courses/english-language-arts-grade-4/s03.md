**T7. Heracles and the Labors. Greek tradition, original retelling after Bulfinch.**

1. Heracles was famous for strength, but strength did not allow him to choose every task. Eurystheus gave him a series of difficult commands. Each time one task ended, another awaited him. A journey to an unfamiliar place might be followed by a struggle, a puzzle or a search. Heracles could not use exactly the same method for every problem. This telling follows several of those labors. It uses the Greek name Heracles for the hero whom Bulfinch calls Hercules. It is one selection from a much longer body of Greek stories.

2. First came a lion that frightened the people of Nemea. Heracles took his club and arrows, expecting his weapons to help. They did not solve the problem. He had to face the lion at close quarters and overcome it with his hands. When he carried it back, Eurystheus was frightened by the evidence of the hero's strength. He told Heracles to report later successes outside the town. Completing a command had not made the king a willing companion. Heracles would travel outward again to meet the next demand.

3. Another command concerned the stables of King Augeas. Thousands of oxen had left the stalls so dirty that clearing them seemed endless. Heracles faced more than a heavy load: he needed a way to remove it quickly. Two rivers flowed nearby. Instead of carrying away every load himself, he turned their waters through the stalls. The moving water cleared the accumulated dirt in a day. His strength mattered, but noticing a different method mattered too. The rivers accomplished what repeated lifting and carrying could not have finished in that time.

4. The Hydra presented a different difficulty. This creature had many heads, and knocking one away caused others to grow. Repeating the same blow increased the problem rather than ending it. Heracles worked with Iolaus, who helped stop the heads from returning by burning the places from which they grew. One head could not be destroyed; Heracles placed it beneath a great rock. This labor required help and a change of method. A strong arm alone had not been enough, even though strength was the quality for which the hero was best known.

5. Later, Eurystheus demanded golden apples that Heracles did not know how to find. After travelling far, Heracles reached Atlas, who carried the heavens on his shoulders. Atlas knew where the apples grew. Yet he could not simply walk away while holding up the sky. Finding someone who knew the answer had brought Heracles to another difficulty: the person with the knowledge could not leave his place to fetch what the king wanted.

6. Heracles offered to hold the heavens while Atlas went for the apples. He took the enormous weight onto his own shoulders. Atlas returned with the fruit, but taking up his burden again did not please him. Eventually he resumed his place. Heracles could then carry the apples back to Eurystheus. The task had involved a search, an exchange and an immense physical effort. It differed from clearing the stables, even though both labors required the hero to notice what someone or something else could do.

7. Another command sent Heracles into the world below to bring back Cerberus, its fierce guardian. He received permission only if he used no weapons. Heracles held the struggling creature and brought him to Eurystheus. After showing that he had completed the labor, he took Cerberus back. Carrying something away was not the final event: returning the guardian also belonged to this task. The labors continued to test how Heracles responded to different conditions. No single success removed the need to think when the next command arrived.

**T8. Anansi Wins the Stories. Akan tradition, original retelling after Barker and Sinclair.**

1. In this telling, the stories people told belonged to Nyankupon, chief of the gods. Anansi the spider wanted those stories to bear his own name. Wanting them was easy; obtaining them would require a bargain. Anansi went to Nyankupon and asked for the right to call the tales Anansi stories. Nyankupon set conditions that seemed difficult for a small spider to fulfil. Anansi would have to bring him a jar of living bees and a great snake. Neither creature was likely to follow a spider merely because he asked. Still, Anansi agreed. He left with a promise to keep and began considering how to complete the tests without relying on strength.

2. Anansi took an earthen jar to a place where bees gathered. He did not begin by chasing them. Instead, he started a loud argument with himself. Could all those bees fit inside the jar? Perhaps they could. Perhaps they could not. He kept changing sides until the bees noticed the noise. They came closer to ask what he was arguing about. Anansi explained that there had been a dispute over whether the jar could hold them. He presented their size as a question that needed settling. The bees wanted to prove what they could do.

3. To settle the argument, the bees flew into the vessel. Anansi waited until they were inside, then closed it. The bees had answered a question about the jar, but they had not understood the purpose of the question. Anansi sent the filled jar to Nyankupon. He had completed one condition through a trick rather than a contest of strength. Yet one success did not finish the bargain. The great snake remained, and Anansi could not expect a snake to behave exactly like a gathering of bees. He needed another plan suited to the next creature.

4. The next day Anansi carried a long stick to the place where the snake lived. Again he began talking as though an argument were already under way. Was the snake as long as the stick? He said it was, then spoke the opposite claim. The snake heard him and wanted to know why anyone doubted its length. Anansi offered a simple test: lie straight beside the stick so the lengths could be compared. When the snake stretched out, Anansi tied it to the stick. What looked like a measuring task became a capture. He sent the snake to Nyankupon, completing the second condition through another deceptive question.

5. Nyankupon now had the things required by this telling's bargain. The small spider had succeeded where a direct struggle might have failed. Nyankupon granted the request: the old tales could be called Anansi stories. That ending explains a name as well as completing a sequence of tests. Anansi gained what he wanted, although the bees and snake had been deceived. A reader can notice both his resourcefulness and its cost to others. Cleverness in a story need not be a rule to imitate. This shortened adaptation ends when the bargain is fulfilled; other tellings include more tests and different details. It does not claim that one printed version contains every Akan account of Anansi.

**T9. Momotaro and His Companions. Japanese tradition, original retelling after Ozaki.**

1. An old couple worked hard near their home. One day the woman went to wash clothes in the river while the man cut grass in the hills. A great peach floated downstream. She brought it home, intending to share it, but a child appeared from inside the fruit before they could eat it. The couple welcomed him and called him Momotaro, the son of a peach. They cared for him as he grew, and he became strong and courageous. This marvellous beginning belongs to a Japanese tale, not to a record of an actual child.

2. When Momotaro was older, he asked to make a journey. On an island lived ogres who had carried people away and taken their belongings. Momotaro wanted to stop them and bring the captives home. The old couple worried about parting, but they let him go. They made rice cakes for the journey. Taking leave was difficult even for a confident traveller. Momotaro promised to return and set out with food from the home he was leaving. His strength did not remove his affection for the people who had raised him.

3. Along the way he met a dog, a monkey and a pheasant. The meetings did not all begin peacefully. Questions, threats and demands came before agreement. Momotaro shared pieces of his cakes and accepted the animals as companions. Each could move and act differently: the dog ran on the ground, the monkey climbed and the pheasant flew. A journey that had begun with one traveller became a journey of four. Food helped bring the group together, but sharing food did not immediately make every companion friendly toward the others.

4. The dog and monkey quarrelled as they walked. Later, the dog and pheasant also fought. Momotaro stopped them and insisted that they travel in harmony. If they kept attacking one another, how could they meet the difficulty ahead? The animals agreed to stop quarrelling. Their progress now depended on more than gathering strong companions. Those companions needed to work together. Having different abilities could help the group only if disagreement did not prevent them from using those abilities. They continued toward the coast with a shared purpose.

5. At the sea, the travellers faced something unfamiliar. The animals had crossed hills and valleys, but the open water frightened them. They asked to remain with Momotaro rather than be left behind. He found a boat, and they sailed together. At first the movement of the water was strange; gradually they became accustomed to it. They watched for the island, talked and passed the time together. The sea crossing separated the gathering of companions from the danger that awaited them at the ogres' stronghold.

6. At the island, the pheasant flew ahead toward the fortress while Momotaro approached with the dog and monkey. High walls and strong gates blocked the way. Two captives showed Momotaro a small back entrance. Their knowledge helped the companions enter where a direct approach had been difficult. Together they overcame the ogres. Momotaro freed the prisoners and gathered the belongings taken from their homes. The purpose stated before the journey could now be fulfilled: the people would no longer remain prisoners on the island.

7. Momotaro and his companions returned. The rescued people reached their families, and the old couple welcomed Momotaro home. The journey ended where it had begun, but he returned with experiences and companions he had not possessed at the start. This adaptation shortens the fighting in Ozaki's telling and omits graphic details. The ogres are fantastic creatures within this story, never a name for an actual people. The ending asks readers to remember both the helpers who made the journey possible and the people whose release gave it a purpose.
