## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How have resources, hazards and climate shaped where people live, and how should we weigh the ways we get resources?"
**Hook, day 144:** Display original location sketches of the Nile, Ganges-Brahmaputra and Mekong deltas beside dated settlement and land-motion evidence from field 10. Students notice productive, inhabited land exposed to flooding, sketch an explanation and raise the compelling question.
**Closing explanation, day 153:** Students explain why resources attract activity despite hazards, using delta evidence, climate records and community accounts, then qualify a resource recommendation.
**Teaching:** Model source checking on day 146 and comparable quantities on day 149. Then step back while students challenge causal arrows and test recommendations against shared criteria.

<!-- skel -->
- **HS-ESS3-1:** Explain with evidence how available resources, natural hazards and climate change have shaped human activity.
- **HS-ESS3-2:** Weigh rival designs for developing, managing and using energy and mineral resources by comparing costs with benefits. Emphasis: conserving, recycling and reusing resources where possible and reducing harm where not; science says what can happen, not what should.

**Reserved packet and assessment sequence:** Everything in the following Harbor Ridge case is invented for practice. Keep it unseen through day 151. It is not a report about a real community. Three independent, fictional record types have stated methods: settlement counts cover all registered residents; gauges use a fixed land benchmark whose motion is unmeasured; relocation records compare pre-event and post-event addresses but omit unregistered households. Supply the location map, count tables, gauge readings and address records without interpretive captions; students infer mechanisms, evidence limits and continuity of physical processes.

Year 0 records list freshwater irrigation, fertile delta soil and coastal farms. Year 20 records list an upland iron mine, gas supply, road and port; employment ledgers list processing and trade jobs. Coastal population rises from 40,000 to 70,000; upland population rises from 8,000 to 18,000. At year 40, coastal population is 55,000 and upland population 33,000. Between years 20 and 40, twenty-year mean annual rainfall falls from 900 to 650 millimetres, relative sea level rises 0.2 metres without a separate land-motion measurement, usable freshwater falls, and salt-sensitive crop area shrinks. Address records identify 9,000 coastal-to-upland movers after repeated flooding and storm surge. Other address changes have blank reason fields. Add this six-event comparison within the fictional record: eruption, earthquake and tsunami each interrupt access to homes or workplaces; flood inundates farmland; drought reduces water supplies; hurricane damages port access. For each, address changes follow disruption; individual reasons are not recorded. No deaths or graphic accounts are used.

The resource contract requires at least 800 tonnes of usable metal over ten years, within a $130,000 annual budget. Each operating year delivers 100 tonnes. Annual costs are paid in all ten years, including downtime, and include annualized construction, operation, transport and reclamation. Compare total ten-year cost per delivered usable tonne; retain nonmonetary outcomes separately. Design P mines all 100 tonnes for $120,000, uses 800 cubic metres of freshwater, and supplies output in nine of ten years. Design Q reuses 20 tonnes of components, recycles 60 tonnes and mines 20 tonnes for $100,000, uses 300 cubic metres, and supplies output in eight of ten years. Minimum reliability is eight years in ten; freshwater limit is 400 cubic metres each year. Both provide the same metal output and useful processing service in an operating year. P uses 100 fuel-energy units from newly pumped gas; Q uses 70 through equipment insulation and waste-heat reuse. These invented test results establish equal useful processing output, not energy creation. Listed costs include energy supply and conservation equipment. P needs a new road across a shared gathering place and leaves a visible pit; Q retains the gathering place but increases truck noise near homes. P offers more extraction jobs; Q requires retraining and collection work. P disturbs habitat and creates mine waste; Q reduces new extraction but needs collection transport and processing. Both need worker protection and waste controls. A cross-border scrap permit makes Q vulnerable to supply restrictions; P depends on continued mining approval. These are stated scenario conditions, not universal claims about extraction or recycling. For a technology test, P can add water recirculation for $8,000 annually, reducing freshwater use to 350 cubic metres. For a rule test, a scrap restriction cuts Q's usable supply to 70 tonnes unless its design changes. For the changed-constraint test in the day 152 product, restore the original scrap supply but lower freshwater allowance to 250 cubic metres. State which designs remain feasible and what test evidence a revised recommendation needs.

## U7. Talk and writing

Ask day 146, "What evidence could separate these causes?" Ask day 151, "Which limit changed your choice?" Day 153's recommendation needs comparable quantities, evidence, a counterargument and an explicit value judgment.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach subsidence, exposure, constraint and reuse with diagrams. Frame: "This evidence supports ___, but cannot establish ___." Read longer sources together; independent reading stays below 200 words daily. Provide large-print patterned maps and recorded responses. Extend by testing a different priority ranking.
