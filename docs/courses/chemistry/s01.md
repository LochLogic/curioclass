## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why do elements in the same column of the table act alike, and how can the table predict what will happen in a reaction no one has run?"

**Hook, day 1:** Display original observation cards recording lithium bubbling in water, sodium moving and bubbling faster, potassium reacting most vigorously, and neon remaining unchanged under ordinary conditions. Students separate observations from explanations before locating the elements. These are published qualitative observations, not measurements from a controlled rate experiment.
**Closing explanation, day 14:** Students explain the anchor using repeated outer-electron patterns, attraction, distance and shielding, then qualify predictions using reaction evidence. Neon has a filled outer level; members of one column behave similarly, not identically.
The teacher elicits competing drawings first, teaches charge and table conventions when needed, then asks students to predict before revealing evidence. By day 13 students choose evidence and revise explanations without a sentence frame.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):


**Question board:** Group day 1 notices into questions. What differs inside atoms? Days 2 to 5 use charge models and PS1.A to connect atomic and column patterns. Can those patterns predict unseen behavior? Days 6 to 9 use table models and PS2.B to test predictions across atomic and bulk scales, including a historical gap. Can we explain products? Days 10 to 13 use PS1.B, evidence-based explanations and repeating electron patterns. Day 14 reconnects answered questions to the anchor; unresolved exceptions remain visible. The assessment transfers to calcium comparisons and an unfamiliar hydrocarbon combustion case.
**Section shares:** Section 1.1 builds atom and table models for HS-PS1-1. Section 1.2 uses those models to predict properties and bonds, then constructs an initial reaction explanation for HS-PS1-2. Section 1.3 constructs and revises reaction explanations, synthesizes both expectations and owns the final assessment and response.
**Limits:** Use main-group examples, qualitative ionization comparisons and ordinary simple bonding. Shell drawings show energy levels, not electron tracks. Electron attraction and repulsion connect atomic structure to contact forces; this is a qualitative model, not a complete quantum account. No orbital notation, numerical ionization calculations, universal octet rule or transition-metal predictions. Bond energetics and intermolecular forces belong later. No engineering expectation is claimed.
**Carried model path:** The particle model starts on day 1; charge evidence revises it on day 5 and bonding evidence on day 7. The periodic-table model starts on day 3, gains outer-electron and ionization patterns on day 4, and changes after gallium evidence on day 9. Patterns begin as tentative day 1 groupings and become tested atomic-to-bulk relationships on day 8. Claim-evidence-reasoning starts with day 1 observation and inference, gains product evidence on day 10, and revises after a second source on day 13. Day 14 joins all four in the consensus model used on day 15.

- **R, meets:** "My sulfur-32 drawing places 16 positive protons and 16 neutral neutrons in the nucleus and 16 negative electrons outside, arranged 2, 8, 6. Proton number 16 fixes its order in the table. Three occupied levels place it in period 3; six outer electrons place it in group 16 with a shared outer-electron pattern. Two shared pairs can fill its outer level, predicting two ordinary covalent bonds. When bulk surfaces approach, interactions involving their outer electrons resist compression before their nuclei touch." Does not: "The nuclei collide to make the contact force" (K).
- **T1, meets:** "Magnesium and calcium each have two outer electrons. Calcium has an additional occupied inner level; greater distance and shielding make its first electron easier to remove despite more protons, supporting greater reactivity with water. In the specified oxide, calcium transfers two electrons to oxygen, making Ca2+ and O2-. Their equal charges require a 1:1 ratio, CaO, in a repeating array of oppositely charged ions, not separate molecules. Hydrogen needs one shared pair; bromine's seven outer electrons need one more, giving HBr with one shared bond. Silicon's four outer electrons permit four shared pairs with four hydrogens, SiH4. Both hydrides have covalent bonds." Does not: "Calcium has more protons, so its outer electron must be harder to remove; CaO is a separate covalent molecule and bromine makes seven bonds" (D, E, M).

Report A detects carbon dioxide but cannot establish that it is the only product: hydrogen atoms must also remain. I predict water. Report A tested only carbon dioxide, so it can be trustworthy within that scope without contradicting B. I revise to 2C4H10 + 13O2 -> 8CO2 + 10H2O: eight carbon, twenty hydrogen and twenty-six oxygen atoms on each side. The same conservation laws apply in every run; broader evidence changes the explanation, not those laws." ## U7. Talk and writing

## U8. Practice that returns

Retrieve charge on day 3, outer patterns on day 5, conservation on day 7 and evidence limits on day 13. Fluency means increasingly independent explanations with varied examples, not speed. Use day 8's comparison packet as the sub-ready day, with sealed answers and the same paper path.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach nucleus, outer electron, group, period, prediction and evidence beside labeled drawings. Offer "My model predicts ... because ...; the evidence changes ..." Read evidence cards aloud; keep independent reading below 150 words per day. Large print, tactile paper counters, narrated diagrams and dictated explanations preserve the goal. Extend by explaining why outer-electron patterns constrain products without uniquely determining them.

## U10. Two rooms

One projector: students commit paper predictions before the teacher reveals each evidence card. At home, the parent reveals the same cards one at a time and asks for reasons. Day 16 follows the same repair and readiness allocation; no chemistry equipment is needed.
