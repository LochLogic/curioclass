## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Where does the mass go, and how can chemists count atoms they cannot see?"
**Hook, day 42:** Students predict readings as the teacher weighs separated ingredients inside a soft bag, mixes without opening, and compares the inflated bag and the opened bag. Record actual readings, including differences. Ask the compelling question before explaining particles.
**Closing explanation, day 50:** Use repeated bag readings, atom counts and mass proportions to explain retained and escaped matter. A balance reading includes measurement effects; inflation changes air buoyancy. Do not promise exact equality or a detectable drop after opening. At these quantities, buoyancy recovery can leave an opening difference smaller than one display step. Retain unresolved results and compare labelled supplied records; never enlarge quantities or squeeze bags to force a signal.
Model one boundary and one conversion when students need them; then let groups choose repeat conditions and defend discrepancies. Revisit the question board before each new representation.

<!-- skel -->
- **HS-PS1-7:** Use math to back the claim that atoms, and so mass, are conserved when a chemical reaction happens. Emphasis: proportional reasoning about the masses of atoms in reactants and products, with the mole as the bridge from atoms to lab amounts; reasoning counts, not memorized problem routines. Limit: No complex chemical reactions.

Use simple equations, supplied formulas and molar masses. No limiting-reactant algorithms, percentage yield, molarity, complex reactions or energy calculations. Matter conservation does not establish energy conservation experimentally here.
Teacher background: coefficients multiply whole formulas; subscripts define substances. One mole counts specified entities, not grams. Ionic solids have formula units rather than discrete molecules. Different species' equal mole amounts need not have equal masses.

**Shared transfer record, invented for practice:** A laboratory report describes magnesium reacting only with oxygen to form solid magnesium oxide. No classroom heating or metal handling. Formulas are Mg, O2 and MgO. Use rounded molar masses 24.3, 32.0 and 40.3 g per mole, and 6.022 times 10 to the twenty-third entities per mole. A closed apparatus weighs 250.0 g before and after cooling. Its solid changes from 4.86 g magnesium to 8.06 g magnesium oxide. Reserve this entire case from instruction, tools and exits.
