## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do living things heal?"
Day 136 opens with an original recovery timeline and non-graphic diagrams: two broken foot bones, interrupted movement and later walking. Students ask what repaired the gap and draw an explanation.
On 154, students explain recovery from structural comparisons, microscopy, growth records and signaling readings: interacting tissues and organs supply growing cells and support movement and sensing.
Model focusing on 141 and comparing text with images on 146. Ask which observation distinguishes explanations; step back on 147.

<!-- skel -->
- **MS-LS1-1:** Investigate to gather evidence that all living things are built of cells, whether one cell or many cells of many kinds. Emphasis: evidence that life is made of cells, telling living from nonliving things, and that organisms may have one cell or many varied cells.
- **~MS-LS1-3:** (begun here; the full expectation is owned and assessed later) Argue from evidence that the body is made of subsystems that interact, each built from groups of cells. Emphasis: cells make tissues and tissues make organs suited to particular body jobs. Limit: Only six body systems (circulatory, excretory, digestive, respiratory, muscle and nerve), and not how one system works on its own apart from the others.
- **~MS-LS1-8:** (begun here; the full expectation is owned and assessed later) Collect and combine information showing that sense receptors react to stimuli by signaling the brain, leading to quick behavior or stored memories. Limit: Leave out how the signals are carried.
- **~MS-LS1-2:** (begun here; the full expectation is owned and assessed later) Build and use a model of how a whole cell works and how its parts help it work. Emphasis: the cell as one working system, and the main job of five named parts: nucleus, chloroplasts, mitochondria, the membrane and the wall. Limit: Structure and function links only for the cell wall and membrane; other parts only in relation to the whole cell; no biochemistry of cells or their parts.

**Investigation record P.** On 141 and 142, practice observation without scores. On 147, six groups of five use six borrowed microscopes, prepared single-celled pond organisms, plant tissue, a second multicellular tissue and a never-living sample. Before 141, the teacher makes the original Cells across scales image set from these prepared slides, including plant, skin, bone, muscle and nerve tissue. Record collector, date, specimen identity, slide supplier and batch, preparation, field, objective, calibrated scale bar and any processing for every image; keep two fields per specimen and the untouched originals. Publish only with the collector's CC BY 4.0 permission. These classroom observations, also used on 143, 144 and 146, are distinct from invented illustrations; label unresolved structures as uncertain. Students choose positions and plan comparable views; each personally focuses and records the four specimens at two fields with scale and source labels. Use five minutes planning, five equipment checks, thirty observing in five turns per group, five interpreting and five cleaning. Students waiting compare supplied fields and plan the next observation. The teacher records each child's procedure once. P has four one-point look-fors: appropriate comparison including varied multicellular cells; safe focusing; repeated identifiable fields and scale; a claim supported by observations with an uncertainty. Give written feedback on 148. On 151 use only ten minutes to resolve flagged fields and revise the same record, not repeat completed performances. Score P on 152. It is reused without another score on 155 and 159. The no-kit version credits analysis of others' observations, never personal microscope competence.

**M packet, invented for practice.** Supply original labeled diagrams and observation descriptions, not fabricated photographs. A yeast organism is a single bounded cell that makes another cell; a living celery specimen contains many varied cells; a dried dead leaf retains cell walls; a glass bead shows no cells in repeated fields at a resolution that reveals the yeast. A proposed plan uses a hand lens on the bead and a microscope on only one celery field. Ask what observations and scale checks would improve it, which records support cellular life or former life, and why cells rather than smaller parts are the smallest living units. Key: compare all sample types using adequate resolution, record more than one field and distinguish organism from tissue; retain the dead leaf's history. Cell presence alone cannot prove current life. Microscopes reveal organization invisible unaided; these samples support, rather than prove exhaustively, the general cellular claim. Wrong: every visible dot in the specimen is a cell (A); every organism has many cells (B); cell walls mean the dead leaf is alive (C); bead's single empty field proves no cells (D); a smaller cell part is independently alive (H). P supplies the already observed performance. M is unscored. Day 148's final fifteen minutes are response, not further assessment.

**T packet, invented for practice, withheld until 155.** Use original scaled diagrams and descriptions. Record A follows an entire pond organism: one cell moves and feeds, then becomes two independent cells. Record B pairs unaided views of a growing rabbit ear with matching microscopic fields at two times. Supply a 0.1 millimeter scale bar for the microscopic fields; cells about 0.01 millimeter across cannot be distinguished in the unaided view. Cells remain about the same size, with more cells and at least two tissue arrangements. Vessels run through the tissues. Record C is a dead twig with recognizable cellular walls. Record D is a quartz grain, with no cells found across repeated fields at the same resolving scale as B. The flawed investigation uses different scales without labels and checks just one field of B. Supply the comparison resolution, specimen histories and two fields per specimen for evaluation. A separate pond-cell trial has an original diagram showing a membrane but no wall. Tracer particles remain outside intact cells but enter matched cells with damaged membranes. A new intact cell is exposed to the same tracer under the same conditions. Ask students to draw its predicted tracer distribution and use both trial results to explain how it can regulate entry without a wall. The model must distinguish membrane control from the supporting wall found in plant cells; no reading supplies the answer. The rabbit observation records a light touch followed by a head turn; a second reading describes touch receptors signaling along nerves to the brain. These are practice records, not measured classroom results. No student or animal is touched for the task.

**Look-fors and scoring.** T1 gives two procedure points for fixing scale and repeated sampling; four organization points for A as one-celled life, B as many varied cells, C as formerly living and D as never living using history plus observations; four explanation points for using A's independent activity to identify the smallest living unit, connecting the supplied scale to visibility, comparing unaided and microscopic evidence of B, and using C or D to justify an inference limit. A definition without case evidence earns none of these points. T2 gives one point for a model predicting tracer outside the new intact cell using the intact-versus-damaged evidence, and one for explaining why a wall is unnecessary for selective passage, contrasting membrane control with plant-wall support. Neither point is earned by definitions alone. T3 gives one each for cellular tissue organization and a supported interaction with blood supply. T4 gives one for the receptor-to-nerve-to-brain route and one for combining both records to explain behavior. All sixteen T points require a practice with an idea in a new case. With the four reused P points, the new-case share is 16 of 20, or 80 percent. Keep formative records from 153 and 154 unscored: first teaching is never converted into a score.

Give written component feedback after 155; on 156 students revise T during the twenty-minute response, then the teacher scores it once on 157. The original work therefore predates feedback, revision and scoring. No reteaching occurs on either assessment day.
