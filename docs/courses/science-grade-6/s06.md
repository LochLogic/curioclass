## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do living things heal?"
Day 136 opens with an original recovery timeline and non-graphic diagrams, invented for practice: two broken foot bones, interrupted movement and later walking. Students ask what repaired the gap and draw an explanation. Unit 5's Item 1 comments return at the bell with the exit; students read them at home, no class minutes or rescoring.
On 154, students explain recovery from structural comparisons, microscopy, growth records and signaling readings: interacting tissues and organs supply growing cells and support movement and sensing.
The teacher models focusing on 141 and comparing text with images on 146, then steps back on 147; on 138 students choose the observation separating two explanations.

Whose access needs shape environments (157 and 158)? Transfer uses unfamiliar rabbit-ear growth and a pond-cell boundary problem.
Teach and sample only membrane control and plant-wall support for ~MS-LS1-2, interacting cells, tissues and organs for ~MS-LS1-3, and receptor-to-brain signaling for ~MS-LS1-8. Exclude organelle surveys, biochemical pathways, division stages, signal mechanisms and diagnosis. Healing need not restore previous function. Cells in dead material do not establish present life; an empty field does not establish absence of cells.

**Investigation record P.** On 141 and 142, practice observation without scores. On 147, six groups of five use six borrowed microscopes, prepared single-celled pond organisms, plant tissue, a second multicellular tissue and a never-living sample. Before 141, the teacher makes the original Cells across scales image set from these prepared slides, including plant, skin, bone, muscle and nerve tissue; the animal tissue slides are commercially prepared non-human slides from a school supplier, with no human tissue or blood. Record collector, date, specimen identity, slide supplier and batch, preparation, field, objective, calibrated scale bar and any processing for every image; keep two fields per specimen and the untouched originals. Publish only with the collector's CC BY 4.0 permission. These classroom observations, also used on 143, 144 and 146, are distinct from invented illustrations; label unresolved structures as uncertain. Students choose positions and plan comparable views; each personally focuses and records the four specimens at two fields with scale and source labels. Use five minutes planning, five equipment checks, thirty observing in five turns per group (the group pre-sets the four slides in the plan's order, so a turn is eight views; if 142 shows this does not fit, record one field per specimen on 147 and the second in the ten minutes on 151), five interpreting and five cleaning. Students waiting compare supplied fields and plan the next observation. Students waiting also compare supplied fields as above. The teacher spot-checks each child's turn for about one minute against a short procedure checklist; the child's recorded steps (slide edges held, no direct light, scale noted) are the evidence for the safe-focusing point, and the no-kit route earns this point only from the checklist on supplied fields. P has four one-point look-fors: appropriate comparison including varied multicellular cells; safe focusing; repeated identifiable fields and scale; a claim supported by observations with an uncertainty. Give written feedback on 148. On 151 use only ten minutes to resolve flagged fields and revise the same record, not repeat completed performances. Score P on 152. It is reused without another score on 155 and 159. The no-kit version credits analysis of others' observations, never personal microscope competence.

**M packet, invented for practice.** Supply original labeled diagrams and observation descriptions, not fabricated photographs. A yeast organism is a single bounded cell that makes another cell; a living celery specimen contains many varied cells; a dried dead leaf retains cell walls; a glass bead shows no cells in repeated fields at a resolution that reveals the yeast, but its field has scattered dark mineral specks at the same scale as the yeast. A proposed plan uses a hand lens on the bead and a microscope on only one celery field. Ask what observations and scale checks would improve it, which records support cellular life or former life, and why cells rather than smaller parts are the smallest living units. Key: compare all sample types using adequate resolution, record more than one field and distinguish organism from tissue; retain the dead leaf's history. Cell presence alone cannot prove current life. Microscopes reveal organization invisible unaided; these samples support, rather than prove exhaustively, the general cellular claim. Wrong: every visible dot in the specimen is a cell (A); every organism has many cells (B); cell walls mean the dead leaf is alive (C); bead's single empty field proves no cells (D); a smaller cell part is independently alive (H). P supplies the already observed performance. M is unscored. Day 148's final fifteen minutes are response, not further assessment.

**T packet, invented for practice, withheld until 155.** Use original scaled diagrams and descriptions. The pond record follows an entire pond organism: one cell moves and feeds, then becomes two independent cells. The ear record pairs unaided views of a growing rabbit ear with matching microscopic fields at two times. Supply a 0.1 millimeter scale bar for the microscopic fields; cells about 0.01 millimeter across cannot be distinguished in the unaided view. Cells remain about the same size, with more cells and at least two tissue arrangements. Vessels run through the tissues. The twig record is a dead twig with recognizable cellular walls. The quartz record is a quartz grain, with no cells found across repeated fields at the same resolving scale as the ear record. The flawed investigation uses different scales without labels and checks just one field of the ear record. Supply the comparison resolution, specimen histories and two fields per specimen for evaluation. A separate pond-cell trial has an original diagram showing a membrane but no wall. Tracer particles remain outside intact cells but enter matched cells with damaged membranes. A new intact cell is exposed to the same tracer under the same conditions. Ask students to draw its predicted tracer distribution and use both trial results to explain how it can regulate entry without a wall. The model must distinguish membrane control from the supporting wall found in plant cells; no reading supplies the answer. The rabbit observation records a light touch, then the rabbit stops chewing, looks toward the touch and steps away; a second reading describes touch receptors signaling along nerves to the brain. These are practice records, not measured classroom results. No student or animal is touched for the task.

**Look-fors and scoring.** T1 gives two procedure points for fixing scale and repeated sampling; four organization points for the pond record as one-celled life, the ear record as many varied cells, the twig record as formerly living and the quartz record as never living using history plus observations; four explanation points for using the pond organism's independent activity to identify the smallest living unit, connecting the supplied scale to visibility, comparing unaided and microscopic evidence in the ear record, and using the twig or quartz record to justify an inference limit. A definition without case evidence earns none of these points. T2 gives one point for a model predicting tracer outside the new intact cell using the intact-versus-damaged evidence, and one for explaining why a wall is unnecessary for selective passage, contrasting membrane control with plant-wall support. Neither point is earned by definitions alone. T3 gives one each for cellular tissue organization and a supported interaction with blood supply. T4 gives one for the receptor-to-nerve-to-brain route and one for combining both records to explain behavior. All sixteen T points require a practice with an idea in a new case. With the four reused P points, the new-case share is 16 of 20, or 80 percent. Keep formative records from 153 and 154 unscored: first teaching is never converted into a score.

Give written component feedback after 155; on 156 students revise T during the twenty-minute response, then the teacher scores it once on 157. The original work therefore predates feedback, revision and scoring. No reteaching occurs on either assessment day.

**Samples.** Meets P: "I compared both tissue kinds, the whole pond organism and the mineral at stated scales, focused safely and recorded two fields each. Their different structures support a cellular organization claim, but another field could change an uncertain identification." Does not meet P: "My partner saw dots, so everything has cells" (A). Meets M: "The lens cannot resolve the bead comparison fairly. Match the resolving scale and repeat fields. The yeast is one-celled life, celery contains many varied cells, the leaf is dead cellular material, and the glass has no cellular evidence. Smaller cell parts do not independently live. Microscopes reveal cells that eyes miss; limited samples do not prove every case." Does not meet M: "Yeast must have many cells" (B). Another miss: "The dead leaf must be alive" (C). Meets T1 (2 procedure, 4 organization, 4 explanation points): "Label and match resolving scales and inspect multiple fields. The pond organism moves, feeds and reproduces as one whole cell, evidence that a cell is the smallest independently living unit. The ear is visible unaided. Each 0.01 millimeter cell spans one tenth of the supplied 0.1 millimeter bar: microscopy reveals many cells in varied tissue arrangements that unaided eyes cannot distinguish. The twig has a dead twig's history and cellular remains; the quartz has a never-living history and no cellular evidence across repeated fields. Thus the twig was living and the quartz was not. Empty views alone cannot prove an absence of cells." Does not meet T1 (0 procedure, 1 organization for the pond record, 0 explanation points): "The pond organism is one-celled life because it moves and feeds. The ear is one cell because I see one ear. The twig is alive because it has walls. The quartz is living too. Use one unlabeled field" (errors B and C where matched; the quartz claim matches no letter, so it gets component feedback only). Meets T2: "My model places tracer dots outside the new cell: intact cells excluded them, unlike damaged ones. Its membrane can control entry without a wall. Plant walls add support, not selective entry." Does not meet T2: "Without a wall, tracer must enter" (E). Meets T3: "Vessels run through the growing ear's tissues, supplying materials to their cells; growth depends on these interactions." Does not meet T3: "The ear's tissues grow independently of the blood supply" (F). Meets T4: "The touch observation and receptor reading together support signals traveling along nerves to the brain and guiding the turn." Other misses: "Walls choose what enters" (E); "Touch travels straight to muscle" (G).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Any visible dot is a cell. | Appearance alone is insufficient; repair by comparing a mineral grain with a bounded cell at matched scale. | From memory; anticipated. |
| B | Every living organism contains many cells. | Misses single-celled life; repair by tracing one whole organism through feeding and reproduction records. | From memory; anticipated. |
| C | Cellular remains establish present life. | Confuses origin with present activity; repair by comparing a dead leaf's history with a living specimen. | From memory; anticipated. |
| D | One empty microscope field proves cells are absent. | Overclaims limited sampling; repair by inspecting another field at adequate resolution. | From memory; anticipated. |
| E | The plant wall selectively controls entry. | Confuses wall support with membrane control; repair by annotating the paired boundary record. | From memory; anticipated. |
| F | Each body tissue functions independently. | Omits interactions; repair by tracing one needed material from blood supply to tissue cells. | From memory; anticipated. |
| G | Touch input passes directly from skin to muscle without brain processing. | Omits the taught signal route; repair with receptor, nerve and brain arrows. | From memory; anticipated. |
| H | A cell part smaller than a cell is independently alive. | Misses the smallest living unit; repair by contrasting a whole one-celled organism with an isolated part. | From memory; anticipated. |

Letters describe separate misconceptions. An incomplete response that matches none receives component feedback without an unrelated letter.
