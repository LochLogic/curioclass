## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Where do natural hazards happen and how do we prepare for them?"
Day 116 opens with an original coastal map, historical water-level traces and a non-graphic account of the 2011 Japan tsunami. Why did an offshore event affect distant shores despite protection? Students list related hazards, build questions and draw explanations. After the retrieval, five minutes within initial-model time hold unit 4's letter repairs (s04 field 7); no rescoring.
On 132, explain the anchor using location patterns, displacement trials and warning records; identify how detection, communication and protection interact.
Model histogram reading on 119 and evidence integration on 129. Step back for trials on 125 and guided arguments on 131.

**Item 1 packet, invented for practice.** A map places Reed Bay on low coast beside a converging plate boundary, Flint town inland on high ground and Ash town below a volcano. Three equal twenty-year observation windows show Reed Bay tsunami counts of 2, 0 and 3. A supplied histogram of all twelve recorded offshore earthquakes has magnitude intervals 5 to below 6, 6 to below 7 and 7 to below 8, with counts 6, 4 and 2. Event cards distinguish shallow vertical seafloor motion from deep earthquakes with no recorded tsunami. Storm records show six of seven intense storms crossing warm water during a recurring warm season. Forecast tracks have widened when steering winds changed. Ash records show ground swelling and increased small earthquakes before two eruptions, but also during one interval without eruption. There is no sign specifying the next earthquake's date.

Reed Bay's warning diagram has a seabed sensor, buoy, relay, warning center and community receivers. The low-coast path leads uphill; a steep stair segment blocks wheeled access. All following design figures are invented cost units, not school purchases. The community limit is 9 units; wetland disturbance is forbidden. A barrier costs 8, crosses the wetland and is overtopped in the larger supplied trial. A combined sensor, visual and spoken warning, with an accessible inland route, costs 8 and avoids the wetland; power loss disables its relay. A siren alone costs 3 but does not reach deaf residents or people outside its audible range. A paper route map costs 1 and works without power but cannot detect an event. No combination guarantees safety.

**Item 1 look-fors, 18 unscored components.** Locate higher tsunami exposure at Reed Bay; explain plate displacement, using the event cards to separate shallow vertical seafloor motion from deep earthquakes with no recorded tsunami; identify historical occurrence in every window except the middle one; use all three windows without calling their spacing a schedule; read the earthquake histogram correctly; distinguish earthquake magnitude frequency from tsunami probability; connect storm patterns to warm-water energy; acknowledge changing steering winds; use volcanic warning signs; recognize their false-alarm case; reject exact earthquake prediction; distinguish post-event detection from prediction; trace the warning chain; choose a technology using its trial evidence; apply the cost limit; apply the wetland constraint; address accessible routes and multiple warning formats; identify a remaining failure and propose a backup. Accept reasoned alternatives meeting the evidence and constraints. Meets: "Reed Bay has higher tsunami exposure than high inland Flint. Plate motion can displace the seabed and water; the event cards show shallow vertical seafloor motion can make a tsunami, while deep earthquakes had none. Two, zero and three events across equal windows show recurrence without a timetable. The histogram has six, four and two earthquakes from smaller to larger magnitude intervals; it does not say which made tsunamis. Warm water supplies storm energy, but changing steering winds make tracks uncertain. Swelling and small earthquakes raise eruption concern, yet one swelling interval had no eruption. Neither record dates the next earthquake. The sensor detects an event already underway; information passes through buoy, relay, warning center and receivers. The barrier was overtopped and violates the wetland constraint. Choose the eight-unit combined system, which avoids the wetland and provides an accessible route and visual and spoken warnings. Add the one-unit paper route map within the nine-unit limit. It preserves route knowledge during relay failure, but cannot provide a fresh warning; that remains a risk." Does not: "The next tsunami is due in twenty years. A wall stops every wave. Every earthquake makes a tsunami." Errors A, D and E.

**Item 2 packet, invented for practice.** Use an unfamiliar valley flood-warning message, withheld until 133. An original text describes a continuously varying analog signal and a digital message represented by distinguishable pulse patterns. The receiver restores recognizable pulses before copying; severe interference can make pulses indistinguishable. A diagram shows the same message sent, saved and copied three times. Under mild interference the continuous trace gains distortion with each copy, while restored pulses preserve the message in transmission and saved copies. Narration with transcript explains that pulse restoration cannot recover a pattern the receiver cannot distinguish. On 134, add a new comparison showing a failed digital relay during power loss and an undamaged stored message elsewhere. Ask students to revise their recommendation using all three formats and distinguish signal reliability from whole-system reliability.

**Item 2 look-fors, one point each, eight total.** Integrate text evidence; integrate diagram evidence; integrate narration evidence; explain pulse coding; explain regeneration under limited noise; compare transmission; compare storage and copying; identify overwhelming interference or power loss as a limit. Meets: "The text contrasts a continuous analog signal with distinct digital pulses. The diagram shows analog distortion accumulating in transmission and repeated stored copies, while restored digital pulses preserve both. The narration limits that advantage to recognizable pulses; overwhelming noise defeats restoration. The new record shows a power failure can block delivery despite an intact stored message, so the system needs a backup." Does not: "Digital is newer, so it always works" (G). Score reasoning, not a preferred device.
