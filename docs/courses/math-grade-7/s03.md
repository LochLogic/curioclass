## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why is the distance around every circle about 3 times its width?"
**Hook, day 34:** Show differently sized paper squares. Can slightly different measurements of the same square change its true perimeter-to-diagonal ratio? Students measure twice, then connect this question to the compelling question about circles. Field 10 names the source structure.
**Questions:** What makes a circle, day 35? What does pi measure, day 36? Why does area grow differently from circumference, day 39? How does rearranging explain the area formula, day 40?
**Closing claim:** Circumference is pi times diameter; area is half the circumference times radius. The measurement investigation on day 36 and sector rearrangement on day 40 support these claims. Saved formula, design and explanation records are scored on day 43.
**Teaching:** Model ruler alignment on day 34 and compass handling on day 35. Step back for tool choice on day 36. Compare student area estimates on day 39 before naming a conjecture; connect sectors to symbols explicitly on day 40.

<!-- skel -->
- **7.G.B.4:** Know the formulas for the area and circumference of a circle and use them to solve problems; give an informal derivation of the relationship between the circumference and area of a circle. Parts, by verb: (a) know both formulas; (b) use both to solve problems; (c) give an informal derivation relating circumference and area.
- **7.RP.A.2a:** Decide whether two quantities are in a proportional relationship, e.g., by testing for equivalent ratios in a table or graphing on a coordinate plane and observing whether the graph is a straight line through the origin. Parts, by verb: decide proportionality by testing table ratios and by checking for a straight graph through the origin.
- **7.RP.A.2b:** Identify the constant of proportionality (unit rate) in tables, graphs, equations, diagrams, and verbal descriptions of proportional relationships. Parts, by verb: identify the constant in (a) tables; (b) graphs; (c) equations; (d) diagrams; (e) verbal descriptions.
- **7.RP.A.2d:** Explain what a point (x, y) on the graph of a proportional relationship means in terms of the situation, with special attention to the points (0, 0) and (1, r) where r is the unit rate. Parts, by verb: explain (a) an arbitrary point; (b) the origin; (c) the unit-rate point.

**Teacher judgment:** E1 requires remembered formulas, radius-to-diameter reasoning and appropriate units. Meets: "C = 2 pi r and A = pi r^2. Diameter is twice 6.5, or 13 centimeters; C = 13 pi centimeters and A = 42.25 pi square centimeters." Does not: "Both answers are 13 pi" (B). E2 requires matching edge and covering to their formulas, substitutions, units and estimates. Meets: "Cord measures length: C = pi d = 15 pi meters, about 47.1 meters. Covering counts unit squares: A = pi(7.5)^2 = 56.25 pi square meters, about 176.625 square meters." Does not: "Use area for the cord" (D). E3 requires preserved area, half-circumference base, radius height, finer-sector approximation and a general formula. Meets: "Finer sectors approach a rectangle with base C/2 and height r; rearranging preserves area, so A = pi r^2." Does not: "The base is C, so A = Cr" (E). E4 requires both tests, five representations of the constant and all three point interpretations. Meets: "Both table ratios are pi. My graph is a straight line through the origin with pi centimeters of rise per centimeter across; my equation is C = pi d. The point (5.2,5.2 pi) means diameter 5.2 centimeters and circumference 5.2 pi centimeters. The origin is the zero-size model, not a physical circle; (1,pi) means diameter 1 centimeter and circumference pi centimeters, the unit rate." ## U9. Supports and extension

Teach radius, diameter, circumference and square unit with labeled diagrams. Offer "I need ___ because the question measures ___." Read short prompts aloud; rehearse reasons before writing.
