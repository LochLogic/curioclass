## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How do islands, coasts and highlands shape who a people are?"
**Hook, day 132.** Meet four living peoples through their own published introductions beside a relief map. What does a map reveal, and what must people explain themselves?
**Supporting questions, drafted together.** Where are the region's places (133)? Why do settlements grow there (134 to 136)? How do land, water and community choices connect with identity (137 and 139)? What do outsiders' accounts leave out (138)?
**Closing claim, day 140.** Answer the compelling question using the Kalinago introduction, COPINH's Lenca cultural statement and the Miskitu protocol. Explain two connections and answer the counterclaim that environment determines identity.
**Teaching.** Model map tools on 133, environmental mechanisms on 134, data symbols on 135, and source perspective and a qualified claim on 138. Students lead comparison on 139. Teach isthmus, archipelago, hurricane, resource, territory, descendant and perspective. Section 10.1 owns every part.

**Task 2 location key.** Mainland countries from northwest toward southeast are Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama; El Salvador lies on the Pacific side south of Guatemala and Honduras. Cuba lies south of Florida; Jamaica south of Cuba; Haiti and the Dominican Republic share Hispaniola east of Cuba; Dominica is in the eastern Lesser Antilles; Trinidad and Tobago lies near Venezuela. Guatemala City is in southern Guatemala; Santo Domingo on the Dominican Republic's southern coast. Teach the remaining Caribbean states and Panama City and Havana through atlas practice on 133; the assessment does not ask for them. Distinguish territories on the reference map without a memorized sovereignty list.

**Task 3 fresh stimulus.** Label this an invented Central American comparison, not observations about an actual community: "At 14 degrees north, 89 degrees west, a tropical valley lies inland from a Pacific port. A river waters fertile volcanic soil. Roads connect farms, a packing centre and the port. The valley holds 30,000 residents; a nearby steep ridge holds 3,000. Seasonal heavy rain and an active volcano threaten both." Supply a matching relief diagram, rainfall description and employment symbols. Population figures are model data. The answer must explain plausible mechanisms, not claim that the data prove one cause. Teach with a different island-port model on 134.

**Portfolio P.** Model symbols and a small invented grid before students map the reserved Felix data. On 135 allocate warm-up five minutes, modelling ten, independent construction thirty and exit ticket five. On 136 allocate retrieval five, explanation modelling ten, independent completion twenty, paired interpretation ten and exit ticket five. Assess each student's independently drawn layer before peer discussion, retaining drafts and recording feedback.

Use the public hurricane archive's Felix record, AL062007, with five positions at noon universal time: 1 September 2007, 12.2 degrees north and 62.8 degrees west; 2 September, 13.0 north and 69.4 west; 3 September, 14.2 north and 76.9 west; 4 September, 14.3 north and 83.2 west; 5 September, 15.5 north and 87.3 west. State that the system changed strength, including after landfall. On a gridded regional outline, students plot these positions, connect them in dated order and create a distinguishing key. Add settlement symbols from this rounded atlas table: Roseau, 15.3 north and 61.4 west; Kingston, 18.0 north and 76.8 west; Bilwi, also called Puerto Cabezas, 14.0 north and 83.4 west. Pair a title, orientation and scale with a two-sentence pattern explanation. These settlement locations are approximate, not a damage inventory or Indigenous population map. A storm's centre line is not its full impact area. Judge placement within the printed grid's resolution, data order, distinct symbols, and an explanation connecting environmental and human patterns without inferring identity or damage from proximity.

**Fresh source portions.** On 138 teach Columbus's 1493 letter to Luis de Santángel through its opening claims of possession and its account of taking people captive. Reserve its canoe-trade paragraph for 141. Write an original, attributed factual summary: Columbus reported large wooden canoes, fast paddling and trade among islands. Explain that the report publicized his voyage to a royal official and circulated in print. Present no hostile labels or sensational claims. Pair the passage with a newly encountered summary of the Miskitu protocol, section 2.2: the organization connects mutual respect and collective practice with resource use. Teach its history and public livelihood paragraphs earlier. These sources concern different peoples and centuries; use them to compare perspectives on relationships with place, not as two eyewitness accounts of one event. Add the museum account of island exchange as contextual corroboration. Modern community voices establish their own present perspectives, not uninterrupted sameness since 1493.
