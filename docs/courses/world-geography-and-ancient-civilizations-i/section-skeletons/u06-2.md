Explain how Phoenician writing differed from the Mesopotamian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs studied, and trace its adaptation toward Greek and Latin. Give one limit on what a smaller sign inventory changed. Answer: Phoenician used about 22 consonant signs; the other systems combined many word and sound signs, with cuneiform usually impressed as wedges and hieroglyphs shaped as images. All represented language. Traders and settlers carried the script; Greeks adapted signs to mark vowels, and Greek forms passed through Etruscan adaptations toward Latin. A smaller sign inventory could ease learning but did not make everyone literate or end older scripts. Wrong: hieroglyphs were pictures without sounds (F); Phoenician had no spoken vowels (N); Romans copied it directly unchanged (O). | section 6.2 |

Answer: more arrivals and larger loads in N suggest greater contact; written orders could carry information between visits. Local vowel signs suggest adaptation; older script use continues. P shows Phoenician script used in Cyprus around 389 BCE, not the route of that bowl or the identity of every writer. R proposes network links, not a voyage log.
