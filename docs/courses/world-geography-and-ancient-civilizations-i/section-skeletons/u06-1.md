| 83 | MA.6.T3d.1 | Locate Greece, Asia Minor, Crete, Phoenicia, the Aegean and the Red Sea on a map of the ancient Mediterranean world. | **1, three minutes.** On a fresh physical outline with a north arrow, locate Greece, Asia Minor, Crete, Phoenicia, the Aegean Sea and the Red Sea. Answer: Greece west of the Aegean, Asia Minor east, Crete south, Phoenicia on the eastern Mediterranean coast, Aegean between Greece and Asia Minor, Red Sea between northeastern Africa and Arabia. | 83 | MA.6.T3d.2 | Explain how Phoenicia's location helped it dominate Mediterranean sea trade from about 1000 to 300 BCE. | **2, four minutes.** A new map shows eastern coastal harbors, nearby cedar forests, shellfish grounds, Cyprus and Carthage. Explain why this location supported Phoenician maritime trading power about 1000 to 300 BCE, including one human choice and one limit. Answer: harbors and timber supported ships; dye and timber could be exchanged overseas; merchants built networks and settlements such as Carthage. Resources enabled choices, not inevitable or exclusive control of the sea. Storms and rival traders remained. Wrong: location alone guaranteed wealth (B); Phoenicia ruled every port (W). | section 6.1 |
| 83 | D2.Eco.3.6-8 | whole indicator (verb from the C3 file) | **3, five minutes.** Case M (invented for practice): "A merchant buys jars from a potter, hires a free packer to load them and borrows silver from a lender, promising repayment with an additional charge." Identify buyer, seller and exchange in goods, labor and financial markets; explain how these permit the voyage. Contrast a partner who invests and shares profits or losses. Answer: merchant buys jars from potter for a price, loading work from packer for wages and use of funds from lender for repayment plus a charge. Cargo, labor and credit equip the voyage. A partner shares ownership risk; a loan normally still requires repayment. Wrong: merchant is always seller (C); lender sells jars (U); the loan is a gift (J). | section 6.1 |
| 83 | D2.Eco.5.6-8 | whole indicator (verb from the C3 file) | **4, four minutes.** Case S: "A weaver seeks oil. Its seller does not want cloth. Both accept silver. One payment is weighed and checked; another is made with recognized coins." Explain two trading costs money can reduce and one cost that remains. Why keep some accepted money for later? Could barter and credit still be used alongside metal and coins? Answer: money reduces searching for matching wants and helps compare prices and pay; weighing, checking quality, travel or theft can still cost time or resources. Saving reserves later payment, though value can change. Coins, metal, credit and barter could coexist. Wrong: coins eliminate every cost (K); saving guarantees fixed purchasing power (L); coins invented trade (D). | section 6.1 |
| 83 | D2.Eco.7.6-8 | whole indicator (verb from the C3 file) | **5, five minutes.** Case V: "A workshop develops a container that loses less dye. A merchant funds a trial shipment instead of buying more familiar cargo. A storm may destroy it; buyers may reject it." Distinguish innovation and entrepreneurship. Explain a market effect, the opportunity cost and who bears risks. Answer: container improvement is innovation; organizing resources for the uncertain venture is entrepreneurship. Lower losses could reduce costs or expand sales; neither profit nor demand is guaranteed. Familiar cargo is forgone. Investors risk funds and workers may face bodily danger. The case does not establish each worker's legal status; students must not infer it from the occupation. Wrong: innovation guarantees profit (E) or every worker voluntarily shares the venture (I). This is an illustrative case, not a documented invention. | section 6.1 |
