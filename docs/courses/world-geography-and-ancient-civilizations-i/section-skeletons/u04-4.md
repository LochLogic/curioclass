## 1. Standards and practices

<!-- skel -->
- **D2.Eco.13.6-8** (days 51, 53): Explain why living standards rise as productivity improves.
- **MA.6.T3b.4** (days 51, 52, 53): Analyze the main features and achievements of early Mesopotamian civilization.
- **MA.6.T3b.2** (days 52, 53): Use a map of archaeological sites to identify where and when the Sumerians, Babylonians and Assyrians rose as successive states and empires.
- **MA.6.T3b.1** (days 53): Explain how the Tigris and Euphrates Rivers supported farming and early complex societies, and why the surrounding region is called the Fertile Crescent.
- **MA.6.T3b.3** (days 53): Describe how irrigation, mining, metalworking, farming, animal domestication and inventions like the wheel, sail and plow helped Mesopotamian settlements grow.
- **D2.Eco.1.6-8** (days 53): Explain how economic decisions affect the well-being of people, businesses and society.
- **MA.6.T3b.4a** (days 53): A complex society with rulers, priests, soldiers, craftspeople, farmers and enslaved people.
- **MA.6.T3b.4b** (days 53): A polytheistic religion, the belief in many gods.
- **MA.6.T3b.4c** (days 53): Monumental building such as the ziggurat, and developed art including relief sculpture, mosaics and carved cylinder seals.
- **MA.6.T3b.4d** (days 53): Cuneiform writing, used for keeping records, collecting taxes, recording laws and writing literature.
- **MA.6.T3b.4e** (days 53): The first epic, the Epic of Gilgamesh, and the Code of Hammurabi, an early surviving written law collection (the framework calls it the first written law code), including its "eye for an eye" principle.
- **D2.His.6.6-8** (days 53): Analyze how the makers' views affected what information their historical sources contain.
- **D2.His.16.6-8** (days 53): Arrange fitting evidence into a clear argument about the past.
- **D2.Civ.12.6-8** (days 53): Assess real and proposed rules and laws as ways to address public problems.

| 53 | MA.6.T3b.2 | Use archaeological sites to identify place and time of successive powers. | **1b, four minutes.** Read the new site cards and map, then place their dated markers on a scaled timeline: Uruk circa 3300 BCE, Babylon circa 1760 BCE, Nineveh circa 650 BCE. "Associate each with Sumerian cities, Old Babylonian power or Neo-Assyrian power; describe location and sequence. Does the later Babylon marker mean all Babylonians disappeared during Assyrian rule?" Answer: respectively southern lower Euphrates region, central Euphrates and northern Tigris; Sumerian cities first, then Old Babylonian and later Neo-Assyrian dominance. Babylonian power returned. These are sample evidence dates, not founding dates. Wrong: bigger BCE number is later (B); wholly separate populations (J). | section 4.1 |
| 53 | D2.Eco.13.6-8 | Explain productivity and living standards. | **2c, three minutes.** "Use Case P's output evidence to build a chain from productivity to specialization and living standards. Must everyone gain?" Answer: more grain per worker in equal time can release labor for other useful goods and services; greater availability can improve living standards, but unequal distribution can leave laborers little better off. Wrong: more workers alone proves productivity (C); equal gains guaranteed (L). | section 4.1 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4a | Explain six roles and analyze relationships. | **3a, four minutes.** The new dossier names six groups. Explain what each did, then label one relationship showing unequal power; distinguish free obligations from ownership. Answer: rulers directed government; priests organized worship and temple resources; soldiers guarded stores; craftspeople made goods; farmers grew crops; enslaved people performed compelled labor under ownership. Rulers collected farmers' grain; free farmers could owe labor without being owned. Wrong: all farmers were enslaved (E); roles were interchangeable (AG). | section 4.2 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4b | Describe polytheism and its institutional connections. | **3b, two minutes.** A new caption names Inanna and Enki. "What makes these beliefs polytheistic, and how could temples connect belief with power?" Answer: belief in several gods; temple institutions organized worship, resources and labor. This describes historical beliefs. Wrong: cuneiform was a god (T), or one divine name proves monotheism (P). | section 4.2 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4c | Describe monuments and each art form; analyze significance. | **3c, two minutes.** Identify four new diagrams: a stepped temple platform, figures raised from a stone background, small colored pieces forming a wall pattern, and an engraved cylinder rolled across clay. "Name each and explain one link to authority or administration." Answer: ziggurat, relief sculpture, mosaic, cylinder seal; monumental labor displayed institutional resources, or seals authorized transactions. Wrong: every object was a tomb (O) or every mark was writing (S). | section 4.2 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4d | Describe cuneiform's four uses. | **3d, two minutes.** Match four new source descriptions to uses: barley rations listed; a tax receipt stored; a king records a legal rule; a hero addresses a boatman. Name the script and explain what these uses preserved. Answer: cuneiform; administrative records, taxes, laws and literature. It recorded different languages. Wrong: only decoration (U), or one language (F). | section 4.2 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4 | Analyze connections among features and achievements. | **3e, two minutes.** "Connect three features in the new town dossier to explain how authority and production supported each other. Name one limit." Answer: farmers' surplus supported specialist scribes; written tax records helped authorities collect grain; temple building directed labor and displayed power. Records do not show every worker's view. Wrong: unconnected list (N), or monuments prove universal prosperity (AE). | section 4.4 |
| 53 | MA.6.T3b.4e | Identify epic and law collection; explain unequal retaliation. | **4a, three minutes.** Read Epic Card E and Law Card L. Identify each work and distinguish its kind of writing. Explain "eye for an eye" and whether everyone received equal penalties. Answer: Epic of Gilgamesh, heroic narrative; Code of Hammurabi, royal law collection. Retaliation prescribed a corresponding bodily injury in some status relationships; other cases used compensation, including payment to an enslaver. Wrong: an eyewitness building report (V), uniform penalties (R), or Gilgamesh authored the laws (AA). | section 4.3 |
