## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Who gained most from the first cities, and who paid for them?"

**Hook, day 39.** Show irrigation tablet 56.81.51 beside a river and canal map. Who organized the work, and who benefited? Open the compelling question before retrieval.
**Supporting questions, drafted together.** Why cities here (39)? Which powers rose where and when (40)? What did higher output cost and enable (41 and 42)? Whose work and beliefs shaped cities (43 to 46)? Whose justice did laws serve (47 to 50)?
**Closing claim, day 51.** Answer from the irrigation record, art, Hammurabi prologue and laws, and household-history account. Weigh benefits and burdens for two groups, explain reasoning, and answer a counterclaim. Different supported judgments are possible.
**Teaching.** Model dated map reading on 40, a productivity chain on 41 and sourced reasoning on 47. Teach vocabulary before excerpts. On 48 compare the royal promise with unequal penalties, distinguishing law from practice. Students evaluate revisions on 49, argue justice on 50, then write independently on 51. Section 4.1 owns geography and economics; 4.2, society and writing; 4.3, laws and sourcing; 4.4, synthesis and review. RH.6-8.6 supports purpose on 47; RH.6-8.1 supports evidence on 50.

- **Taught and assessed in general form:** Every class studies the named Mesopotamian places and periods; no item assumes where students live.

**D2.Eco.1.6-8:** Explain how choices affect individuals, businesses and society. Parts: explain effects for each.
**D2.Eco.13.6-8:** Explain why increased output per worker can improve living standards. Parts: explain the connection.
**D2.His.6.6-8:** Analyze how creators' perspectives shape information their sources preserve. Parts: analyze selection and omission.
**D2.His.16.6-8:** Organize relevant evidence into a connected historical argument. Parts: select and organize evidence; argue.
**D2.Civ.12.6-8:** Assess actual and proposed laws against public problems. Parts: assess each against the same problem.
**Limits.** Teach overlapping histories, not three peoples replacing one another completely. Cuneiform is a script, not a language. Slavery is coerced ownership of people; debt bondage and other labor obligations varied. No enactments, graphic images or personal faith disclosures. Study beliefs historically. No claim that cities made people superior or benefits reached everyone.
**Carried.** Keep unit 3's map tools, regional frame, timeline, sourcing, claims, continuity and change, and unit 2's trait checklist. Retrieve opportunity cost on 41; tie it to the chain on 42. Withdraw completed chains on 42. Retrieve sourcing on 47, model the ancient prologue, then withdraw prompts on 50. Retain reference maps and timeline on 52; assessment maps have fresh layouts.

"Explain the productivity change and who might gain; identify the opportunity cost." Output per worker rose, but distribution limits household gains; cart repair was forgone (C for worker-count confusion; L for guaranteed equal gains; D for no forgone alternative).
- **Source.** A palace scribe, commissioned to praise a ruler, writes: "Our canal made every household prosperous." A household account instead lists increased grain dues. "Use both records to explain the scribe's perspective and what remains uncertain." Praise selects royal benefits; dues record an obligation, not household satisfaction. Neither proves universal prosperity or enforcement (G for promise as outcome; Q for dismissing partial evidence; AC for unsupported judgment).
