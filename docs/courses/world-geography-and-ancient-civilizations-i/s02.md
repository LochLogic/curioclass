## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Did the turn to farming make life better?"

**Hook, day 11.** Show the flint sickle insert, dated 3100 to 2650 BCE, beside an original grain-store drawing. Could stored food mean a better life? Keep answers provisional.
**Supporting questions, drafted together.** How do interpretations change (days 11 to 14)? What changed with farming, and for whom (days 15 to 18)? What makes societies complex (days 19 to 21)? How do societies meet (day 22)?
**Closing claim, day 23.** Answer from the Çatalhöyük settlement account and Larsen study summary. Explain a benefit, a cost and a continuity; name a competing claim. Different judgments about better are defensible.
**Teaching.** Source accounts before comparison. Model classification on day 12, comparison on day 13 and claim, evidence and reasoning before writing on day 14. Compare Irhoud's 2007 estimate around 160,000 years with 2017's roughly 315,000 years. Explain dating without calculations. Teach tools, fire, hunting, gathering, painting and sculpture on day 15. Day 16 locates independent farming origins in Southwest Asia, China, New Guinea, Africa and the Americas; names Jericho; distinguishes domestication from taming; and places later copper working before bronze and iron. Days 19 and 20 teach every trait. Day 21 tests supported, uncertain and absent evidence. On day 23 identify agreement between the settlement and health accounts before judging better; withdraw worked answers. Section 2.1 owns all parts.


**D2.His.9.6-8:** classify source kinds behind a secondary interpretation. **D1.2.6-8:** explain expert agreement about ideas and interpretations within the compelling question. **D2.His.2.6-8:** classify sequences of developments as change, continuity or both. Each owns the whole indicator.
**Limits.** Era boundaries vary regionally. The Paleolithic predates our species; farming and metalworking were neither simultaneous nor universal. Complex society is a debated description, never a ranking. No genetics calculations, faith disclosures, ancestry tests or graphic human-remains images.
**Carried.** Keep unit 1's timeline and unprompted sourcing. Add dates on days 11, 15 and 16. Tie claim, evidence and reasoning to writing on day 14; remove the frame on day 23. Reintroduce continuity and change on day 18; remove sorted examples on day 24. Retain the trait checklist for later units.

**Assessment pack, reserved until day 24.** Reserve excerpts, glosses and cases together. Print the linked source headers and rights notes from field 10. Glosses are Curio Class explanations, separate from the authors' words. Read technical words aloud. Instruction and exits use different cases.

- **Account A, earlier record within Hershkovitz and colleagues' 2018 abstract:** "around 90,000 to 120,000 years ago". Gloss: the authors describe the previously known modern human fossils outside Africa at Skhul and Qafzeh. Presence does not establish the first departure.
- **Account B, revised record in the same abstract:** "dated to 177,000 to 194,000 years ago"; "consistent with recent genetic studies"; "full-fledged Levallois technology". Gloss: the Misliya jaw and teeth receive an older date. Genetic research independently suggests an earlier dispersal; it does not directly date this jaw. Levallois names a stone-tool technique associated with the find. Sharp and Paces' 2018 critique says the age "relies on flawed interpretations of uranium-series data". Gloss: they dispute how dating evidence is read. The 2018 response defends the older date using other methods. The precise age remains a claim to examine. Supply an outline world map with named continents and country boundaries, but no routes or site labels.
- **Account C, Zeder's 2011 abstract:** "domestication of various crops and livestock occurring, sometimes multiple times in the same species, across the entire region". Gloss: domestication involved several locations across Southwest Asia.
- **Account D, Fuller, Willcox and Allaby's abstract, published online in 2011 and in print in 2012:** "crops were domesticated slowly in different locations around the Near East rather than emanating from a core area". Gloss: evidence favors a prolonged process across multiple places. C and D are research reviews for scholars, comparing evidence about agricultural origins, not eyewitness accounts.
- **Practice Sequence, invented, not an excavation:** "At a valley settlement, families first hunted and gathered and cut plants with stone. Later, families cultivated grain, bred goats and stored more grain through winter; gathering and stone cutting continued, while grinding work increased. Still later, some workers shaped copper as stone tools remained in use." Beside this, print the public-domain photograph of a real object students have not seen, the primary source of the task, with its record: "Hollow-base projectile point 26.253.59, chert, Fayum, Egypt, Neolithic, circa 7000 to 4500 BCE." The object is real; its caption is modern. Do not connect it to the invented valley.
- **Practice Site, invented, not Çatalhöyük:** "Granaries contain more food than annual needs. Many households cluster in neighborhoods. Records describe inherited unequal land rights. Some people make pots year-round while other households supply food. A council issues rules; families gather for seasonal rites; apprentices learn skilled work. Cast tools, woven water-lifting baskets, painted walls and a stone platform forty metres across, built by many work crews, survive. Trained record keepers pass land histories orally between generations." The oral-record detail is supplied testimony in this fictional case, not an inference from a silent excavation.
- **Contact Cards, invented:** "Two towns exchange grain and stone. Visitors teach a weaving method. Families move into another valley. People adopt visitors' faith. An army defeats a neighboring ruler and imposes authority. Settlers establish a community governed by their distant homeland." These are practice cases, not claims about named peoples.
