## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can we know anything about people who lived thousands of years ago?"
**Hook, day 1.** Show the Drehem tablet (museum title: "balanced account of Dugga", a person) and an original Ur and Drehem map. What could these marks reveal? It was found at Drehem, not Ur.
**Supporting questions, drafted together.** What does each field ask and need (days 1 and 2)? Why combine evidence (day 3)? How can drawings show deep time honestly (days 4 to 6)? How did environments change possibilities (days 7 and 8)?
**Closing claim, day 8.** Answer from the Drehem tablet and Beringia reconstruction, explaining an inference and limit. Address the competing claim that one source tells everything.
**Teaching.** Model observation, inference and claim on day 1. Day 3 is a constructed investigation of Ur's kingdom; its layer, river diagram and authority card are labelled "constructed". The tablet's label reads "an account from Drehem, a livestock centre in Ur's kingdom"; it does not verify them. Distribution does not prove trade. Day 7's supported work reads the Sahara maps; day 8 is for the claim. On day 8 remove the worked example claim from display before students write.

## 5. End-of-unit assessment, whole

Day 9 (End-of-unit assessment): End-of-unit assessment: questions sorted by field with what each asks and what evidence it needs, one example of fields combining their evidence on an unseen site plan, events placed on the scale timeline across BCE and CE with era, century, millennium and circa, one climate change explained for what it let people do, and a short written answer to the unit's question from the site plan

**Assessment source pack, unseen until day 9.** Print an original diagram titled "Canal Site: constructed practice case, not an actual excavation." A canal runs along its west edge; a storehouse stands beside it; two houses stand east of the storehouse. Report cards state that excavators dated the rooms to the same period, a record keeper wrote that wool was received from herders, and the storehouse door carried a royal seal. These invented cards test combining evidence; never attribute them to Ur. Reserve the diagram, record, climate cards and dates for assessment; instruction and exits use different cases. Allow about eight minutes each for items 1, 2 and 4, four for item 3 and eight for item 5 (36 of 40 minutes).

**Scoring and samples.** Each numbered part receives 2 when its listed look-fors are present, 1 for accurate but incomplete reasoning, and 0 for the corresponding error. Record parts separately, not only a total. These are planning anchors, ours, untested.

- **Item 1 look-fors:** all five fields, appropriate evidence, distinct approaches, and a past-to-present comparison. Meets: "An archaeologist studies layers and objects; a historian tests written accounts and their makers; a geographer maps location and routes; an economist studies production and distribution; a political scientist studies authority and rules. To study a town today, they could use excavated objects, council records, route maps, accounts and laws. Some sources overlap, but the questions differ." Does not meet: "They all dig for old things; present-day towns do not need these fields" (I).
- **Item 2 look-fors:** contributions from all five fields, an explicit combination, and a closing answer from the record with one inference and one limit. Meets: "The archaeologist dates the rooms; the historian reads the wool record; the geographer maps canal access; the economist connects wool receipts to distribution; the political scientist examines the royal seal. Together the plan and record support movement of goods under official oversight. The record lets us infer that wool was collected here, but it cannot tell us whether every household benefited." Does not meet: "The king's seal proves everyone was rich, so nothing else is needed" (B, G).
- **Item 3 look-fors:** identify authority as the key idea and explain why a question belongs to political science. Meets: "Who authorized the wool collection? asks about authority and control, so it is a political-science question; the official seal could help answer it." Does not meet: "It is history because it happened long ago" (A).
- **Item 4 look-fors:** three proportional placements in order, the read distance, circa and era explained, the counted cross-boundary span and one century label. Meets: "The Paleolithic card is at 1 centimeter, the farming card at 6 and the town at 7. The first two are 5 centimeters apart, so 10,000 years. Circa means approximately; they belong to different eras. From 3 BCE to 2 CE is 4 years because there is no year zero. 1450 BCE is in the fifteenth century BCE." Does not meet: "Space every card equally, put 6000 BCE first, and count a year zero for 5 years" (C, D, E).
- **Item 5 look-fors:** place all supplied changes in order; describe ice, sea level, coastlines and Sahara moisture; connect a changed resource or route to a possible human response. Meets: "The colder stage came first, followed by warming and rising water, then the dust record. Water stored as ice lowered seas and exposed land; melting later submerged coastlines. More rain supported lakes, plants and animals in parts of the Sahara; drying reduced those resources. The dust is measured; less plant cover is what scientists infer from it. Drying supports people moving toward remaining water, but cannot identify everyone's route." Does not meet: "Warmer weather always exposes more land, and climate forced everyone to move by the same route" (F, H).

Read sources aloud, enlarge print and maps, or accept a scribed or oral response; students still supply the explanations and placements unaided.
