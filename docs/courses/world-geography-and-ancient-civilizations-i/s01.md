## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can we know anything about people who lived thousands of years ago?"

**Hook, day 1.** Show the Drehem tablet (museum title: "balanced account of Dugga", a person) and an original Ur and Drehem map. What could these marks reveal? It was found at Drehem, not Ur.
**Supporting questions, drafted together.** What does each field ask and need (days 1 and 2)? Why combine evidence (day 3)? How can drawings show deep time honestly (days 4 to 6)? How did environments change possibilities (days 7 and 8)?
**Closing claim, day 8.** Answer from the Drehem tablet and Beringia reconstruction, explaining an inference and limit. Address the competing claim that one source tells everything.
**Teaching.** Model observation, inference and claim on day 1. Day 3 is a constructed investigation of Ur's kingdom; its layer, river diagram and authority card are labeled "constructed". The tablet's label reads "an account from Drehem, a livestock centre in Ur's kingdom"; it does not verify them. Distribution does not prove trade. Day 7 teaches ice and coastlines. Day 8 teaches Sahara change before the claim and a brief out-of-Africa map handoff. On day 8 remove the worked example claim from display before students write.


**D1.1.6-8:** explain how a question expresses a field's central ideas. MA.6.T1.1: explain each approach, for past and present societies. MA.6.T1.3: give examples of collaboration. MA.6.T2.8: build and read scaled period timelines; use every named term and order BCE dates. MA.6.T2.1: describe environmental changes and explain their consequences for human life. Section 1.1 owns all parts.
**Limits.** Count BCE and CE without negative-number rules or year zero. The boundary is not an event. Period boundaries vary regionally. Unit 2 develops origins and farming; contemporary climate causation belongs to science.
**Carried.** Retrieve spacing on day 4; connect dates and distances on day 6. Label both scales and their magnification. State the reference year for "years ago"; distinguish BCE. Retain the timeline; withdraw examples on day 9. Use source-sourcing unprompted from day 1; revisit misses after attempts.

- **Item 1 look-fors:** all five fields, appropriate evidence, distinct approaches, and a past-to-present comparison. Meets: "An archaeologist studies layers and objects; a historian tests written accounts and their makers; a geographer maps location and routes; an economist studies production and distribution; a political scientist studies authority and rules. To study a town today, they could use excavated objects, council records, route maps, accounts and laws. Some sources overlap, but the questions differ." Does not meet: "They all dig for old things; present-day towns do not need these fields" (I).
- **Item 2 look-fors:** contributions from all five fields, an explicit combination, and a closing answer from the record with one inference and one limit. Meets: "The archaeologist dates the rooms; the historian reads the wool record; the geographer maps canal access; the economist connects wool receipts to distribution; the political scientist examines the royal seal. Together the plan and record support movement of goods under official oversight. The record lets us infer that wool was collected here, but it cannot tell us whether every household benefited." Does not meet: "The seal is enough; no other specialist is needed" (B). Separate example: "The record proves every household benefited" (G).
- **Item 3 look-fors:** identify authority as the key idea and explain why a question belongs to political science. Meets: "Who authorized the wool collection? asks about authority and control, so it is a political-science question; the official seal could help answer it." Does not meet: "It is history because it happened long ago" (A).
- **Item 4 look-fors:** three proportional placements in order, the read distance in years and millennia, circa and era explained, the counted cross-boundary span and one century label. Meets: "The Paleolithic card is at 1 centimeter, the farming card at 6 and the town at 7. The first two are 5 centimeters apart, so 10,000 years or 10 millennia. Circa means approximately; an era is a named period whose length can vary. From 3 BCE to 2 CE is 4 years because there is no year zero. 1450 BCE is in the fifteenth century BCE." Does not meet: "Space every card equally" (C). Separate examples: "6000 BCE is earlier than 16000 BCE" (D); "3 BCE to 2 CE is 5 years because year zero counts" (E); "A millennium is 100 years" (K).
- **Item 5 look-fors:** place R, S and T proportionally at 5.75, 2.75 and about 1.25 centimeters left of present; describe ice, sea level, coastlines and Sahara moisture; connect a changed resource or route to a possible human response. Meets: "R is 5.75 centimeters left of present, S is 2.75 and T about 1.25. The colder stage came first, followed by warming and rising water, then the dust record. Water stored as ice lowered seas and exposed land; melting later submerged coastlines. More rain supported lakes, plants and animals in parts of the Sahara; drying reduced those resources. The dust is measured; less plant cover is what scientists infer from it. Drying supports people moving toward remaining water, but cannot identify everyone's route." Does not meet: "Warmer weather always exposes more land" (F). Separate example: "Climate forced everyone to move by the same route" (H).
