## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How could a dammed river be changed so its fish return while the people who rely on it still get the water and power they need?"
**Hook, day 124:** Compare fish-access schematics and traced shoreline outlines before and after Elwha dam removal, sourced in field 10. Students mark puzzling changes before meeting the compelling question.
**Closing explanation, day 137:** Explain restored passage and sediment transport, delayed biological recovery and the tested design's remaining costs in the report, using monitoring evidence and design records.
**Teaching:** Model criteria on day 125. Day 128 models evaluating recycling evidence; students independently evaluate fish-recovery claims against texts and records, noting limits.

**New river packet:** Habitat fragmentation threatens migratory fish worldwide. Here a dam blocks breeding access and traps sediment; the town depends on treated water and electricity. Baseline monitoring finds 100 returning adults, four native species and six milligrams of dissolved pollutant per litre. A public brief requires at least 200 adults and seven native species, at most three milligrams of pollutant per litre, treated water on at least 95 of 100 days, at least 90 electricity units daily and cost no greater than 90 credits. Flood setbacks and documented access to fishing places are non-negotiable. These are fictional needs and units, not statements about any real nation. Rank biodiversity first among feasible designs, then reliability, then cost; justify other rankings if all constraints remain protected.

| Candidate | Adults | Native species | Pollutant, milligrams per litre | Water days out of 100 | Electricity units daily | Cost, credits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Passage only | 180 | 6 | 6 | 85 | 95 | 30 |
| Phased removal, treatment and replacement power | 230 | 8 | 3 | 96 | 95 | 80 |
| Removal alone | 260 | 9 | 3 | 70 | 40 | 50 |

These are predictions for an ordinary year. Each design maintains setbacks and fishing-place access. Passage retains a visible structure. The phased package changes the skyline and has one construction season of noise. The pollutant-treatment component targets dissolved material; sediment settling alone cannot remove every dissolved pollutant. Removal alone restores an open skyline but loses service reliability. Cost includes maintenance for the comparison period. No predicted count proves long-term recovery.

**Transfer task, 30 minutes:**
1. In seven minutes, connect this local barrier to the worldwide problem, distinguish a criterion from a constraint, and propose a measurable and a descriptive success condition serving named needs. Key: passage, species persistence and reliable services must be considered together. An example measurable condition is seven native species; a descriptive condition is keeping fishing places accessible. Award five points each for the global-local mechanism, measurable condition with units, descriptive condition and named need with a defensible constraint.
2. In nine minutes, propose a feasible design and predict its biodiversity performance. Then uncover the test panel: in a dry-year trial the phased package gives 190 adults, seven species, pollutant four, water 96 days and power 91 units. Sediment falls equally in both, dissolved pollutant stays unchanged and paved area is constant. How well does this support installing screens everywhere? Key: equal fish gains and sediment changes do not isolate a screen effect; unchanged pollutant gives no evidence of pollution reduction. Compare litter removal and longer records before extending the recommendation. A shared trend alone does not establish cause (C).
- Day 129, HS-ESS3-4 and HS-ETS1-3: A boatyard can cut material orders, reuse intact panels or reprocess scrap. A large-scale proposal instead grows seaweed and sinks it to store carbon, with uncertain storage duration and food-web effects. Compare their mechanisms and rank one benefit against one risk. Key: reduction avoids production, reuse extends service and recycling recovers material; seaweed takes up carbon, but sinking it does not prove lasting storage. Defend a ranking using waste reduction, uncertain storage and ecological risk; unsupported certainty gets direct feedback.
- Day 130, HS-ESS3-4: A proposed fishway admits predators that eat native juveniles; native adults eat predator eggs. A water intake reduces pumping when river depth falls. Draw both feedback loops and explain how each changes the design's impact. Key: more predators mean fewer juveniles and adults, less egg predation and more predators, reinforcing native-fish loss. Falling depth reduces withdrawal, helping depth recover, a counteracting loop. These supplied model relations need testing; an open chain is not a loop (D).
- Day 131, HS-LS2-7 and HS-ETS1-3: An invented creek needs six native species, reliable water and costs below 65 credits. A bypass costs 58, predicts seven species and retains water; removal costs 49, predicts nine species but interrupts water. Choose a design and predict performance against ranked criteria. Key: bypass is feasible; predict seven species and retained supply, prioritising constraints before biodiversity preference; predictions still need testing (E).
- Day 132, HS-LS2-7 and HS-ESS3-4: A screen promises to lower a canal's litter from 37 to 11 kilograms weekly; two trials leave 24 and 28, above its 15-kilogram limit. Did it meet the criterion, and what testable revision would you propose? Key: both trials fail; change screen spacing or collection frequency and retest under comparable flow. A prediction is not a successful test; give direct feedback without assigning an unrelated error letter.
- Day 133, HS-LS2-7, HS-ESS3-4 and HS-ETS1-3: A basin must remove 16 kilograms of sediment daily; its test removes 10. Vegetated edges raise removal to 19 but require more land. Refine and compare the solution with the original. Key: add edges and retest; 19 meets the threshold, roots slow flow and trap sediment, but land demand rises. Claiming the shared trend proves cause needs a comparison (C).
- Day 134, HS-ETS1-3 and HS-ETS1-1: Both tested options meet safety, water and access limits. A costs 42 credits, keeps the skyline and supports five species; B costs 53, changes the skyline and supports nine. Compare cost, safety, reliability, appearance and social, cultural and environmental effects, then defend priorities. Key: constraints tie; B favours biodiversity, A cost and appearance; access ties, other effects remain unknown. State those uncertainties; give direct feedback on rankings unsupported by the supplied evidence.
- Day 135, HS-LS2-7, HS-ESS3-4 and HS-ETS1-3: Drought reduces a tested refuge's depth below its minimum. Diverting water restores depth but leaves downstream habitat dry. What refinement and comparison test would address both effects? Key: try shaded pools or reduced withdrawal, measure both reaches under matched drought conditions, and revise only if both limits hold. A high local benefit cannot override downstream constraints (E).
- Day 136, WHST.9-10.7 and WHST.9-10.8: For a new report on habitat access, the index offers the federal fish text and recycling text in field 10. Give an initial and refined multi-term query, then choose a source and explain how it would combine with design tests. Key: refine "river restoration" to "dam removal fish habitat access"; choose and cite the fish text for access mechanisms, reject recycling as less useful, and use tests for local predictions. Unsupported authority judgments get direct feedback.
- Day 137, WHST.9-10.1, WHST.9-10.7 and WHST.9-10.8: A draft says, "Choose barrier removal. A trial supports eight species; the untreated reach has three. It costs more than screens." Write a formal recommendation integrating the fish text's habitat mechanism, these tests and the counterclaim. Key: cite that text consistently, paraphrase the access mechanism, link trial evidence to habitat protection, acknowledge cost and the comparison's causal limits, and conclude with a qualified recommendation (C).
- Day 138, HS-ETS1-3: A bridge proposal claims "cheapest means best" but omits annual maintenance and fishing-place access. Rewrite the justification using both omissions. Key: compare complete costs and establish whether access is a constraint before ranking. A total score cannot override a hard constraint (E).
