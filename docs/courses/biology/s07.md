## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did lake sticklebacks lose their armour, and what do DNA, bodies, embryos and fossils say about where all living things come from?"
**Hook, day 95:** Compare original sea and lake fish outlines and published plate-count patterns, without explanatory captions. Students notice different armour despite similar bodies, then meet the compelling question. Field 10 identifies the research.
**Closing explanation, day 109:** Explain shared ancestry and population change using molecular comparisons, anatomy, developmental sequences, fossils and selection evidence. Distinguish supported mechanisms from an untested claim about a particular lake's pressure.
**Teaching:** Model source comparison on day 96 and causal arrows on day 99. Model explanatory organisation on day 106; then students choose evidence and revise without an answer script.

**Item 1 packet and prompt:** Marsh lizard lineages S, T and U have these aligned differences: across 12 DNA positions, S and T differ at one, S and U at four, and T and U at four; across eight amino-acid positions, the respective differences are zero, two and two. S and T share an unusual ankle-bone arrangement absent in U. Matched developmental records show forelimb buds before hindlimb buds in S and T, the reverse in U. Older fossils show the shared general limb plan; younger fossils show the S and T ankle arrangement before either modern form appears. A copied account claims T descended directly from living S because their proteins match.

Communicate a branching explanation using each evidence type, including a numerical comparison. Explain why repeated agreement supports a scientific theory, what dependable conflicting evidence would require revising, and the assumption about past natural laws. The packet supplies an empty branching diagram and evidence headings; annotations and short prose suffice.

**Item 1 key, 12 points:** Two points for a branching model placing S and T together relative to U without making living S T's ancestor. Six points, one each, explain what DNA, amino acids, anatomy, development, fossil order and independent agreement add. Molecules reveal inherited sequence patterns; anatomy reveals shared structure; development reveals order; fossils constrain historical sequence. Different molecules are related evidence, not wholly independent experiments. Give one point each for the numerical comparison, a theory as a repeatedly tested explanation, investigation and possible revision of the affected branch when credible contradictory evidence appears, and the assumption that natural laws operated in the past as now. Similarity alone is not certainty about an exact fossil ancestor.

Meets: "S and T share a more recent ancestor: one DNA difference rather than four, matching proteins, the ankle arrangement and matching developmental order agree. Fossils place that structure before the modern forms. These are repeated tests at different scales, not a guess. Reliable conflicting evidence could change this branching inference. We assume the same natural laws applied then." Does not: "T came from living S because identical protein proves they are identical species" (A). That answer supplies neither a supported branch nor the other evidence lines.

**Item 2 packet and prompt:** Forty adult S lizards produce 240 hatchlings; limited prey supports only 80 recruits. Parents vary in toe ridges before a wetter period. Common-environment offspring records show that ridge differences are inherited; previous work establishes mutation and sexual reproduction as sources of inherited variation. With equal starting groups of ten parents, matched wet-rock trials produce 30, 50 and 90 surviving young from low-, middle- and high-ridge groups. Grip tests show more ridges improve prey capture on wet rock. Compare the causal selection explanation with "wetness made each parent grow the ridges it needed." Explain all four factors, including how mutation and sexual reproduction supply inherited variation, and predict the direction of change if ridge differences were not inherited. Add causal arrows to the model and identify a controlled comparison that strengthens the mechanism.

**Item 2 key, 16 points:** Eight points, two per linked factor: growth potential exceeds replacement; inherited variation; limited prey creates competition; unequal survival and reproduction increase the contribution of high-ridge parents. Within inherited variation, one point requires mutation creating genetic variants and sexual reproduction recombining them; one requires inherited differences predating the pressure. Four points connect the reproduction and grip records to an evidence-based mechanism rather than individual need. Two distinguish inherited population change from untransmitted within-life differences: without inheritance, a reliable cumulative increase through that trait's transmission is not predicted. Two identify a matched comparison, such as wet and dry rocks with prey, starting trait proportions and predators held constant. Avoid claiming the four factors exhaust all evolutionary mechanisms.

Meets: "Many more hatchlings are produced than the prey can support. Mutation creates genetic variants; sexual reproduction recombines them. Existing inherited ridge differences affect grip, and matched groups with more ridges leave more young. Their descendants therefore tend to contribute a larger share next generation. Wetness did not supply needed mutations. Without inheritance this transmission chain breaks; compare wet and dry rock trials while holding the other conditions constant." Does not: "All parents needed better grip, so they grew new inherited ridges" (C). Need is not evidence of a directed inherited change.

**Item 3 packet and prompt:** Supplied frequencies of a high-ridge-associated gene variant across several generations change from 25 to 65 percent on wet rocks and from 25 to 15 percent on dry rocks in matched trials. A separate matched wet-rock comparison changes from 25 to 30 percent with a ridge-gripping predator present and from 25 to 65 percent without that predator. No migration occurs in these trials; inheritance and reproduction records support the trait link. Explain adaptation under one nonliving and one living contrast, including why the direction differs. Do not calculate gene frequencies or treat a gene association alone as proof of causation.

**Item 3 key, six points:** Two compare the wet-rock increase from 25 to 65 percent with the dry-rock decline from 25 to 15 percent, linking both directions to condition-dependent reproductive differences over generations. Two use predator contrast to explain a reduced advantage under a living pressure, citing 30 versus 65 percent. Two recognise that adaptation depends on conditions and requires the supporting inheritance and reproduction evidence. The variant is associated with the trait, not declared its sole cause.

Meets: "Inherited reproductive differences favour the high-ridge trait on wet rocks, consistent with the variant rising from 25 to 65 percent. On dry rocks, reproductive differences favour the alternative, consistent with the decline from 25 to 15 percent. Supporting inheritance and reproduction records connect these changes to population adaptation. - **Day 98:** New vertebrate lineages V, W and X have DNA differences V-W: 2, V-X: 7, W-X: 7. Eye pigment appears before hindlimb buds in V and W, afterward in X. Wrist bone counts are V: 6, W: 8, X: 6. Older fossils have the general limb plan but no preserved wrists. Which branching inference is best supported, and what does the conflicting record require investigating or revising in this scientific theory? Key: molecular and developmental evidence favour a closer V-W branch; wrist counts suggest V-X instead. Fossils support the broader history without resolving that conflict. Check wrist homology, sampling and further independent records; reliable disagreement may revise the affected branch in a repeatedly tested explanation, not make a theory a guess. Wrong: "V is W's living ancestor" (A); "The conflict means a theory is only a guess" (B).
- **Day 101:** Two seedling variants are inherited, produce excess offspring and compete for limited light; one leaves more descendants in shade. How do the four factors explain a population shift, and what would fail if the difference were not inherited? Key: all four causal links with transmission essential. Wrong: "Shade makes seedlings develop the inherited difference they need" (C); "A leaf injury will automatically pass to descendants" (D).
- **Day 105:** An unseen insect's inherited trait improves reproduction in shade; its share starts at 30 percent and ends at 50, 60 and 70 percent in repeated shaded runs; supplied associated gene frequencies rise from 20 to 55 percent in shade but fall to 10 percent in light. What statistical evidence supports a tendency, and how does the contrast explain conditional adaptation? Key: all repeats increase, mean final share 60 percent, while the gene evidence and reproductive link support environment-dependent adaptation without guarantees. Wrong: "Every individual with the trait will reproduce" (H); "The trait is best in light too" (F).
- **Day 110:** New snails have matching DNA and body-structure evidence for shared ancestry; inherited shell variants face scarce food, with different reproductive success on rough and smooth surfaces. In a labelled explanation, connect ancestry to the four-factor mechanism and state what measured pattern would test adaptation. Key: branching evidence plus linked factors, then repeated trait distributions and supplied gene frequencies across surfaces. Wrong: "Matching DNA makes one living snail lineage the ancestor of the other" (A); "Survival alone proves reproductive success" (E); "The same shell is best on both surfaces" (F).

## 6. Error register

| | The error or first idea | What it shows | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | One living lineage is the direct ancestor of another because their sequences match. | Confuses branching with a ladder. Repair: put both living lineages at tips joined through an earlier ancestor. | From memory. |
| B | A scientific theory is merely a guess. | Misses repeated evidential testing. Repair: contrast a new conjecture with a model supported by independent tests. | Academy reference in field 10. |
| C | Need directs a useful inherited change. | Assigns intention to variation. Repair: sequence cards showing variation before environmental selection. | From memory. |
| D | An acquired body difference is automatically inherited. | Omits transmission evidence. Repair: contrast inherited variants with an untransmitted injury. | From memory. |
| E | Survival alone establishes evolutionary success. | Omits reproductive contribution. Repair: compare survivors leaving zero and several offspring. | Academy natural-selection definition. |
| F | An advantageous trait is advantageous in every environment. | Omits the environmental condition. Repair: compare matched contrasting conditions. | From memory. |
| G | More individuals with a trait always means a greater trait proportion. | Omits the denominator. Repair: compare 5 of 10 with 8 of 40. | From memory. |
| H | A helpful trait guarantees an individual's reproduction. | Confuses probability with certainty. Repair: compare repeated population outcomes with individual exceptions. | From memory. |
