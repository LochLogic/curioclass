**Assessment packet:** All plant cases and numerical records below are **invented for practice**. Keep them out of teaching, tools and rehearsals. Students use the consensus model to reason about this new organism. Reading and checking on days 92 and 93 contain no reteaching.

**Item 1, unseen model:** A greenhouse plant's nucleated leaf and petal cells contain matching chromosome pairs. An enlarged chromosome is one long DNA molecule with a protein-coding gene P, a nearby noncoding regulatory region R and a segment U whose function is unknown. Petals contain much pigment-making protein P; leaves contain little, although both have the gene. A seed receives one member of each chromosome pair from each parent. An observational record links a variant near R with darker petals, but light conditions also differ.

Ask five model-derived questions, each with the observation or comparison that could answer it: one about chromosome, DNA and gene transmission; one about protein instructions and the trait; one about noncoding regions; one about different use of shared DNA; and one that distinguishes a cause from a correlation. Refine vague questions into answerable comparisons. For each, point to the model relationship that prompted it. No molecular procedure or meiosis phase is requested.

**Item 1 answer and look-fors:** Five components, two points each. Award one for a scientifically coherent question about its named relationship and one for suitable evidence. Accept different questions, not just these examples. Compare parent and offspring chromosome markers at P to ask which instructions each parent passed. Compare P gene variants, protein activity and pigment to ask how instructions affect a trait. Compare gene use with R active or inactive while holding P and growing conditions constant to test a regulatory role, leaving U unknown. Compare gene activity in leaves and petals with matching DNA to ask why protein amounts differ. Compare replicated plants differing at R under matched light, or randomise light within each genotype, to separate the observational alternatives. Questions that merely request a definition do not clarify a model relationship.

Meets: "Which parental chromosome carries each seed's P version? Parent and offspring markers could show transmission. Does the P version change protein activity and pigment under matched conditions? Measure both. Does R change P activity when P itself is unchanged? Compare matched plants. Do leaf and petal cells use P differently despite matching DNA? Measure their activity. Does R explain darker petals independently of light? Compare replicated genotypes within the same randomised light treatments." Does not: "What is DNA? Leaves must lack P because they lack its protein (C). The dark-petal association already proves R causes it (H)."

**Item 2, variation records:** In the greenhouse plant, chromosome records show parental segments carrying A with B and a with b; some gametes contain A with b, with no new allele sequence. A separately tracked copying event produces a new viable sequence variant without a recorded exposure. That variant occurs in a gamete-producing lineage and in offspring. In two other controlled studies, each with matched untreated controls, ultraviolet exposure and a mutagenic chemical treatment separately produce more sequence changes in gamete-producing cells; surviving offspring inherit some changes. These are research records, not classroom procedures. Finally, genetically matched plants become taller in different growing conditions with no sequence change detected; a separate leaf-only variant is absent from the gamete lineage and offspring.

Defend a claim that inherited variation has three sources against this supplied alternative: "Every difference between offspring requires a new DNA mutation, so any environmental effect on a plant must be inherited." Cite the records, explain each mechanism at chromosome scale and distinguish the last two observations from inherited DNA changes. State one evidence limit.

**Item 2 answer and look-fors:** Six components, two points each: new combinations in meiosis including exchanged chromosome segments, viable copying error, environmental mutation supported by both exposure comparisons, reproductive-lineage transmission, expression without detected mutation, and an explicit rebuttal with a limit. One point gives the correct inference, the second connects a specific record to the mechanism or limitation. No allele has to change sequence for recombination to change a combination. A viable copying change can be transmitted through reproduction. External factors can cause mutations, but survival and transmission are not guaranteed. A leaf-only change absent from the gamete lineage is not transmitted in this record. No detected sequence change supports an expression explanation, not proof that every possible DNA change has been ruled out.

Meets: "A with b is a new combination made during meiosis, not a newly created allele. The tracked copying variant is a different source and reached offspring. Each controlled exposure study supports environmental mutation because changed sequences increased relative to its control; some survived and were transmitted through gametes. Taller matched plants need not have mutated, and the leaf-only variant was not in the lineage making gametes. The alternative confuses combinations and expression with DNA change. These records cannot establish that every exposure produces an inherited change." Does not: "Recombination must invent alleles (D). Every change in the leaf reaches offspring (F). Taller plants prove a mutation occurred (G)."

**Item 3, distribution records:** A separate greenhouse cross is Pp with Pp. Each parent independently contributes P or p with equal probability. In this invented plant, PP and Pp produce purple petals and pp produces white under the stated common conditions. Among 72 offspring, 49 are purple and 23 white. A separate random sample of 36 plants grown together has heights of 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 centimetres with frequencies 4, 8, 12, 8 and 4. The sample is not the same plants as the cross. In a controlled experiment with a single genotype, replicated plants are randomly assigned soil solutions containing 1, 3 and 5 grams of salt per litre, giving mean heights of 16, 12 and 8 centimetres at the same age. Other conditions are held constant. These supplied records are not a classroom salt-treatment procedure. Within this short interval a straight line reasonably represents the means.

Use a four-outcome sample space to predict petal colours, compare expected and observed counts, graph and summarise the height distribution, and use an algebraic relationship to predict mean height at 4 grams of salt per litre. Explain what these records show about genes, environment, sample variation and causal evidence.

**Item 3 answer and look-fors:** Six components, two points each: probability from the sample space; expected versus observed counts with sampling reasoning; a labeled frequency graph; centre and spread interpreted as trait variation; algebraic interpolation with units and bounded range; and a genes-and-environment explanation tied to experimental design. One point is for the accurate representation or inference and one for interpretation using the core idea. PP, Pp, pP and pp give purple probability three quarters and white one quarter. Expect 54 purple and 18 white, not a guarantee for 72. The symmetric frequency graph centres at 8 centimetres; mean is 8 and range is 4 centimetres. A suitable equation is height in centimetres = 18 minus twice the salt concentration in grams per litre, giving 10 centimetres at 4 grams per litre. The slope is negative two centimetres per gram per litre. This is an empirical local model, not a universal growth law. Random allocation and matched conditions support a salt-concentration effect for this genotype. The mixed population's height spread alone cannot partition genetic and environmental contributions.

Meets: "The four equally likely outcomes give three quarters purple, so 54 of 72 are expected; 49 does not by itself refute the model. My graph has height on the horizontal axis and plant count on the vertical axis, peaking at 8. The mean is 8 centimetres and range 4. The line has slope negative two and intercept 18, predicting 10 centimetres at 4 grams per litre. The cross models inherited differences; the randomised salt study shows different expression with the same genotype. Height spread in the separate sample cannot by itself identify a cause." Does not: "The model fails unless exactly 54 are purple (I). The frequency of 12 means those plants are each 12 centimetres tall (J). The population's height spread proves all differences are genetic (K)."
