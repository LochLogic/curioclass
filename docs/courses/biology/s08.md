## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did the air come to hold oxygen, and how have changes in the Earth made some species and ended others?"

**Hook, day 113:** Compare original drawings of transported pyrite grains in ancient sandstone and younger red sandstone, with age bands and modern oxidation observations. Withhold explanations. Ask the compelling question. Goto and colleagues supply the mineral evidence, field 10; the transition was prolonged, not an exact worldwide switch at 2.4 billion years.
**Closing explanation, day 120:** Explain the rock contrast through photosynthetic oxygen production and oxygen consumption by weathering. Use soil, reef and species evidence to argue that conditions also reshape life. Oxygen accumulation did not immediately produce animals.
**Teaching:** Model an evidence-linked arrow on day 114 and a qualified claim on day 117. Thereafter students select evidence, challenge alternatives and revise independently.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation:


**Question board:** Group notices into minerals, living causes and species outcomes. Why did oxidation change? Days 113 and 114 connect photosynthesis with oxygen sinks. Could life change land too? Day 115 compares soil formation and reef erosion records. Would every species benefit? Day 116 compares deforestation, fishing, runoff, drought and flood. Could separation create species? Day 117 tests shrimp evidence. Why might rapid change prevent adaptation? Day 118 compares rates. How far can old evidence support a claim? Days 119 to 121 judge timescales and competing explanations. Revisit unresolved questions daily. Transfer uses an unfamiliar island and beaver-shaped valley.
**Limits:** Qualitative mechanisms and supplied timelines suffice. No isotope calculations, molecular clocks, full Earth-system inventory or inevitable path toward complexity. Microbes contribute to soil alongside physical processes; oxygen affects organisms differently. The middle-school design codes belong only to the next readiness check.
**Carried:** Keep unit 7's ancestry, selection, data-graph, scale-proportion and argument models. Day 113 retrieves plate-model through past-position evidence and builds an initial Earth-life system model. Day 114 revises arrows using oxygen sources and sinks; day 115 adds soil and reef feedbacks. Day 117 revises plate-model to connect changing geography with barriers, retaining reproductive evidence as a separate requirement. Day 118 adds rate limits to selection; day 119 adds evidence uncertainty to argument. Day 121 establishes consensus models for day 122. Nothing is withdrawn.

**Sitting:** Five minutes read both packets, 20 minutes for each item, three check and two collect papers. No teaching or response occurs on day 122. All assessment records below are **invented for practice**, withheld from teaching examples and exit tickets.

**Item 1 packet:** On an island, clearing opens sunny ground. Grass P rises from 75 to 225 plants in six generations; matched uncleared plots stay near 75. Inherited sun-tolerant variants leave more surviving offspring in cleared plots. A sea channel closes gradually over 600,000 years, dividing shallow-water marine fish populations. Descendant groups differ genetically and produce no fertile offspring together in repeated controlled crosses; within-group crosses produce fertile offspring. A snail known only from that island loses all mapped habitat in a two-year drought. Repeated high-detection surveys across its entire known range find none; captive populations do not exist. Suitable habitat area fell by 24 square kilometres in two years there, compared with 24 square kilometres over 600,000 years during the slow isolation record.

**Prompt:** Evaluate the evidence that environmental change increased P, produced new fish species and caused snail extinction. For each, distinguish evidence from an unsupported conclusion. Calculate and compare annual habitat-change rates, and explain why rapid loss can prevent adaptation.

**Key and scoring:** For each outcome award one point for an accurate evidence comparison, one for the causal mechanism and one for a limit, nine points total. P triples with inherited reproductive advantage; the control supports clearing as a cause, though other changed factors need testing. Geographic isolation plus genetic and reproductive differences supports speciation, without proving every barrier produces species. Whole-known-range habitat loss and repeated searches support extinction more strongly than local absence; an undetected refuge remains possible. Award the remaining three points for 12 versus 0.00004 square kilometres per year, the 300,000-fold rate comparison, and explaining that inherited variation and enough generations are required, without guaranteeing adaptation under slow change. Accept an equivalent comparison in common larger time units.

Meets: "P's increase and the unchanged control support a response to clearing; inheritance and offspring counts support selection, though other changed factors need testing. Isolation and the fish crosses support speciation, but do not prove every barrier creates species. The snail evidence covers its whole known range, but a missed refuge is possible. The annual losses are 12 and 0.00004 square kilometres, so the fast loss is 300,000 times faster and can remove habitat before helpful variants spread." Does not: "The fish are new species because the channel closed" (B).

**Item 2 packet:** An unfamiliar valley is monitored for eight years. In year zero its stream is confined to a channel, with trees on a dry terrace. Beavers build a dam in year one. By year two, water spills across the terrace and flows more slowly; sediment accumulates and deeply flooded trees die. A nearby undammed reach receives similar rainfall but shows neither terrace flooding nor new sediment deposits. In year four, a high-flow event breaches the dam. Floodwater drains, leaving exposed sediment. Willow seedlings establish there in year five and form patches of young trees by year eight. Their establishment follows the new deposits rather than the old dry terrace. Water levels and seedling counts were measured annually, so seasonal changes may be missed. Supplied channel-test records show that a barrier raises upstream water, slows flow and promotes sediment deposition; these are paper records, not a classroom experiment.

**Prompt:** Use your revised model to argue against the claim, "River conditions change life, but life never changes river conditions." Trace the changes through time using the field and channel-test records. Weigh the alternative that rainfall alone explains the changes. Identify an evidence limit and an additional comparison that would test your explanation. State the natural-law assumption that connects the channel test to the valley.

**Key and scoring:** Award one point for an evidence-supported reciprocal claim. Award three for linking dam construction to flooding, slower flow and sediment deposition using the field sequence and channel test. Award three for linking changed physical conditions to tree death, exposed sediment after the breach and subsequent seedling establishment. Award two for evaluating the rainfall alternative against the undammed comparison while acknowledging that other site differences remain possible. Award one each for a justified evidence limit, a relevant further comparison and explaining that the same physical laws govern both settings despite differences in scale. Twelve points total. Temporal order supports the argument but alone does not prove causation.

Meets: "Beavers changed the channel by building a dam. Flooding and slower flow followed, as in the channel test, and sediment accumulated. Deep flooding killed trees; after the breach, exposed deposits supported seedlings. Thus life changed physical conditions that then changed life. Similar rainfall without these changes at the undammed reach weakens the rainfall-only claim, although the reaches may differ in slope. Annual readings can miss seasonal events. Comparing matched slopes and water levels would help. The same flow and settling laws apply in the channel test and valley; their sizes need not match." Does not: "Only the river changes organisms; beavers cannot change river conditions" (E).

**Retained spoken evidence:** Day 120 uses the anchor, soil and reef records studied earlier. Teach argument structure on day 113 and evidence coverage on day 119. Six groups of five have three minutes each, 18 minutes total; each learner contributes a claim linked to an evidence record and answers a counterclaim. During these talks, others prepare evidence. Then everyone writes independently for 22 minutes; the final ten-minute revision block includes eight minutes revising evidence links and two collecting papers. The teacher gives each learner written feedback by day 121, when students amend their own reasoning in writing. Keep the day 120 contribution record with that amendment; first score on day 122, without another talk. Award one point for a defensible reciprocal claim with evidence and one for evaluating a counterclaim with a stated limit. Meets: "Soil supports plants, whose roots then retain material; the ordered records support feedback, though order alone cannot exclude climate change." Does not: "Plants have no effect on soil" (E). A missing record stays missing evidence until a later supported opportunity.

**Point balance and access:** The new island and beaver cases require argument and evidence with core ideas and causal or stability reasoning for all 24 written points. Excluding the two retained anchor points, transfer earns 24 of 26 points, about 92 percent. Read aloud, enlarge diagrams, supply a glossary, permit a calculator and accept dictated answers with the same reasoning demand. Give identical evidence access to a child working with one adult.

**Teaching evidence:** Keep one unscored notebook record per learner daily: day 113 initial arrows; day 114 oxygen source and sink; day 115 comparison of text with a soil or reef diagram, stating agreement or contradiction; day 116 five environmental contrasts; day 117 barrier claim with reproductive evidence; day 118 rate comparison; day 119 claim scope and natural-law assumption; day 120 individual argument; day 121 corrections added to that record. Day 119's two short Curio Class arguments, each labelled "invented for practice", present competing claims, "All mineral change proves global oxygenation" and "A local mineral record requires independent checks before a global claim." Neither argument is a published claim or a summary of Goto and colleagues. Students judge reasoning and evidence coverage. Day 120 writing introduces a precise claim, fairly develops a counterclaim, links reasons and evidence, maintains an objective tone and concludes. Literacy evidence remains alongside science, without extra scored items.

**Section exit tickets:** Each is one question, at most two parts, five minutes inside the named day. Cases are **invented for practice** and not used in that day's tasks. Other days use notebook evidence.

- **Day 116:** In new matched ponds, fertiliser input increases algae and reduces submerged plants, while reference ponds remain steady. Algae release oxygen in light, but decomposition consumes oxygen at night; low nighttime oxygen limits oxygen-using animals. What evidence supports different species outcomes, and how would your model show life changing water conditions that then constrain life? Key: matched changes support different outcomes, and the source-and-sink loop makes effects reciprocal. Wrong: organisms have no effect on water conditions (E); oxygen release guarantees dissolved-oxygen accumulation despite consumption (F).
- **Day 121:** In new woodland records, a 15-unit habitat change takes three years in one interval and 300 years in another. Greater tree cover accompanies more water released through transpiration and greater downwind rainfall; wetter plots have higher seedling survival. How do the rates constrain an adaptation claim, and what reciprocal Earth-life argument do the records support under consistent natural laws? Key: five versus 0.05 units per year, faster change may exceed adaptive capacity; trees supply atmospheric water through transpiration, and rainfall supports tree survival, a feedback consistent with the records but not proved by correlation alone. The same water-cycle processes apply across both intervals. Wrong: rates are equal (D); trees have no effect on water conditions (E). Retain this formative response separately from the amended spoken record.
- **Day 123:** After response, use an unseen coast where kelp reduces wave disturbance, while clear water permits kelp growth; sudden sediment input removes most clear habitat. Use a reciprocal model to explain the species outcome, and predict how reducing sediment input could change the rate of habitat loss. Key: kelp affects water motion and clarity affects kelp, while rapid habitat loss can outpace adaptation; less sediment could slow loss, a prediction to monitor. Wrong: only Earth affects life (E); need creates new inherited tolerance (A). This is formative, within the first 40 minutes.
