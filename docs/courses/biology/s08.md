## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How did the air come to hold oxygen, and how have changes in the Earth made some species and ended others?"
**Hook, day 113:** Compare original drawings of transported pyrite grains in ancient sandstone and younger red sandstone, with age bands and modern oxidation observations. Withhold explanations. Ask the compelling question. Goto and colleagues supply the mineral evidence, field 10; the transition was prolonged, not an exact worldwide switch at 2.4 billion years.
**Closing explanation, day 120:** Explain the rock contrast through photosynthetic oxygen production and oxygen consumption by weathering. Use soil, reef and species evidence to argue that conditions also reshape life. Oxygen accumulation did not immediately produce animals.
**Teaching:** Model an evidence-linked arrow on day 114 and a qualified claim on day 117. Thereafter students select evidence, challenge alternatives and revise independently.

- **HS-LS4-5:** Weigh evidence that changing environments can make some species grow in number, give rise to new species over time, and drive other species extinct. Emphasis: cause and effect between changes such as deforestation, fishing, fertilizer, drought or flooding, and their pace, and how traits spread or vanish in species.
- **HS-ESS2-7:** Argue from evidence that Earth's systems and life on Earth have changed each other together over time. **Sitting:** Five minutes read both packets, 20 minutes for each item, five check. No teaching or response occurs on day 122. All assessment records below are **invented for practice**, withheld from teaching examples and exit tickets.

**Item 1 packet:** On an island, clearing opens sunny ground. Grass P rises from 75 to 225 plants in six generations; matched uncleared plots stay near 75. Inherited sun-tolerant variants leave more surviving offspring in cleared plots. A sea channel closes gradually over 600,000 years, dividing shallow-water marine fish populations. Descendant groups differ genetically and produce no fertile offspring together in repeated controlled crosses; within-group crosses produce fertile offspring. A snail known only from that island loses all mapped habitat in a two-year drought. Repeated high-detection surveys across its entire known range find none; captive populations do not exist. Suitable habitat area fell by 24 square kilometres in two years there, compared with 24 square kilometres over 600,000 years during the slow isolation record.

**Prompt:** Evaluate the evidence that environmental change increased P, produced new fish species and caused snail extinction. For each, distinguish evidence from an unsupported conclusion. Calculate and compare annual habitat-change rates, and explain why rapid loss can prevent adaptation.

**Key and scoring:** For each outcome award one point for an accurate evidence comparison, one for the causal mechanism and one for a limit, nine points total. P triples with inherited reproductive advantage; the control supports clearing as a cause, though other changed factors need testing. Geographic isolation plus genetic and reproductive differences supports speciation, without proving every barrier produces species. Whole-known-range habitat loss and repeated searches support extinction more strongly than local absence; an undetected refuge remains possible. Award the remaining three points for 12 versus 0.00004 square kilometres per year, the 300,000-fold rate comparison, and explaining that inherited variation and enough generations are required, without guaranteeing adaptation under slow change. Accept an equivalent comparison in common larger time units.

Meets: "P's increase and the unchanged control support a response to clearing; inheritance and offspring counts support selection, though other changed factors need testing. Isolation and the fish crosses support speciation, but do not prove every barrier creates species. The snail evidence covers its whole known range, but a missed refuge is possible. The annual losses are 12 and 0.00004 square kilometres, so the fast loss is 300,000 times faster and can remove habitat before helpful variants spread." Does not: "The fish are new species because the channel closed" (B).

**Item 2 packet:** An unfamiliar coastal archive has three sequences. All ages are millions of years before present. In the oldest, chemical evidence supports oxygen-producing photosynthesis at 2,700, widespread iron-oxide coatings appear at 2,200, and animal fossils at 550; each age has uncertainty of ten million years. A supplied oxidation experiment shows that exposed iron-bearing minerals consume oxygen. On land, microbial weathering occurs at 480, thicker organic soil at 450, and rooted plants at 400, each with uncertainty of five million years. Rooted layers retain more sediment. Shallow-water corals occur at 12, a reef ridge at ten, and sheltered-habitat fossils with reduced wave disturbance at eight; deepening interrupts reef growth at six. These four ages each have uncertainty of 0.2 million years. The dates are archive observations, not global first appearances.

**Prompt:** Use your revised model to construct an argument against the claim, "Geology changes life, but life never changes geology." Explain what each sequence supports about reciprocal change, including oxygen production and consumption, soil and plants, and reefs and erosion. Identify one uncertainty and one additional comparison that would strengthen a causal claim. Explain the assumption about natural laws that permits using the oxidation experiment to interpret ancient rocks.

**Key and scoring:** Award three points for each sequence: evidence from its order, a life-to-Earth mechanism and an Earth-to-life consequence, nine points. Microbes release oxygen, oxidation consumes it, and later oxygenated habitats support oxygen-using life; early production alone does not date atmospheric accumulation or animal origin. Microbes help form soil, soil supports rooted plants, and roots then retain sediment. Coral growth builds wave-buffering relief and habitat, while water depth constrains coral growth. Award one point each for a justified evidence limit, a relevant comparison such as matched reef and reef-free wave records, and consistent natural laws rather than identical past conditions. Temporal order alone is insufficient to establish causation.

Meets: "Microbes supplied oxygen while minerals consumed it, so production could precede accumulation. Oxygenated habitats support oxygen-using organisms, but this sequence does not establish what caused animal origins. Microbial weathering helped soil form, soil supported plants, and roots retained more sediment. Corals built the ridge and sheltered habitats, but deepening stopped growth. These reciprocal links challenge the one-way claim. Matched sites without reefs would help test the wave explanation; fossil gaps limit certainty. Oxidation follows the same laws even though past oxygen levels differed." Does not: "Geology controls everything; organisms leave no geological effects" (E).

**Retained spoken evidence:** Day 120 uses the anchor, soil and reef records studied earlier. Teach argument structure on day 113 and evidence coverage on day 119. Six groups of five have three minutes each, 18 minutes total; each learner contributes a claim linked to an evidence record and answers a counterclaim. During these talks, others prepare evidence. Then everyone writes independently for 22 minutes; the final ten minutes revise evidence links. The teacher gives each learner written feedback by day 121, when students amend their own reasoning in writing. Keep the day 120 contribution record with that amendment; first score on day 122, without another talk. Award one point for a defensible reciprocal claim with evidence and one for evaluating a counterclaim with a stated limit. Meets: "Soil supports plants, whose roots then retain material; the ordered records support feedback, though order alone cannot exclude climate change." Does not: "Plants have no effect on soil" (E). A missing record stays missing evidence until a later supported opportunity.
