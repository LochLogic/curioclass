## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "What has kept the island's moose from eating it bare, and what changed when the wolves nearly disappeared?"

**Hook, day 58:** Display original graphs of the island's historical wolf and moose estimates beside an original island outline. Students mark unexpected rises and crashes before hearing the compelling question. Sources are in field 10.
**Closing explanation, day 72:** Explain the island pattern using resource limits, contrasting population runs, survey coverage and qualified group-hunting evidence. Distinguish what the records establish from what remains uncertain.
**Teaching:** Model the supplied population update on day 60 and evidence evaluation on day 66. Thereafter ask students to predict, test, challenge and revise before building the class account.

**Transfer packet:** Every ecosystem record below is Curio Class original, **invented for practice**, withheld until assessment. These are expected model populations and simplified observations, not field findings.

**Item 1 packet and prompt:** A closed pond of 0.1 hectare starts with 100 snails; a separate regional wetland of 10 hectares starts with 10,000. Both begin at 1,000 snails per hectare. One step is one year. The supplied rule is new population equals old population plus births minus deaths. Births are 20 percent of the old population. Deaths are 10 percent below the food-and-space threshold, 30 percent at or above it. Thresholds are 120 snails and 12,000 snails respectively. There is no movement across either model boundary. First predict and calculate three baseline steps. Then reset: drought reduces the pond threshold to 80, while deep-water refuges preserve the regional threshold. Predict and calculate two changed steps. Label units, compare scales and explain the resource mechanism. A separate park record describes limited drinking water, competition for plants, a disease outbreak and increased predation. Explain which assumptions would need changing to represent that park and why the same factor need not dominate every boundary. Compare the model's expected values with pond observations of 100, 109, 119 and 106 snails across the baseline years; state a limitation.

**Item 1 answer and look-fors:** The five scored components are rule and units, baseline calculations with observation comparison, changed calculations and mechanism, scale comparison with park factors, and a justified model limitation. Baseline pond values are 110, 121 and 108.9; regional values are 11,000, 12,100 and 10,890. Changed values are 90 and 81 in the pond, 11,000 and 12,100 regionally. Fractions represent expected numbers, not fractional animals counted. Model and observation broadly agree but differ by 1, 2 and 2.9 snails. Deaths exceeding births reduce expected population after crowding or drought; a resource threshold is not a hard population cap. Both original thresholds are 1,200 snails per hectare; after drought the pond threshold is 800. Water, climate, competition, disease and predators can alter birth or death assumptions at different scales. The closed model omits migration, variable weather and several interacting causes; fitting a trend does not identify its cause.

Meets: "I predict growth below the threshold and decline once deaths exceed births. My model adds 20 percent births and subtracts either 10 or 30 percent deaths from the old expected population each year. Pond steps are 110, 121 and 108.9 snails; regional steps are 11,000, 12,100 and 10,890. The pond model exceeds observations by 1, 2 and 2.9 snails. After drought I predict pond decline: 90 then 81; the region still gives 11,000 then 12,100. Both original thresholds are 1,200 snails per hectare; only the pond changes to 800. Water, space, food, competing grazers, disease and predators can affect births or deaths differently across boundaries. A fixed death rule misses changing park disease; the closed model also omits migration. Matching counts would not isolate one cause." Does not: "A threshold of 120 means 121 is impossible (A). The regional population is larger, so its resource threshold per hectare must be larger (B)."

**Item 2 packet and prompt:** Equal-area local plots have plant richness 4, 6, 5 before drought and 8, 9, 7 afterward; abundance is 20, 30, 25 before and 40, 50, 45 afterward. A ten-plot regional network near permanent water records pooled richness 40 then 36 species and abundance 2,000 then 1,800 plants. Calculate local means and ranges; draw side-by-side comparisons and explain abundance and richness at both scales. Write an initial explanation before opening a second card: a matched before-and-after survey of 100 plots spread across every regional habitat records pooled richness 80 then 56 and abundance 20,000 then 12,000. Revise the explanation using proportional changes and sampling reach. The broader survey records drying in exposed habitats and persistent water in refuges. Identify a supported resource explanation and a causal limit.

**Item 2 answer and look-fors:** Score local calculations, labeled graphs, abundance distinguished from richness across scales, revision from the second survey, and resource reasoning with sampling limits. Local mean richness rises from 5 to 8; its range remains 2. Mean abundance rises from 25 to 45; its range remains 10. Regional first-network richness and abundance each fall 10 percent. Broader richness falls 30 percent; abundance falls 40 percent. Use these within-network changes rather than comparing raw totals from different-sized networks. A local gain cannot describe the region; persistent-water sites understate losses in dry habitats. Water limitation is consistent with the records but competing causes remain untested. Richness counts kinds; abundance counts organisms. Pooled regional richness is not the sum of plot richness because species overlap.

Meets: "My two labelled before-and-after graphs show mean species per local plot increasing from 5 to 8, with range 2 both times, and mean plants per plot from 25 to 45, with range 10 both times. More individuals and more kinds are separate findings. Initially I explain local gains and a 10 percent regional decline in both measures. The broader regional survey instead shows losses of 30 percent of pooled richness and 40 percent of plants. I revise the small-loss explanation because the first network favored water refuges. The broader survey reaches dry habitats. Within-network percentages permit comparisons despite different plot numbers; raw pooled richness totals do not. Persistent water could support local gains while regional drying limits plants, but these surveys do not isolate that cause." Does not: "More plants means more species (C), so these local plots prove improvement everywhere (D)."

**Item 3 packet and prompt:** Evaluate two claims: "Every disturbance causes collapse" and "Every ecosystem returns to its former community." Under stable woodland conditions, the same plant, bird and insect community has annual abundance 160, 152 and 168 and richness 18, 18 and 19. Following a windstorm, abundance falls to 104 and richness to 11, then returns near 160 and 18 after six years with the former community present. After repeated fires, total abundance returns to 160. Judge the first evidence, then open the composition card: former canopy trees and shade-dependent plants remain absent twelve years later, replaced by grasses and shrubs while recurring burns prevent canopy recovery. Use a revised stability model to judge both arguments, explain interactions under stable conditions and limits after modest or extreme disturbance, and state what evidence could change your judgment.

**Item 3 answer and look-fors:** Score evidence for relative stability, windstorm recovery, persistent replacement, revision and evidence coverage, and a reasoned counterargument with further evidence. Normal variation is compatible with stability: resource availability, feeding interactions and recruitment can keep numbers within a range. The windstorm record contradicts inevitable collapse; recovery includes returning kinds, not only totals. Repeated fires change habitat and resources, supporting a different community rather than universal recovery. Revise the apparent recovery inferred from equal totals when composition arrives. Twelve years supports persistence over that interval, not permanence. Continued fire and species records could revise the account.

Meets: "The stable record varies from 152 to 168 organisms with similar kinds. Plants supply food, and feeding and recruitment help maintain numbers under those conditions. The windstorm site recovered former kinds and near its old abundance in six years, contradicting inevitable collapse. At first the fire case's total of 160 suggested recovery. I revise that judgment: missing trees and shade-dependent plants change the model's food and habitat links. Recurring burns support a new community, so universal recovery is unsupported. These observations support persistence for twelve years, not forever. A fire-free interval and returning former species could change my judgment." Does not: "The fire case has 160 organisms again, so it is the former ecosystem (E)."

**Item 4 packet and prompt:** An invented reed bird nests alone or with an adult sibling that guards and feeds young. Researchers follow one marked breeder at each of 20 helper nests and 20 solitary nests for a year. Sixteen marked breeders survive at helper nests, compared with 12 at solitary nests. Thirty young from helper nests and 18 from solitary nests survive to their next breeding season. Helper nests occupy food-richer patches. Evaluate the claim that cooperation improves survival and reproduction and guarantees the species' survival. Distinguish individual from group actions, use numerical evidence, weigh an alternative cause, propose a stronger comparison and explain how benefits to individuals and relatives could favor inherited tendencies toward cooperation over generations. State what further evidence warrants a species-level inference.

**Item 4 answer and look-fors:** Score behavior distinction, numerical survival and recruitment evidence, causal evaluation and improved comparison, evolutionary reasoning including relatives, and justified species-level limits. Guarding and provisioning by siblings are cooperative; solitary nest care is individual behavior. Marked-breeder survival is 80 versus 60 percent; recruits average 1.5 versus 0.9 per nest. These observations support an association with both survival and reproduction, but richer food can contribute. Compare replicated nests matched for food, predator exposure and breeder age, with repeated seasons. Inherited behavioral tendencies can become more common when bearers or relatives leave more surviving descendants; animals do not choose evolutionary goals. One population over one year cannot guarantee species persistence. Wider population and multigeneration evidence about survival, reproduction and group-related costs would strengthen a conditional inference, without requiring evolution for the species' benefit.

Meets: "Sibling guarding and feeding are cooperative; solitary parental care is individual behavior. Marked breeders survive at 80 rather than 60 percent, and recruits average 1.5 rather than 0.9 per nest. This supports an association with survival and reproduction. Food-rich patches could explain part of it. Repeated comparisons matched for food, predator exposure and age would strengthen the inference. More surviving offspring or relatives can pass on shared inherited tendencies, allowing cooperation to become more common across generations without an evolutionary goal. We need repeated populations and generations, including group-related costs, before inferring an effect on species persistence. One year cannot guarantee survival of a species." Does not: "Birds choose to evolve helpers to save the species (G); this one study guarantees species survival (H)."
