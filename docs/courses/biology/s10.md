## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can a simulation show whether a rule will save a fishery or a whale before it is tried on the real ocean?"
**Hook, day 141:** Display dated cod landings beside a survey trend, then whale deaths by cause. Students notice why catches cannot measure abundance and why one protection might miss another threat. Field 10 names the records.
**Closing explanation, day 152:** Use checked calculations, observed records and contrasting runs to explain why harvest limits and protection rules have different consequences under changed demand. Distinguish conditional predictions from field evidence.
**Teaching:** Model boundaries on day 142 and formula entry on day 143. On day 144 students narrow a self-generated recovery question and synthesize survey and catch sources. Release edits to students on day 146. Day 152 builds a formal argument with a counterclaim, evidence links and a qualified conclusion. On days 149 to 151, ask for predictions before tests and evidence before revisions; students choose improvements.

**Transfer packet, invented for practice:** An unfamiliar bay supports a fishery and a threatened diving seabird that eats these fish. Start with 100 tonnes of fish and 50 birds. Population has grown from 400 to 600 residents. Use two one-year steps. Demand equals residents times annual use per person. Harvest is the smallest of demand, the cap and 20 percent of starting fish stock. Next fish stock equals starting stock plus growth minus harvest. Growth is 10 percent of starting stock at or above 80 tonnes, zero below 80. This artificial recovery threshold is a testable assumption, not a field estimate. Bird recruitment is four birds when starting fish stock is at least 100 tonnes, otherwise two. The supplied bird formula adds recruitment and subtracts vessel deaths but wrongly omits gear deaths. Revise it to subtract both. Floor all populations at zero; recruitment uses the stock at the start of each step, before harvesting. These simplified rules test consequences, not real demographic rates.

Ordinary use is 0.025 tonnes per resident yearly. A demand measure lowers it to 0.015 and costs three credits. Gear protection changes annual gear deaths from three to one and costs five. Broad speed protection changes vessel deaths from two to one and costs four. Independent encounter records show every recorded strike occurred in a narrow corridor; targeted speed protection there costs three, predicts the same vessel reduction but has lower reliability. Ordinary options achieve delivery on 19 of 20 days; targeted protection achieves 18. Costs are total two-year credits, including maintenance. Food distribution relies on refrigerated transport. Cheaper extraction could increase gear effort, an unintended effect outside these numerical rules; explain how adding that relation could change predictions.

The budget has changed from 12 to 11 credits. After two years, require at least 100 tonnes of fish, at least 53 birds, harvest meeting the reduced or ordinary demand each year, and delivery on at least 18 of 20 days. All packages retain emergency access, safe working procedures and access to a gathering place. Broad markers change the skyline more than targeted markers. These fictional conditions do not represent any community's preferences. Rank feasible choices by bird persistence, then lower cost; justify alternatives that meet every constraint.
