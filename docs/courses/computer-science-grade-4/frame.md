# Course frame: Computer Science, Grade 4

Course `computer-science-grade-4` (catalog id `cs-k12`), grade 4, one year. Draft frame, 2026-11-25, written on the shape of the kindergarten pilot (docs/courses/computer-science-kindergarten/frame.md and its fix report); the scope and sequence sets the days later.

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 5, one for each concept area at its grade 4 depth, in the order the ideas build: when it breaks and how
  it connects (systems and security, so the troubleshooting steps exist before the year's first device day that
  depends on them), algorithms in words (algorithms and design: a written algorithm with an event, a repeat and a
  choice, and who two algorithms help), tables, charts and people (data: a table in a computer tool, a chart that
  reports an investigation, and data about people), the class quiz, built in pieces (programming: trace, compare,
  debug as you build, then a team program with comments), and machines that learn and people who make tools
  (machine learning beside computing and society, the year's closer).
- **Days.** 32 course days: 28 in units, 2 for course-level checks (1 after unit 2, 1 after unit 4) and 2 respond
  days (one after each check). One lesson a week of about 40 minutes, 32 weeks: the computer science default for
  grades 3 to 5 (docs/courses/computer-science/STANDARD.md section 2, Cory, Desk q-fix-cs-1), a specials rotation,
  so course day n falls in week n. The 40 minutes hold set-up, clean-up and collecting work: a session opens with a
  three-minute retrieval trace (APPROACH.md, Pacing, "How a day opens"), and device sessions keep five minutes at
  the end to save or print work, shut lids and return devices, so a session teaches for about 32 minutes. Each unit's
  days include its end-of-unit assessment and end with a respond day (APPROACH.md, Pacing), with the pilot's one shape
  for a unit that a course-level check follows directly: in units 2 and 4 the check stands for the unit's own
  end-of-unit assessment and the check's respond day is the unit's respond day (pilot For the lead 2). So units 1 to 5
  teach on 4, 4, 4, 5 and 5 days, 22 of the 32. Days follow the load of the rows, not equal shares: unit 4 owns four
  programming rows and five ids, three of them device days with a trace, modify and make order and a team product with
  a feedback session before its score, and gets 5 teaching days; unit 5 owns three rows and five ids, two of them the
  rows the progression read counts as "two sessions in practice" (g4-ml-learning, g4-history-limits;
  docs/reviews/progression-computer-science-2026-11-19.md), and gets 5; unit 1's four systems rows are single
  unplugged or station sessions and get 4; unit 2's two rows get 4, three of them for the written algorithm (trace,
  modify, make); unit 3's three rows get 4, two for the investigation and its written report. 32 sessions for 16 rows
  is the read's "about two sessions a row", counted with checks. Each course-level check takes one 40-minute check
  session and one respond session. No unit runs over 25 days, so there are no mid-unit assessments.
- **Check weeks.** The grade 4 core checks take weeks 8, 9 (math's alternative), 10, 12, 16, 19, 21, 23, 27, 30, 31
  and 32 (docs/courses/CALENDAR.md, target weeks: ELA 16 and 32, math 12 (or 9), 23 and 31, science 10, 19 and 30,
  social studies 8 (or 12), 21 and 27). This course's checks sit in week 11 (mid-year, after unit 2) and week 24
  (year-end, after unit 4), with their respond days in weeks 12 and 25 (respond days are not check days). Week 24
  holds the year-end check so unit 5 has the seven weeks 26 to 32 its five ids need, as kindergarten's unit 5 keeps
  weeks after its year-end check; the weeks free of core checks after 24 (25, 26, 28, 29) are too few to fit unit 5
  before a later check. Units 3 and 4 run across weeks 13 to 23, which include the core check weeks 16, 19, 21 and
  23, because they hold no course-level check; unit 5's own end-of-unit assessment falls in week 31, a unit
  assessment, not a course-level check. Overlaps with other after-core subjects' check weeks are the lead's calendar
  pass (For the lead 1).
- **Standard.** The 19 E4 ids of the 2026 CSTA PK-12 Computer Science Standards, all taught here (STANDARD.md
  section 2): E4-ALG-PS-01, E4-ALG-ML-02, E4-ALG-IM-03, E4-PRO-PD-04, E4-PRO-PD-05, E4-PRO-VD-06, E4-PRO-RD-07,
  E4-PRO-TR-08, E4-DAT-DC-09, E4-DAT-DI-10, E4-DAT-IM-11, E4-SYS-HW-12, E4-SYS-SE-13, E4-SYS-NT-14, E4-SYS-IM-15,
  E4-SOC-HI-16, E4-SOC-ET-17, E4-SOC-HU-18, E4-SOC-CE-19. Each is taught at the depth its owner row's notes set, in
  our own words (STANDARD.md "Depth": the rows were checked against the boundary statements on 2026-11-21, which are
  never copied or quoted), and "What students learn" says what is out. Grade 4 writes an algorithm in words; the
  flowchart is grade 5's (STANDARD.md "Depth"). Plans cite ids and state each action in a short phrase of our own
  (STANDARD.md "Quoting"); the credit line "Standards: 2026 CSTA PK-12 Computer Science Standards (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)"
  goes on any day that leans on a statement. Check set: each unit plan checks itself against the computer science bar
  (docs/ADOPTION-BAR-SUBJECTS.md part 5, items 1 to 9) and the lens (docs/agents/lenses/computer-science.md), and
  reads the partial base unit it adapts for order; the "Check set adds" column names what the writer expects that to
  add. Values rule (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-12) holds. No Specialty id, and no X + CS id, is claimed.
- **Practices and concept areas.** The Units table's Practices and concept areas column names, for
  computer science, the concept area each unit teaches and the CSTA practices it teaches inside the content, by code
  and our label (STANDARD.md section 3), as bar items 1 and 2 ask. Each unit plan names the practice's day and task.
  Across the year: CT6 define the problem (units 1, 2, 3), CT7 find patterns and make reusable parts (units 2, 4, 5),
  CT8 build and plan (units 2, 4), CT9 test, debug and refine (units 1, 4), IC3 explain and give feedback (units 2 to
  5), IC4 run a project with shared goals and documented work (unit 4), IC5 be a reliable teammate (unit 4, driver
  and navigator), HCD10 learn from and include different users (units 2, 5), ESR1 weigh who gains and who is harmed
  (units 1, 2, 3, 5), ESR2 respect people and protect information (units 1, 3, 4: credit in a comment). Dispositions
  are named in the unit plans, not here.
- **Base text.** Partial: England's open computing curriculum, the Teach Computing Curriculum, archived edition
  (raspberrypi.org/curriculum/archived/key-stage-2, resources dated 2023), the same fixed edition the kindergarten
  frame maps (its For the lead 4). Year 5 is the grade 4 age (Year 1 is kindergarten in the pilot, Year 3 grade 2).
  License as the pilot states: the Open Government Licence v3.0 "unless expressly stated otherwise" on the publisher's
  terms page; the archived unit pages carry no license line of their own, so each file's license line is read when the
  store folder is made. Adapted unit by unit and rephrased for an American audience (STANDARD.md section 2, Cory
  2026-11-16): US spelling, school years named as US grades, no money in pounds, no product or company name, our own
  browser editor and table tool or paper in place of the named block language, database program and microcontroller.
  Three of its six Year 5 units feed units 1, 3 and 4 (see Source map); its two creating-media units and its
  physical-computing unit are left out, and the web-search half of "Systems and searching" is left to the library
  course's method. Units 2 and 5 are our own. The source's name appears only in a day's credit line, once a day.
  Pending(docs/sources/BASE-TEXTS.md#teach-computing-curriculum): the source store holds no copy of this base, so the
  Source map below rests on the public unit pages (lesson titles and unit overviews, opened 2026-11-25), and each
  adapted lesson's check for third-party images, text and tool screenshots waits on the store folder (For the lead 2).
- **Day kinds.** As docs/GLOSSARY.md (row "teaching day") defines them, in computer science's words (APPROACH.md):
  Understanding is an unplugged or trace session, the first teaching of a construct or idea (a written choose-your-path
  algorithm followed before anyone writes one, a counter program traced in a trace-table before it runs, a network
  acted out). Fluency is a modify session: change one line of a working algorithm or one part of a working program in
  small, checked steps; grade 4 has some of it (unit 2 session 2, unit 4 session 3, and the retrieval openings).
  Application is a make, design or society session: write one's own algorithm and have a partner follow it (unit 2
  session 3), run a data investigation and write its report (unit 3 sessions 2 and 3), build the team program (unit 4
  sessions 4 and 5), or judge cases with a chart (unit 2 session 4, unit 3 session 4, unit 5 sessions 3 to 5). Check is
  the end-of-unit assessment or a course-level check; Respond acts on it by error letter. Inside a unit: understanding
  before modify before make, and no make day before a trace or modify day on the same construct.
- **Evidence.** Grade 4 writes its answers (APPROACH.md, "The form of daily evidence"): trace and predict sessions end
  in an exit ticket with a short program or algorithm and "what happens?", wrong answers sorted by error letter;
  modify and make sessions are read from the program or algorithm itself against two or three look-fors, with one line
  from the student on what they tested and what they changed; society and data-impact sessions end in a short claim on
  the chart. Every end-of-unit assessment is a sitting with unseen items, never the unit's own anchor, about 30
  minutes, with a paper version: read and trace items plus a short build, debug or audit task, each with look-fors.
  Each owned id has its own look-for: a "create" id (E4-ALG-PS-01, E4-DAT-DI-10) is met by the student's own written
  algorithm or report made in the sitting or in counted draft and revision days (the report in the table tool's report
  view), a "compare", "evaluate", "distinguish" or "analyze" id by a written judgment with its reason on new cases,
  never by recognition of a supplied answer, an "apply" id (E4-SYS-HW-12) by a fault the student finds, fixes with a
  safe step and checks, and a "debug" id (E4-PRO-TR-08) by a test-table the student fills while finding the piece that
  broke, fixing it and running the tests again. One scored product: unit 4's team quiz, built in session 4, given a
  reader's written note and revised in session 5, then scored from its revised version with a record for each member
  (frame-write.md rule 19); it carries E4-PRO-PD-05 and E4-PRO-RD-07 into the year-end check. Units 2 and 4 are
  assessed in the course-level check that follows them (Days). No score comes from a session that first teaches a
  row. The respond day reteaches by error letter and repeats the missed item in a new form.
- **Devices.** One device per pair is the default on a device day, never needed per child. Device days: unit 1
  session 1 (staged faults at stations), unit 3 sessions 1 to 3 (the table tool: records entered, a column sorted, a
  chart made, a short report written beside the chart in its report view), unit 4 sessions 1 to 5 (our own block
  editor with its text view and step-through trace), and unit 5 session 1 (a one-screen sorter). That is 10 of the 22
  teaching sessions, so a little over half the teaching sessions are away from a device, about APPROACH.md's "about
  half" for grades 3 to 5 (ours, untested). Every device day is written four ways (STANDARD.md "Devices and rooms"):
  one device per pair, a few devices at stations with the other pairs on the paper version of the same idea, one
  screen with a student at the device for each step, or no device. Two owned ids need a device to be met in full:
  E4-DAT-DC-09 asks for a computational tool, and E4-DAT-DI-10 asks for a digital report made with one, a chart and its
  short narrative in one document (STANDARD.md "Devices and rooms"; boundaries read 2026-11-26). On the no-device path
  unit 3 teaches records and attributes on the paper table and the report by hand with a hand-drawn chart, and the
  course record marks E4-DAT-DC-09 and E4-DAT-DI-10 "part met without a device", never met. Every other id is met in
  full on paper: printed programs with trace-tables and test-tables and a partner acting as the computer meet
  E4-PRO-VD-06, E4-PRO-PD-04, E4-PRO-TR-08, E4-PRO-PD-05 and E4-PRO-RD-07 (a paper program with written comments is a
  program a team made and documented); picture fault cards that show the device's response to each chosen step, and a
  toy device built from pictures, meet E4-SYS-HW-12; the class acting as the sorter meets E4-ALG-ML-02.
- **Paper path.** Every session runs on paper, cards and objects: printed programs with their text view, trace-tables
  and test-tables beside the editor; a paper data-table, a hand-drawn bar graph and a handwritten report beside the
  table tool; the class as
  the sorter beside the one-screen sorter; picture fault cards beside the staged faults. No tool asks for a student
  account, stores a student's name or sends anything off the device; work is saved on the device or printed
  (STANDARD.md "Privacy and safety").
- **Reading level and vocabulary.** Grade 4 reads short instructions and programs of up to ten lines (APPROACH.md,
  Reading load); error messages are plain words we write. Each unit names five to eight words taught in depth, with the
  everyday words that have a computing meaning taught as two meanings (bug, run, table, record, train, network). The
  long words authentication and authorization come last in unit 1 session 3, after login and permission are sorted
  (progression read). Sentence frames: "First the program ___, then it ___" and "I predict ___ because line ___ says
  ___". A student may answer a trace in numbers, arrows or the home language, and each key says which wrong answers
  can come from reading the words rather than from the idea.
- **Swap or leave out.** STANDARD.md section 3 lets a school swap or leave out a named unit for its community's
  reasons. In this course every unit carries the only ids of its concept area, so units are swapped, not left out:
  unit 5's biographies may be swapped for people from the school's own community or region who added to computing (the
  same id, E4-SOC-HI-16, still with a dated source each), and its worker cases for local workplaces; unit 3's class
  question for another question about things. So the course always claims the data ids and the society ids (the bar
  part 5 fails a course with nothing on data or society). Units 1, 2 and 4 carry the troubleshooting steps, the written
  algorithm and the block program the rest of the year and grade 5 use and are not swapped. A school that leaves out
  a unit anyway names in the course page and the unit's family note the ids not claimed that year.
- **Safety.** No lab or field work, so no laboratory or field safety guide applies. Staged faults are set by the
  teacher with the device's own settings and its accessory cables (a mouse or headphone cable, the volume, a closed
  window, airplane mode); students never touch a power cord or a wall socket, never open a device's case, and never
  change a setting the teacher did not stage. Unit 1's acted-out network uses a string or yarn line held at waist
  height, walked, not run. Devices are carried with two hands and cords stay behind the table. The scope and sequence
  copies these lines into each station and device day.
- **Costs.** Provisional, for a class of 30, beyond the school's own devices: unit 1 $6 (printing fault cards and
  troubleshooting step cards, the two network-diagram mats, the door-and-rooms card sort, device-life strips; one ball
  of yarn), unit 2 $3 (printed choose-your-path algorithms and case cards, benefit-risk charts), unit 3 $4 (paper
  data-table sheets, graph paper, case cards), unit 4 $5 (printed programs with their text view, trace-table and
  test-table sheets for the paper path), unit 5 $4 (sorter picture cards, biography cards and the timeline strip, worker
  case cards), about $22 in all.
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md);
  no outside reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14). Unit 1 has security and personal-information content: the agent
  checks the login-and-permission section carries health's "If a student tells you something" steps word for word
  (docs/courses/health/APPROACH.md; APPROACH.md, Sensitive ground), that every login is a pretend paper one, and that
  no card asks what devices a child has at home. Unit 2's two algorithms for picking teams and sharing a supply touch
  disability and being left out: before it reads, the agent studies the National Center on Disability and
  Journalism's Disability Language Style Guide (ncdj.org/style-guide/) and checks that talk stays on the algorithm and
  the case cards' invented people, never on a classmate. Unit 3 has data about people: the agent checks every class
  question is about things and every case is invented for practice. Unit 5 has Native, Black and women's history
  (Mary Golda Ross, of the Cherokee Nation; Annie Easley; Jerry Lawson; the first programmers) and work change that can
  touch a family: before it reads, the agent studies a starting pack: the Cherokee Nation's own pages (cherokee.org,
  the page the agent records), the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian (americanindian.si.edu), NASA's
  Annie Easley biography (nasa.gov), and the NCDJ guide above. The agent widens the pack to the brief's five
  sources, two of them owned by the communities whose people the cards show, and records them. The course's checks get
  the answer-key agent's read. No unit is hands-on in the laboratory sense, so no safety agent read is planned beyond
  the Safety lines above, which the sensitivity read confirms are on each day.
