## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How close has the country come to its founding ideals?"
**Hook:** On day 7, place the Declaration's equality claim beside the 1777 Black petitioners' demand for freedom. What work can an ideal do while slavery remains legal?
**Supporting questions:** Compose and revise together: What did consent promise (day 7)? How does a constitution limit power (day 8)? Why disagree over ratification (day 9)? What changed, for whom (days 10 and 11)? How do people press for change (day 12)? How should conflicting values be weighed (day 13)?
**Closing claim:** Day 14 answers the compelling question through the reserved Massachusetts preamble, national provisions and Voting Rights Act, with a counterclaim and a limit on what legal text proves.
**Teaching:** Model author, audience, context and one interpretive lens on day 7. Teach all seven ideas through provisions on days 7 and 8. Model comparison and faction on day 9; students research independently on day 10 and present on day 11. Model a fair paraphrase before day 12's seminar, then step back. Day 9 also addresses RH.11-12.5, RH.11-12.6 and RH.11-12.8; argument instruction does not wait for English language arts.

**Day 11 individual presentation:** Research how a founding principle changed through one later document. Choose the Thirteenth, Fourteenth or Fifteenth Amendment, the Nineteenth Amendment or the Civil Rights Act of 1964. First teach a short timeline of all five; give each student a founding provision, the chosen later text and an accessible historical explanation with complete citations. Students choose evidence and formulate their own research question. Submit dated notes and a two-source explanation. Students present in groups of three, ninety seconds each, naming the principle, two sources and the change; the teacher circulates and records, and each student's written record is collected. Day 10 notes receive written feedback on context, source use and the claimed change before day 11. Allow fifteen minutes for first presentations, fifteen for comparing findings and twenty for questions and revision, including five to revise notes before presenting. ## 8. Tools and supplies across the unit

None. Supply printed excerpts, timeline, glossary, branches diagram, argument guides, hearing cards, observation sheets and pencils. Projection duplicates print. Read difficult passages aloud; allow rehearsal, large print and dictation.

## 9. Family note

Students will test founding ideals against documents and people's efforts to change civic life. They develop evidence-based positions without sharing family politics or experiences of discrimination.
