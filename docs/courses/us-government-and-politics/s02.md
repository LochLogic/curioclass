## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How close has the country come to its founding ideals?"
**Hook:** On day 7, place the Declaration's equality claim beside the 1777 Black petitioners' demand for freedom. What work can an ideal do while slavery remains legal?
**Supporting questions:** Compose and revise together: What did consent promise (day 7)? How does a constitution limit power (day 8)? Why disagree over ratification (day 9)? What changed, for whom (days 10 and 11)? How do people press for change (day 12)? How should conflicting values be weighed (day 13)?
**Closing claim:** Day 14 answers the compelling question through the reserved Massachusetts preamble, national provisions and Voting Rights Act, with a counterclaim and a limit on what legal text proves.
**Teaching:** Model author, audience, context and one interpretive lens on day 7. Teach all seven ideas through provisions on days 7 and 8. Model comparison on day 9; students research independently on day 10 and present on day 11. Model a fair paraphrase before day 12's seminar, then step back. Teach the seven ideas, faction and deliberation through examples. Day 9 also addresses RH.11-12.5, RH.11-12.6 and RH.11-12.8; argument instruction does not wait for English language arts.

**Day 11 individual presentation:** Research how a founding principle changed through one later document. Choose the Thirteenth, Fourteenth or Fifteenth Amendment, the Nineteenth Amendment or the Civil Rights Act of 1964. First teach a short timeline of all five; give each student a founding provision, the chosen later text and an accessible historical explanation with complete citations. Students choose evidence and formulate their own research question. Submit dated notes and a two-source explanation. Students present in groups of three, ninety seconds each, naming the principle, two sources and the change; the teacher circulates and records, and each student's written record is collected. Allow fifteen minutes for presentations, fifteen for comparing findings across groups and twenty for individual questions and responses. No student timer is displayed. Use original Article I, section 2, or Massachusetts Article I as founding choices, never the reserved provisions.

**Day 12 seminar:** Ten minutes: the reteach station for flagged students while the rest read a short passage from Douglass's 1852 Fifth of July speech. Twenty minutes, everyone: compare Douglass with the amendments and contemporary efforts, give the 1963 Birmingham context, and read a bounded selection of King's argument about nonviolent pressure and the costs of delay, with support. Ask: When does preserving public order obstruct equal civic standing? Students cite, paraphrase a differing interpretation, ask a question and answer a reason. Do not debate whether segregation was acceptable. Use fifteen minutes for the seminar and five for the exit ticket. In paired rounds within the seminar, each student contributes evidence and answers a reason. The teacher listens along a planned route and records every student's exchange; a written contribution read aloud is eligible. Do not mark an unobserved exchange secure.

Students choose their own positions; citizens and officials are procedural roles. Hold small hearings with rotating chairs. Reserve five minutes to read the case, twenty-five for hearings, fifteen for writing and five for the exit ticket.
