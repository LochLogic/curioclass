# Course frame: English Language Development, grades 9 to 12

Course `english-language-development-grades-9-to-12` (catalog id `eld-9-12`), grades 9, 10, 11 and 12, a band
course of four grade years. Draft frame, written 2026-11-25 on the shape of the English language development pilot
frame (grades K to 2); the scope and sequence sets the days later, one year at a time. Each year's part of this file
(its units, its end-of-unit assessments, its two course-level checks) is that grade year's frame, which meets
STANDARD.md section 2 ("one frame per grade year").

## The course in brief

- **Units.** 36: 9 in grade 9 (units 1 to 9), 9 in grade 10 (units 10 to 18), 9 in grade 11 (units 19 to 27), 9 in
  grade 12 (units 28 to 36). One unit per content row of the progression: each year has nine (LA Narrate, Inform
  and Argue; MA, SC and SS Explain and Argue), since cluster 9-12 gives ELD-MA, ELD-SC and ELD-SS no Narrate or
  Inform id. The four SI rows of each year ride inside the content unit whose key language use they share. Each unit
  is built around one key language use, in both modes. SI in every unit (bar item 1; STANDARD section 3): the unit's
  own SI row, or one SI use named as review in What students learn (units 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16, 18, 21,
  24, 25, 26, 27, 29, 30, 34, 35 and 36), each with its talk move on the host content and its look-for.
- **Days.** 640: 160 a year for four years. Each year: 154 days in units and 6 for two course-level checks (two
  check days and one respond day each). One lesson of 45 minutes a day, five a week, 32 weeks, the protected daily
  ELD period settled in STANDARD.md section 2 and APPROACH.md "Pacing" (120 hours a year). It is not ELA time and
  does not stand in for ELA (bar item 8). The 45 minutes hold the talk set-up, handing out and collecting cards,
  frames and drafts, and the exit ticket; there is no lab or field clean-up, since ELD runs no investigation. Each
  unit's days include its end-of-unit assessment (and, in units over 25 days, its mid-unit assessments): no unit is
  over 25 days, so none holds a mid-unit assessment. Every unit's end-of-unit assessment is two days (a sitting with
  unseen items: an interpretive part on a new grade-level text or task of the same kind, then a talk task heard by
  the teacher in a small seminar and a writing task, at each entry point), followed by one respond day inside the
  unit. Days follow the host windows first: each unit runs in the weeks its host is taught, and the two checks sit
  in fixed weeks, so a unit's length is mostly the gap between hosts. Within those windows the load sets the rest:
  an LA row (a host passage taken apart and an own paragraph or scene built, in both modes) weighs most, a content
  row next, an SI row least, since it rides on the same unit's talk. Five units are tight for what they hold, and
  say what they leave to the respond day: unit 8 (13 days, its host window closes into world history's last unit),
  unit 9 (13 days), unit 11 (14 days, two world history units in three weeks), unit 24 (13 days) and unit 32 (13
  days, before the grade 12 mid-year check). Each keeps its Fluency days to the forms it owns and leaves review
  forms to the respond day's groups; the scope and sequence may move days within a year, never across a check.
  Years run in school order: grade 9 is band days 1 to 160, grade 10 band days 161 to 320, grade 11 band days 321
  to 480, grade 12 band days 481 to 640; days below are given within the year (grade 10 day 84 is band day 244).
  These are course days: the school calendar (docs/courses/CALENDAR.md) places them on school days and weeks, and at
  five a week a year's course day d falls on school day d, in week d divided by 5, rounded up.
- **Standard.** English language development standards (WIDA 2020, data/standards/wida-2020.txt), ids and our own
  words only: no Language Expectation, proficiency level descriptor or other framework wording appears in any page
  (STANDARD.md section 1); public pages say "English language development standards" with the id. The band's 52
  rows own 18 ids: grade 12, the owner year of cluster 9-12 (the owner rule of STANDARD section 2), owns all 18
  (LA Narrate, Inform and Argue; MA, SC and SS Explain and Argue, each in both modes). Grades 9, 10 and 11 carry the
  same 18 ids (`~`, each unit naming its ids in What students learn with "carried; owned in grade 12"), and every
  year carries the four SI 4-12 ids (owned in grade 4, in the ELD grades 3 to 5 course). Ids in full: each grade 12
  unit names, in our words, every part of its owned ids' expectations (how a text or task is read in the
  interpretive mode, and each stage of the product in the expressive mode), and its end-of-unit task samples every
  part; those parts are our reading of the framework's 9-12 lists, checked against the progression rows and from
  memory, not yet against the printed pages (For the lead, item 2). Check set: bar part 10 items 1 to 14
  (ADOPTION-BAR-SUBJECTS), the PRIME 2020 criteria in our words (docs/RUBRIC-ELD.md, a planning reference only;
  Curio Class claims no alignment) and the California criteria map rows of STANDARD section 4. Values rule
  (DESK-ANSWERS 2026-11-12) holds.
- **Language through content.** Every unit names its host: the core unit of the same grade that the class studies
  that month (STANDARD section 3), cited with the host frame's own course days. The host column of the Units table
  (headed Host unit and key language uses, in place of C3) gives it. Where a row names two hosts, the unit uses the
  one that runs alongside it and retrieves the other where it was taught earlier; a host taught later in the year
  is not used, and the unit says so (units 1, 4, 10, 14, 19, 21, 22, 24 and 29 each leave one later host out).
  Some units run just after their host (unit 3 after Algebra I unit 2, unit 8 after World History I unit 9, unit 22
  after ELA grade 11 unit 3, unit 24 after US History I unit 5, unit 35 after Earth and Space Science unit 7) or a
  few days ahead (units 16, 27, 29 and 33, each with an "If later" line): these are the nearest fits that keep
  every row on a host of its own grade with two checks a year, and the scope and sequence may move days within a
  year without changing units. Each host is checked against the host row's skill, not its title. Content is the
  grade's own, made reachable with help; it is never swapped for an easier text. For an LA unit the host is the ELA
  unit it pairs with (STANDARD section 5): ELD takes one passage of the ELA text apart and names its language, and
  the reading and writing lessons stay in ELA.
- **Grade 12 hosts.** Grade 12 students take different math and science courses, or none (the rows name each). Each
  grade 12 MA and SC unit sets its shared model and interpretive part on one named host, read from that course's
  Curio Class pages with ELD's scaffolds, and each student's talk and writing tasks use the matching unit of the
  course the student takes: Statistics, Calculus or Precalculus for math (a student in none uses Algebra II from
  grade 11, retrieved); Environmental Science or Earth and Space Science for science (a student in none uses Physics
  from grade 11, retrieved). No unit assumes an elective was taken: the fallback row is always a core course the
  student had (lesson 32). US Government and Politics (fall) and Economics (spring) are each a half year; the SS
  units use whichever runs in their weeks.
- **Key language uses as the spine.** Each year builds all four uses its ids name, and each returns with a harder
  text or task (bar item 13): grade 9 narrate (1, 3), explain (2, 3, 8), argue (4, 5, 6, 9), inform (7, 9); grade
  10 inform (10, 17), explain (10, 11, 15), argue (12, 14, 16, 18), narrate (13, 18); grade 11 narrate (19, 27),
  inform (20, 25), explain (21, 23, 26), argue (22, 24, 25, 27); grade 12 narrate (28, 34), explain (29, 30, 31),
  inform (30, 33), argue (32, 34, 35, 36). Inside each year every key language use returns in a later unit, through
  the unit's own row or its SI review use, and what gets harder is named in that unit: grade 9 narrate 1 to 3 (an
  orientation day told to a peer and an adult grows to a problem-solving session recounted in order for an absent
  adult), inform 7 to 9 (a seminar summed up for one absent classmate grows to two historians' positions summed up
  side by side), explain 2 to 3 to 8, argue 4 to 9; grade 10 inform 10 to 17 (a lab-safety briefing grows to a
  synthesis of two sources with a question answered), narrate 13 to 18 (a club recount for a visitor grows to the
  research path told formally); grade 11 narrate 19 to 27 (an interview recount grows to a recount of how a class
  study was run, for a reader who must judge it), inform 20 to 25 (a call to an office grows to a written request
  for a study's data); grade 12 narrate 28 to 34 (an interview answer and email grow to the story of a test, told so
  its limits show), inform 30 to 33 (an orientation for younger students grows to a formal email that defines and
  asks). Explain and argue return in every half-year. Argue weighs most in high school, as the cluster's ids do: four
  of nine units each year.
- **Every unit, the same moves** (bar items 9 to 12; APPROACH, "Across the unit"): the key language use taught
  openly from a model taken apart, built with the teacher, then made alone; forms taught directly at the text, the
  sentence and the word (each form named with its job); structured talk every day with frames, before any writing;
  both modes (interpretive: listening, reading and viewing the host text or task; expressive: speaking, writing and
  representing in a labeled model or diagram); one change of audience or purpose; one moment where students name a
  form and its job and judge their own language on a self-check page they keep; the host-teacher note (bar item 14:
  the content used and the language taught, two lines).
- **The talk routine.** One named routine a year, the same every week: grade 9 "point, build, ask" (make a point,
  build on a partner's, ask one question), grade 10 "point, source, sum up" (a point, the source behind it, a
  summary of where the group agrees), grade 11 "point, objection, answer" (a point, a partner raises an objection,
  answer it), grade 12 "propose, concede, hold" (a proposal, a fair point conceded, the main claim held). Each grows
  from the one before, so a class that never had the earlier year learns it in that year's first unit, week 1.
- **Entry points.** Three, by proficiency (STANDARD section 2): levels 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6. Every entry point
  does the same task on the same content at the same thinking demand; levels change the help, never the task (bar
  item 2). Help is withdrawn on the progression's default days: for 5 and 6 the frame comes off after the first
  application day; for 3 and 4 before the end-of-unit writing or talk task; 1 and 2 keep the word bank and lose the
  cloze frame before the check. Each year's rows take off one more frame for levels 3 and 4 (the row notes name
  it), so in grade 12, the owner year, levels 3 and 4 write from the model alone. A student moves to the next entry
  point when the teacher's paper sheet shows the unit's look-fors "seen" in the end-of-unit assessment and in one
  course-level check, or on the school's own progress check (bar item 7). Placement and exit use the school's
  proficiency score; Curio Class gives no placement test and keeps no scores. Every unit has notes for newcomers,
  long-term English learners (many high school English learners are long-term: the note names academic register,
  which they often lack beside fluent social English) and English learners with disabilities, and a line on telling
  a language need from a learning need (APPROACH, "Sensitive ground": a gap in English is never read as a
  disability).
- **Newcomer strand.** A 20-day on-ramp beside each year, outside its 160 days (STANDARD section 2; Desk
  q-fix-eld-1): every year works the four SI 4-12 ids and WIDA.ELD-LA.9-12.Inform only, since cluster 9-12 has no
  MA, SC or SS Inform id. Its content is the school day itself (a schedule, a locker, asking a teacher for help, a
  counselor's office) and the current host unit's pictures and diagrams. A newcomer arriving at any point takes it,
  then joins the class's current unit at entry points 1 and 2. A newcomer in grades 9 to 12 who needs early reading
  uses the ELA K to 2 foundational rows, with a note (STANDARD section 5). The strand is written with the year's
  scope and sequence and gets its own sensitivity read before use.
- **Day kinds.** As docs/GLOSSARY.md (row "teaching day") and APPROACH.md "Kinds of day in ELD": Understanding is
  the first teaching of a form, a text's structure or a model (the form shown in the host text, named, explained);
  Fluency practices forms already taught (oral rehearsal with the frame, sentence combining and taking apart, word
  work and cognate sorts) and drafts nothing; Application uses the forms in talk and then in writing on the content
  (every day that drafts or revises is Application); Check is an end-of-unit assessment or course-level check day;
  Respond reteaches the forms the sheet shows "not yet". Fluency is a smaller share in high school (about a fifth of
  teaching days) than in K to 2, since the weight moves to written academic register; oral rehearsal before writing
  stays every day.
- **Readiness and retrieval.** Every row grade 9 assumes was taught in grade 8, in English Language Development,
  grades 6 to 8 (Pending(docs/courses/english-language-development-grades-6-to-8/frame.md): that frame's grade 8 unit numbers are cited here), and every row grades 10, 11 and 12 assume was taught the year
  before in this band; so each opens as a retrieval task inside a day of the unit that first needs it (day 2, after
  day 1's hook), in the same words as sequence-write rule 2; no unit needs a readiness check. The count starts from
  the student's own grade year. Many high school English learners enter the program in grade 9 or later: a student
  who joins without the earlier year meets the retrieval task at entry points 1 and 2 with the earlier year's model
  on the card, and a newcomer takes the strand first. A host's earlier unit is retrieved the same way (named in
  each unit). A mixed-grade group uses the year of its youngest grade, and the teacher page says so (STANDARD
  section 2).
- **Base text.** None of its own (docs/reviews/eld-open-material-2026-11-16.md: no open ELD course exists). Texts are
  the host units' own: ELA grades 9 to 12's public-domain anchors and US government works, credited through the ELA
  courses, and the math, science and social studies units' Curio Class pages, data sets and source sets. ELD adapts
  EL Education's Language Dive routine (a sentence from the text taken apart and rebuilt; EL Education K-5 Language
  Arts, 2017 edition, CC BY 4.0; the 2025 edition is CC BY-NC and is not used), credited once a day where used.
  Curio Class writes the scaffolds (word banks, the frames per level, the model paragraph or scene each unit builds
  with the teacher, labeled diagrams) and each end-of-unit assessment's new content of the same kind, named per
  unit. No source map: ELD places no base units.
- **Paper path.** Every lesson runs on paper, cards and talk. Any tool a unit names (a model-sentence replay tool:
  hear and replay the day's model sentence; a word deck with pictures; audio of the host passage) has a paper path
  beside it (the teacher reads the model or passage aloud twice, the cards on the table) and needs no student
  account. No tool records a student's voice or writing, scores pronunciation or places a student at a level
  (APPROACH, "What a tool may and may not do").
- **Devices.** None per student. One shared display is optional for the model passage and the replay tool. No owned
  code needs technology: "representing" is a labeled diagram, chart or claim chart on paper, so a paper-path class
  meets every id in full.
- **Costs.** Printing only: word banks, frames and the four model sheets on card stock, reused across a year, about
  $4 a unit for a class of 30 (a planning figure, not a quote); no supplies are bought.
- **Home languages and family notes.** A help, never a ranking (bar item 6): talk or plan in the home language
  before English, cognates named (and the false friends the rows name), and teacher notes on how Spanish does the
  same thing (other languages when a reader exists, APPROACH "Home languages"). No student translates for another,
  for the class or for a family. Family notes are in English and Spanish. A student 18 or over gets the family note
  themselves and makes their own leave-out or swap choice (AGENTS rule; Desk q-prog-sel-1); the school's own rules
  still apply.
- **Safety.** ELD runs no investigation and no field work. Students talk and write from the host's own records,
  data sets, diagrams and photographs; ELD repeats no host test, and nothing is heated, mixed, tasted or shone in
  eyes. Where a host unit's data came from a lab, the host frame's safety sheet and safety read cover it.
- **Grade 12 calendar.** The last weeks hold teaching: unit 36 runs days 137 to 157 and the year-end check sits in
  week 32 (days 158 to 160), the finals week. If seniors leave early, the year-end check and its respond day move to
  the seniors' last three days, and unit 36 folds up to 5 days, in this order, never its end-of-unit assessment or
  respond day: its Fluency days on review forms (2), the second change-of-audience day (1), and the second
  independent application day (2). A school whose seniors leave more than 5 days early places the 160 course days
  so they end on the seniors' last day (CALENDAR.md), rather than cutting the unit (For the lead, item 5).
- **Sensitivity reads.** Every unit gets the fresh sensitivity agent's read (docs/pipeline/briefs/sensitivity-read.md,
  kind `sensitivity`, community: multilingual learners and immigrant families), and every Spanish note and family
  note gets a `spanish` read (APPROACH, "Sensitive ground", Cory 2026-11-21). Before reading, the agent studies the
  APPROACH page's Sensitive ground rules, the federal English learner toolkit (US Department of Education, Office of
  English Language Acquisition, free online, https://www.ncela.ed.gov/educator-support/toolkits/english-learner-toolkit),
  the National Academies 2017 report on children and youth learning English (free to read online), and, for units
  whose host is about war, conquest or loss, the National Child Traumatic Stress Network's free guide for educators
  (https://www.nctsn.org/resources/child-trauma-toolkit-educators). Flagged units name more below: unit 1 (death and
  loss in suspense stories), units 8 and 11 (conquest, migration and colonial rule), unit 13 (stories of colonial
  rule), unit 18 (war and its peace, retrieved), unit 23 (slavery and the coming of the Civil War), unit 24
  (Reconstruction and its overthrow), units 1, 13, 19, 28 and 36 (own-story and own-plan prompts), units 19 to 23
  and 28 to 31 (applicant and plan prompts: invented applicants allowed), unit 32 (policy debate). The newcomer strand
  and all family notes get the read before use. No outside human reader is planned (Cory, 2026-11-14); the course's
  checks get the answer-key agent's read.

### Where the threads end

Grade 12 is the last ELD year. Every content thread ends in the owner year's unit (28 to 36), checked by a task the
student does alone at levels 3 and 4 from the model with no sentence frame (the row notes' grade 12 withdrawal).
The four SI threads end here too, carried since their owner year, grade 4. The school's own exit score, not this
course, decides when a student leaves English learner status.

weeks of each grade as CALENDAR.md shows them today: grade 9 weeks 9, 10, 12, 13, 16, 18, 19, 20 and 22; grade 10
weeks 8, 9, 10, 11, 18, 19, 20 and 21; grade 11 weeks 10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 19, 22, 23 and 24; grade 12 weeks 8, 9,
10, 11, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23, 24, 25 and 30 (week 32 is every course's finals week). So the mid-year checks
sit in week 17 in grades 9, 10 and 11 and week 16 in grade 12 (week 17 holds grade 12's ELA, Calculus and US History
II checks), and every year-end check sits in week 32. That is why each year's last unit (9, 18, 27 and 36) ends on
day 157 with its own end-of-unit assessment and the year-end check follows it; the check draws its items from the
earlier units, so the cost is stated plainly: the ids of units 9 (SS Argue), 18 (SS Argue), 27 (MA Argue) and 36
(LA Argue, owner year) are checked in their year by their end-of-unit assessment only; grade 9, 10 and 11's are
checked again in the next year's checks through the matching unit, and unit 36's record goes to the school's team.
Owned rows every check samples: all others. `frame.py calendar` lists these weeks beside other after-core checks;
the weeks stay until the lead's calendar pass across the after-core frames.

**How a check runs in 45 minutes.** The interpretive parts (the teacher reads the new text aloud once, students
read it again themselves) and the writing task go to the whole class at once; the talk task is heard in small
seminars of three, about six minutes a group with the teacher listening, while the others write, over the two days.
Each student is heard once in a check. The respond day groups students by form and by entry point (at most four
groups; the others rehearse in pairs from the frame). No scores are stored; the teacher's paper sheet stays with
the teacher (promise 2).
