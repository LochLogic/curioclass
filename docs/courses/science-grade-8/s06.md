## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How could things living today be connected to the things that lived long ago?"
**Hook, day 132:** Compare original giant fossil penguin and living penguin diagrams. Notice shared flippers and different proportions, sketch possible connections and hear the compelling question.
**Closing explanation, day 155:** Explain possible penguin relationships using fossil order, anatomy and selection evidence. Inherited variation and unequal reproductive success can change populations across generations; the fossil need not be a direct ancestor. Embryo evidence extends the explanation on day 156.
**Teaching:** Model comparison on day 138; students select and challenge evidence. Revisit questions after each lesson set.

- **MS-LS4-1:** Use fossil data to find patterns of when life forms existed, varied, died out and changed over Earth's history, assuming natural laws have always worked the same way. Emphasis: patterns in how complex body structures became and the time order in which fossils show up in rock layers. Limit: Leave out names of single species and of geologic eras.
- **MS-LS4-2:** Use science ideas to explain body likenesses and differences among living organisms and fossils, and infer how they are related. Emphasis: relationships judged from the overall look of body structures.
- **MS-LS4-4:** Explain from evidence how differences in inherited traits make some individuals more likely to survive and reproduce in a given environment. Emphasis: explanations that use simple statements of probability and proportional reasoning.
- **MS-LS4-6:** Use math to back up explanations of how natural selection can make traits more or less common in a population over time. Emphasis: mathematical models, probability and proportional reasoning about population trends. Limit: Leave out Hardy-Weinberg calculations.
- **MS-LS1-4:** Argue from evidence and reasoning how typical animal behaviors and special plant parts raise the chance that animals and plants reproduce.
- **MS-LS4-3:** Compare pictures of embryos of several species to find patterns that show relationships hidden in the adult bodies. Emphasis: inferring general relatedness from diagrams or pictures of whole embryos. Limit: Comparisons use only the overall look of body structures during development.

**M1 evidence, invented for practice:** Three well-sampled, undisturbed marine layers span 30, 20 and 10 million years ago. At 30, form R has a plain shell and form S has a ribbed shell. At 20, R and S remain and form T has a coiled shell. At 10, S and T remain and form U has a plain shell with a hinge; R is absent. Later extensive sampling finds no R, including a supplied present-day survey. Analyze what lived when, the count of forms, R's possible extinction, layer order and structural change. Eight points: existence and order, 2; diversity from two to three forms, 2; R's last occurrence and probable extinction with sampling caution, 2; changing structures under the same preservation processes, 2. Absence from one layer alone cannot prove extinction; the missing later samples and the survey make extinction probable, not certain. Plain and ribbed shells coexist; the record does not show a universal complexity ladder.

**M2 evidence, invented for practice:** Original diagrams show fossil swimmer A and living walker B sharing one upper limb bone, two lower bones and five jointed digits. A has broad digits and B slender digits. Living swimmer C has a supporting rod and fin rays, without that joint pattern. Explain A and B's similarities and differences, infer a relationship relative to C, and state what the fossil cannot establish. Six points: cite shared arrangement and contrasting proportions, 2; explain inherited structures modified in different lineages rather than equating swimming with kinship, 2; infer supported ancestry without claiming A is B's direct ancestor, 2. Diagrams are hypothetical anatomical evidence, not named species.

**T1 evidence, invented for practice:** Four undisturbed plant-bearing layers run oldest to youngest. Layer 1 contains V, with forked stems and no leaves, and W, with small unbranched leaves. Layer 2 contains W alone. Layer 3 contains V, W and X, which has broad veined leaves. Layer 4 contains W, X and Y, which has narrow veined leaves. Later extensive samples and a supplied modern survey find W, X and Y but no V. Explain existence, diversity, disappearances and structural change using the class model. Four points: ordered occurrences and diversity of two, one, three, three forms, 1; V reappears after its first gap, so that gap was not extinction, while repeated later absences support probable extinction, 1; differing leaf and stem structures coexist without establishing a particular branching history or universal complexity ladder, 1; consistent preservation processes support interpretation with sampling caution, 1.

**T2 evidence, invented for practice:** Original diagrams show fossil N and living O with three separate lower-jaw bones on each side, the same jaw-joint position, and matching shapes of vertebral projections. N has short pointed teeth; O has broad crushing teeth. Living Q has the same pointed tooth function as N but one lower-jaw bone per side, a different joint position and different vertebral projections. Explain whether an N-O or N-Q grouping is better supported. Four points: shared jaw and vertebral arrangements, 1; tooth and jaw differences, 1; evidence-linked common ancestry supporting N-O over shared tooth function, 1; these limited structures and fossil age cannot establish direct ancestry, 1.

**T3 evidence, invented for practice:** A stream-living beetle population begins with 40 long-legged and 40 short-legged adults. Leg length is inherited in the supplied family observations. After a flood, 30 long-legged and 10 short-legged adults survive the fast current. In this model, survivors reproduce asexually and offspring copy parental leg length, with no mutation or migration. Each survivor has the same expected number of surviving offspring. Explain why long legs may become more common in descendants, calculate survival probabilities and the expected long-legged share among offspring under these assumptions, and explain uncertainty. Eight points: pre-existing inherited variation, 2; survival probabilities 30/40 = 0.75 and 10/40 = 0.25 tied to the environment, 2; differential reproduction leading to an expected 30/40 = 75 percent long-legged descendants, 2; chance and model assumptions mean no guaranteed individual outcome or exact observed ratio, 2. Do not infer that beetles grew longer legs or that the flood produced directed mutations.
