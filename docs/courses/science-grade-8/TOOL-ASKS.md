# Tool asks: Science, Grade 8

## Collisions with varied mass and speed

Requested by [unit 1](s01.md), field 8. Key tool, spec only; does not exist yet. Days 13 and 14 each use ten minutes to show why. It represents collisions too brief or energetic to reproduce safely in class.

1. Let learners vary mass while fixing speed, then speed while fixing mass. State kilograms, metres per second and joules on every relevant display.
2. Show separate kinetic-energy graphs with labeled axes, equal input intervals and the fixed variable stated. Display idealized calculated data as model output, not measurements. Use proportional mass and square-speed relationships without requiring a formula derivation or function notation.
3. Slow the representation of contact. Put equal opposing contact-force arrows on separate objects. Distinguish force from energy and deformation from damage. Do not imply that equal forces cause equal motion changes or equal damage.
4. Require a prediction, a changed input, recorded output and a revised explanation. Keep student choices visible on a printable record. Provide keyboard control, direct numeric entry and non-color labels.
5. Supply equivalent printed model-output tables and graph grids. Paper users still choose a controlled comparison and justify it. Say plainly that supplied records give weaker evidence of conducting an investigation.
6. Preserve unit 1's assessment number sets and contexts for independent checks. The teacher judges explanations; the tool does not diagnose understanding or make injury-protection claims.

The physical impact tests on days 20 to 24 also need separately verified calibrated force-time records. An ordinary kitchen scale or slow force scale cannot supply a reliable collision peak. Their absence is an evidence gap, not a reason to label a proxy as force.

## Selection over generations

Requested by [unit 6](s06.md), field 8. Key tool, spec only; does not exist. The ideal instrument lets students test how inherited variation and different survival and reproductive probabilities change population proportions. This cannot be reproduced across many generations in a classroom period.

1. Day 148 uses fifteen minutes to show why: predict a trait proportion, choose one environmental condition and hold the initial population and inheritance rules constant. Within this block, use ten minutes to write and execute an ordered update: read starting counts, apply survival probabilities, generate offspring under stated inheritance rules, total the next generation, calculate each proportion, and repeat. Label input, output, assumptions and chance outcomes. The algorithm does not make organisms change traits because they need them.
2. Day 149 uses twenty minutes to show why: repeat the chosen comparison, record counts and totals by generation, and plot proportions. Keep both numerator and denominator visible, with generation and proportion axes labeled. Display runs separately before combining results. Explain that different random outcomes do not by themselves disprove the mechanism.
3. Day 150 uses ten minutes to show why: compare predator conditions and resource conditions in separate controlled investigations. Retain both base lesson 9's predation comparison and lesson 10's food-distribution comparison. Let the learner change one cause, predict, run and explain which evidence supports the prediction. A trait can be advantageous in one environment and disadvantageous in another.
4. Day 154 uses five minutes to show why: test a claim against an already recorded run while revising the penguin model. Label the simulation as a simplified mechanism, not a reconstruction of a particular penguin ancestor. Reserve the remainder of the day's argument block for feedback-led revision of the retained numerical product.
5. Provide printable run records with counts, denominators, assumptions, graphs and space for a revised explanation. A one-projector room chooses the conditions together, then each learner records a prediction and interpretation. A paper-only room chooses among equivalent supplied comparisons; supplied outputs are weaker evidence of generating a run. No account or automated judgment of open writing.
6. Provide keyboard controls, direct numerical entry, non-color labels and plain descriptions of every visual change. Offer deterministic expected-value examples separately from random runs. State whether reproduction is asexual or sexual and what inheritance rule the model assumes. Do not silently equate parent and offspring trait proportions under an unspecified sexual inheritance model.
7. Keep every unit assessment and exit-ticket case and number set out of the tool's teaching examples. The seed-coat assessment is a hypothetical expected-value model; the tool must label model output separately from empirical observations. No living bacteria, cultures, specimens or health experiments are used.
