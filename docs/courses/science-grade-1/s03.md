## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Are the little trees under our big maple like it? Exactly like it?"
- **Hook, day 22.** Show the dated schoolyard seedling photograph beside the pressed leaves saved in unit 2. Ask the compelling question before offering a claim. Location alone does not establish parentage. Use M22's labelled model to explore the pattern; leave the schoolyard relationship unresolved without a local seed record.
- **Closing, day 25.** Dictate a plant-and-animal explanation from the leaf strip and animal charts: shared features form a pattern, while observed differences show individuals are not exact copies. Return to the schoolyard question without claiming an undocumented parent.
- **Teaching moves.** Model pointing from one observed feature to a claim on day 22. On days 23 to 25, ask children which observation supports or changes their account; let partners challenge it before offering language.

<!-- skel -->
- **1-LS3-1:** Observe and explain, with evidence, that young plants and animals look a lot like their parents but are not exact copies. Limit: Leaves out how traits are inherited, animals that change form as they grow (metamorphosis), and hybrids.
<!-- /skel -->

- **Question board.** Group notices as shared parts, differences and relationships. Day 22 asks which leaf features match, raising whether animals show this pattern. Day 23 tests dogs, raising whether another animal family agrees. Day 24 tests cats, raising whether all members of one kind look identical. Day 25 compares additional adults and young of each kind and closes the anchor. Day 26 transfers to unfamiliar oak and rabbit family diagrams.
- **Section 3.1.** The standard asks children to "Make observations" and "construct an evidence-based account". Observe features to construct an explanation of resemblance and variation, using repeated similarities and differences as pattern evidence. The teacher page distinguishes visible resemblance from proof of parentage.
- **Limits.** No genes, inheritance mechanism, trait prediction, hybrids, metamorphosis or comparisons involving children and relatives. Do not explain a difference's cause from appearance alone. An individual leaf is evidence about its plant, not a young plant itself.
- **Carried.** Keep unit 2's parts chart as vocabulary support; retain its design cycle and shape-and-job model for later units. Day 22 begins a comparison model with pictured organisms, shared-feature links and difference labels. Day 23 revises it with dog evidence; day 24 adds cat counterexamples to exact copying. Day 25 adds variation among adults and among young, reaching consensus. Children use this model to organize day 26's new observations. Patterns and claim-evidence-reasoning remain available, never withdrawn.

**Assessment drawing checks.** O26 must show each whole plant with its labelled blade and seed path; R26 must show all four rabbits with their specified markings. These masters fix every feature used in the keys and samples; do not substitute a different image at printing.

**Look-fors and points.** Each case has six one-point components: use a visible feature to support parent-young resemblance; use a visible difference to reject exact copying; compare young of the same kind using a shared feature and a difference; do the same for adults; distinguish the contrasting kind using a visible feature; connect the observed pattern to an alike-but-not-exactly account. The plant difference must include the directly ordered leaves. Each point requires an observation used in reasoning, not naming an organism. Thus 12 of 12 points require constructing an account with LS3.A or LS3.B and patterns on a new case. All components are taught on days 22 to 25.

**Samples, invented for practice.** These illustrate the scoring, not uncollected results. Plant meets: "These oak leaves share rounded lobes. OY1 is shortest, then OY2, then OP. OY2 is wider than OY1. OP and OA share lobes, but OA is wider. ON has needles. The shared features and differences show young resemble their parent without being exact copies." Does not meet: "They are exact copies because both have leaves" (A). Animal meets: "The rabbits share long ears and fur. RP has white paws; RY1 has a white chest and RY2 a white muzzle. RA has a white ear tip, which RP does not have. RF has fins. These observations show resemblance and differences, not identical copies." Does not meet: "A different patch means it cannot be a rabbit" (B). Equivalent observations from O26 and R26 count.

## U7. Talk and writing

Day 22: "What did you observe?" Day 24: "Does that difference erase the similarities?" Day 25: "Which evidence changed our model?" Dictate the closing account; judge whether it links observed similarities and differences to a claim about both plants and animals.

## U8. Practice that returns

Retrieve the parts chart on day 23 and leaf alignment on day 25. Repeated comparisons improve precision, without time pressure.

## U9. Supports and extension

Teach alike, different, young, parent, kind and feature with pictures. Offer "Alike in __; different in __" for dictation. Adults read everything. Use large prints, raised outlines and neutral descriptions; keep the reasoning demand. Extend by testing whether another visible feature supports the same claim.

## U10. Two rooms

One child compares the same sets with an adult, who scribes without supplying comparisons. One shared display is optional; every record and picture prints. On day 27 return feedback before revision and retain the final account for later units.
