## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How does a tree stay standing in a storm, and can we copy it to keep our tower from tipping?"
- **Hook, day 10.** Show the standing background trees in the storm photograph beside a paper tower tipping under gentle teacher fanning. Ask the compelling question before showing roots.
- **Closing, day 18.** Dictate how spreading supports helped, using root observations and tests. Roots anchor trees in soil; cardboard feet only model spreading support. Some trees fall in storms, as the full photograph shows.
- **Teaching moves.** Model an evidence arrow on day 11. Children propose shapes on day 16 and justify changes with tests on day 18.

**Transfer packet, first opened day 20.** The problem is headed "invented for practice": a paper museum object must stay untouched when a broad cardboard visitor marker brushes the outside of its cover. The cover must stand over the object, leave a visible gap above it, and have one open side for viewing. Use only paper, cardboard and tape; no weights, sharp pieces or wearable designs. Children slowly slide the broad marker along the table toward the cover, stopping on contact; an adult supervises. Record whether it touches the protected object. Keep the same starting mark and direction; no striking or dropping. Recheck only a changed cover on day 21. This problem and its evidence drawings were not used on days 10 to 19. Day 20's plant card uses held-back, dated photographs of a different class shoot before and after a side light changes position. An adult must collect this separate sequence before the unit opens, keeping the plant position fixed and documenting the light change; retain originals and times. It must show an actual bend, never an altered or invented outcome. This archive is not yet on file. The tortoise card is an original diagram headed "invented for practice", showing sensing movement and withdrawing into its shell. Captions describe the supplied evidence without labelling the job: roots in water, leaves raised on stems, the shoot before and after light changes, and the tortoise before and after movement nearby. Children infer jobs and responses. They contain no cover design.

**Schedule for 30 children.** Day 20: 2 minutes to launch, 14 for the evidence account and initial design, 12 for sketching, making and one criterion check, 2 to collect and clean. Teacher circulation records each child's contribution and the marker test, without scoring or reteaching. Children evaluate their own cover against the three criteria, then improve one feature if needed. After class, allow 30 minutes to write one specific evidence comment and one model comment on each child's work, each tied to a picture cue and a shared spoken prompt. Day 21 uses the allowed 35 minutes: 2 to launch, 5 to read feedback, revise records and adjust existing covers, six 3-minute linked-account turns for five children each, then 10 for response including cleanup. The two item rows share the 18 conference minutes, not two rounds. Each child has about 36 seconds, with a prepared pointing sequence. Written marks and the product supply detail; ask only about unresolved reasoning. A trial already documented on day 20 is not repeated. Children waiting match familiar cards independently. No new build or teaching occurs during the observation window.

**Scoring.** Score day 20 work only after day 21 revision and the linked account. Award one point for each of six design components: interpret plant part-job evidence, interpret animal part-job evidence, interpret the plant response sequence, interpret animal sensing-response evidence, construct a solution copying the protective part, and use the recorded check to judge the cover against the criteria. For each of the first four points, the child must use the evidence to explain a need or response, not merely name a part. Award four model points: represent the object and its problem, show the relevant cover shape, connect shape to protection and viewing, and communicate these links by pointing through the sketch. The first four points use observations to construct accounts with LS1.A and LS1.D; the next two use designing solutions with LS1.A; the last four use an evidence-grounded model with ETS1.B. Structure and function ties the design points to the new problem; the unfamiliar organisms supply new evidence cases for the first four points: 10 of 10 points need a practice with an idea on a new case. The class consensus chart remains available, without a model answer for this problem.

**Samples.** Design meets: "The roots get water. The stem holds leaves up where they receive light. The shoot bent toward the changed light. The shell protects the tortoise; it saw movement and pulled in. My arch copies the shell to protect this object. Our marker touched the cover, not the object; the gap stayed and the open side let us see inside." Component points, in scoring order: water, stem support and leaf light together; shell protection; changed-light bend; seeing movement then withdrawing; shell-inspired construction; the recorded contact, clearance and viewing check. Does not meet: "Plants cannot respond" (B), with no part evidence or copied solution. Model meets: "These sides hold this curved roof above the object. This opening lets a visitor see inside." Does not meet: "I drew a tortoise on the roof so it works" (D), with no shape-to-job account. Other correct safe designs count when evidence and criteria support them.

**Access.** Read every prompt aloud; children mark pictures on paper. Use picture cues for individual written feedback. Read the shared component prompts aloud together while children follow their indicated cues, then revise; clarify a cue without giving an answer. Offer enlarged or tactile outlines, adapted marking and dictated labels. An adult may cut to the child's indicated shape; the child chooses, assembles and explains it. Each child has an identifiable product. No independent reading, handwriting speed or personal hearing test is scored. Unfinished revision remains unscored until completed; do not score unrevised work for convenience.
