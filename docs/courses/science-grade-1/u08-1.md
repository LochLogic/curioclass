- **1-ESS1-2, this section's share.** The whole standard: "Observe at different times of year and connect how much daylight there is to the season." "Observe at different times of year": dated strips and reports added across the school year and read together (days 9, 62, 63), then unfamiliar records (days 64, 65). "Connect how much daylight there is to the season": sort the year's records into fall, winter and spring (day 62), then compare light parts, winter against fall and against spring (days 63 to 65).
- **Limits that bite here.** Only more or less daylight is compared. Children never count hours, and there are no fractions, rotation, tilt, orbits or cause of seasons. A strip shows calculated daylight, sunrise to sunset, not sunshine through cloud. Use local season words. Winter being shortest is a finding for our place and the sampled days, not a rule for everywhere. Adults handle clock changes; a clock change does not add daylight. Never look at the sun. Children are asked about evenings only as "light or dark", and family reports are optional and never graded.
- **Connections.** Math 1.MD.C.4, up to three groups (day 62, retrieval only). Unit 1, the dated sky-watch archive and predicting from earlier looks (days 9, 63). Unit 3, strips laid with bases together and tips showing longer (day 63). Unit 7, the evidence habit (days 62, 63, 65). Kindergarten weather watch (days 9, 62).
- **What the teacher page must explain.** A day strip is one bar for a whole day, the same length for every date. Its light part runs from a sunrise picture to a sunset picture; the rest is dark. Strips are drawn by adults from the federal sunrise and sunset table in standard time (unit field 10), so they are calculated records, not our own sightings. An evening report is one moment: at 5:30 the evening is light in fall and spring and dark in midwinter, which answers the compelling question for our place. A fall strip from November is shorter than one from October and still fall; the class model says winter is shortest and the question board keeps "why do the light parts shrink?" open, since the cause is not taught.

Day 63: the strip key (light part between its two pictures), predicting from earlier looks, bases together and tips showing longer. Day 64: none, the check samples earlier days only. Day 65: the strip key and the day 63 account. Each monthly look retrieves the dated square.

## 9. Supports and extension

- **English learners.** Words, each with a picture: daylight, light part, dark part, sunrise, sunset, fall, winter, spring, longer, shorter. Frame: "Winter has less daylight than fall; these light parts show it." Pointing and home-language words are accepted.
- **Access.** Strips use high-contrast shading and raised glue lines at sunrise and sunset. A child who cannot see them has them described neutrally (shading, dates, labels) with no comparison given. A child who cannot point answers by eye gaze, a cue card or dictation.
- **Check accommodations.** Every prompt is read aloud. Accept pointing, dictation and enlarged strips. A re-reading is marked "shown with help".
- **Reading load.** None. Each student page stays under 100 words; everything else is read aloud by the teacher.
- **Extension, same standard.** A child says why two evening reports that both say "light" cannot show that two days are equally long.

## 10. Two rooms

- **Teaching one child.** Day 9: look at the strip, trace the light part with a finger, color two guesses. Monthly: add the new strip and say longer, shorter or about the same. Day 62: sort the dated strips under fall, winter and spring. Day 63: slide the strips until the sunrise pictures meet and dictate which season has least daylight. Day 64: three new strips; the adult marks the child's pointing and gives no hint. Day 65, the last day of the unit: fix one picture, try three fresh strips and dictate the answer. The adult writes the child's words and does not correct.
- **One projector.** Show strips large; every child marks their own paper. On day 64 the turns run in order at one table.
