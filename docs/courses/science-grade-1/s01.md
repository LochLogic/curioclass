## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "Why is our shadow stick's shadow on one side in the morning and the other side after lunch?"
- **Hook, day 1.** Compare the fixed pencil's live shadow with its dated morning tracing. Ask the compelling question before offering an explanation. Use the schoolyard; windows may block later sunlight.
- **Closing explanation, day 6.** Children dictate that changing apparent sun position accompanies changing shadow direction, using repeated fixed-stick marks. Sunlight comes from the side opposite the shadow. Moon sequences show another apparent path; clear-sky records distinguish daytime and nighttime star visibility.
- **Teaching moves.** Model dating one mark on day 1. Children choose landmarks and record day 2 times; adults read clocks. Its three two-minute looks and 24-minute lesson total 30 minutes. Later, ask which observation supports each prediction rather than supply it.

- **Question board.** Group notices as shadow changes, sky paths and visibility. Where will the next shadow fall (day 2)? Does it repeat (day 3)? Does the moon follow a path too (day 4)? What else shows at night (day 5)? Will these patterns hold elsewhere (day 6)? Day 7 transfers to an unfamiliar hillside observation station.
- **Limits.** No rotation, orbits, moon phases, constellation changes, compass bearings or measured shadow lengths. Predict from supplied observations, never assume the moon appears at the same clock time daily. Other stars remain present when unseen; night visibility needs a clear, sufficiently dark sky. No engineering expectation is claimed.
- **Carried: patterns.** Day 1 draws the stick and first shadow. Day 2 adds ordered marks from the fixed stick; day 3 adds repeated evidence. Day 4 adds the moon's apparent path. Day 5 revises visibility with daytime moon evidence. Day 6 reaches consensus from the other-site records: repeated observations support predictions. Keep this chart available on day 7.

**Day 7 schedule, 30 children:** three minutes to launch, six minutes for paper responses, six three-minute groups of five for individual pointing and talk, three minutes to collect. Each item uses two paper minutes and six minutes distributed across the group turns, totaling 24 minutes. No product from teaching days contributes points. Waiting children use familiar unscored cards independently. Teacher records each child's marks, path drawing and evidence, not a group answer. Day 7 collects work without scores, feedback or reteaching. Written feedback follows after class; revision and scoring follow on day 8.

**Daily evidence and section exits.** Days 1 to 6 leave an individual picture mark and notebook drawing tied to that day's goal; hear a rotating group of about six reasons daily. Day 1 records the puzzling change and a question; day 2 records three marks and a prediction; day 3 compares dates; day 4 traces a moon path; day 5 compares visibility; day 6 tests each predicted pattern. These are formative records, not scores. Day 4's three-minute exit uses a held-back moon sequence and shadow record: "What repeating paths do these records show, and where would each next mark go?" Key: each continues its observed path. It tests section 1.1, with B and A for stationary paths. Day 8's three-minute exit, inside its ten-minute fresh-prediction block, shows unused sun, daytime moon and star records: "Which pattern pictures fit these records, and what pictures should come next?" Key: both apparent paths continue; other stars appear in the clear dark-night record. Use A to D only where the response matches. Every child marks paper as prompts are read aloud; hear six rotating reasons. No exit reuses that day's worked case. Section plans must supply the held-back masters before use.

**Scoring after day 8 revision.** On day 7 children first place time-labelled pictures in the guided chart. Use day and night headings for visibility; include sun absent at night and moon present on daytime and nighttime records. Each item earns three points: describes the pattern, predicts from it, and identifies evidence. For T, the description point also requires distinguishing daytime nonvisibility from nonexistence; an explicit disappearance claim loses that point (D). All nine points require analyzing observations or schematic records with ESS1.A and patterns on new cases, exceeding 80 percent. No earlier notebook work is scored. Look for movement across a fixed view rather than a recited direction word. Hear one linked reason per child, covering the three records by pointing as needed. Item S meets: "It crossed from that edge toward this edge; these two pictures show it. Next it will be lower near that edge." Does not: "It stays there" (A). Item M meets: "It goes from the pole past the barn toward the ridge. D shows that, so the covered picture should be lower above the ridge." Does not: "It stays there" (B). Item T meets: "Night records show stars, so the next clear night should too. The blank day record means we cannot see them, not that they stopped existing." Does not: "Stars stop existing each morning and come back at night" (D), even with correct picture choices. Accept pointing to evidence, drawing or communication symbols with the same reasoning demand.

**Accommodations.** Read every prompt aloud. Each child marks pictures on paper; offer enlarged or raised-line records, verbal descriptions of positions, adapted marking or a scribe. Do not require handwriting, independent reading or home observations.
