| 39 | 5-LS1-1 | Argue with evidence that plants get the material they grow from mostly from air and water. | Q1, 37 minutes, 12 points. Invented for practice: a radish investigation reports dry whole-plant weights of 2 g initially and 28 g finally, with dry soil changing from 600 g to 599 g. Shed material was collected separately. Another radish grows without soil in water containing a total of 1 g of added minerals; its dry weight changes from 2 g to 24 g. Matched groups with equal water, minerals, temperature and light end at 16 g in ordinary air and 25 g in air enriched with the part plants use. Write an argument for the main matter sources, using all three comparisons. Draw entry routes and explain sunlight's role. Key: mainly air and water, not soil alone; plant gain far exceeds soil loss; growth without soil still included mineral inputs; necessity was not tested; controlled air differences support air's contribution. Roots take in water and minerals; leaves take in part of air. Sunlight supplies energy. Award two points each for the evidence-backed chief-source claim, soil constraint, water-grown comparison, air comparison, entry routes and energy distinction. Within each pair give one for an accurate relation and one for explaining its evidential meaning. Wrong: mostly soil (A), water alone (B), minerals unnecessary (C), sunlight adds material (D). | section 2.1 |
| 40 | 5-LS2-1 | Make a model of how matter passes between the surroundings and plants, animals and decomposers. | Q2, 28 minutes, 14 points. Invented for practice: in a dune garden, grasses and shrubs grow; beetles eat leaves, mice eat seeds, owls eat mice, and fungi and bacteria break down dead remains and wastes. Water enters the soil; organisms exchange gases with air. Build a model explaining how these different organisms meet their needs and how matter returns to the environment for plants to use. Then add an introduced snail that eats seedlings and has no predator in this invented garden; revise and explain two consequences. Key: trace environment to plants, plants to consumers, mice to owls, remains and wastes to decomposers, and decomposer outputs back to soil, water and air; roots and leaves take in nonfood matter used to make plant food. Award two points each for plant inputs, feeding and predator links, gases and water entering and leaving organisms with wastes returning, decomposer return routes, a complete reuse loop, multiple species' dependencies, and a justified newcomer revision with two linked effects. Example: fewer seedlings can mean fewer later seeds and less mouse food, affecting owls. Wrong: dead matter vanishes (E), no gas exchange (J), newcomer cannot affect others (K). | section 2.1 |
| 40 | 5-PS3-1 | Use models to show that the energy in animals' food, used to grow, repair, move and stay warm, first came from the sun. | Q3, 14 minutes, 10 points. On the same new dune model, add a second arrow style with a legend. Trace energy from its original source into a mouse's food and on to an owl. Explain its use for growth, repair, motion and warmth, distinguish the material food supplies for growth and repair, and show energy leaving the system. Key: sunlight to plants to seeds to mouse to owl; energy transfers and eventually reaches the surroundings as warmth, while matter can be reused. Award one point for the sunlight origin, one for energy leaving as warmth without returning to plants, two for the connected food-energy path, one for each of the four uses linked to food, and one each for material used in growth and repair. Wrong: energy returns to plants in a cycle (F), food supplies no material (G), animals obtain food energy directly from sunlight (H). | section 2.1 |
