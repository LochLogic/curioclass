## 2. Standards, limits, and what is carried

- **Compelling question (frame):** "How can a community keep its water clean, and how do we know our filter works?"
Day 100 opens with a public runoff photograph and an original parking-lot-to-stream drawing, labeled "invented for practice". The downstream town takes stream water for treatment. Children notice the brown plume, draw routes and ask the compelling question before explanations.
On day 113 children explain the anchor using community sources, trial graphs and failure records: protection can reduce material entering streams; classroom filters change cloudiness, without establishing drinking safety.
Model source comparison on 101 and a controlled-test record on 105. Then step back: pairs justify choices, retain inconvenient trials and ask what evidence would change their claims.



Three dimensions of each performance expectation (names and own-word lines from data/standards/ngss-pe-dimensions.csv; the Next Generation Science Standards are never named in student, teacher or family copy):

| 3-5-ETS1-3 | Planning and Carrying Out Investigations: Plan and carry out an investigation with others to gather data for evidence, using fair tests that control variables and think about how many trials to run. | ETS1.B Developing Possible Solutions: Tests are often set up to find where a design fails or struggles, which shows what to improve; ETS1.C Optimizing the Design Solution: Several solutions must be tested to find which best solves the problem within its goals and limits. | Systems and System Models: Describe system parts and their interactions, a supporting unit connection; this expectation assigns no primary crosscutting element. |

The question board follows the evidence: Where does runoff go (100)? How do communities protect connected systems (101 and 102)? What can our filter achieve (103 and 104)? How can we compare fairly (105 to 108)? What should change (109 to 111)? What protection claim survives both sources and trials (112 and 113)? Day 114 transfers the reasoning to unfamiliar plans. Assessments introduce Philadelphia street runoff and unfamiliar runoff catchers.
Three-dimensional goals: combine trustworthy information with human-impact ideas to explain interacting system parts (100 to 102); collaboratively test controlled designs to locate failures and compare solutions within limits (103 to 111); join both explanations (112 to 114).
Standard verbs: "Obtain and combine" information; "Plan and carry out" fair tests.
Limits: no treatment recipe, chemical or microbial testing, pollution chemistry, advocacy or household investigation. Cloudiness is an ordered comparison, never an average or safety test. Orbital debris supplies a brief human-impact comparison, without orbital mechanics.
Carried: keep system-model and cause-effect, redrawn on 100; revise on 102 with watershed, wetland, air and space evidence and on 112 with filter limits. Keep data-graph: initial trial table on 105, revisions from measured repeats on 108 and 111. Design-cycle begins on 103, adds alternative predictions on 104, failure evidence on 108, a proposed change on 109 and retests on 110. All reach consensus on 113 and support the transfer tasks on 115 and 116. Unit 5's scale-proportion model rests.

Does not: "A future benefit has already been measured" (C), or "It looks clear, so it is safe" (D).

Q2 problem, "invented for practice": catch a small runoff pulse, allowing no more than 10 milliliters to escape in five seconds, with no side leakage. Both catchers must fit the printed footprint and use the supplied materials. Teacher-prepared identical shallow channels differ only in barrier height. Children state the changed feature and agree fixed input volume, water mixture, pour position, duration and apparatus reset. Every pour uses 20 milliliters from freshly stirred classroom muddy water; pour for two seconds, collect outlet water until five seconds, read volume using an unused medicine cup marked every milliliter, then drain and reset identically. Prewet both first. The timer measures water movement, never student speed.

Each four-minute turn allows 35 seconds for five individual planning contributions, six alternating 25-second trial-and-reset cycles, 35 seconds for individual failure observations and 20 seconds to finish records. The first five pours give each child a test action; the sixth repeats the first child's action. Record who planned, poured, measured and interpreted; group success alone earns no child's points. The six transition minutes sit outside these turns. Waiting students complete Q2b's plan section; the ten independent minutes after all turns finish their explanations using their own results.

Q2a scoring per child: planning contribution identifies a fixed condition, changed feature or purpose of repetition, two points; personally conducts a controlled test action, two; records every measured result with units, two; identifies a specific observed failure or supported limitation, two. Meets: "I held the pour position fixed, poured trial B2 and recorded its outlet volume. A overtopped at the low barrier." Does not: "I watched the group and copied its winner"; practical components are not yet observed, not inferred from the written answer.

Q2b scoring: compare both designs with the numerical criterion, one; check footprint and side-leak limit, one; locate a failure using repeated evidence, one; revise the model with one improvement and predict a retest, one. Meets: "A repeatedly releases more than the limit over its low barrier; B meets the volume goal. Both fit the footprint, and neither leaks at the sides. Raise A's barrier within the footprint, then repeat the same three trials." Does not: "B's first trial released less than A's, so that single comparison proves B is best" (F); an unsupported improvement is H. Accept another conclusion if the child's actual observations warrant it. Never supply a desired result during live assessment.

No-kit Q2 records are labeled "invented for practice": A outlet volumes 18, 17 and 18 milliliters; B 8, 9 and 8, with no side leakage and both inside the footprint. A repeatedly overtops. Written planning and interpretation can be scored; conducting remains "not yet observed". A simulation cannot fill that cell. Day 119 reuses the same per-child record without new collection or scoring.

Read source cards, prompts and units aloud; offer enlarged diagrams, a neutral scribe and accessible grips. The child retains source selection, test decisions, measurement and reasoning. A directed test action using an adapted grip can count when observed; record assistance precisely.
