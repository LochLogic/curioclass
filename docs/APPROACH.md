# The Curio Class approach to teaching

This page says how Curio Class teaches, in every subject, and why. It is written for a teacher or a parent who
wants to know what a Curio Class course will ask of them and of their students. It is layer 3 of a course: it is
built on [FOUNDATION.md](FOUNDATION.md) (what Curio Class is and will not become) and on the standards, and every
spine, stretch and unit plan is built on it. Each subject adds its own page; the first is
[APPROACH-MATH.md](APPROACH-MATH.md).

Two honest notes before the principles. Nothing in Curio Class has been tried with children yet, so this page
makes no claim about results; it explains choices. And every claim here about how students learn rests on a
source that was opened and read for this page. The list at the end says how much of each one was read. Where a
federal practice guide rates its own evidence (strong, moderate, or minimal), that rating is given.

## The role of the standards

The standards decide **what** a student should know and be able to do by the end of a grade, and in what words.
A Curio Class check uses the standard's own verb, so "explain" is not quietly swapped for "pick".

The standards leave the rest to us, and say so: they "do not dictate curriculum or teaching methods", and they do
not define the supports for students who are behind, ahead, or learning English (Common Core State Standards for
Mathematics, introduction). So the order of the year (the spine), how each idea is taught (this page), the
practice, the checks and the supports are our choices. This page is where those choices are explained.

## The principles

**1. Start from what students already know.**
*What we do.* Each new idea is tied to an earlier one by name, and we find out what students know before we teach.
*Why.* New ideas are learned by connecting them to what is already in memory, and "ready" means "has the earlier
knowledge" (Deans for Impact). What students already believe can also mislead them and has to be brought into
the open (How People Learn II, conclusion 5-1). These are summaries of a large body of research, not single
trials.
*At Curio Class.* Every stretch begins with a ten-minute readiness check on the standards it builds on, and the
spine names those standards by code.

**2. Teach new ideas clearly, in small steps, with models and worked examples. Then take the help away.**
*What we do.* When something is new, the lesson shows it, step by step, with examples worked in full and the
reason for each step. Students then practise with help, and the help is faded until they do it alone.
*Why.* Working memory is small, and beginners learn more from studying worked examples than from solving the
same problems unaided (Rosenshine; Clark, Kirschner and Sweller 2012). The federal guide on organizing
instruction recommends alternating worked examples with problems to solve and rates the evidence moderate
(Pashler et al. 2007, recommendation 2). As skill grows the advantage reverses, which is why the help must fade.
*At Curio Class.* A lesson tool must show a model before it asks, and the unit plan names the day that gives the
model and the day the model is withdrawn.

**3. Explore first only where it helps, and always teach afterwards.**
*What we do.* A lesson may open with a short question or a prediction. It does not leave students to find new
content by themselves. Open tasks and investigations come after the basics are taught, or in the hands of a
teacher who then teaches from what students tried.
*Why.* This is a real debate. One side holds that for beginners full guidance beats partial guidance, and that
unguided work can widen gaps (Clark, Kirschner and Sweller 2012, which is written as advocacy). The other side
holds that well-supported problem-based and inquiry teaching is not "unguided" at all (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and
Chinn 2007; we could read only the abstract), and a widely used open curriculum is built on students working on
a problem before the idea is explained, followed by a teacher-led synthesis (Illustrative Mathematics K-5 design
principles). A 2021 review of 53 studies found that problem solving before instruction helped understanding and
transfer on average, did nothing for procedures, and in grades 2 to 5 did not help at all; in every study the
problem was followed by explicit teaching (Sinha and Kapur 2021). The National Mathematics Advisory Panel found
that research does not support using only student-centered or only teacher-directed teaching.
*At Curio Class.* In the elementary grades a lesson opens with a few minutes of "what do you think?", then
teaches. A "see why" tool has the student set up a case and predict before each reveal. From about grade 6, a
unit plan may open a concept (not a procedure) with a problem, if the next step is teaching that builds on what
students tried. Science stretches open with something to wonder about, with a teacher present, and the
explanation is then taught.

**4. Practise over time, mixed, and from memory.**
*What we do.* Practice on an idea is spread across weeks and months, mixed with other ideas once each has been
learned, and much of it is recall with the notes closed.
*Why.* The federal guide rates spacing as moderate evidence and quizzing with feedback as strong (Pashler et al.
2007, recommendations 1 and 5b). A review of ten study techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice
highest (Dunlosky 2013). Mixing problem types looks promising, above all in math, but the same review says it
needs more research and did not help in every study.
*At Curio Class.* The spine carries a "where it comes back" table, a five-minute warm-up opens each day from
printable generators, and cumulative checks return to earlier stretches.

**5. Build understanding, fluency and use together.**
*What we do.* Students learn why an idea works, become quick and accurate with it, and use it on real problems.
No one of the three waits for the others to finish.
*Why.* Facts and procedures held in long-term memory free attention for harder thinking, and knowledge transfers
when students see the structure under problems that look different (Deans for Impact). The math page gives the
subject's own sources.
*At Curio Class.* Each day in a unit plan is marked for which of the three it serves, and the spine counts them.

**6. Check in ways that change what happens next.**
*What we do.* Checks are short, frequent and low-stakes, and each one comes with a next step.
*Why.* Checking helps learning when the evidence is used to adapt teaching, and feedback helps when it is about
the work and how to improve it, not a mark (Black and Wiliam 1998; Leahy et al. 2005). A test at the end, with
nothing after it, is too late to help. The research on feedback is real but uneven: effects vary widely and
feedback given badly can harm (EEF feedback report).
*At Curio Class.* Each stretch has a readiness check, a daily exit question, and an end check with half a period
kept to respond; the year has cumulative checks, each followed by a respond day. Answer keys name the common
wrong answers, what each one means, and what to do if many students made it. What FOUNDATION costs us here: no
accounts means no live dashboard and no tool that remembers a student from one day to the next. The teacher
holds the record, on score codes and a printed class sheet, and that is slower.

**7. Talk and writing are part of learning, not extras.**
*What we do.* Students explain, question and argue aloud and on paper in every subject.
*Why.* Asking students for explanations of why and how is rated strong evidence (Pashler et al. 2007,
recommendation 7). For English learners, building spoken and written language into content lessons is rated
strong; regular structured writing is recommended on minimal evidence (WWC English learner guide, recommendations
2 and 3). Classroom talk helps when the teacher keeps it purposeful (EEF metacognition report).
*At Curio Class.* The unit plan names the discussion questions and the written task. A tool cannot judge open
writing, so a teacher does, with a rubric, sample pieces and a look-for sheet on paper (FOUNDATION).

**8. Every learner works on grade-level content, with supports built into the lesson.**
*What we do.* Support changes how a student reaches the grade's work. It does not swap in easier work.
*Why.* Teaching a small set of academic words in depth over several days is rated strong evidence (WWC English
learner guide, recommendation 1). Guidance written for English learners asks that language support sit inside
the lesson, that the challenge be kept, and that students' own languages and knowledge be treated as resources
(ELSF math guidelines; WIDA 2020, Big Ideas; EdReports multilingual learner guide). Universal Design for
Learning asks for more than one way to take in an idea, to respond, and to stay engaged (CAST 2024). We read the
UDL guidelines themselves, not the research behind them, so we treat them as a design framework.
*At Curio Class.* Every spec and unit plan carries three hooks: an unaided check the teacher can see, English
learner supports (the language the lesson asks for, key words with cards, sentence frames), and access (print
that works in black and white, read aloud, more time, the model allowed). The stretch task for a student who is
ahead goes deeper on the same standard, not into next year's.

**9. Knowledge builds on knowledge, so order matters.**
*What we do.* Ideas are taught in an order where each rests on the last, and topics are chosen so that knowledge
adds up across a year.
*Why.* What a student already knows makes the next thing easier to learn (How People Learn II, conclusion 5-1),
and the summary we read for teachers draws from this the case for a well-ordered, knowledge-rich curriculum
(Deans for Impact). This is a finding about how memory works more than a tested recipe for ordering any one
course.
*At Curio Class.* The spine gives "builds on" and "where it comes back" for every stretch. In English language
arts the stretches are built on sets of texts about one topic.

**10. Use a screen for what a screen does well.**
*What we do.* A tool is used where acting on something and seeing it respond teaches more than reading about it,
and for practice with feedback. It is not the lesson.
*Why.* The evidence is thin. The National Academies conclude that no technology is right everywhere and that its
value depends on the learner, the goal and the support around it (How People Learn II, conclusions 8-1 and 8-2).
The National Mathematics Advisory Panel found the research too weak to say what makes software work under
ordinary conditions. Interviews by a simulation research group found that students learned from changing things
themselves and seeing the result, and learned little from watching an animation (Adams et al. 2008; interviews,
many with college students, not a controlled trial). Pictures with words beat words alone, with the words kept
next to the picture and nothing decorative that pulls attention away (Pashler et al. 2007, recommendation 3,
moderate).
*At Curio Class.* A tool is built only when a unit plan names its job, and it must pass the teaching test: a
model, the standard's own verb, at least five items that differ, thinking made cheaper than guessing.

**11. Hands and the real world come first where the idea lives there.**
*What we do.* Students measure, build, fold, pour and observe real things, and the lesson then ties what they did
to the picture and the symbol.
*Why.* Linking concrete and abstract forms of an idea is rated moderate evidence, with a warning that concrete
examples alone may not carry over to new cases (Pashler et al. 2007, recommendation 4). The classroom studies
Rosenshine reports found hands-on work most useful after the basic material was taught. We did not read a source
that tests hands-on science teaching as such, so that part rests on FOUNDATION and on the standards.
*At Curio Class.* The physical activity is written into the lesson plan with a supplies list, and the science
spine counts its hands-on days.

**12. Motivation comes from success and purpose, not from gimmicks.**
*What we do.* Work is set so that effort usually succeeds, the goal is clear, students have small real choices,
and praise is for the work.
*Why.* Students are more willing to work when they feel they belong, have some control, and see themselves
getting better (How People Learn II, conclusions 6-1 and 6-2). Rewards for performing can lower interest in the
task, though the findings are mixed (same source, chapter 6). Decoration that is not about the idea hurts
learning (Deans for Impact). Belief that effort counts is linked to better results in math (National Mathematics
Advisory Panel).
*At Curio Class.* Pictures and stories in a tool serve the idea being taught. Practice begins where most students
succeed and climbs. Feedback says what to try next.

## What a tool is for, in this approach

A tool does three jobs well, and each belongs to a principle above.

- **Show why.** The student changes something and sees what follows, after predicting (principles 3, 10 and 11).
- **Practise with feedback.** Many differing items, answered from memory, with a response to each (principles 4
  and 6).
- **Check unaided.** A short check with no help, whose result the teacher can see through a score code
  (principle 6).

A tool does not replace the teacher's explanation, hold a discussion, judge an open answer or a piece of
writing, stand in for real materials, or know a student from one day to the next. The unit plan gives those
jobs to people and paper.

## The two rooms

A Curio Class unit must work for a class of thirty with one teacher and for one child at home with a parent.

One-to-one teaching has unusually good evidence. A 2020 review of 96 randomized trials of tutoring found an
average gain of about a third of a standard deviation, with weaker results for parent and volunteer tutors than
for teachers and trained staff (Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan 2020; a working paper). The famous older claim of a
gain more than five times that size rests on two doctoral studies of three weeks of teaching (Bloom 1984) and
should not be repeated as a rule. In the same paper, ordinary classes that tested, gave feedback and retaught did far better than
classes that only tested, which is the idea behind principle 6.

None of this says a parent with a Curio Class unit will get those gains. It says the home room is worth serving
well. So every unit carries a "teaching one child" note, answer keys explain instead of only listing answers,
and discussion questions are ones a parent can ask. In the classroom, the same unit gives the teacher
all-student responses, the exit question and the class record sheet in place of the one-to-one conversation.

## Where the evidence is thin or contested

- Whether to open with a problem or with teaching. Contested; it seems to depend on age and on whether the goal
  is an idea or a procedure (principle 3).
- What makes a learning tool work in an ordinary classroom. Thin (principle 10).
- Mixing problem types in practice. Promising, not settled.
- Feedback. Helpful on average, with wide variation and some harm.
- Structured writing for English learners. Recommended on minimal evidence.
- Universal Design for Learning. A design framework; we did not read the studies behind it.
- Hands-on work in science. We read no study of it for this page.
- Rewards and games. Mixed findings.
- Whether a parent at home gets the benefit found for trained tutors. Not known.
- Practice guides and evidence summaries are themselves the judgment of expert panels reading the research. We
  relied on them and did not read the studies under them.
- Curio Class itself. Untested.

## What this asks of every unit plan

1. Name what the unit builds on, and check it first.
2. Say which day gives the model and worked examples, and which day they are taken away.
3. If the unit opens with a question or problem, say how long it lasts and what teaching follows it.
4. Mark each day as understanding, fluency or use.
5. Give the daily exit question, with the common wrong answers and what to do about each.
6. Say where this unit's ideas are practised again later.
7. Name the discussion questions and the written task, and who judges the writing with what.
8. Fill the three hooks: the unaided check, the English learner supports, access.
9. Give each tool one named job (show why, practise, or check), and say what is done by hand and with what
   supplies.
10. Include the "teaching one child" note and the one-line projector path.
