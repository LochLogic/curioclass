title: Still Life Shading Study
grade: 11
order: 5
kind: Art
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: three objects (an egg, a mug, a crumpled paper bag or a piece of fruit), a desk lamp or a flashlight, plain paper, a regular pencil, an eraser, a tissue or cotton swab
nice: drawing pencils (2H, 2B, 6B), a kneaded eraser, a sheet of toned or brown paper and a white pencil

## The big idea
A pencil can make a plain egg look like a museum piece. The secret is light: once you can see where it hits, where
it rolls away and where it bounces back, flat drawings turn into solid objects. Old painters called this
**chiaroscuro**, the drama of light and dark. Set up one lamp, turn off the rest, and make something look real.

## Goals
- A finished shaded drawing of a three-object still life lit from one side.
- A value scale you made yourself, showing seven steps from white to black.

## Objectives
- I can find the highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow on an object.
- I can make smooth value changes with layers instead of pressing hard.
- I can arrange objects for a strong composition.

## Get ready
Darken the room. Place the lamp low and to one side, about 45 degrees from your objects. Sit so the light falls
across, not straight on. Squint: everything should split into clear light and dark shapes.

## Steps
1. Make a value scale (10 minutes): seven boxes in a row, from paper white to as dark as your pencil goes. Build
   darks by layering light strokes, not by pressing hard.
2. Arrange your still life. Overlap at least two objects. Put the tallest one off-center.
3. Block in shapes lightly (10 minutes). Draw the big outlines and the shape of each cast shadow. Shadows are
   shapes, so draw them like objects.
4. Map the values. On each object, find these five: highlight, light side, core shadow (the darkest band on the
   object), reflected light (a faint glow near the shadowed edge), cast shadow.
5. Shade in layers (30 minutes). Start with mid tones over everything in shadow, then go darker, then darkest. Blend
   gently with a tissue only where you want softness.
6. Lift highlights with the eraser. The brightest spot is often just clean paper.
7. Step back three meters and squint. Darken anything that looks too timid.

## Make it yours
- Use toned paper: shade with pencil, then add highlights with a white pencil for a dramatic result.
- Make it strange: a lit egg next to a toy dinosaur and a teacup, and give the drawing a story title.
- Do a 5-minute, 2-minute and 30-second version and compare which captures the light best.

## Stuck?
- It looks flat: your darks are not dark enough. Push the core shadow and cast shadow harder.
- Everything is smudgy: rest your hand on a scrap sheet and work from top left to bottom right (top right if left-handed).

## Show it off
Prop the drawing next to the real setup with the lamp still on and let your family compare.

## Clean up
Turn the lights back on, return the objects, and put the drawing between two clean sheets so it does not smear.

## For the grown-up
This is the core of observational drawing taught in high school and early college art: value, form and
composition. It trains patient looking that carries into any visual work. **Ask at dinner:** "Where is the
reflected light on this plate right now?"
