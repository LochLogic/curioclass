time: 90-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: a cereal box or cardboard for the board, paper, index cards or cut paper for cards, markers, a die, coins or bottle caps for pieces, scissors
nice: a second die, sticky notes for quick changes

## The big idea
In most games, the players fight each other. In this one, everyone is on the same side, and the board is the enemy. A flood is rising, a dragon is waking, a spaceship is falling apart. You'll design a game where the table wins or loses together, and where the game is hard enough that winning feels like a miracle.

## Goals
- A playable cooperative board game with a board, cards and a rules page.
- At least two playtests, with a rule you changed after each one.

## Objectives
- I can design a **threat engine**, the rules that make the game fight back.
- I can balance difficulty by changing numbers and testing again.
- I can write clear rules someone else can learn without me.

## Get ready
Pick your threat. Ideas: a city during a rising flood, a lighthouse crew in a storm, healers during a strange fever, a ship's crew patching leaks. Lay out cardboard, cards and markers.

## Steps
1. Write the win and loss conditions in one sentence each. Win: "Rescue all 6 villagers." Lose: "The water reaches the top row, or the deck runs out."
2. Design the threat engine (15 minutes). After each player's turn, flip a threat card: the water rises in one area, a storm blocks a road. Make 20 cards; about a third should be nasty.
3. Give each player a **role** with one special power: the Engineer can build a wall, the Medic can move two spaces. Different powers force teamwork.
4. Draw the board: 12 to 20 spaces or zones, with paths between them.
5. Write the turn: "Take 3 actions: move, rescue, repair or share a card. Then flip a threat card."
6. **Playtest solo** by playing two roles yourself (20 minutes). Track how close the game came. A good co-op game is lost about half the time.
7. Tune it. Too easy? Add a card or remove an action. Too hard? Give one extra action when players share a space.
8. Write a one-page rulebook with a setup diagram.

## Make it yours
- Add a traitor card: one player might secretly be on the threat's side.
- Make the threat smarter, so it gets faster each time a certain card shows up.
- Give the game a story campaign: three short scenarios where each one changes the next.

## Stuck?
- Players just do the same thing every turn: give each role one thing only they can do.
- It's boring: add a decision with a cost, like "spend an action to peek at the next threat card."

## Show it off
Teach it to your family after dinner and play one round. Watch who argues about strategy: that means it works.

## Clean up
Put cards, pieces and rules in an envelope taped to the back of the board.

## For the grown-up
Game design is systems thinking: probability, balancing numbers, and clear technical writing. Co-op games also make a great family night because nobody loses to anybody. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the most dangerous card in your game, and why did you put it in?"
