title: Model Wind Turbine
grade: 11
order: 1
kind: Build
time: 75-120 min
where: Either
mess: Medium
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: a cardboard box, a paper cup, a wooden skewer or pencil, a straw that fits loosely over the skewer, scissors, tape, a ruler, a protractor or a folded-paper angle guide, string, a few paper clips, a fan or a windy spot
nice: a hot glue gun, a small hobby motor and a little bulb (from an old toy), a craft knife and cutting mat

## The big idea
A town on a windswept island has hired you to keep its lights on. Every real turbine is a fight between three
things: blade count, blade angle and drag. You will build a test rig, run actual trials, and find the blade design
that lifts the most weight. Engineers call it optimizing; you can call it winning.

## Goals
- A working turbine on a stand that spins in a fan or real wind.
- A winning blade design, chosen by data, that winds up the heaviest load of paper clips.

## Objectives
- I can change one variable at a time and keep the rest fixed.
- I can measure output by timing how fast the turbine lifts a load.
- I can explain why more blades or steeper angles do not always mean more power.

## Get ready
Clear a table near an outlet for the fan, or find a doorway or balcony with a steady breeze. Cut a 30 cm tall tower
from the box and tape it into a triangle so it stands on its own. Gather four or five spare cardboard pieces for
blades.

## Steps
1. Build the axle. Push the skewer through the straw, then tape the straw across the top of the tower so the skewer
   spins freely. It should turn with one breath.
2. Make the hub. Push the paper cup's base onto the front of the skewer and tape it tight. Blades will tape to the cup.
3. Cut your first set of blades (15 minutes): three blades, each 12 cm by 4 cm. Tape them to the hub at about 20
   degrees to the wind. Use the protractor, or fold a paper guide.
4. Build the load. Tie 50 cm of string to the back end of the skewer and hang a paper clip from it. As the skewer
   spins, it winds the string up like a tiny crane.
5. Run your trials (30 minutes). Pick one variable to test: blade count (2, 3, 4, 6), blade angle (10, 20, 35, 50
   degrees) or blade shape (rectangle, tapered, curved). Time how long it takes to lift the load to the top. Run each
   design three times and average.
6. Add paper clips until each design stalls. Record the max load in a simple table.
7. Build your champion: combine the best count, angle and shape. Does it beat every single-change design?

## Make it yours
- Design for a storm: make blades that feather (bend back) in strong wind so the turbine does not tear itself apart.
- Build a vertical-axis turbine from two halves of a cup sliced lengthwise, and race it against your best horizontal one.
- Add the hobby motor and bulb from an old toy and see whether you can make a light flicker.

## Stuck?
- It will not spin: the axle has too much friction. Widen the straw hole or use a smoother skewer.
- Results jump around wildly: keep the fan at the same distance (mark the spot with tape) and on the same setting.

## Show it off
Stage a demo: run your worst design and then your champion side by side, and show the table that proves the winner.

## Clean up
Unplug the fan, recycle cardboard scraps, and keep the champion on a shelf.

## For the grown-up
This is a full engineering design cycle: controlled variables, repeated trials, averages and an evidence-based final
design, all physics and math skills from high school science. A craft knife is fine with your permission; a cutting
mat or thick cardboard underneath saves the table. Keep fingers clear of fan blades. **Ask at dinner:** "What did
the data tell you that you did not expect?"
