title: Field Guide to Backyard Mythical Creatures
grade: 7
order: 10
kind: Story
time: 60-120 min
where: Either
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: paper or a small notebook, a pencil, colored pencils, a clipboard or hard book to press on, a ruler
nice: a magnifying glass, a stapler for binding

## The big idea
Look closely at any yard, balcony or windowsill and you'll find evidence: a hole in a leaf, a strange scratch, a pile of seeds. Scientists would say a bug or a squirrel did it. You're going to report what *really* happened. Write the official field guide to the mythical creatures living right outside your door.

## Goals
- A field guide with at least five creature entries, each with a drawing and labels.
- At least three entries based on real "evidence" you found.

## Objectives
- I can observe closely and describe what I see in detail.
- I can write in the style of a real field guide.
- I can build a believable invented creature from real clues.

## Get ready
Set up your guide: fold three sheets of paper into a booklet. Write a title page: "A Field Guide to the Hidden Creatures of [your street]" and your name as "Chief Naturalist."

## Steps
1. **Go on an evidence walk (20 minutes).** Yard, balcony, a park with permission, or even windowsills and houseplants. Find at least five odd things: a chewed leaf, a hole in the dirt, a twig in a weird shape, a feather, a crack in the pavement. Sketch each one fast.
2. **Ask "what made this?"** For each clue, invent a creature that would leave exactly that sign. The chewed leaf? A Leafwhisker, a moth with a mustache that only eats at midnight.
3. **Study real field guides in your head.** A good entry has: common name, a fancy scientific name (*Folius munchus*), size, habitat, diet, call or sound, how to spot it, and a "Danger Level" from 1 to 5.
4. **Write the entries (40 minutes).** One page per creature. Draw it big with labels and arrows ("tail used to sweep away footprints"). Draw a small "actual size" silhouette in the corner.
5. **Add tracks and signs.** Draw each creature's footprint or the sign it leaves behind.
6. **Write a warning page.** What to do if you meet the most dangerous one. ("Do not make eye contact with a Gutter Grumbler. Offer it a bottle cap and back away slowly.")

## Make it yours
- Make it a food web: draw arrows showing who eats whom among your creatures.
- Add "seasonal behavior": where do they go in winter? What do they do in a thunderstorm?
- Write it as the field notebook of a naturalist who slowly realizes the creatures are watching *them*.

## Stuck?
- Can't find evidence: look low. Under rocks (put them back), in cracks, behind pots, under window ledges.
- Drawings look flat: add a shadow under each creature and draw it from the side, not straight on.
- Running out of creature ideas: combine a real animal with a household object (a spider with umbrella legs).

## Show it off
Lead a family "guided tour" to the exact spots where the evidence was found and read the entries aloud.

## Clean up
Bring everything inside, put back any rocks you moved, and wash your hands.

## For the grown-up
This mixes real outdoor observation skills (the core of life science) with creative and informational writing. If they want to go past the yard, it's with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the most dangerous creature in our yard, and how do we avoid it?"
