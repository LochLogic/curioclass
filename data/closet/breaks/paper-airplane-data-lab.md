materials: 6 or more sheets of paper the same size, a tape measure or measured string, paper clips, a pencil, graph paper or plain paper for a chart, tape for a launch line
nice: a calculator, colored pencils for the graph, a long hallway or outdoor space with no wind

## The big idea
Everybody thinks their paper airplane is the best. Real engineers don't guess; they test. Build three designs, fly each one five times, and use data to prove which plane truly wins. Then try to beat the champion with one smart change.

## Goals
- You build three different paper airplane designs and test each one five times.
- You make a bar graph of average distances and name the winner with evidence.

## Objectives
- I can run repeated trials and calculate an average.
- I can make a bar graph from my data.
- I can change one variable and measure its effect.

## Get ready
Find a long space with no breakables: a hallway, a gym-like room, or outside on a calm day. Tape a launch line on the floor. Make a data table: Design, Throw 1-5, Average.

## Steps
1. **Design A: the dart.** Fold a sheet in half long-ways and unfold. Fold the top corners to the center line. Fold the new slanted edges to the center again. Fold in half, then fold each wing down to line up with the bottom.
2. **Design B: the glider.** Fold the top 5 cm of the paper down. Fold the top corners to the center. Fold in half, then fold wide wings, about 3 cm from the bottom.
3. **Design C: your own.** Invent one, or change the wing shape on one of the others.
4. Throw each plane five times from the launch line, with the same arm motion each time. Measure from the line to where the nose lands. Record every throw (20 minutes).
5. Find each average: add the five distances and divide by 5.
6. Make a bar graph: one bar per design, height equals the average.
7. Now improve the winner. Change ONE thing: add a paper clip to the nose, fold the wing tips up about a pencil's width, or make the wings narrower. Test it five times and compare.

## Make it yours
- Test for time in the air instead of distance. Is the farthest plane also the longest flier?
- Hold an accuracy contest: land in a box or hoop 3 meters away.
- Test different paper (printer paper, notebook paper, newspaper) with the same design.

## Stuck?
- Planes nosedive: fold the back edge of each wing up slightly to lift the nose.
- Planes curve to one side: make sure both wings are the same size and angle.
- Results jump around: throw more gently and the same way every time, or do 10 throws instead of 5.

## Show it off
Present your graph to your family, name the champion, and do a live demo flight.

## Clean up
Collect every plane (check under furniture), and recycle the crumpled ones.

## For the grown-up
This is real data science: repeated trials, averages, bar graphs, and testing one variable. Those skills show up in sixth grade math and science. Remind your child not to aim at faces or pets. **Ask at dinner:** "Which plane won, and what does your data say about why?"
