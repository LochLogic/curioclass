title: Tensegrity Floating Table
grade: 11
order: 13
kind: Build
time: 75-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Medium
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: stiff cardboard (a box), strong string or thread, scissors, a ruler, a pencil, tape, a hole punch or a sharp pencil to poke holes
nice: thin wood scraps or craft sticks, fishing line, a craft knife and cutting mat, a glue gun

## The big idea
A tensegrity table looks impossible: the top floats above the base, held up by what looks like a few loose strings.
It works because of **tension** and **compression** in balance. One string pulls up, others pull down, and the
whole thing locks. Build one small enough to fit in your hand, then try to make it hold a mug.

## Goals
- A working tensegrity table where the top seems to float.
- A load test showing how much weight it can hold before tipping.

## Objectives
- I can explain how tension and compression can balance in a structure.
- I can measure and adjust string lengths to make a stable structure.
- I can test a design and improve it based on how it fails.

## Get ready
Clear a table. Cut two cardboard squares, each 12 cm by 12 cm (stack three layers and tape them for stiffness). Cut
two L-shaped arms, each about 10 cm tall and 6 cm long at the foot.

## Steps
1. Glue or tape one L-arm to the base so it rises from one edge and hooks over the center. Tape the other L-arm
   upside down to the top square, so it hangs down toward the center.
2. Poke a small hole near each corner of both squares and at the tip of each arm.
3. Tie the **main tension string** (15 minutes): from the tip of the base arm to the tip of the top arm, about 3 cm
   long. The top should now hang from the base arm with the two arms overlapping.
4. Tie four corner strings from each corner of the base to the matching corner of the top. Pull them just tight
   enough that the top stops tilting.
5. Balance it. Shorten or lengthen strings a few millimeters at a time until the top sits level.
6. Load test (15 minutes). Add coins or small objects to the center. Note where it fails: does a string slip, does a
   corner sag, or does it twist?
7. Fix the weak point and test again.

## Make it yours
- Scale it up with wood scraps or sturdy cardboard tubes and try to support a book.
- Build a three-level tower where each level floats on the one below.
- Hide the strings with fishing line so it looks like magic.

## Stuck?
- The top flips over: the main string is too long. Shorten it so the arms overlap more.
- It wobbles: the corner strings are uneven. Measure them all with a ruler and match their lengths.

## Show it off
Set a cup of water on it at dinner and ask everyone how they think it stays up before you explain.

## Clean up
Trim loose ends, recycle cardboard scraps, and put the scissors away.

## For the grown-up
This builds physics (forces, tension, compression) and engineering problem solving through testing and fixing. If
they use a craft knife or glue gun, a cutting mat and a clear table help. **Ask at dinner:** "Which string is doing
the most work?"
