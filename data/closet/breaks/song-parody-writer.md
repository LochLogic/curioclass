time: 45-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: paper, a pencil, an eraser, a song tune you know well
nice: a hairbrush microphone, pots and spoons for a beat, a costume piece

## The big idea
A parody is a song with a famous tune but brand new, silly words. "Twinkle, twinkle, little star" could become "Stinky, stinky, gym sock pair." Today you are the songwriter. Pick a tune, pick a topic, and write a song your family will be humming all night.

## Goals
- You write at least two verses of new words to an old tune.
- You perform your parody for an audience.

## Objectives
- I can match my words to the beat and syllables of a tune.
- I can use rhymes at the ends of lines.
- I can sing with clear words and good timing.

## Get ready
Pick a tune everyone knows. Old songs work best: "Twinkle, Twinkle," "Row, Row, Row Your Boat," "Old MacDonald," or "Happy Birthday." Sing it twice to warm up.

## Steps
1. Pick a funny topic. Ideas: doing the dishes, a cat who rules the house, losing a sock, Monday mornings.
2. Write the first line of the old song. Clap the syllables. "Twin-kle twin-kle lit-tle star" is 7 claps.
3. Brainstorm a word list for your topic (5 minutes). Then find rhyming pairs: sock and clock, dish and fish.
4. Write your new first line with the same number of claps. "Stink-y stink-y gym sock pair" is 7.
5. Keep going, one line at a time (20 minutes). Sing each line as you write it. If it feels bumpy, change a word.
6. Write a second verse. Make it build up to a funny ending line.
7. Practice the whole thing three times. Add one dance move or a big pause before the last line.

## Make it yours
- Write a "sad ballad" about something silly, like the last cookie, and sing it very dramatically.
- Make it a duet. Split lines between you and a family member.
- Add a spoken part in the middle, like a radio announcer.

## Stuck?
- A line has too many syllables: swap a long word for a short one.
- Nothing rhymes: change the word at the end of the line first, then find its rhyme.
- You feel shy: sing it to a stuffed animal first, then to a person.

## Show it off
Announce your song with a title. Hand your audience the words so they can join the last verse.

## Clean up
Put your lyric sheet somewhere safe. Return any pots or spoons to the kitchen.

## For the grown-up
Writing a parody builds rhythm, syllables, rhyme, and word choice, which all support reading and poetry. Old tunes like these are free for anyone to use. Expect some very silly topics. **Ask at dinner:** "Can you sing us your best line again?"
