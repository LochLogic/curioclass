title: Water Wheel
grade: 6
order: 8
kind: Build
time: 60-120 min
where: Either
mess: Medium
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: 2 paper or plastic plates (or 2 large plastic lids), 6 to 8 plastic spoons or small cups, a pencil or wooden skewer for the axle, tape, glue, scissors, string (about 50 cm), a paper clip, a pitcher or watering can, a big plastic tub or the bathtub, a ruler
nice: a hot glue gun with permission, waterproof tape, small coins as weights, a garden hose outside

## The big idea
For thousands of years, people used rivers to grind grain, saw wood, and pump water, with no engines at all. The trick was the water wheel. Build your own and see if it can do real work: lifting a load with nothing but falling water.

## Goals
- You build a water wheel that spins when water pours on it.
- Your wheel winds up a string and lifts a small weight.

## Objectives
- I can explain how falling water's energy turns into motion.
- I can test different paddle designs and compare results.
- I can measure how much weight my wheel can lift.

## Get ready
Work outside, in the bathtub, or over a big tub. Poke a hole in the exact center of each plate (fold it in half twice to find the center).

## Steps
1. Glue or tape the spoons (or small cups) between the two plates around the edge, all facing the same direction, like a hamster wheel with scoops. Space them evenly; for 8 paddles, put one every eighth of the way around.
2. Push the pencil through the center holes. It should stick out about 5 cm on each side. Tape it so the plates turn with the pencil.
3. Hold the ends of the pencil loosely in your fingers (or rest them on two cups over the tub). Pour water onto the paddles from about 20 cm above. Watch it turn!
4. Count spins. Pour one full pitcher and count how many times the wheel turns. Write it down.
5. Make it do work. Tie the string to one end of the pencil and hang a paper clip on the other end of the string. As the wheel spins, the string winds up and lifts the clip.
6. Add weight (20 minutes). Hook coins or washers onto the paper clip one at a time. How many can your wheel lift?
7. Redesign one thing: more paddles, cups instead of spoons, or pour from higher. Test and compare.

## Make it yours
- Build a "mill": put a paper flag on the axle that waves, or a cardboard arm that taps a bell each turn.
- Compare an overshot wheel (water poured on top) to an undershot wheel (water pushing paddles at the bottom).
- Make a tiny "village" around your mill and write the job it does.

## Stuck?
- The wheel won't turn: the paddles may be facing the wrong way. They should catch water like scoops.
- It wobbles: the axle hole is off center. Make a new hole or tape the axle straighter.
- Glue lets go when wet: use lots of tape, or ask about the hot glue gun.

## Show it off
Give a demo: show how many coins your wheel can lift, then explain one change that made it stronger.

## Clean up
Dump the water on a plant or down the drain, wipe up spills, and let your wheel dry.

## For the grown-up
This combines engineering and physics (energy changing form, simple machines like the wheel and axle) with some history. If your child uses a hot glue gun, please give permission and show them where to set it down safely. Bathtub water play is fine at this age; just check the floor's dry after. **Ask at dinner:** "How many coins could your wheel lift, and what made it stronger?"
