title: Advice Guide for New Ninth Graders
grade: 11
order: 10
kind: Kindness
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: 4 or 5 sheets of paper, a stapler, pens or markers, scissors
nice: colored paper, old magazines for collage, a glue stick

## The big idea
You remember being a ninth grader: the confusing hallways, the lunch table question, the first big test. You know
things now that would have saved you a lot of stress. Make a pocket-sized survival guide, funny and honest, for
someone who is about to start high school.

## Goals
- A stapled mini-guide of 8 to 16 pages full of real, useful advice.
- A plan for who gets it: a younger sibling, cousin, neighbor or the school's welcome table.

## Objectives
- I can choose advice that is truly useful, not just obvious.
- I can write with a warm, honest voice for a younger reader.
- I can design pages that are easy to scan.

## Get ready
Fold three or four sheets in half and nest them to make a booklet. Staple the spine. Brainstorm for 5 minutes: what
did you wish someone had told you?

## Steps
1. Pick your best 10 ideas from the brainstorm. Cut anything a ninth grader would roll their eyes at.
2. Plan your sections: **Survival** (lockers, schedules, lunch), **Social** (friends, groups, awkward moments),
   **Academic** (tests, teachers, asking for help), **Secret level** (the stuff nobody says out loud).
3. Write each tip in three parts: the tip, a short true story, a one-line takeaway. Example: "Ask the teacher after
   class, not in front of everyone. I did this in tenth grade and got two extra days on an essay."
4. Add humor. A "Worst Mistakes I Made" page or a ranking of the best hallway shortcuts.
5. Design it (20 minutes). Big headings, short paragraphs, small doodles or collage. One idea per page.
6. Add a final page: "It gets better. Here's how I know."

## Make it yours
- Make it a field guide: hall monitors as wildlife, cafeteria as a habitat, with "spotting tips."
- Include a fold-out map of your school with hidden shortcuts and quiet spots.
- Make three short versions for three kinds of students: sporty, artsy, quiet.

## Stuck?
- The advice feels generic: go back to a specific moment you remember and write that story instead.
- You run out of ideas: ask someone a year older and someone a year younger what they would add.

## Show it off
Read your favorite page aloud at dinner, then hand the guide to its new owner.

## Clean up
Recycle scraps and put markers back.

## For the grown-up
This builds writing for an audience, design and empathy, and it lets your teen reflect on their own growth. **Ask at
dinner:** "What's the one tip you wish you'd had on your first day?"
