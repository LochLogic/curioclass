title: Homemade Seismograph
grade: 10
order: 14
kind: Science
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: a sturdy cardboard box (shoebox or bigger), a paper cup, string, a marker or pen, coins or small stones for weight, tape, scissors, a long strip of paper (tape sheets end to end, or use receipt paper), a ruler
nice: a sheet of plastic wrap, a stopwatch, a helper to pull the paper

## The big idea
Seismographs record earthquakes by keeping a heavy pen still while the ground moves underneath it. You'll build one from a box, a cup and a marker, then become the earthquake. You'll record small and big "quakes," measure their size and figure out how far they were from the box.

## Goals
- A working seismograph that draws a line on a moving strip of paper.
- A set of labeled readings, from a tiny tremor to a big quake.

## Objectives
- I can explain how **inertia** keeps a heavy hanging mass still while the frame moves.
- I can measure amplitude (wave height) on a recording.
- I can compare readings and connect them to distance and strength.

## Get ready
Stand the box on its side, with the open side facing you. Poke a hole in the top center.

## Steps
1. **Build the pendulum** (15 minutes). Poke two holes near the rim of the cup and tie on a string handle. Poke a hole in the cup's bottom and push the marker through, tip down, so it just reaches the bottom of the box. Fill the cup with coins around the marker.
2. Thread the string up through the hole in the box top. Adjust so the marker tip just touches the box floor, then tape the string.
3. Slide the paper strip under the marker along the box floor so it can be pulled through.
4. **Baseline.** Pull the paper slowly and steadily without bumping anything. You should get a flat line.
5. **Earthquakes** (20 minutes). Pull the paper while someone taps or shakes the table: a soft tap, a medium bump, a big shake. If you're alone, pull with one hand and tap with the other. Label each quake on the strip.
6. **Measure.** Use a ruler to measure the amplitude (highest point to the center line) of each quake in millimeters.
7. **Distance test.** Stomp on the floor 1 m, 3 m and 5 m from the table. How does the reading change? Seismologists use the same idea to estimate how far away quakes happen.

## Make it yours
- Make your own Richter-style scale: classify each quake from 1 to 5 by amplitude.
- Record a "day in the house": leave it near the front door and pull it during a busy moment.
- Build a "shake table" from a tray on marbles and test which block tower designs survive.

## Stuck?
- The marker wobbles on its own: the cup needs more weight, or the string is too long.
- The line is faint: lower the string a few millimeters so the tip presses a little harder, or use a fresh marker.

## Show it off
Lay out your recordings on the floor and challenge your family to guess which quake was which.

## Clean up
Cap the marker and keep the box for future tests.

## For the grown-up
This teaches earth science (seismic waves, measurement) and physics (inertia, amplitude), and shows how real instruments collect data. **Ask at dinner:** "How does a seismograph know the ground moved?"
