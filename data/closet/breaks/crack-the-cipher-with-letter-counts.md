## The big idea
In a substitution cipher, every letter is swapped for a different letter. It looks impossible to read, but there's a weakness: some letters, like E and T, show up way more than others. Codebreakers have used letter counts to crack ciphers for over a thousand years. An agent left you this intercepted message. Break it.

> RTH DHWLHR JOHQR TZI RTH PJG ZQDZIH J TAXXAU RLHH QHJL RTH HIOH AY RTH YALHDR. QAFAIE UZXX RTZQN RA XAAN RTHLH. RA HQRHL RTH FJDH, NQAWN RTLHH RZPHD JQI DJE RTH GJDDUALI: GCLGXH GHQOCZQ.

## Goals
- You crack the intercepted message using frequency analysis (counting letters).
- You write your own cipher message for someone else to crack.

## Objectives
- I can count how often each letter appears and find the most common ones.
- I can use patterns, like common short words, to guess letters.
- I can create a substitution cipher with a key.

## Get ready
Copy the secret message onto paper, leaving a blank line under every line of code for your guesses. Write A through Z across the top of another page for your tally chart.

## Steps
1. Count every letter in the message (15 minutes). Make a tally mark under each letter on your chart. Be careful; one missed letter can throw you off.
2. Find the top three. In English, the most common letters are usually E, T, A, O, I, N, in roughly that order.
3. Make your first guess: the most common code letter is probably E. Write E under every place it appears, in pencil.
4. Look for a three-letter word that shows up a lot. It's probably THE. If it ends in your E, you just got T and H too.
5. Fill those in everywhere. Now hunt for patterns: a one-letter word is almost always A or I. A double letter is often LL, EE, SS, or OO.
6. Keep guessing and checking (20 minutes). When a guess makes a non-word, erase and try something else. Keep a key: code letter on top, real letter below.
7. Once it's cracked, write your own message for a family member, using a new key. Make it at least two sentences long, so they have enough letters to count.

## Make it yours
- Write a message with no E at all, to make frequency analysis harder. (It's tough!)
- Use a keyword key: write a word like JUNGLE at the start of the alphabet, then the rest of the letters in order, skipping repeats.
- Leave a chain of coded clues around the house, each one leading to the next.

## Stuck?
- Nothing makes sense: double-check your tally. Count the top letter one more time.
- You have E, T, H but nothing else: look at the eight-letter word right before the colon. It's what you'd need to get into a secret base.
- Totally stuck on the last two words: they're a password. Both start with the same letter, and one of them is an animal.

## Show it off
Read the decoded message aloud like a secret agent. Then hand your own coded message to someone and time how long they take.

## Clean up
Keep your key secret (fold it up or hide it). Recycle your scratch paper.

## For the grown-up
This is real cryptography and also strong reading and logic practice: patterns in language, testing guesses, and persistence. The decoded message ends with the password "purple penguin" if you want to check the work. If your child cracks it fast, the "no letter E" twist is a great next challenge. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the first word you cracked, and how did you know?"
