materials: a shoebox or tissue box, rubber bands of different thicknesses, straws, glass jars or glasses, water, a pencil, tape, scissors, a ruler, paper
nice: a wooden spoon, bottles with caps, a tuning reference (a real instrument at home, or just your own singing voice)

## The big idea
Every instrument in history was invented by someone messing around with sound. Today you're the inventor. Design a brand new instrument that can play at least three different notes, give it a name, and write the first song ever played on it.

## Goals
- You invent and build an instrument that plays at least 3 different pitches.
- You compose and perform a short tune on it.

## Objectives
- I can explain how size, tightness, and length change pitch (how high or low a sound is).
- I can tune my instrument by adjusting its parts.
- I can write down a simple tune so I can play it again.

## Get ready
Test the three sound families first. Pluck a stretched rubber band (string). Blow across a straw cut short (wind). Tap a glass with a pencil (percussion). Pick one family, or combine two.

## Steps
1. Play with pitch (10 minutes). Stretch a rubber band tighter: higher or lower? Cut a straw shorter: higher or lower? Add water to a glass: higher or lower? Write down the rule for each.
2. Sketch your invention. Combine ideas: a box with rubber bands AND a row of straw pipes on the side; a set of jars mounted on a cardboard stand.
3. Build the body. For strings, stretch rubber bands over an open box and slide a pencil under them as a bridge. For winds, tape straws side by side in a row from longest to shortest.
4. Tune it (15 minutes). Make each note step up from the last. Hum "do, re, mi" and adjust (tighter bands, shorter straws, more water) until it matches.
5. Name your instrument and label each note on it with tape: 1, 2, 3, 4...
6. Compose a short tune. Write it as numbers: "1 1 5 5 6 6 5." Try a few until one sounds good.
7. Practice it until you can play it through without stopping.

## Make it yours
- Make it play chords (two or three notes at once) by plucking or blowing together.
- Add a percussion part with a built-in drum or shaker.
- Write a fake history of your instrument: "Invented in 1743 by a bored lighthouse keeper."

## Stuck?
- No clear note: rubber bands need a bridge to ring. Straws need a sharp, steady blow across or into the end.
- Notes are all the same: make bigger differences in length, tension, or water level.
- Glasses slosh over: use less water and tap gently on the side, not the rim.

## Show it off
Give a concert. Introduce the instrument (and its fake history), then play your tune. Invite someone to try it.

## Clean up
Pour out water, wipe spills, and keep your instrument somewhere safe for the next concert.

## For the grown-up
This combines the physics of sound (pitch depends on length, tension, and size) with music composition. It may get noisy; a closed door helps. **Ask at dinner:** "What makes a note go higher on your instrument?"
