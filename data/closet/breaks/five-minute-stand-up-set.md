title: Five-Minute Stand-Up Set
grade: 10
order: 8
kind: Pretend
time: 90-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: paper, a pen, index cards, a "microphone" (a hairbrush or wooden spoon), a lamp for a spotlight
nice: a stool, a clock to time yourself, a mirror

## The big idea
Stand-up comics make it look like they're just talking. They're not. Every laugh is planned: a **setup**, a **punchline**, a **tag**, and a lot of rewriting. You'll write, polish and perform a five-minute set of clean, original material about your actual life. The living room is your comedy club.

## Goals
- A written five-minute set with at least 3 bits (topics).
- One live performance.

## Objectives
- I can write jokes with a setup that creates an expectation and a punchline that breaks it.
- I can use the rule of three and a callback.
- I can perform with timing, pauses and confidence.

## Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Write "What's weird about my life?" at the top of a page.

## Steps
1. **Mine for material** (15 minutes). List 20 things you've noticed that bug you, confuse you or make you laugh: group chats, little siblings, cafeteria rules, your grandpa's texting style, being the shortest one.
2. Pick the 3 with the most energy. Under each one, write what's true and specific. Comedy lives in details.
3. **Write jokes.** A setup states something everyone believes. The punchline flips it. Setup: "My mom says I'm on my phone too much." Punchline: "She tells me this by text."
4. Use the **rule of three**: two normal things and one surprising one. "My dog has three moods: hungry, asleep, and hungry while asleep."
5. Add **tags**: extra punchlines after the first one, so one setup gets two or three laughs.
6. Plan a **callback**: bring back a joke from early in the set at the very end.
7. Write one word per bit on index cards to keep you on track. Practice out loud 4 times with a clock. Cut anything that drags (30 minutes).
8. Perform it. Pause after punchlines. Never laugh at your own joke first.

## Make it yours
- Do a "character" set as someone else: a time traveler from 1850 who just discovered toasters.
- Write a whole set on one topic, start to finish.
- Add crowd work: a bit where you ask the audience a question and riff on the answer.

## Stuck?
- It's not funny on paper: jokes often look weak until they're said out loud. Test them before you cut them.
- A joke punches down: aim at yourself, situations or the powerful. Never at someone's identity.

## Show it off
Do a "comedy night" with a lamp spotlight. Ask a family member to introduce you.

## Clean up
Keep your joke notebook. Comedians save everything, even the bits that bombed.

## For the grown-up
Stand-up builds writing skills (economy, structure, word choice), public speaking and confidence. Laughing helps. Your teen should keep it clean and kind, and aim jokes at themselves or situations, not at people's identities. **Ask at dinner:** "What was your best joke, and where did it come from?"
