title: Mock Supreme Court
grade: 11
order: 27
kind: Pretend
time: 75-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 2+
materials: paper, pens, a table and chairs, a timer, at least one partner (a sibling, a friend, or grown-ups after dinner)
nice: a robe (a bathrobe works), a wooden spoon gavel, name cards

## The big idea
The year is 2071. A robot has painted a masterpiece and wants to own it. A town banned pets that can fly. A student
claims a dream they recorded is private property. These cases have reached the highest court, and you are writing
the case, arguing it and ruling on it, using the real way appeals are argued.

## Goals
- A one-page fictional case with a clear question and two sides.
- A hosted oral argument with a written ruling at the end.

## Objectives
- I can frame a legal question that has strong arguments on both sides.
- I can build an argument using reasons, examples and precedent.
- I can write a short ruling that explains a decision.

## Get ready
Arrange a "bench" (a table with chairs behind it) facing two seats for lawyers. Decide who is a justice and who
argues. With two people, you can each argue, then both rule together.

## Steps
1. Write the case (20 minutes). Name it like a real case: "Unit 7 v. Gallery of Modern Art." Give the facts in five
   sentences and the **question**: "Can a machine own something it creates?"
2. Each side writes three main points and one example, like a past made-up ruling (**precedent**).
3. Practice a two-minute opening statement.
4. Hold the argument. Each lawyer gets five minutes. Justices can interrupt with questions at any time, just like in
   real courts.
5. Justices step away, discuss for five minutes, then vote.
6. Write the ruling (15 minutes): the decision, two reasons, and a **dissent** from any justice who disagreed.

## Make it yours
- Hold a whole season of cases where earlier rulings become precedent for later ones.
- Make the case about something in your house: "Can the youngest sibling claim the remote control?"
- Add a jury of stuffed animals that can be "persuaded" by the best performance.

## Stuck?
- One side is too weak: change the facts so each side has a good point.
- The lawyers run out of things to say: the justices ask harder questions.

## Show it off
Read the ruling and dissent aloud at dinner and ask everyone how they would have voted.

## Clean up
Put chairs back and keep the case files for a future season.

## For the grown-up
This builds civics, argument, public speaking and listening skills, with a feel for how real courts weigh both
sides. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the best argument the side you voted against made?"
