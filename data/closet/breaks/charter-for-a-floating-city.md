title: Charter for a Floating City
grade: 9
order: 14
kind: Pretend
time: 60-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: paper, pens, index cards or scraps, a large sheet of paper or the back of a poster
nice: a gavel (a wooden spoon), costumes or name tags, people at home to play citizens

## The big idea
A city of 5,000 people has just been built on a ring of platforms in the middle of the ocean. No country owns it. You're one of the founders, and there are no rules yet. Who decides things? What happens when two people disagree? What if a storm hits? You'll write the city's founding charter and then test it in a town meeting full of trouble.

## Goals
- A one-page charter with a government structure and at least 5 rights.
- A town meeting role-play where the charter faces three crisis cards.

## Objectives
- I can compare forms of government, like direct democracy and representative democracy.
- I can write rules that are clear enough to settle a real argument.
- I can play a role and argue a point of view that isn't mine.

## Get ready
Draw a quick map of the city: 6 platforms, each with a purpose (fishing docks, school, farms, power station, homes, harbor). Gather paper and cards.

## Steps
1. **Choose the structure** (15 minutes). Will every citizen vote on everything, or will people elect a council? How long do leaders serve? Who can't hold power? Write your reasons.
2. Write the **rights**: at least 5 things no law can take away. Be specific: "Every citizen can speak at an open meeting once a month."
3. Write how a law gets made and how the charter itself can be changed. Make it possible, but hard.
4. Write how disputes are settled: a judge, a jury of 7 citizens, a council vote.
5. **Make crisis cards** (10 minutes). Write 6 on index cards: "A storm destroys the farm platform. Who pays to rebuild?" "A ship of 300 refugees asks to dock." "The power station workers go on strike."
6. **Town meeting.** Draw a card. Play at least three citizens with different views: a fisher, a teacher, a newcomer. If family is home, hand them roles. Use your charter to decide (30 minutes).
7. After each crisis, note any gap the charter didn't cover and write an **amendment**.

## Make it yours
- Write the charter's opening statement, the way founding documents begin with a big promise.
- Run it as two rival founder groups, each with its own charter, and see which one handles crises better.
- Jump ahead 50 years and write a newspaper front page from the city's future.

## Stuck?
- Every crisis ends in a tie: add a tie-breaking rule, like the council president's vote.
- It feels like a textbook: play the citizens as big, specific characters with names and grudges.

## Show it off
Read the charter aloud at dinner, then let the family draw one crisis card and vote on it.

## Clean up
Fold the map and charter into a folder. You may want to add amendments later.

## For the grown-up
This is civics through play: forms of government, rights, checks on power and compromise, all topics in high school government and history. It also builds argument and empathy. **Ask at dinner:** "Which crisis broke your charter, and how did you fix it?"
