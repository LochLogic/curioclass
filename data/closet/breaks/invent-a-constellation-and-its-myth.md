title: Invent a Constellation and Its Myth
grade: 6
order: 30
kind: Story
time: 60-90 min
where: Either
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: black or dark paper (or white paper colored dark with pencil), white paper, a pencil, a white crayon or light colored pencil, a pin or pushpin, a flashlight
nice: a clear night and a window or doorway with a view of the sky, a star chart from a book

## The big idea
Thousands of years ago, people looked at the stars and connected the dots: a hunter, a bear, a swan. Then they told stories about how those figures ended up in the sky. Now it's your turn. Invent a constellation and write the myth that explains it.

## Goals
- You design an original constellation with at least 7 stars.
- You write a myth about how your figure got into the sky, and present it like an ancient storyteller.

## Objectives
- I can create a constellation by connecting points into a shape.
- I can write a myth that explains something in nature.
- I can tell a story aloud with expression.

## Get ready
If it's a clear night, look out a window or step outside with a grown-up's okay. Notice which stars are bright and which are faint. If it's cloudy or daytime, sprinkle dots on paper with your eyes closed and use those as your "sky."

## Steps
1. Find your shape. Look at the real sky (or your dot sheet) and find 7 to 12 points that make a figure: a fox, a teapot, a giant, a ship, a skateboard.
2. Draw it on dark paper with a light crayon. Make bright stars as big dots and faint stars as small dots. Connect them with thin lines.
3. Name it. Ancient names often had a character: "The Wandering Fox," "The Weaver's Broken Spindle."
4. Write the myth (25 minutes). Answer: who was this character on Earth? What did they do (brave, foolish, kind)? Why did they get placed in the sky as a reward, punishment, or to protect someone?
5. Add a "why" detail. Myths often explain something: "That's why the fox's star blinks: it's still looking for its way home."
6. Make a star projector card. Poke a pinhole through each star on your drawing. In a dark room, hold a flashlight behind it and shine your constellation on the ceiling.
7. Practice telling the myth out loud, slowly, like it's been told for a thousand years.

## Make it yours
- Invent a second constellation nearby that's part of the same myth, like a rival or a friend.
- Write a "star map" page for your whole made-up sky with three constellations.
- Tell the myth from the point of view of the star itself.

## Stuck?
- Your dots don't look like anything: turn the page around. Shapes often appear upside down.
- The myth is too short: add a problem the character faced before they reached the sky.
- No clear sky: the dot-sheet method works just as well.

## Show it off
Turn off the lights, shine your constellation on the ceiling, and tell the myth to your family like an ancient storyteller.

## Clean up
Put away the pin safely and keep your star card for another night.

## For the grown-up
This mixes astronomy (real constellations and how people named them) with writing a myth, which shows up in sixth grade reading. If your child wants to go outside at night, please go with them or agree on a safe spot. Keep pins away from younger children. **Ask at dinner:** "How did your constellation end up in the sky?"
