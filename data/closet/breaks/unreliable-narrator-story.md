title: Unreliable Narrator Story
grade: 11
order: 2
kind: Story
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: paper or a notebook, two pens in different colors, sticky notes or scrap paper
nice: a timer, a highlighter

## The big idea
Some of the best stories are told by someone who is lying, wrong, or hiding something, and the reader slowly figures
it out. You are going to write a short story where the narrator says one thing and the clues say another. The trick
is to play fair: every clue is on the page, and the reader still does not see it coming.

## Goals
- A finished short story, 800 to 1,500 words, told by a narrator who cannot be trusted.
- A second reader who catches the truth only at the end, or on a reread.

## Objectives
- I can plant clues that mean one thing on a first read and another on a second.
- I can give a narrator a reason to mislead, whether on purpose or by self-deception.
- I can control what the reader knows and when they learn it.

## Get ready
Find a quiet spot. Draw a line down the middle of a page. Label the left side "What the narrator says" and the right
side "What really happened."

## Steps
1. Pick your narrator type. A **liar** (knows the truth, hides it), a **self-deceiver** (believes their own version),
   or a **limited** narrator (a child, a pet, a stranger who misreads everything).
2. Write the real events first (10 minutes): five or six plain sentences on the right side. Example: "Dev broke the
   window. He blamed his brother. His brother got grounded."
3. Write the narrator's version on the left. Example: "Some things just happen around me. Windows, for example."
4. Plant three clues. Ideas: a detail the narrator mentions and quickly drops, other characters reacting oddly, or a
   time gap ("Anyway, by Tuesday it was sorted").
5. Draft the story (35 minutes). First person, past tense is easiest. Stay in the narrator's head the whole time.
6. Write the reveal. It can be one line, a final action, or a quiet image. Do not explain it.
7. Reread in the second color pen and underline every clue. If you can spot them in under a minute, bury one deeper.

## Make it yours
- Tell it as a diary with dates, and let the gaps between entries do the lying.
- Make the narrator an object, like a house, a ring or a ship's log, that misunderstands the humans around it.
- Write a second short version from another character's view that exposes the first.

## Stuck?
- The twist is too obvious: cut your two most direct clues and keep only the subtle one.
- The story feels flat: give the narrator a strong want, like being liked, being innocent or being right.

## Show it off
Hand it to someone at home with no setup. When they finish, ask when they first got suspicious, and show them your
underlined clues.

## Clean up
Keep your clue map with the draft. It is a useful planning tool for later stories.

## For the grown-up
This is literary craft at the level of high school English: point of view, dramatic irony and foreshadowing, built
by writing instead of analyzing. It is also good practice at revision. **Ask at dinner:** "What's the clue you are
proudest of hiding?"
