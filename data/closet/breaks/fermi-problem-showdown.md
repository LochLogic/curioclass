title: Fermi Problem Showdown
grade: 11
order: 16
kind: Puzzle
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1+
materials: paper, a pencil, a ruler or tape measure, a kitchen scale if you have one, a calculator or long math on paper
nice: a bag of rice or dry beans, a measuring cup, a friend or family member to compete

## The big idea
How many grains of rice would fill your bedroom? How many heartbeats have you had? The physicist Enrico Fermi could
estimate wild things like this in minutes by breaking them into smaller guesses he could reason through. Today you
will tackle five outrageous questions and see how close a clever estimate can get.

## Goals
- Five finished estimates, each with a clear chain of reasoning.
- One estimate checked with a real measurement.

## Objectives
- I can break a big question into smaller ones I can estimate.
- I can use powers of ten to keep numbers manageable.
- I can check whether an answer is within a factor of ten.

## Get ready
Write five questions on a sheet. Starters: grains of rice to fill your room, number of words you have spoken in your
life, how many sheets of paper reach the Moon if stacked, liters of water your family uses in a year, how many
heartbeats you have had.

## Steps
1. Try the first one as a warm-up (10 minutes). Grains of rice in a room: measure the room's volume, estimate grains
   in one cup, and convert.
2. Write the chain. Every number gets a label and a reason. Example: "Room: 3 m by 4 m by 2.5 m = 30 cubic meters."
3. Use powers of ten. Round to 1, 2 or 5 times a power of ten. 30 cubic meters is 3 x 10^1.
4. Do the remaining four (35 minutes). Write each as a small tree of guesses.
5. Check one. If you have rice, count the grains in a tablespoon (or a smaller spoon) and scale up. How far off was
   your guess?
6. Rate your confidence for each: within 10x, within 100x, or wild.

## Make it yours
- Make a Fermi showdown: you and a family member answer the same question separately, then compare chains.
- Invent five strange questions of your own, like "how many cats fit in the house?"
- Estimate something real and useful: how much money the family spends on snacks in a year.

## Stuck?
- You have no idea where to start: ask "what would I need to know to answer this?" and write those things down.
- Numbers are huge: write them in powers of ten and add exponents when you multiply.

## Show it off
Read one question at dinner and let everyone guess before you reveal your reasoning and answer.

## Clean up
Put the rice back and keep your estimate sheets.

## For the grown-up
This is estimation and scientific reasoning used by physicists and engineers, plus scientific notation and unit
conversion. **Ask at dinner:** "Which estimate surprised you the most?"
