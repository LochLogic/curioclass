title: Take Apart an Old Gadget
grade: 12
order: 21
kind: Science
time: 60-90 min
where: Indoors
mess: Medium
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: an old, broken, battery-powered or unplugged gadget nobody wants (a toy, a remote, a keyboard, a calculator, a computer mouse, a clock, a cassette or disc player), small screwdrivers, a muffin tin or egg carton for screws, paper and a pen, a towel to work on
nice: a magnifying glass, pliers, a magnet, a flashlight

## The big idea
Engineers call it **reverse engineering**: taking something apart to understand how it was designed. Inside every
gadget is a story of choices, like where to save money, how to make it easy to build, and how to make it last (or not).
Open one up, map what every part does, and figure out the designer's mind.

## Goals
- A gadget fully disassembled, with its parts sorted and labeled.
- A labeled diagram showing how the parts work together.

## Objectives
- I can disassemble a device carefully and track the order of parts.
- I can identify common components like circuit boards, motors, switches, springs and gears.
- I can explain how a device works from input to output.

## Get ready
Check with a grown-up that the gadget is truly unwanted and that it is not a wall-plug device with a big power
supply, a microwave, an old tube TV or anything with a swollen or rechargeable battery. Remove any AA or AAA batteries.
Lay a towel down so screws do not roll away.

## Steps
1. Sketch the outside (5 minutes). Note every button, light and port.
2. Find the screws. Many hide under rubber feet or stickers. Put screws from each step into a separate muffin cup.
3. Open it slowly. Pry gently at seams. Take a quick sketch before you remove each layer.
4. Identify the parts (25 minutes). Common finds: a **circuit board** (green or brown, with tiny parts), a small
   **motor**, **gears**, a **speaker** (has a magnet), **switches** under buttons, and rubber pads that complete a
   circuit when pressed.
5. Trace the path: what happens from a button press to a light or sound? Draw arrows.
6. Look for the designer's choices: what is glued vs screwed? Was it made to be repaired?
7. Make your final diagram, labeling each part and its job.

## Make it yours
- Make a "Frankenstein" sculpture or creature from the parts.
- Rebuild it, and see if it works again (bonus if it was broken).
- Write a "design review": what you would change to make it last longer or easier to repair.

## Stuck?
- It will not open: check under labels and feet again, or look for clips you need to press while pulling.
- You have extra screws: check your sketches and muffin cups to see where they belong.

## Show it off
Lay the parts out like an exploded diagram on the table and walk the family through how it worked.

## Clean up
Bag the parts, put tools away, and take electronic scraps to e-waste recycling when you can (not the trash).

## For the grown-up
This builds engineering thinking, mechanical understanding and curiosity about how things work. Only small,
unplugged, battery-free devices are safe. Avoid microwaves, old TVs, large power supplies and lithium or swollen
batteries; never puncture a battery. **Ask at dinner:** "What surprised you most inside the gadget?"
