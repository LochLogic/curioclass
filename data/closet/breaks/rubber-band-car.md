title: Rubber Band Car
grade: 8
order: 1
kind: Build
time: 60-120 min
where: Either
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: a piece of stiff cardboard about 10 by 25 cm, 2 drinking straws, 2 wooden skewers or thin dowels (or 2 long pencils), 4 bottle caps or cardboard circles for wheels, several rubber bands of different sizes, masking tape, scissors, a ruler, a pushpin or nail to poke holes, a tape measure
nice: old CDs or jar lids for big back wheels, a hot glue gun (with permission), balloons cut into rings for tire grip

## The big idea
A stretched rubber band is stored energy waiting for a job. Your job is to give it one: build a car that winds up, lets go, and drives itself across the floor. Then tune it until it goes farther than you thought cardboard could.

## Goals
- A car that drives at least 2 meters on rubber band power alone.
- A test log showing what you changed and how far each version went.

## Objectives
- I can explain how stored (elastic) energy turns into motion.
- I can change one variable at a time and measure the result.
- I can reduce friction and improve traction in a design.

## Get ready
Clear a smooth, straight test track at least 4 meters long: a hallway, a hard floor, or a driveway. Put a strip of tape at the start line.

## Steps
1. **Make the chassis.** Cut the straws to the width of the cardboard and tape one across the front and one across the back on the underside. They're your axle holders (bearings).
2. **Make the wheels.** Poke a hole in the exact center of each cap or circle. An off-center hole makes the car wobble, so measure.
3. **Add axles.** Slide a skewer through each straw and push a wheel on each end. Tape or glue the wheels so they turn with the axle. Give it a push: it should roll straight and easily.
4. **Rig the motor.** Cut a notch in the front of the chassis. Tape one end of a rubber band to the middle of the rear axle (or hook it on a small piece of tape stuck to the axle). Stretch the other end to the front notch.
5. **Wind and launch.** Roll the car backward so the band wraps around the rear axle. Set it down and let go. Measure the distance.
6. **Tune it (40 minutes).** Now experiment, changing one thing per round and running three tests each time: two bands instead of one, a longer or shorter band, bigger rear wheels, rubber "tires" for grip, a lighter chassis. Record the best distance for each change.
7. **Final build.** Combine your best changes into one ultimate car and do three official runs.

## Make it yours
- Go for speed instead of distance: time it over 2 meters.
- Build a car that carries a "passenger" (an egg-sized ball of foil or play dough) without dropping it.
- Design a body for it: a race car, a beetle, a moon rover, cut and folded from cardboard.

## Stuck?
- The wheels spin but the car doesn't move: add grip. Wrap a rubber band or ring of balloon around the back wheels.
- It veers to one side: the axles aren't parallel. Re-tape the straws using a ruler.
- It barely rolls: friction. Make sure the axles turn freely inside the straws and the wheels don't rub the chassis.

## Show it off
Hold a "grand prix" for your family: three official runs, a measured finish line, and a trophy you make for yourself (or for whoever wins with your car).

## Clean up
Sweep up scraps, put the skewers away carefully, and park your car on a shelf.

## For the grown-up
This is physics and engineering: energy transfer, friction and traction, and fair testing, all in eighth grade science. Skewers are sharp, so trimming the points with scissors is a good idea. A glue gun is fine with your permission. **Ask at dinner:** "What was the one change that made the biggest difference?"
