title: Fairy Tale from the Villain's Side
grade: 10
order: 12
kind: Story
time: 60-120 min
where: Indoors
mess: Low
grownup: On your own
players: 1
materials: paper, a pen
nice: a book of old fairy tales, index cards for notes

## The big idea
Every villain is the hero of their own story. The wolf was hungry. The stepmother was scared of being forgotten. The giant was minding his own business when a boy climbed into his house and robbed him. You'll retell a classic fairy tale in the first person, from the villain's side, and make readers question everything they thought they knew.

## Goals
- A retold fairy tale of 1,000 to 1,500 words narrated by the villain.
- A story where the reader understands the villain, even if they don't forgive them.

## Objectives
- I can write in first person with a strong, consistent voice.
- I can use an **unreliable narrator**, someone whose version can't be fully trusted.
- I can build sympathy without excusing harmful choices.

## Get ready
Pick a fairy tale you know well. Write out the main events of the original version in 6 bullet points.

## Steps
1. **Interview your villain** (10 minutes). Write their answers to: What did you want? What were you afraid of? What do you think everyone gets wrong about you? What's one thing you regret?
2. **Find the voice.** Is your villain proud? Sarcastic? Tired? Write 5 sentences in their voice, about breakfast. Pick the voice that's most fun.
3. **Pick the moments** that change from their side. In the original, the giant chases Jack. From the giant's side, Jack is a thief who broke into his home.
4. Decide what your narrator **hides** or twists. Readers should catch them leaving things out.
5. **Draft** (40 minutes). Open with a line that grabs: "I'd like to set the record straight about the porridge."
6. Include one moment where the mask slips and readers see what the villain really did.
7. **End** with a line that makes readers rethink the whole story.

## Make it yours
- Tell it as a trial testimony, or as a letter to the hero 20 years later.
- Move the fairy tale to a modern setting: a big city, a school, a company.
- Write a short second section from the hero's side, arguing back.

## Stuck?
- The villain is just nice now: keep a real flaw. Sympathetic doesn't mean innocent.
- The voice keeps slipping: reread the first page aloud and match its tone.

## Show it off
Read it aloud at dinner without naming the fairy tale. See how fast they guess.

## Clean up
Keep the story and your villain interview in your notebook.

## For the grown-up
This builds English skills like point of view, voice, characterization and unreliable narration, and it encourages seeing multiple sides. **Ask at dinner:** "Do you think your villain was actually wrong?"
