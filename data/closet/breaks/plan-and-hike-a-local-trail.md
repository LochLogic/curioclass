materials: a map of a local park, trail or loop (a paper park map, a printed map from a grown-up, or one you draw), a backpack, water (at least 500 ml each), a snack, a jacket, a watch or clock, paper and a pen, a hiking partner (a friend, a sibling or a grown-up)
nice: a compass, a small first aid kit, a whistle, binoculars, a phone for emergencies

## The big idea
A real hike starts at a table with a map, long before your boots hit the trail. You'll plan a route, estimate the time like a mountain guide, pack the essentials, leave a trip plan at home, and then go. It's your expedition, with a partner and a plan.

## Goals
- A written trip plan with route, distance, time estimate and turnaround time.
- A completed hike with field notes from the trail.

## Objectives
- I can read a map's scale and estimate distance.
- I can estimate hiking time using a rule of thumb.
- I can pack and plan for safety.

## Get ready
Get permission to go, choose a partner, and pick a nearby trail, park loop or green path you're allowed to reach. Check the weather. Plan to stay on marked paths.

## Steps
1. **Measure the route** (10 minutes). Lay a piece of string along your route on the map, then compare it to the scale bar. Pick a route of 3 to 5 km for a 90-minute outing.
2. **Estimate time.** A common rule: about 5 km per hour on flat ground, plus 1 minute for every 10 meters of climbing. Add 15 minutes for breaks.
3. **Set a turnaround time.** If you haven't reached your end point by then, you turn back. Real hikers live by this.
4. **Pack the essentials**: water, a snack, a jacket, the map, the trip plan, and a phone for emergencies if you have one.
5. **Leave a trip plan** with a grown-up: where you're going, who with, your route, and when you'll be back. Then go.
6. **On the trail** (60 to 90 minutes). Every 15 minutes, stop and write a field note: the time, where you are on the map, and one thing you see, hear or smell.
7. **Back home**, compare your real time to your estimate. Were you faster or slower? Why?

## Make it yours
- Make it a quest: before you go, write five things to find, like a split tree, a bird's nest, or a bridge.
- Draw your own trail map afterward, with your own names for landmarks.
- Plan a sunrise or late-afternoon hike to see the light change (with permission and timing that gets you home before dark).

## Stuck?
- No trail nearby: plan a 3 to 5 km "urban hike" around your neighborhood using sidewalks and parks.
- You lose your place on the map: stop, find two landmarks you can see and match them to the map.

## Show it off
Show the family your map with the route drawn on and read your three best field notes.

## Clean up
Bring back everything you took. Wash out your water bottle and hang up wet gear.

## For the grown-up
This builds map reading, estimation, planning and judgment, plus confidence outdoors. Your teen should get your permission, go with a partner, leave you a written plan, stay on marked paths, and be home by the time they say. **Ask at dinner:** "Was your time estimate right? What slowed you down?"
