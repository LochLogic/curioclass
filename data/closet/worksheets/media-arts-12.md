# 1. Capstone Concepts
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can develop and compare short film concepts and choose one I can make well.
directions: Answer the questions about loglines. Develop three different short film concepts with a logline and a theme. Score each one for feasibility, then choose one and defend your choice.
mistakes: Students write loglines that are summaries of the whole plot or a theme in disguise ("a film about friendship"). A logline names a character, a goal and an obstacle in one sentence. Feasibility scores are often wishful: a car chase, crowds or night exteriors are hard for a student crew. Push students to name real locations, actors and gear they can get.
notes: A good capstone short is often 3 to 7 minutes with a few characters and one or two locations. Discuss safety and permissions: no risky stunts, permission to film at any location, and releases from anyone on camera. A concept with a lower score can still win if the student has a clear plan for its hardest part.

## Warm up
example: **Logline:** When her grandfather's hearing aid breaks the day before his recital, a shy 16-year-old must convince the town's grumpiest repairman to fix it overnight. **Theme:** asking for help takes more courage than doing it alone.
choice: Which is the strongest logline?
  - A film about friendship and growing up.
  * When the class hamster escapes the night before the science fair, two rival students must team up to find it before the doors open.
  - The main character has a long, sad day and then it ends.
  - This film uses lots of cool shots.
choice: What is the difference between a logline and a theme?
  * A logline tells who wants what and what stands in the way; a theme is the idea underneath the story.
  - They are the same thing.
  - A theme is always one word; a logline is always one word.
  - A logline is the title of the film.
choice: Which concept is most feasible for a student crew in one month?
  - A space battle with a hundred extras
  * Two friends arguing in a kitchen and on a bus stop bench
  - A car chase through a busy city at night
  - A film shot on three continents

## Main: Three concepts
text: For each concept write: working title, logline, theme, main locations, cast needed and the hardest part to film.
write: **Concept A**
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Each concept has a one-sentence logline (character, goal, obstacle), a theme stated as an idea, named real locations, a cast the student can get and an honest hardest part.
write: **Concept B** (different from A in genre, tone or setting)
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary; B should differ clearly from A, not be a variation of the same idea.
write: **Concept C** (different again)
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary; C should differ clearly from A and B.

## Main: Feasibility scores
text: Score each concept 1 (very hard for us) to 3 (very doable).
table: Criterion | Concept A | Concept B | Concept C
  - Locations we can use | | |
  - Actors we can get | | |
  - Gear we have | | |
  - Fits our time (4 to 6 weeks) | | |
  - Safe and allowed | | |
  - Total (out of 15) | | |

## Choose
write: Which concept will you make? Defend your choice with your scores and your theme, and explain how you will handle its hardest part.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the concept, cites the total and the most important scores, explains why the theme matters to the filmmaker or audience, and gives a concrete plan for the hardest part (for example, shooting the bus stop scene early on a Sunday with permission, using two friends as actors).

# 21. Portfolio Selection
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can select and sequence work for a portfolio to fit its audience.
directions: Read about Rosa's work and the portfolio she needs. Answer the questions. Choose 10 of her 16 pieces, put them in order, and give a reason for each. Then explain what you cut and why.
mistakes: Students include everything that looks busy, or keep near-duplicates. A portfolio is judged by its weakest piece, so cutting is a skill. Students also forget the order: open with a strong, complete project and end strong, and put a process page right beside the project it explains.
notes: Rosa and her work are fictional. The key shows one strong selection; other selections are fine if the reasons hold up. Five pieces have clear problems: 4 (near-duplicate), 6 (copied from a tutorial), 10 (a photo, not design work), 13 (unfinished, placeholder text), 14 (old, weaker work). The sixth cut is a judgment call. Students can apply the same steps to their own portfolios.

## Read: Rosa's work
example: Rosa is applying to a college graphic design program. It asks for **10 pieces** that show her best work, her range and how she thinks.
table: # | Piece | Notes
  - 1 | Bakery rebrand: logo, packaging, menu | Full system, polished, her strongest project
  - 2 | Sketch page for the bakery logo | Shows her process: 30 thumbnails, notes
  - 3 | Jazz band concert poster | Bold type, strong layout
  - 4 | Second jazz poster | Same layout as 3, only the color changed
  - 5 | Town recycling infographic | Clear data, clean icons
  - 6 | Copy of a famous poster made by following an online tutorial | Neat, but not her design
  - 7 | Three book covers for a public-domain novel | Strong series, consistent style
  - 8 | Library app screen mockups | Shows interface design range
  - 9 | Hand-lettered alphabet study | Good skill, plain presentation
  - 10 | Unedited photo of her dog | Cute, but not design work
  - 11 | School wayfinding icon set | Shows system thinking
  - 12 | Zine about neighborhood history | Editorial layout, original writing
  - 13 | Magazine spread | Half done, placeholder text still in it
  - 14 | Birthday invitation from 8th grade | Older work, weak type
  - 15 | Animated bakery logo (shown as stills) | Shows motion range
  - 16 | Packaging for a fictional tea brand | Polished, strong color

## Warm up
choice: Why cut piece 4?
  - It is too colorful
  * It nearly repeats piece 3, so it adds nothing new
  - Posters are not allowed
  - It is too new
choice: Where should piece 2, the sketch page, go?
  - First, to open the portfolio
  - Last, to end it
  * Right after the bakery rebrand it explains
  - It should be cut

## Main: Select and order
chart: Order (1 to 10) | Piece # | Why it is in | Why it goes here
  rows: 10
  key: One strong order: 1, 2, 15 (or 9), 7, 5, 3, 12, 8, 11, 16. The bakery rebrand opens as the strongest complete project, the sketch page and animated logo stay beside it, the middle shows range (book covers, infographic, poster, zine, app, icons) and the polished tea packaging closes.
write: Which six pieces did you cut? Give a short reason for each.
  lines: 4
  key: Cut 4 (near-duplicate of 3), 6 (copied from a tutorial), 10 (a photo, not design), 13 (unfinished, placeholder text), 14 (old, weaker work). The sixth is a judgment call: 9 (plain presentation) or 15 (overlaps the bakery project). One strong order: 1 bakery rebrand, 2 sketch page, 15 animated logo (or 9), 7 book covers, 5 infographic, 3 jazz poster, 12 zine, 8 app mockups, 11 wayfinding icons, 16 tea packaging. It opens and closes with polished projects and keeps process beside its project.
