# 1. Capstone Concepts
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can develop and compare short film concepts and choose one I can make well.
directions: Answer the questions about loglines. Develop three different short film concepts with a logline and a theme. Score each one for feasibility, then choose one and defend your choice.
mistakes: Students write loglines that are summaries of the whole plot or a theme in disguise ("a film about friendship"). A logline names a character, a goal and an obstacle in one sentence. Feasibility scores are often wishful: a car chase, crowds or night exteriors are hard for a student crew. Push students to name real locations, actors and gear they can get.
notes: A good capstone short is often 3 to 7 minutes with a few characters and one or two locations. Discuss safety and permissions: no risky stunts, permission to film at any location, and releases from anyone on camera. A concept with a lower score can still win if the student has a clear plan for its hardest part.

## Warm up
example: **Logline:** When her grandfather's hearing aid breaks the day before his recital, a shy 16-year-old must convince the town's grumpiest repairman to fix it overnight. **Theme:** asking for help takes more courage than doing it alone.
choice: Which is the strongest logline?
  - A film about friendship and growing up.
  * When the class hamster escapes the night before the science fair, two rival students must team up to find it before the doors open.
  - The main character has a long, sad day and then it ends.
  - This film uses lots of cool shots.
choice: What is the difference between a logline and a theme?
  * A logline tells who wants what and what stands in the way; a theme is the idea underneath the story.
  - They are the same thing.
  - A theme is always one word; a logline is always one word.
  - A logline is the title of the film.
choice: Which concept is most feasible for a student crew in one month?
  - A space battle with a hundred extras
  * Two friends arguing in a kitchen and on a bus stop bench
  - A car chase through a busy city at night
  - A film shot on three continents

## Main: Three concepts
text: For each concept write: working title, logline, theme, main locations, cast needed and the hardest part to film.
write: **Concept A**
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Each concept has a one-sentence logline (character, goal, obstacle), a theme stated as an idea, named real locations, a cast the student can get and an honest hardest part.
write: **Concept B** (different from A in genre, tone or setting)
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary; B should differ clearly from A, not be a variation of the same idea.
write: **Concept C** (different again)
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary; C should differ clearly from A and B.

## Main: Feasibility scores
text: Score each concept 1 (very hard for us) to 3 (very doable).
table: Criterion | Concept A | Concept B | Concept C
  - Locations we can use | | |
  - Actors we can get | | |
  - Gear we have | | |
  - Fits our time (4 to 6 weeks) | | |
  - Safe and allowed | | |
  - Total (out of 15) | | |

## Choose
write: Which concept will you make? Defend your choice with your scores and your theme, and explain how you will handle its hardest part.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the concept, cites the total and the most important scores, explains why the theme matters to the filmmaker or audience, and gives a concrete plan for the hardest part (for example, shooting the bus stop scene early on a Sunday with permission, using two friends as actors).

# 7. Sound Spotting
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can spot a scene for sound and write a spotting list with timecodes and durations.
directions: Practice timecode math at 24 frames per second. Read the picture notes for the scene, then complete the spotting list: the type of each cue, its timecode in and out, and its duration. Answer the questions about the choices.
mistakes: Timecode is not a decimal: after frame 23 comes the next second, so 01:00:05:23 plus 1 frame is 01:00:06:00. The safest way to subtract is to turn both timecodes into total frames (seconds x 24 + frames), subtract, then turn the answer back into seconds and frames. Students also mix up the cue types: ambience is the background bed of a place, Foley is sound performed to match an action on screen (like footsteps), and sound effects are specific sounds such as a door or a machine.
notes: Timecode reads hours:minutes:seconds:frames. Many edits start their program at 01:00:00:00, so this scene runs from 01:00:00:00 to 01:01:00:00. A spotting session is when the director, editor, composer and sound team watch the cut together and decide where each sound and music cue goes. The scene is made up. If you have a short scene from a film in class, students can spot it with the same chart.

## Warm up: Timecode math at 24 fps
example: How long is 01:00:05:18 to 01:00:07:06? In frames: 7 x 24 + 6 = 174, and 5 x 24 + 18 = 138. 174 - 138 = 36 frames = 1 second 12 frames, written 1:12.
fill:
  - 01:00:10:20 plus 8 frames is 01:00:{11:04}
  - 2 seconds 12 frames is {60} frames.
  - 01:00:30:00 to 01:00:32:06 lasts {2:06} (seconds:frames).

## Main: Picture notes
text: **Night Shift at the Lighthouse** (01:00:00:00 to 01:01:00:00). Wind and surf are heard through the whole scene. At 01:00:04:12 the keeper opens a creaky iron door; the creak ends at 01:00:06:00. She climbs the metal stairs from 01:00:06:00 to 01:00:14:18. At 01:00:15:06 the radio crackles with static for exactly 6 seconds. Tense strings begin at 01:00:20:00 as she sees the lamp is dark, and end at 01:00:41:12 on the clunk of the lamp mechanism restarting, which lasts until 01:00:43:00. A warm theme plays from 01:00:43:00 to the end of the scene.

## Main: Spotting list
table: Cue | Type (ambience, SFX, Foley, music) | Description | TC in | TC out | Duration (s:f)
  - 1 | {ambience} | wind and surf | 01:00:00:00 | {01:01:00:00} | {60:00}
  - 2 | {SFX} | iron door creak | {01:00:04:12} | 01:00:06:00 | {1:12}
  - 3 | {Foley} | {footsteps on metal stairs} | 01:00:06:00 | {01:00:14:18} | {8:18}
  - 4 | {SFX} | radio static | {01:00:15:06} | {01:00:21:06} | 6:00
  - 5 | {music} | {tense strings} | {01:00:20:00} | 01:00:41:12 | {21:12}
  - 6 | {SFX} | lamp mechanism clunk | 01:00:41:12 | {01:00:43:00} | {1:12}
  - 7 | {music} | {warm theme} | 01:00:43:00 | 01:01:00:00 | {17:00}

## Main: Questions
choice: Apart from the ambience under everything, which two cues overlap?
  - 2 and 3
  * 4 and 5
  - 5 and 7
  - 6 and 7
choice: Why does the music change exactly when the lamp clunks on?
  - The composer ran out of strings
  - Music must change every 20 seconds
  * The sound marks the turn in the story, from danger to relief, so the music turns with it
  - The clunk covers a mistake in the edit
choice: Why is the climb spotted as Foley, not as a library sound effect?
  * The footsteps must match the keeper's exact steps on screen, so they are performed to picture
  - Foley is always louder
  - Footsteps cannot be recorded
  - Libraries do not have footsteps

## Stretch
write: Add one more cue that would make the scene stronger. Give its type, description, TC in and out, and the reason for it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: SFX, a gull cry, 01:00:02:00 to 01:00:03:00, to set the place before the door opens. A strong answer has a TC out after its TC in, fits inside the scene, and explains what the sound adds to place, mood or story.

# 20. Copyright, Trademark, License
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain copyright, trademark and licenses and decide what a designer may do in common situations.
directions: Read the passage. Then decide each of the 6 scenarios and explain your reasoning for the last one.
mistakes: Students think anything online is free to use, or that giving credit always makes a use legal. Credit is required by some licenses (like CC BY) but does not replace permission. Students also mix up copyright and trademark: copyright protects creative expression; trademark protects brand identity so customers are not confused.
notes: This sheet describes U.S. law in general terms; it is not legal advice, and other countries differ. Facts: copyright protects original works fixed in a tangible form, automatically; registration is required before suing over a U.S. work; ideas, names, titles, short phrases and slogans are not covered by copyright; works by individual creators last for life plus 70 years. As of January 1, 2026, works published in the United States in 1930 or earlier are generally in the public domain. Typeface designs are not protected by U.S. copyright, but font software is. All scenarios and businesses are made up. Extension: have students keep a license record (what it is, where it came from, its license, what the license allows) for every image, font and sound in their next design.

## Read
passage: Three Rules in Every Design Studio
  Every time a designer opens a blank page, three kinds of rules are already in the room: copyright, trademark and licenses. Knowing the difference keeps your work, and your clients, out of trouble.
  Copyright protects original creative works, such as photos, illustrations, songs, films, writing and computer code, as soon as they are fixed in a form others can see or hear: saved to a file, painted on a canvas, recorded. In the United States you do not need to register or add a © symbol to own a copyright, although registration is needed before you can sue over a U.S. work. Copyright protects expression, not ideas: anyone may paint a sunset over a city, but no one may copy your painting of one. Names, titles, short phrases and slogans are not protected by copyright. For works made by an individual today, copyright lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years. After that, a work enters the public domain, where anyone may use it freely; in 2026, that includes most works published in the United States before 1931.
  A trademark is a word, logo, slogan, sound or even a color that tells customers who makes a product or service. Trademark law exists to prevent confusion: a new business cannot use a name and logo so close to a famous company's that customers might think the two are connected. Rights come from using the mark in business, and registering it with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office makes them stronger. The ® symbol may be used only after registration, while ™ simply claims a mark. A trademark can last as long as it stays in use, and a logo can be protected both by copyright, as artwork, and by trademark, as a brand.
  A license is permission from an owner to use a work on certain terms. Stock photo sites, music libraries and font makers all sell licenses, and the terms matter: a font licensed for print may not cover a website. In the United States the design of a typeface is not protected by copyright, but font software is, so copying a font file without a license is not allowed. Creative Commons licenses let creators give permission in advance. CC BY allows any use, even commercial, as long as you credit the creator. NC means noncommercial use only, ND means no changes, and SA means anything you make from the work must be shared under the same license. CC0 places a work in the public domain.
  When none of these permissions applies, a designer may still be able to rely on fair use for commentary, criticism or parody, but fair use is decided case by case. The safest habit is simple: know where every image, font and sound came from, and keep a record of its license.

## Main: Six scenarios
choice: A designer uses a CC BY photo on a poster for a paid concert and credits the photographer. May she?
  - No, because the concert charges money
  * Yes, because CC BY allows commercial use with credit
  - Only if she changes the photo
  - Only after the photographer dies
choice: A student prints a CC BY-NC illustration on T-shirts and sells them. May he?
  - Yes, as long as he credits the artist
  - Yes, because T-shirts are not posters
  - Yes, because the illustration is online
  * No, not without separate permission, because NC means noncommercial only
choice: A new juice bar uses a name and logo nearly identical to a well-known smoothie chain's. What is the main problem?
  * Trademark: customers may think the two businesses are connected
  - Copyright: the juice bar copied the recipe
  - License: the juice bar did not credit the chain
  - There is no problem if the juice bar is small
choice: A designer uses a poster first published in the United States in 1925 in a collage for a client. May she?
  - No, every poster is under copyright forever
  - Only if she pays the printer
  * Yes, because the poster is in the public domain
  - Only if the collage is noncommercial
choice: A designer copies a font file from a friend's laptop to use on a client's website. What is the problem?
  - None, because typeface designs are not copyrighted
  * The font software is licensed, and he has no license to install or use it
  - Fonts can only be used in print
  - It is a trademark problem
choice: A bakery owner wants to copyright the slogan "Fresh Every Morning." What should the designer tell her?
  - It is copyrighted automatically as soon as she says it
  - She must add a © symbol to protect it
  - Slogans can never be protected in any way
  * Short slogans cannot be copyrighted, but it may be protected as a trademark if she uses it to identify her bakery
write: In scenario 6, explain why the law treats a slogan differently from a poster design. Use the passage.
  lines: 3
  key: Copyright protects creative expression, and short phrases and slogans are too short to count, so anyone may use the words. A trademark protects a slogan only when it identifies the source of a product or service, to prevent customer confusion. A poster design is original creative expression, so it is protected by copyright automatically.

# 21. Portfolio Selection
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can select and sequence work for a portfolio to fit its audience.
directions: Read about Rosa's work and the portfolio she needs. Answer the questions. Choose 10 of her 16 pieces, put them in order, and give a reason for each. Then explain what you cut and why.
mistakes: Students include everything that looks busy, or keep near-duplicates. A portfolio is judged by its weakest piece, so cutting is a skill. Students also forget the order: open with a strong, complete project and end strong, and put a process page right beside the project it explains.
notes: Rosa and her work are fictional. The key shows one strong selection; other selections are fine if the reasons hold up. Five pieces have clear problems: 4 (near-duplicate), 6 (copied from a tutorial), 10 (a photo, not design work), 13 (unfinished, placeholder text), 14 (old, weaker work). The sixth cut is a judgment call. Students can apply the same steps to their own portfolios.

## Read: Rosa's work
example: Rosa is applying to a college graphic design program. It asks for **10 pieces** that show her best work, her range and how she thinks.
table: # | Piece | Notes
  - 1 | Bakery rebrand: logo, packaging, menu | Full system, polished, her strongest project
  - 2 | Sketch page for the bakery logo | Shows her process: 30 thumbnails, notes
  - 3 | Jazz band concert poster | Bold type, strong layout
  - 4 | Second jazz poster | Same layout as 3, only the color changed
  - 5 | Town recycling infographic | Clear data, clean icons
  - 6 | Copy of a famous poster made by following an online tutorial | Neat, but not her design
  - 7 | Three book covers for a public-domain novel | Strong series, consistent style
  - 8 | Library app screen mockups | Shows interface design range
  - 9 | Hand-lettered alphabet study | Good skill, plain presentation
  - 10 | Unedited photo of her dog | Cute, but not design work

# 33. Rough Cut Review
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can review a documentary rough cut for structure, fairness and clarity and write useful notes to the editor.
directions: Read the rough cut log and the interview transcript. Answer the warm up. Find at least two problems in each area (structure, fairness, clarity), with timecodes, and write a note to the editor for each. Then choose the most important note.
mistakes: Notes like "make it better" or "the middle is boring" give the editor nothing to act on. A useful note has a timecode, names the problem and explains its effect on the viewer; a suggested fix helps. Students often miss the fairness problems because the film's side seems sympathetic; check every edit of a person's words against what they actually said.
notes: Who Owns the Creek? and everyone in it are made up. The key lists the planted problems; students may find others, which are fine if they are supported. In professional practice, a rough cut screening is followed by written notes with timecodes, and fairness checks include confirming that edited quotes keep the speaker's meaning and that people criticized in the film have a chance to respond.

## Warm up
text: **Who Owns the Creek?** (rough cut, 7 minutes 30 seconds) is about a dispute between a family farm and a kayaking club over how much water the farm pumps from Miller Creek in summer.
table: Time | Picture | Sound
  - 0:00 to 0:45 | Drone shots of the creek; no title or place name | Slow piano music
  - 0:45 to 2:30 | Kayak club president Dan, seated interview; no name on screen | Dan: "...the farm's riparian rights don't give them the whole creek."
  - 2:30 to 2:40 | Farmer Lucia in her field; no name on screen | Lucia: "people think we're draining it." A low, ominous drone starts under her words.
  - 2:40 to 5:40 | Three kayakers, one after another, each says the creek is too low | No new information after the first one
  - 5:40 to 5:43 | A graph of creek levels, shown for 3 seconds; no source, labels too small to read | Music
  - 5:43 to 7:30 | Dan again; ends on his line "They'll ruin it for everyone." | Fade to black and credits
source: Interview transcript, farmer Lucia (the full answer the editor cut from)
  "I understand why people think we're draining it, but we've cut our pumping by a third since 2020, and we're on the state's water meter like everyone else."
choice: Which kind of note is most useful to an editor?
  - "The middle is boring."
  - "I didn't like it."
  * "2:40 to 5:40: three kayakers repeat the same point, so the film stalls; keep the strongest one and add a new voice."
  - "Make it better."

## Main: Review notes
chart: Timecode | Problem and its effect on the viewer | Note to the editor
  labels: Structure; Structure; Fairness; Fairness; Clarity; Clarity
  height: 0.75
  key: Structure: 0:00 to 0:45, a long opening with no title, place or question, so viewers do not know what the film is about; add a title and a clear question early. Structure: 2:40 to 5:40, three kayakers repeat one point and the film stalls; keep the strongest and use the time for new information. (Also: the ending is one side's opinion, not a resolution.) Fairness: 2:30 to 2:40, Lucia's quote is cut so it sounds like a confession, while she actually said they have cut pumping by a third; restore her full answer. Fairness: the ominous drone under Lucia tells viewers she is a villain; remove it or use neutral sound. (Also: only the kayak side gets more than 10 seconds; give Lucia or an independent expert time, and end on something other than an attack line.) Clarity: 0:45 to 2:30, "riparian rights" is never explained; add a short definition or a graphic. Clarity: 5:40 to 5:43, the graph has no source, cannot be read and is on screen for only 3 seconds; add the source, enlarge the labels and hold it longer. (Also: no names or roles on screen for Dan or Lucia; add lower-third titles.)

## Main: Priorities
choice: Which problem is the most serious, because it could mislead viewers about a real person?
  - The long drone opening
  - The unreadable graph
  - The missing name titles
  * The edit that cuts Lucia's answer so it means the opposite of what she said
write: Write your single most important note to the editor, as you would hand it in: timecode, problem, effect and suggested fix.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: "2:30 to 2:40: Lucia's answer is cut to 'people think we're draining it,' with an ominous drone under it, which makes her sound like she is admitting it. Her full answer says the farm cut pumping by a third and is metered by the state. Restore the full answer, remove the drone, and give her a longer section so viewers hear both sides."

## Stretch
write: The director says, "The film is about the kayakers' point of view, so it does not have to be balanced." Respond: what does a point-of-view documentary still owe the people in it?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer agrees that a documentary can have a point of view but says it must still be accurate and fair: quotes must keep their meaning, facts must be sourced, people who are criticized should get a chance to respond, and music and editing should not make claims the evidence does not support. Viewers should be able to tell argument from fact.
