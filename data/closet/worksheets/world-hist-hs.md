# 1. From Copernicus to Newton
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can sequence key discoveries of the Scientific Revolution and explain how each challenged an older authority.
directions: Place the letter of each discovery on the timeline. Match each discovery to the older authority or idea it challenged. Then explain how the Scientific Revolution changed the way Europeans decided what was true.
mistakes: Students often say the Scientific Revolution was "science versus religion." Most of these thinkers were religious, and the deeper change was in method: trusting observation, experiment and mathematics over ancient texts (Aristotle, Ptolemy, Galen) and inherited authority. Watch for students who credit Galileo with proposing that Earth orbits the Sun; Copernicus did, in 1543, and Galileo's telescope gave new evidence for it.
notes: Dates are publication dates (Tycho's is the year he observed the new star; he published in 1573; Leeuwenhoek's is his 1676 letter to the Royal Society). Kepler is the stretch on purpose: his first two laws of planetary motion appeared in 1609, one year before Galileo's telescope report, which is too close to show on this timeline. Galileo was tried by the Roman Inquisition in 1633 for his 1632 Dialogue and spent his last years under house arrest.

## Warm up: The old authorities
match: Match each ancient authority to the field Europeans followed him in for centuries.
  - Galen = Medicine and the human body
  - Aristotle = Physics and the nature of the heavens and Earth
  - Ptolemy = Astronomy: an Earth-centered universe
  - Euclid = Geometry, which the new scientists kept and built on

## Main: Place 10 discoveries
timeline: 1540 1690 10
  - 1543 | Copernicus publishes On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres: the Sun, not Earth, is at the center
  - 1572 | Tycho Brahe observes a new star in the sky
  - 1600 | William Gilbert publishes On the Magnet: Earth itself is a giant magnet
  - 1610 | Galileo reports seeing moons orbiting Jupiter through his telescope
  - 1620 | Francis Bacon publishes the New Organon, calling for knowledge built from experiments
  - 1628 | William Harvey shows that the heart pumps blood around the body in a circle
  - 1637 | René Descartes publishes Discourse on Method: doubt everything until reason proves it
  - 1665 | Robert Hooke's Micrographia names "cells" seen under a microscope
  - 1676 | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek reports tiny living things in a drop of water
  - 1687 | Isaac Newton's Principia: one law of gravity rules Earth and the heavens
match: Which older idea did each discovery challenge?
  - Copernicus's Sun-centered system = Ptolemy's teaching that Earth sits still at the center
  - Tycho's new star = Aristotle's idea that the heavens are perfect and never change
  - Galileo's moons of Jupiter = The belief that everything in the sky circles Earth
  - Harvey's circulation of blood = Galen's teaching that the liver makes blood that the body uses up
  - Bacon's method = Relying on Aristotle's logic and ancient books instead of experiment
  - Newton's universal gravity = Aristotle's idea that the heavens follow different laws than Earth

## Explain
write: Choose two discoveries from the timeline. For each, name the authority it challenged and the kind of evidence it used (observation, experiment or mathematics).
  lines: 5
  key: Galileo (1610) challenged Ptolemy and Aristotle using observation through a telescope: moons circling Jupiter proved not everything orbits Earth. Harvey (1628) challenged Galen using experiment and measurement: the amount of blood pumped each hour was far more than the body could make, so it must circulate.
write: Why might the Catholic Church and many university scholars have resisted some of these ideas?
  lines: 4
  key: Their teaching, and their authority, rested on combining Scripture with Aristotle and Ptolemy; a moving Earth seemed to contradict some Bible passages and undercut who decided truth. Galileo's 1633 trial shows the conflict, though many scientists were devout and saw their work as studying God's creation.

## Stretch: Where does Kepler go?
write: In 1609 Johannes Kepler showed that planets move in ellipses (ovals), not perfect circles. Where would Kepler fall on the timeline, and what older idea did he challenge, even among Copernicans?
  lines: 3
  key: Between Gilbert (1600) and Galileo (1610), just before Galileo. He challenged the ancient belief, kept by Ptolemy and even Copernicus, that heavenly motion must be in perfect circles; his laws were built from Tycho's careful observations of Mars.

# 6. John Snow's Cholera Map
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencil; a projected copy of John Snow's 1854 map if available
ican: I can read a dot map, describe its spatial pattern and explain how the pattern supports a conclusion.
directions: Read the background. The grid is a simplified model of Snow's map: each dot is one death. Mark the five pumps, count the deaths closest to each pump, and explain what the pattern shows. Then compare your model with Snow's real map.
mistakes: Students say Snow "discovered germs." He did not see the cholera bacterium; he reasoned from the pattern of deaths and from case details that something in the water spread the disease. Robert Koch identified the bacterium in 1883. Students also think removing the pump handle ended the outbreak; deaths were already falling, partly because many people had fled. The handle's removal mattered more as a test and a lesson for the future.
notes: The grid is a simplified teaching model, not Snow's data: it has 32 deaths to count, while the real outbreak killed about 600 people. It keeps the real pattern (deaths clustered around one pump, few near others) and two real exceptions. Snow reported that none of the roughly 70 workers at the Broad Street brewery died (they drank beer and the brewery had its own well) and that only 5 of 535 people in the Poland Street workhouse died (it also had its own well). The handle was removed on September 8, 1854; Snow published the map in 1855. The key marks the pumps in red. Show the real map (widely available, public domain) after students finish.

## Background
text: In late August 1854, cholera struck the Soho neighborhood of London. In about ten days, hundreds of people died. Most doctors believed cholera spread through bad air, called miasma. Dr. John Snow thought it spread through dirty water. He made a list of who died and where they lived, marked each death on a street map, and looked at which water pump was closest. On September 8, local officials removed the handle of the pump on Broad Street.

## Explain the pattern
write: Describe the pattern of dots in one or two sentences, as a geographer would.
  lines: 2
  key: The deaths are clustered tightly around pump A on Broad Street and thin out with distance from it; the areas around the other pumps have almost no deaths.
write: Why does the pattern point to the Broad Street pump and not to "bad air"?
  lines: 3
  key: Bad air would spread across the whole neighborhood, so deaths would be spread evenly or follow the wind. Instead they cluster around one water source, and people closer to other pumps mostly stayed well, which points to something in the Broad Street water.
choice: The brewery is next to the cluster, but none of its workers died. Which explanation fits Snow's water theory best?
  - Brewery workers breathed cleaner air indoors.
  * Brewery workers drank beer and water from the brewery's own well, not the Broad Street pump.
  - Brewery workers were all younger and stronger.
  - The brewery was closed during the outbreak.

## Stretch: The limits of a map
write: A dot map shows where people died, not why. Name one more piece of evidence Snow would need to be sure the pump caused the deaths, and explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: evidence that people who died far from the pump had drunk its water (Snow found a woman in Hampstead, miles away, who had Broad Street water carried to her because she liked its taste, and she died). That links the disease to the water itself, not just to the neighborhood. (Also accept: a test of the water, or showing the well was near a leaking cesspool, which was found in 1855.)

# 18. MAIN Causes of War
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, scissors, glue, textbook chapter on the causes of World War I
ican: I can sort the long-term causes of World War I and argue which one mattered most.
directions: Cut out the 12 event cards. Sort each one under the cause it shows best: militarism, alliances, imperialism or nationalism. Then use the events to argue which cause mattered most.
mistakes: Students treat the assassination of Franz Ferdinand as the cause of the war. It was the spark; the four long-term causes turned a regional crisis into a world war within six weeks. Several cards fit more than one cause (the Moroccan crises tested alliances as well as empires; the naval race grew from imperial rivalry), so accept a different column when the student can explain it. Students also forget Italy: it was in the Triple Alliance but joined the Allies in 1915.
notes: MAIN is a teaching memory aid, not a historian's theory; many historians stress decisions by leaders in July 1914 as much as these long-term causes. The Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) dates from 1882, building on the 1879 Dual Alliance. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 completed the Triple Entente of France, Russia and Britain. Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914.

## Warm up: Know the terms
match: Match each cause to its meaning.
  - Militarism = Building up armies and navies and planning for war
  - Alliances = Agreements to defend each other if attacked
  - Imperialism = Competing to control colonies and markets around the world
  - Nationalism = Strong pride in one's nation, or a people's wish for a nation of their own

## Main: Sort the events
sort: Glue each event under the cause it shows best.
  - Militarism: Britain launches HMS Dreadnought and a naval race with Germany speeds up (1906); Germany adopts the Schlieffen Plan for a two-front war (1905); France extends military service to three years (1913)
  - Alliances: Germany and Austria-Hungary form the Dual Alliance (1879); France and Russia sign an alliance (1894); Britain and Russia settle their rivalry, completing the Triple Entente (1907)
  - Imperialism: European powers divide Africa at the Berlin Conference (1884 to 1885); Germany challenges France over Morocco in the First Moroccan Crisis (1905); a German gunboat at Agadir sets off a second Moroccan crisis (1911)
  - Nationalism: Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia, angering Serbian nationalists (1908); Serbia doubles its land in the Balkan Wars (1912 to 1913); a Bosnian Serb nationalist assassinates Archduke Franz Ferdinand (1914)

## How the spark spread
fill:
  - After the assassination, Austria-Hungary declared war on {Serbia}.
  - Russia mobilized its army to protect {Serbia}, a fellow Slavic nation.
  - Germany declared war on Russia and France, then invaded neutral {Belgium} to reach France.
  - That invasion brought {Britain} into the war.

## Argue: Which mattered most?
write: Which of the four causes mattered most in turning a crisis in the Balkans into a world war? Make a claim, use two events from your sort as evidence, and explain why another cause mattered less.
  lines: 7
  key: Sample: Alliances mattered most. The alliances of 1879, 1894 and 1907 meant that a war between Austria-Hungary and Serbia pulled in Russia, then Germany, then France and Britain within weeks. Nationalism lit the spark, but without the alliances the Sarajevo assassination could have stayed a local war, as the Balkan Wars of 1912 to 1913 did. (Accept any cause with two accurate events and a reason another mattered less; for militarism, the Schlieffen Plan forced Germany to attack France quickly through Belgium.)

## Stretch: Cause or spark?
write: Explain the difference between a long-term cause and an immediate cause (a spark), using one example from this sheet for each.
  lines: 3
  key: A long-term cause builds tension over years and makes war likely, such as the naval race or the alliance system. An immediate cause is the event that sets off the war at a particular moment, such as the assassination of Franz Ferdinand on June 28, 1914.

# 25. The Great Depression Worldwide
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator, colored pencils
ican: I can use economic data to explain how the Great Depression spread from country to country.
directions: Study the two data tables. Graph world trade, answer the questions and write an explanation of how the crash spread, citing numbers.
mistakes: Students often compare raw numbers across countries that were counted differently: the German figure is a count of registered unemployed people, not a percentage, and the British and American rates come from different sources. Ask them to compare change over time within each country. Another trap: reading the trade spiral as a fall in the amount of goods only; it is value in gold dollars, so falling prices make up part of the drop.
notes: Sources. US unemployment, percent of the civilian labor force: Stanley Lebergott's estimates (Historical Statistics of the United States). UK unemployment, percent of the workforce: Bank of England "A millennium of macroeconomic data," via FRED series UNRTUKA (insured-worker rates of the time were higher, about 22 percent in 1932). Germany, registered unemployed, yearly average in millions, rounded (the official count peaked at about 5.6 million in 1932). World trade: total imports of 75 countries in January of each year, millions of gold dollars, from Charles Kindleberger, The World in Depression, 1929 to 1939. Change 1929 to 1932: Blum, Cameron and Barnes, The European World (1970), as reported in many textbooks. Teach France as the exception: unemployment there stayed low in percentage terms even as trade fell.

## Read the data
fill:

  - In which year did unemployment in the UK peak? {1932}

## Explain: How the crash spread
choice: Which statement does the data support best?
  - The Depression stayed inside the United States.
  * Trade and production shrank in every country shown and unemployment rose wherever it is reported, so the crisis was worldwide.
  - Trade fell, but unemployment did not change.
  - Germany recovered by 1930.
write: Explain how a stock market crash in New York could cost jobs in Britain and Germany. Use at least two numbers from the tables and the ideas of loans, trade and tariffs.
  lines: 6
  key: After the 1929 crash, American banks stopped lending abroad and called in loans; Germany, which depended on those loans, saw registered unemployment rise from 1.9 to 5.6 million by 1932. Countries raised tariffs to protect their own industries (the US Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930), so trade collapsed: world imports fell from 2,998 to 992 million gold dollars by 1933, and US foreign trade fell 70 percent. Fewer sales abroad meant factories in Britain and Germany cut workers, so UK unemployment doubled from 7.2 to 15.4 percent.

## Stretch: Politics
write: Germany's unemployment peaked in 1932, and Hitler became chancellor in January 1933. Using the data, explain one way an economic crisis can weaken a democracy. What other causes would a historian also want to examine?
  lines: 5
  key: Mass unemployment (5.6 million registered in 1932) made many voters lose faith in the Weimar Republic and turn to extreme parties that promised jobs and blamed others. A historian would also examine the Treaty of Versailles, the 1923 inflation, political violence, the choices of conservative elites who brought Hitler into government, and the Nazis' use of propaganda.

# 30. Wartime Deaths Data

materials: pencil, calculator

directions: The table gives rounded estimates of deaths in World War II for seven countries. Complete the calculations, plot each country on the graph, and answer the questions. Then think about what these numbers leave out.
mistakes: Students compare raw totals only and conclude the United States or Britain "suffered most" among Western Allies without looking at population; percent of population changes the picture sharply (Poland lost about one in six people). About 6 million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust; they are counted in the totals of the countries where they lived. # 42. Winners and Losers of Globalization
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can use evidence to evaluate how globalization creates gains and losses for different groups.
directions: Read the 12 pieces of evidence. For each group, write the number of its gain and its loss in the chart. Then decide whether globalization has been more gain or more loss for one group and explain.
mistakes: Students label whole countries winners or losers ("China won, the US lost"). The evidence shows that gains and losses fall on groups inside each country: some Mexican farmers gained while others lost. Students also treat the gain and the loss as equal in weight. Push them to ask how many people, how much money, and whether the loss could be made up some other way.
notes: Figures are rounded from widely reported sources: Bangladesh's garment industry (about 4 million workers, mostly women); the Rana Plaza collapse near Dhaka on April 24, 2013 (more than 1,100 deaths); US manufacturing employment (about 17 million in 2000, about 12 million in 2010, with research by economists such as David Autor linking part of the drop to Chinese imports); NAFTA (1994) and Mexico's corn and avocado trade; Kenya's cut flower exports to Europe; India's IT services industry (about 5 million workers). Check current numbers if students research further.

## Evidence
source: Twelve pieces of evidence about globalization
  [1] US manufacturing jobs fell from about 17 million in 2000 to about 12 million in 2010. Economists link part of the drop to competition from imports.
  [2] Kenya is one of the world's biggest exporters of cut flowers, mostly to Europe, and the flower farms employ well over 100,000 people.
  [3] After NAFTA began in 1994, cheap corn from the United States poured into Mexico, prices fell, and many small corn farmers left their land.
  [4] India's information technology industry, built by serving companies overseas, employs about 5 million people.
  [5] Bangladesh's garment industry employs about 4 million workers, most of them women earning wages of their own.
  [6] Since the 1990s, prices for clothing and televisions in the US have risen far less than prices overall, and TVs cost a fraction of what they once did.
  [7] Many tech workers in India work through the night to match business hours in the United States, and their jobs could move to cheaper places or be done by software.
  [8] Mexican farmers growing avocados, tomatoes and berries for the US market saw their exports grow many times over.
  [9] In 2013 the Rana Plaza factory building near Dhaka, Bangladesh, collapsed, killing more than 1,100 garment workers.
  [10] Many shoppers also work in industries that face import competition, so lower prices can come with less job security in their own towns.
  [11] US factories that export airplanes, machinery and chemicals gained customers abroad, and jobs at exporting plants tend to pay above-average wages.
  [12] Flower farmworkers in Kenya have reported health problems from pesticides, and farms near Lake Naivasha have been blamed for drawing down the lake.

## Weigh it
write: Pick one group. Has globalization been more gain or more loss for that group? Use both of its pieces of evidence and explain how you weighed them.
  lines: 5
  key: Sample: for Bangladesh's garment workers, more gain than loss, but at a high cost. About 4 million jobs (5) gave many women their own wages for the first time, which a farm economy could not. But Rana Plaza (9) showed that weak safety rules can be deadly; the gain depends on enforcing building and safety laws, which Bangladesh and global brands promised after 2013. (Accept any group with both pieces of evidence and a clear way of weighing them.)
write: Choose one loss from the chart. Suggest one policy that could reduce it without ending trade.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: for US factory workers who lost jobs (1), job retraining and wage insurance paid for by the government (such as Trade Adjustment Assistance) could help them move into new jobs. (Also accept: factory safety inspections in Bangladesh; limits on pesticide use in Kenya; support for small corn farmers in Mexico.)

## Stretch: Who decides?
write: The gains from globalization are often spread thinly over many people, while the losses fall heavily on a few. Use one example from the evidence to explain this idea, and why it matters in politics.
  lines: 4
  key: Lower clothing and TV prices (6) save each shopper a little, spread across millions of people, while factory closings (1) cost particular workers and towns their whole income. People who lose a lot are more likely to organize and vote on the issue than people who gain a little, which helps explain political pressure for tariffs and protection.
