# 1. Alcohol's Path Through the Body
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can trace how alcohol moves through the body and explain its effects.
directions: Read about alcohol's path. Put the five stops in order on the path line. Match each stop to what happens there. Then draw the path on the body outline and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think the stomach digests alcohol like food. Alcohol is not digested; it is absorbed straight into the blood, mostly in the small intestine. Many believe coffee, a cold shower or exercise can sober someone up. Only time lets the liver break alcohol down.
notes: Facts follow the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) and the CDC. About 20% of alcohol is absorbed through the stomach and most of the rest through the small intestine; blood from the gut passes through the liver first, but the liver can only break down a small amount at a time, so the rest travels on to the heart and brain. The brain keeps developing into the mid-20s. The legal drinking age in the U.S. is 21. If a student shares a family situation, follow up privately and offer the school counselor.

## Warm up: The trip
text: Alcohol is a drug that slows down the brain. It is not digested like food: it soaks straight into the blood. Blood from the gut always passes through the liver before it travels on to the heart and the rest of the body, but the liver can only break down a little alcohol at a time. Think about where a drink goes after it is swallowed.

## Main: Put the stops in order
timeline: 1 5 1
  - 1 | Mouth and throat
  - 2 | Stomach
  - 3 | Small intestine and bloodstream
  - 4 | Liver
  - 5 | Brain

## Main: What happens at each stop?
match: Match each stop to what happens there.
  - Mouth and throat = Alcohol passes through and can irritate the lining
  - Stomach = About 20% is absorbed; food in the stomach slows this down
  - Small intestine and bloodstream = Most is absorbed and carried to every organ
  - Liver = Breaks alcohol down slowly, at a steady rate; heavy drinking can scar it
  - Brain = Slows reaction time, judgment, coordination and memory
draw: Draw a simple body outline. Draw alcohol's path with arrows and label the five stops. | 2.4

## Think about it
choice: What is the only thing that lowers the amount of alcohol in someone's blood?
  - Drinking coffee
  - Taking a cold shower
  * Time, while the liver breaks it down
choice: Why is alcohol especially risky for teens?
  - Teens' livers do not work at all
  * The teen brain is still developing until about age 25, so alcohol can harm learning and memory
  - Alcohol has no effect on teens
write: Alcohol slows down the brain. Name two ways that could lead to an injury.
  lines: 3
  key: Slower reaction time and poor coordination can cause falls, crashes or drowning; poor judgment can lead to risky choices like riding with an impaired driver.

## Stretch
write: Jordan's cousin says she will "sleep it off" after drinking at a party. Using what you know about the liver, why does that not make her safe to drive in the morning?
  lines: 3
  key: The liver breaks down alcohol at a steady, slow rate. If she drank a lot, there may still be alcohol in her blood in the morning, so her reactions and judgment could still be impaired. Only enough time clears it.

# 7. Decision Model: The Invite
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a six-step decision model to make a safe choice about alcohol.
directions: Read about Tessa's invitation. Work through all six steps of the decision model for her. Then answer the stretch question.
mistakes: Students often list only two options (go or don't go). Push for at least three, such as going to a different plan or going late with a set exit. In step 3, watch for consequences that are all about getting caught; also ask about health, safety, trust, goals and the law. In step 4, students may restate the options instead of naming values (safety, health, honesty, goals, family trust).
notes: Tessa is a character made up for Curio Class. Facts follow the NIAAA and CDC: the legal drinking age in the U.S. is 21; the brain keeps developing into the mid-20s, and alcohol can affect memory, learning and judgment; teens who drink are more likely to be hurt or to make risky choices. Many health programs teach this model as DECIDE or a similar set of steps. A family code word or text ("X plan") that means "call me and come get me, no questions tonight" is a common, practical exit plan. If a student shares a family or party situation, follow up privately.

## Warm up: The invite
text: Tessa is 13 and plays club soccer. On Wednesday, Dani, a ninth grader she wants to be friends with, invites her to a party on Saturday night. Dani says, "My parents are away. There will be drinks. You should come!" Tessa wants Dani to like her. She has a soccer tournament on Sunday morning, and her parents trust her to tell them where she is.

## Main: The six steps
write: **Step 1. Define the decision.** What does Tessa need to decide?
  lines: 1
  key: Whether to go to Dani's party on Saturday, where there will be alcohol and no adults, and how to respond to Dani.
write: **Step 2. List the options.** Give at least three.
  lines: 2
  key: Go and drink; go and not drink; say no and suggest another plan with Dani, like hanging out Saturday afternoon; say no and make other plans; talk to her parents first.
chart: Option | Possible positive results | Possible negative results
  labels: Go to the party; Say no and suggest another plan
  height: 0.85
  key: Go: time with Dani, feels included. Negative: pressure to drink, alcohol is illegal under 21 and harms judgment and the developing brain, risk of injury or a ride with a driver who has been drinking, breaks her parents' trust, plays badly at Sunday's tournament. Say no and suggest another plan: stays safe and rested for the tournament, keeps her parents' trust, still shows Dani she wants to be friends. Negative: Dani may be disappointed or not invite her again.
write: **Step 3. Weigh the consequences.** Which consequence matters most to you for Tessa, and why?
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Safety. With alcohol and no adults, someone could get hurt or Tessa could be pressured to drink, and that could affect her health, her family's trust and her tournament.
write: **Step 4. Name the values.** Which of Tessa's values and goals help her decide?
  lines: 2
  key: Her goal of playing well at the tournament, being honest with and trusted by her parents, staying healthy and safe, and following the law.
write: **Step 5. Decide and act.** What should Tessa do? Write what she says to Dani.
  lines: 3
  starter: Tessa decides to ...
  key: Tessa decides to say no to the party and offer another plan: "Thanks for asking! I can't Saturday night, I've got a tournament early Sunday. Want to get pizza Saturday afternoon?"
write: **Step 6. Evaluate.** How will Tessa know if she made a good decision?
  lines: 2
  key: She stayed safe and healthy, felt rested for her tournament, kept her parents' trust, and felt good about her choice. If Dani pushes back, she can think about whether that friendship supports her values.

## Stretch
write: Suppose Tessa goes to a friend's house and finds out there is alcohol there. Plan her exit: what can she say or do?
  lines: 3
  key: She could text a family code word or call a parent to pick her up, say "My mom needs me home," leave with a friend who is not drinking, and never ride with anyone who has been drinking.

# 20. Mental Health Check
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can show what I know about managing stress, mindset, sleep, warning signs and supporting a friend.
directions: Answer all 10 items. Then try the stretch.
mistakes: Watch item 4 (sleep): many students think 7 hours is enough for teens; the recommendation for ages 13 to 18 is 8 to 10 hours. On item 8, some students think a good friend keeps every secret; when a friend's safety is at risk, telling a trusted adult is the right move.
notes: Sleep: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18. Mindset follows Carol Dweck's research on growth mindset. Warning signs that last two weeks or more, or that get in the way of daily life, are a reason to talk to an adult or doctor. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers calls and texts at 988, any time. Use this as a review, not a test of anyone's own mental health.

## Stress control
choice: Your body's stress response gets you ready to act. Which of these is part of it?
  - Slower breathing and a slower heartbeat
  * A faster heartbeat and tense muscles
  - Feeling sleepy and relaxed
choice: Which is a healthy way to calm stress before a big game?
  - Skipping meals to feel lighter
  - Scrolling on a phone until midnight
  * Slow, deep breathing and a short walk

## Mindset
choice: Which statement shows a growth mindset?
  - "I'm just not a math person."
  * "I can't do this yet, but practice will help."
  - "If I have to try hard, I must not be smart."

## Sleep
fill:
  - Teens ages 13 to 18 need {8 to 10} hours of sleep a night.
choice: Which habit helps teens fall asleep?
  * Putting screens away about an hour before bed
  - Drinking soda after dinner
  - Going to bed at a very different time each night

## Warning signs
choice: Which could be a warning sign that someone needs help?
  - Feeling nervous before one test
  - Being sad for a day after losing a game
  * Pulling away from friends and losing interest in things for weeks
choice: How long do warning signs usually last before they are a reason to talk to an adult or doctor?
  - One afternoon
  * Two weeks or more, or any time they get in the way of daily life
  - A whole year

## Supporting a friend
choice: Your friend says she feels hopeless and makes you promise not to tell. What should you do?
  - Keep the promise no matter what
  - Tell her to stop being dramatic
  * Tell a trusted adult right away, even though you promised
fill:
  - In the U.S., anyone can call or text {988} to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.
write: Write one thing you could say to a friend who seems stressed.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: "You seem stressed lately. Do you want to talk or take a walk? I'm here." Listening without judging and offering to go with them to a trusted adult are strong answers.

## Stretch
write: Explain how sleep, stress and mindset are connected. Use an example of a made-up student.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: When Rae sleeps only 6 hours, she feels more stressed and gives up faster, thinking "I'll never get this." With 9 hours of sleep, she handles stress better and can use a growth mindset: "I'll try another way." Sleep helps the brain manage emotions and learn.

# 21. Kinds of Boundaries
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can name different kinds of boundaries and respect other people's boundaries.
directions: A boundary is a limit a person sets to feel safe and respected. Cut out the 12 cards. Glue each one under physical, emotional, digital or time boundaries. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Digital and physical boundaries get mixed up when a phone is involved ("Don't take my phone" is about belongings, so physical; "Don't post my photo" is digital). Some boundaries fit two columns; accept a second answer with a good reason.
notes: Every card is said by a story character, so no student needs to share their own boundaries. Stress that boundaries go both ways: we set our own and respect others'. A healthy friend or partner accepts a boundary without guilt-tripping. If someone keeps crossing a boundary, students should tell a trusted adult.

## Warm up
choice: Which is the best example of setting a boundary?
  - "You're so annoying. Go away forever."
  * "I'm not okay with tickling. Please stop."
  - Saying nothing and hoping it stops

## Main: Sort the boundaries
sort: Glue each card under its kind of boundary.
  - Physical: "I'd rather high-five than hug.", "Please knock before coming in my room.", "Ask before you borrow my things."
  - Emotional: "I'm not ready to talk about that yet.", "Please don't joke about my grades.", "I need some quiet time before we talk this out."
  - Digital: "Ask before you post a photo of me.", "Please don't read my messages.", "I don't share my passwords."
  - Time: "I can't hang out until my homework is done.", "I need to leave by 5:00.", "I can help with your project for one hour on Saturday."

## Respecting boundaries
write: Eli tells his friend Sam, "Please don't post that video of me." Write what Sam could say to respect Eli's boundary.
  lines: 2
  key: "Okay, I won't post it. Thanks for telling me." Any reply that agrees without arguing, teasing or guilt-tripping.
write: Lena keeps texting Priya late at night, and Priya is tired. Write a time boundary Priya could set, using a calm and kind voice.
  lines: 2
  key: "I really like talking to you, but I turn my phone off at 9:00 on school nights. Let's talk tomorrow."

## Stretch
write: Priya set her boundary, but Lena keeps texting late and says, "If you were a real friend, you'd answer." What should Priya do next?
  lines: 3
  key: Repeat the boundary calmly and keep it ("I'll answer in the morning"). Guilt-tripping is not respecting a boundary. If it keeps happening, she can take a break from the friendship and talk to a trusted adult.

# 33. Five Questions for Any Media Message
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use five key questions to analyze a media message about a health product.
directions: Read the pretend ad. Answer the five key questions about it in the chart. Then write a short verdict about the ad.
mistakes: Students often answer question 1 with the athlete in the ad. The athlete is paid to appear; the creator is the company that makes Energy Drink X and the ad agency it hired. On question 4, push students to name what is left out (the caffeine amount, the warning, who should not drink it), not only what is shown.
notes: The ad and Energy Drink X are made up for Curio Class. The five questions are adapted from the Center for Media Literacy's Five Key Questions. Facts: the American Academy of Pediatrics says children and teens should not drink energy drinks; the FDA says up to 400 mg of caffeine a day is generally safe for healthy adults but has not set a safe amount for children. Large amounts of caffeine can cause a fast heartbeat, anxiety and trouble sleeping.

## Warm up: The five key questions
text: **1. Who made this message?** **2. What tricks does it use to grab my attention?** **3. How might different people understand it differently?** **4. What values and points of view are shown, and what is left out?** **5. Why was this message sent?**

## Read: A pretend ad
source: Pretend video ad for Energy Drink X (made up for this sheet)
  Loud electric guitar music. A skateboarder in a neon helmet flies off a ramp in slow motion. Cheering crowd. The can glows green.
  Voice: "Unleash your power. Never stop. Never slow down. Energy Drink X: fuel for champions."
  On screen for 2 seconds, in tiny print: "300 mg caffeine per can. Not recommended for children, people sensitive to caffeine, or pregnant or nursing women."

## Main: Ask the five questions
table: Question | Your answer about the ad
  height: 0.75
  - 1. Who made it? | {The company that sells Energy Drink X (and its ad agency). The skateboarder was likely paid.}
  - 2. What tricks grab attention? | {Loud music, slow-motion stunt, bright colors, a glowing can, a cheering crowd, the words "champions" and "power."}
  - 3. How might people see it differently? | {A teen skater may think it will help them win; a parent or doctor may see a lot of caffeine aimed at young people.}
  - 4. What values are shown? What is left out? | {Shows thrill, power and winning. Leaves out health risks of caffeine and that the warning says it is not for children; the warning flashes too small and too fast.}
  - 5. Why was it sent? | {To make viewers, especially young people, want to buy Energy Drink X so the company makes money.}

## Check it
choice: The ad's tiny print says 300 mg of caffeine per can. Why does it matter that it shows for only 2 seconds?
  - It saves money on the ad
  * Viewers may miss the warning that it is not for children
  - Caffeine numbers are not important
choice: Which question asks about the purpose of a message?
  - Who made this message?
  - What tricks does it use?
  * Why was this message sent?

## Verdict
write: Write a 3 to 4 sentence verdict. Is this ad fair to its viewers? Use at least two of your answers as evidence.
  lines: 5
  key: Model: The ad is not fair to young viewers. It uses a thrilling stunt, loud music and "fuel for champions" to link the drink with power and winning, but it hides the warning in tiny print for 2 seconds. Since the company wants to sell cans, it leaves out that 300 mg of caffeine is a lot and that doctors say teens should not drink energy drinks.

## Stretch
write: Rewrite the ad's voice line so that it is honest about the product.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: "Energy Drink X has 300 mg of caffeine, as much as about three cups of coffee. It is for adults only."
