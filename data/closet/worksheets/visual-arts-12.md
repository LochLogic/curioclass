# 1. What Is Art?
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter; the teacher shows Alfred Stieglitz's 1917 photograph of Duchamp's Fountain
ican: I can explain four theories of art and test each against a challenging work.
directions: Read the passage and highlight the core claim of each theory. Look at the photograph of Fountain. Then decide whether each theory counts it as art, giving a reason, and argue for the theory you find most convincing.
mistakes: Students often treat the question as a matter of taste ("I don't like it, so it isn't art"). Each theory gives a test; the task is to apply the test fairly. Also check that students separate "is it art?" from "is it good art?": the institutional theory, for example, can count something as art and still judge it poor. In the Fountain table, the key gives the usual readings; accept a different verdict when the reason applies the theory's own test accurately.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 630 words). Fountain (1917) was a factory-made porcelain urinal, turned on its back and signed "R. Mutt 1917," submitted under that name to the first exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists in New York, which had promised to show any work by a member who paid the fee. It was not displayed. The original is lost; it survives in Alfred Stieglitz's 1917 photograph (public domain) and in replicas authorized by Duchamp in the 1950s and 1960s. Some scholars have argued that the idea came from the artist Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven; mention this only if it serves the discussion, as the claim is debated.

## Read
passage: Four Answers to an Old Question
  "What is art?" sounds like a question with an obvious answer until you try to answer it. Philosophers have proposed many definitions, and each one fits some works perfectly and fails on others. Four theories have been especially influential: mimetic, expressive, formal and institutional. Testing them against hard cases is one of the best ways to understand what each one gets right.
  The oldest is the mimetic theory, from the Greek word mimesis, meaning imitation. Plato and Aristotle both described art as an imitation of the world. Plato distrusted art for this reason: a painting of a bed, he argued, is only a copy of a real bed, which is itself only a copy of the ideal idea of a bed. Aristotle was kinder, suggesting that we learn and take pleasure from imitation. Under this theory, the better the imitation, the better the art. The theory struggles, though, with music, abstract painting and patterned designs, which do not copy anything in the world.
  The expressive theory moved the focus from the world to the artist's feelings. In What Is Art? (1897), the Russian novelist Leo Tolstoy argued that art is the transmission of feeling: an artist experiences an emotion and creates a work that passes the same emotion on to others. This theory explains why a simple, rough drawing can move us more than a polished copy. But it raises hard questions: How can we know what an artist felt? Is a work that leaves us cold no longer art?
  The formal theory says that what matters is form: the arrangement of lines, colors, shapes and spaces. In his 1914 book Art, the English critic Clive Bell proposed that every true artwork shares one quality, which he called "significant form," a combination of lines and colors that stirs a special aesthetic emotion. For formalists, the subject of a painting is almost beside the point. This theory welcomed abstract art and helped people see the beauty of objects from many cultures. Its weakness is that it struggles to explain why form matters, and it ignores meaning and context.
  The institutional theory, developed by the philosopher George Dickie in the 1970s and building on ideas from Arthur Danto, takes a completely different approach. It does not look for a special quality inside the object at all. Instead, it says that something becomes art when people within the art world, such as artists, curators, critics and museums, treat it as a candidate for appreciation. Art, in this view, is a social status, a little like the status of being married or being a citizen. This theory can account for almost anything artists have done in the twentieth century, but critics say it is circular: art is whatever the art world says is art, and the art world is the people who deal in art.
  No work tested these theories harder than Fountain. In 1917 Marcel Duchamp bought a standard porcelain urinal, turned it on its back, signed it "R. Mutt 1917" and submitted it to an exhibition in New York that had promised to show any work by a member who paid the fee. The organizers refused to display it. Duchamp had not shaped, painted or carved anything. He had chosen an object, given it a title and a signature, and placed it in an art context. More than a century later, many critics still name it one of the most influential artworks of the twentieth century, while others still say it proves how far art has lost its way.
  Which theory is right? Perhaps none completely. Each one is a tool for noticing something. Applying all four to a single difficult work shows not only how the work functions but also what we ourselves believe art should do.

## Check the theories
match: Match each theory to its central claim.
  - mimetic = art imitates the world
  - expressive = art passes the artist's feeling on to the viewer
  - formal = art is defined by significant form, the arrangement of line, color and shape
  - institutional = art is what the art world treats as a candidate for appreciation
choice: Why was Fountain such a strong test for the theories?
  - It was a very skilled imitation of a real object.
  * Duchamp made nothing by hand; he chose an object and placed it in an art context.
  - It was the first abstract painting.
  - It was shown in every museum in New York in 1917.

## Test each theory on Fountain
table: Theory | Is Fountain art under this theory? | Reason
  - Mimetic | {probably not} | {it does not imitate anything; it is the actual object}
  - Expressive | {doubtful} | {no clear feeling is transmitted, though it may provoke surprise or anger}
  - Formal | {possibly} | {its smooth curves and white surface can be seen as significant form, as some admirers argued, but that was not its point}
  - Institutional | {yes} | {the art world has treated it as art for over a century: museums, critics and artists}

## Argue
write: Which theory do you find most convincing? Defend it, and answer the strongest objection to it.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. A strong response picks one theory, applies it accurately to Fountain or another work, names a serious objection (for example the circularity of the institutional theory or the mimetic theory's failure with abstract art) and responds to it with reasoning.

## Stretch
write: Propose your own one-sentence definition of art. Test it on Fountain and on one work that is not from Europe or the United States.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong definitions are clear enough to test, and the student honestly reports whether each example passes and what that reveals about the definition.

# 5. The 1913 Armory Show
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Marcel Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase (No. 2), 1912
ican: I can analyze critical reactions to a controversial artwork and identify the criteria behind them.
directions: Read the background and the four press reactions, all paraphrased from 1913. For each one, name the criterion the writer used to judge the painting. Then evaluate the criteria and consider what Duchamp was trying to do.
mistakes: Students often treat the jokes as proof the painting failed, or the painting's later fame as proof the critics were foolish. Both skip the analysis: the task is to name the standard each critic applied and ask whether it fits the work. Note that Roosevelt found something to praise in the show even while mocking the Cubists.
notes: The International Exhibition of Modern Art opened February 17, 1913, at the 69th Regiment Armory in New York, organized by the Association of American Painters and Sculptors, and later traveled to Chicago and Boston. The four reactions are paraphrased, not quoted, except Roosevelt's phrase "lunatic fringe." Source 1: Theodore Roosevelt, "A Layman's Views of an Art Exhibition," The Outlook, March 29, 1913 (public domain; worth reading in full). Source 2: the phrase "an explosion in a shingle factory" is widely attributed to the writer Julian Street. Source 3: the magazine American Art News offered a small cash prize for the best explanation of the painting. Source 4: a cartoon in the New York Evening Sun parodied the painting as a rush-hour crowd on subway stairs. The painting was sold at the show to a San Francisco dealer, Frederic C. Torrey, and is now in the Philadelphia Museum of Art. Duchamp was influenced by chronophotography, photographs by Étienne-Jules Marey and Eadweard Muybridge that showed a body's motion in overlapping stages. Extension discussion: name a recent work of art, music or film that critics mocked at first; what criterion did they use, and was it the right test?

## Background
text: In 1913 about 1,300 works went on view in a New York armory. For most Americans it was the first look at European Cubism, Fauvism and Futurism. The most mocked work was Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase, which shows a figure broken into overlapping, angular planes in browns and ochres, repeated in stages down a diagonal. Earlier, in 1912, Cubist organizers of a Paris exhibition had asked Duchamp to change the title, and he withdrew it instead.

## Four reactions (paraphrased from 1913)
source: 1. Adapted from Theodore Roosevelt, "A Layman's Views of an Art Exhibition," The Outlook, March 29, 1913.
  Roosevelt granted that the show had no trace of the commonplace or of smug, conventional art. But every forward movement, he warned, has its "lunatic fringe," and he placed the Cubists and Futurists there. A good Navajo rug in his bathroom, he wrote, made a far more satisfying and decorative picture than the canvas he called "A naked man going down stairs," and naming the rug "A well-dressed man going up a ladder" would fit it just as well.
source: 2. A description widely attributed to the writer Julian Street, 1913.
  The painting looked, he said, like an explosion in a shingle factory.
source: 3. American Art News, a New York art magazine, 1913.
  The magazine offered a cash prize to the reader who could best explain the painting, or find the nude in it.
source: 4. A newspaper cartoon, New York Evening Sun, March 1913.
  The cartoon redrew the painting as a jumble of angular commuters shoving down the stairs of a subway station at rush hour.

## Warm up
choice: Why was the Armory Show such a shock to American viewers?
  - It was the first art exhibition ever held in New York.
  * Most had never seen European Cubism, Fauvism or Futurism before.
  - It showed only works by American artists.
  - It banned realistic paintings.
choice: What were source 3 and source 4 mainly doing?
  - praising Duchamp's use of color
  - explaining Cubist theory to readers
  * mocking the painting by treating it as a puzzle or a joke
  - reporting the price of the painting

## Main: Name the criteria
table: Source | What the writer objects to | The criterion behind it
  height: 0.7
  - 1 Roosevelt | {the painting is less decorative and sincere than a rug; the title does not match anything visible} | {a painting should be decorative, sincere, and its subject should be recognizable from its title}
  - 2 Street | {it looks like chaos, splintered wood} | {a picture should show a coherent, recognizable subject, not fragments}
  - 3 American Art News | {nobody can find the nude} | {a nude should be legible as a human figure; the subject should be readable}
  - 4 Evening Sun cartoon | {it looks like a crowd shoving, not a figure} | {clear representation; the work should not look absurd}
write: What do the four criteria have in common?
  lines: 3
  key: Nearly all judge the painting by whether you can recognize its subject: they expect a nude, a figure that looks like a body, as in academic painting. They do not ask what else a painting could do, such as show motion or time.
write: Duchamp wanted to show a body's motion through time, in overlapping stages, as photographs of movement did. Judged by that aim, how does the painting do? Use details you can see.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer points to the repeated, overlapping planes stepping down the diagonal from upper left to lower right, the curved arcs and dotted lines that suggest swinging motion, and the narrow brown palette that keeps attention on movement rather than color or flesh. By that aim it succeeds: the figure reads as movement more than as a body.

## Evaluate
write: Were the critics' criteria fair? Argue a position, and grant one point to the other side.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. A strong argument states a position, names the critics' main criterion (recognizable subject), tests it against Duchamp's aim, and concedes a point: for example, that the title promised a nude and viewers had reason to expect one, or that Roosevelt was right that a new movement can attract weak work.

# 15. Other Ways to Show Space
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Andrei Rublev's Trinity icon, a section of Zhang Zeduan's Along the River During the Qingming Festival, and The Court of Gayumars from the Shahnama of Shah Tahmasp
ican: I can compare spatial systems from different art traditions and explain how each fits its purpose.
directions: Read the three descriptions and look at each work. Fill in the comparison chart. Then explain why each system suits its purpose and argue whether linear perspective is better or only different.
mistakes: Students often describe these systems as mistakes, as if the artists tried and failed to use linear perspective. Each is a deliberate system with its own logic. Watch also for "flat" as the answer for everything; push for the specific rule (lines that spread apart, a moving viewpoint, higher means farther).
notes: Rublev's Trinity (about 1411 or 1425 to 1427, Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow) is a Russian icon in the Byzantine tradition and a classic example of reverse perspective: the footstools and table widen as they recede, as if the vanishing point were in the viewer's space. Along the River During the Qingming Festival (Zhang Zeduan, twelfth century, Palace Museum, Beijing) is a handscroll more than 5 meters long, viewed an arm's length at a time and unrolled from right to left. Chinese painters described ways of seeing a landscape from shifting viewpoints; the eleventh-century painter Guo Xi named "three distances" (high, deep and level). The Court of Gayumars is attributed to Sultan Muhammad, about 1522 to 1525, from the Shahnama made for Shah Tahmasp of Iran (Aga Khan Museum, Toronto). If an image is hard to find, any Byzantine icon, Chinese handscroll and Persian manuscript painting will do.

## Read
text: **Icon (Rublev, The Trinity).** Three angels sit around a table. A building, a tree and a rock stand behind them on a flat gold ground, with no horizon line. The lines of the table and footstools spread apart as they go back instead of coming together. This is called reverse perspective: the space seems to open toward the viewer, as if the viewer stood inside the picture's world.
text: **Handscroll (Zhang Zeduan, Along the River During the Qingming Festival).** A viewer unrolls the scroll a section at a time, moving along a river from quiet countryside into a busy city. The view is from a high, shifting point; the edges of buildings and boats stay parallel instead of meeting at a vanishing point, so no single spot is the viewer's position.
text: **Manuscript painting (Sultan Muhammad, The Court of Gayumars).** A king and his court sit on a rocky mountainside crowded with figures, animals and trees. Things higher on the page are farther away, but they barely shrink. Rocks, streams and figures are shown from whichever angle shows them best, in bright, even color with no cast shadows.

## Main: Compare the systems
table: Question | Icon | Handscroll | Manuscript painting
  height: 1.0
  - Where are the vanishing points? | {in front of the picture, in the viewer's space (lines spread apart)} | {none: parallel lines stay parallel} | {none: no single viewpoint}
  - Where is the viewer? | {facing the holy figures, almost inside the scene} | {moving along above the scene as it unrolls} | {everywhere at once, seeing each thing from its best side}
  - How is distance shown? | {barely; a flat gold ground, no horizon} | {higher on the scroll and hazier is farther; mist and empty space separate places} | {higher on the page is farther; things barely shrink}
  - What is it for? | {prayer: bringing the viewer into the presence of the sacred} | {a journey through time and place, unrolled by one viewer} | {telling a story and showing a whole court clearly, like a paradise garden}

## Explain
choice: Why might a handscroll avoid a single vanishing point?
  - Chinese painters had never noticed that lines converge.
  * A single fixed viewpoint would not work for a picture seen section by section as it unrolls.
  - Paper cannot hold straight lines.
  - Vanishing points were forbidden in China.
write: Choose one of the three works. Explain how its way of showing space serves its purpose better than linear perspective would.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: In the Trinity, reverse perspective opens the space toward the viewer, so the worshipper seems invited to the empty place at the table; linear perspective would set the scene behind a window, at a distance from the viewer, which works against the icon's purpose of presence.

## Stretch
write: Argue for or against this claim: "Linear perspective is the most advanced way to show space." Use two of the three works as evidence.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. A strong argument defines "advanced," recognizes that linear perspective solves one problem (a single fixed viewpoint at one moment), and shows with evidence how the other systems solve different problems: a moving viewer, a sacred presence, a complete view of a story.

# 21. Portfolios for Different Audiences
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; optional: the portfolio guidelines of real programs your students are considering
ican: I can compare what different audiences look for in a portfolio and plan one for a specific purpose.
directions: Read the four audience cards. Fill in the comparison chart, then choose one audience and plan a portfolio for it.
mistakes: Students often send the same portfolio everywhere. Each audience asks a different question: art schools ask about potential and voice, colleges about curiosity and commitment, employers about skills for a job, competitions about one strong idea. Requirements vary widely: the chart describes common patterns, so always check the specific guidelines.
notes: Numbers here are typical ranges, not rules; every program and contest sets its own. If students have real targets, have them print or copy those guidelines and use them in the stretch. Competitions often have strict rules on originality, size and the use of reference images; read them closely.

## Read: Four audiences
text: **Art and design schools** want to see potential and a personal voice. They often ask for about 12 to 20 pieces, value strong observational drawing, range of media and experiments, and sometimes ask for sketchbook pages.
text: **College admissions (non-art majors)** often accept an optional art supplement. Readers want evidence of sustained interest, commitment and growth, usually in a short selection of about 8 to 10 works with brief descriptions.
text: **Employers and clients** want skills that fit the job: a focused set of polished work in the field (illustration, design, animation), shown professionally, often online, with work that matches the style they need.
text: **Competitions** usually ask for one to a few works that fit a theme or category, judged on originality, technical skill and how well the work answers the prompt. Rules on size, medium and originality are strict.

## Main: Compare
table: Audience | What they look for | How many works (typical) | What to leave out
  - Art school | {potential, personal voice, observational drawing, range} | {about 12 to 20} | {copies of other art, too many pieces in one style}
  - College admissions | {sustained interest, commitment and growth} | {about 8 to 10} | {unfinished or unrelated pieces, long explanations}
  - Employer or client | {polished, job-ready skills in the field} | {a focused set} | {work outside the field, student exercises}

## Questions
choice: A student has a strong series of 15 observational charcoal drawings and some sketchbook experiments. Which audience is the best match?
  * an art school application
  - a company hiring a logo designer
  - a competition asking for one digital poster
write: Why might the same piece be perfect for one audience and wrong for another? Give an example.
  lines: 3
  key: Each audience asks a different question. A rough sketchbook page shows thinking and process, which art schools may want, but an employer wants polished finished work; a strong poster may win a competition but not show the range an art school wants.

## Stretch: Plan one portfolio
write: Choose one audience. List the pieces you would include (titles or descriptions), the order, and one sentence on why the set fits that audience.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. A strong plan matches the audience's priorities from the chart, stays within a typical number, opens and closes with strong pieces, and explains the fit in one clear sentence.

# 25. Installation Diagram
minutes: 60
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator; measurements of a real wall in your school or a practice wall from your teacher
ican: I can plan a professional installation with accurate measurements, consistent hanging height and suitable lighting.
directions: Work the hanging and spacing problems first. Then draw a scaled elevation of one wall of your final show with every measurement labeled, and plan the lighting and labels.
mistakes: The most common error is measuring to the top of the frame or to the nail instead of to the center of the work; the 57-inch rule sets the center. The second is forgetting the drop: the nail sits above the hanging wire's tightest point, not at the top of the frame. In spacing, students often forget that the end spaces count as gaps.
notes: Many museums and galleries hang works so their centers sit 57 inches from the floor, roughly average adult eye level, which gives a consistent line across a room. Nail height = 57 + (height of the work / 2) - (drop from the top of the frame to the wire pulled tight). Equal spacing: subtract the total width of the works from the wall width, then divide by the number of gaps (works + 1 when the end spaces match the gaps). Lighting: a common guideline aims track lights at about 30 degrees from vertical to limit glare and frame shadows. Light-sensitive works on paper and textiles are often kept to about 50 lux; oil and acrylic paintings tolerate more. Labels usually go beside the work, often to the right, at a consistent height.

## Warm up: Hanging height
example: A work is 30 inches tall. Its wire, pulled tight, sits 4 inches below the top of the frame. The center must be at 57 inches, so the top of the frame is at 57 + 15 = 72 inches, and the nail goes 4 inches lower: **68 inches**.

  - A work 48 inches tall with a wire drop of 6 inches: nail at {75} inches.
  - A work 16 inches tall with a wire drop of 2 inches: nail at {63} inches.
  - A work 36 inches tall with a wire drop of 5 inches: nail at {70} inches.

## Warm up: Spacing
fill:

  - A wall is 120 inches wide. Two works are each 24 inches wide. Each of the 3 equal spaces is {24} inches.

text: Scale: 1 inch on paper = ______ feet. Wall width: ______ Wall height: ______
text: Draw the wall to scale. Show each work as a rectangle with its size, the 57-inch center line, the spaces between works, and where labels and lights go.

## Lighting and labels
write: Describe your lighting plan: where the lights are, the angle and how you protect sensitive works.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong plan places lights so they hit each work at about 30 degrees from vertical to avoid glare and frame shadows, lights works evenly, and keeps works on paper at a lower level (about 50 lux) or away from sunlight.
write: Where will labels go, and how will you keep them consistent?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Beside each work (often to the right), all at one height and the same distance from each frame.

## Stretch
write: A 60-inch-tall painting would sit with its bottom edge only 27 inches from the floor at a 57-inch center. Would you keep the rule or break it? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Keeping it holds the room's center line consistent; breaking it (raising the work) may protect it from bumps or let it be seen over a crowd. A strong answer weighs consistency against the work's safety and viewing distance.

# 35. Prototype and Test
minutes: 60
materials: pencil; materials for three quick studies; sticky notes for viewers
ican: I can test prototypes of an artwork with viewers and use their responses to decide a direction.
directions: Choose the question your studies will test. Build 3 quick studies that differ in one main way. Ask at least 3 viewers neutral questions about each and record what they say. Then look for patterns and decide which direction to develop.
mistakes: Students often ask leading questions ("Do you see how sad it is?") and then count the yes answers as evidence. Ask open questions and record the exact words. Others change everything between studies, so they cannot tell what caused a response. And some let the vote decide: viewer responses are evidence, but the artist still decides with reasons.
notes: A study (or prototype) is a quick, small version made to test an idea before committing to a finished piece. Neutral questions: "What do you notice first?" "What do you think it is about?" "Where does your eye go?" "What word comes to mind?" Viewers can be classmates, other teachers or family members, recorded only as Viewer A, B and C; no names are needed. Answers vary throughout; the warm-up has keys.
