# 1. Six Panels with Sound Cues
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can plan a picture story with sounds and words.
directions: (Read aloud.) Match each thing to its sound. Then plan your own 6-panel story. Draw each panel. In the chart, write a sound cue and one line a character says for that panel.
mistakes: Students sometimes write a sound cue that does not fit the picture (a dog barking in a panel with no dog). Ask, "What would we hear in this picture?" Dialogue lines often run long: one short line per panel, like a speech bubble.
notes: A sound cue is a note that tells the sound maker what to play and when. Brainstorm story ideas first: a lost kite, a puppy at the park, a rainy day picnic. Students may use one of these or their own. Pairs can perform each other's storyboards: one reads the lines, one makes the sounds.

## Warm up: Sounds we know
match: Write the letter of its sound next to each thing.
  - Rain on a roof = pitter-patter
  - A door shutting = slam
  - A bee = buzz
  - A clock = tick-tock
example: Panel 1: A girl holds a kite on a windy hill.
  Sound cue: whoosh of wind
  Line: "Let's fly it!"

## Main: Panels 1 to 3
text: My story is about: ________________________________
draw: Panel 1 | 1.2
draw: Panel 2 | 1.2
draw: Panel 3 | 1.2
chart: Panel (1 to 3) | Sound cue | Line of dialogue
  rows: 3

## Main: Panels 4 to 6
draw: Panel 4 | 1.2
draw: Panel 5 | 1.2
draw: Panel 6 | 1.2
chart: Panel (4 to 6) | Sound cue | Line of dialogue
  rows: 3

## Stretch
write: Which panel needs its sound the most? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a panel and says what the sound adds, such as "Panel 4, because the crash shows the kite broke even before you read the words."

# 7. Recording Plan
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; your 6-panel storyboard
ican: I can plan what to record for each part of a story and who will do it.
directions: (Read aloud.) Decide if each thing is a photo, a voice or a sound. Then use your storyboard to plan your recording: for each panel, write the photo to take, the voice to record, the sound to make and who will do it.
mistakes: Students mix up voices and sounds. A voice is a person talking (a line from the story); a sound is everything else we hear (wind, a door, a dog). Watch for one person doing every job: each crew member should have at least one job.
notes: Use the storyboard from the Six Panels with Sound Cues sheet, or any 6-panel plan. A panel may need no voice or no sound: students write "none" rather than leaving it blank, so the plan shows they thought about it. Recording works best in a quiet corner with one person saying "Quiet on set!" Answers for the plan vary; a strong plan has something in every box and names a person for each panel.

## Warm up: Photo, voice or sound?
table: Thing to record | Photo, voice or sound?
  - a picture of the swings at the park | {photo}
  - Mia says, "Let's go!" | {voice}
  - a dog barking | {sound}
  - the wind blowing | {sound}
  - a picture of Mia holding a kite | {photo}
  - Ben says, "Look how high it flies!" | {voice}

## Main: My recording plan
example: Panel 1. Photo: Mia on the hill holding a kite. Voice: Mia says, "Let's fly it!" Sound: whoosh of wind. Who: Ava takes the photo, Leo says the line, Sam makes the wind sound.
chart: Photo to take | Voice to record | Sound to make | Who does it
  labels: Panel 1; Panel 2; Panel 3; Panel 4; Panel 5; Panel 6
  height: 0.75
  key: Answers vary. Every box is filled ("none" is fine), each photo matches its panel's picture, each voice is a line a character says, and each sound fits what is happening.

## Stretch
write: (Read aloud.) Which part will be the hardest to record? How will your crew make it work?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one recording, says why it is hard (a noisy room, a sound nobody can make) and gives a plan, such as recording at lunch when the room is empty or using paper to make the wind sound.

# 20. Rehearsal Checklist
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, a timer or clock with a second hand; your slides or storyboard
ican: I can rehearse a presentation and check its timing, voices and order.
directions: (Read aloud.) Answer the timing questions. Then rehearse your show 3 times. After each rehearsal, write the time and check each thing your crew did well. Write one thing to fix before the next try.
mistakes: Crews often skip writing the fix, so rehearsal 2 repeats the mistakes of rehearsal 1. Make "one thing to fix" a rule before starting again. Timing is often off because someone waits too long between slides: one person can say "next" as a cue.
notes: Set a goal time for each crew before they start (3 minutes works well for a short show). One student can be the timer. Answers in the table vary; look for the fixes to change from one rehearsal to the next, and for more checks by rehearsal 3. Rehearsal times can be written in whole minutes for this grade.

## Warm up: Timing
example: Our goal is 3 minutes. Rehearsal 1 took 5 minutes. That is 2 minutes too long.
fill:
  - Our goal is 3 minutes. A rehearsal took 4 minutes. That is {1} minute too long.
  - Our goal is 3 minutes. A rehearsal took 2 minutes. That is {1} minute too short.
  - Our goal is 4 minutes. A rehearsal took 7 minutes. That is {3} minutes too long.

## Main: Three rehearsals
table: Check | Rehearsal 1 | Rehearsal 2 | Rehearsal 3
  height: 0.6
  - Time (minutes) | | |
  - On time? (yes or no) | | |
  - Voices loud and clear | | |
  - Slides in the right order | | |
  - One thing to fix | | |
note: Answers in the table vary. Look for a fix written after each rehearsal and fewer problems by rehearsal 3.

## Stretch
write: (Read aloud.) What changed the most from rehearsal 1 to rehearsal 3? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one change, such as "We got faster because Max said 'next' when it was time for a new slide," and tells what caused it.

# 21. Set Up the Room
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can plan a room so everyone can see and hear a show.
directions: (Read aloud.) Answer the questions. Fill in the sentences with words from the word bank. Then draw the room from above, like a bird looking down. Draw and label the screen, the seats, the presenter and the sound helper.
mistakes: Students often put the presenter in front of the screen, blocking it. Others face the seats away from the screen or crowd every seat to one side. Drawing "from above" is new: show a quick example on the board with a rectangle for a desk and circles for seats.
notes: Before drawing, walk the real room and decide where the screen is. A bird's eye plan uses simple shapes: a long rectangle for the screen, small squares or circles for seats, stick figures or letters (P for presenter, S for sound helper). Leave a clear path to the door.

## Warm up
choice: (Read aloud.) Which way should the seats face?
  * Toward the screen
  - Toward the door
  - Toward the back wall
choice: (Read aloud.) Where should the presenter stand?
  - In front of the screen
  * Beside the screen
  - Behind the seats

## Main: Use the word bank
text: Word bank: beside, screen, computer, path
fill:
  - The seats face the {screen}.
  - The presenter stands {beside} the screen so everyone can see.
  - The sound helper sits by the {computer} to start the sounds.
  - Leave a clear {path} to the door.

## Main: Draw the room from above
draw: My room plan: label the screen, seats, presenter and sound helper | 4.2

## Stretch
write: A friend who uses a wheelchair is coming to the show. Where will they sit? Mark it on your plan and tell why.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A good spot has a clear view of the screen and an open path to get there, such as the end of a row or a space at the side near the front, not squeezed behind the seats.

# 33. Pictures Say Things Too
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell what a picture in an ad is trying to say without words.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read about each pretend ad. Look at what the picture shows: the people, the clothes, the place and what they are doing. Write what the picture is saying about the product, like sporty or grown-up.
mistakes: Students often describe the picture ("a boy running") instead of what it suggests ("this water makes you fast and sporty"). Ask, "What does the ad want you to think about the product?" Some write the product's job ("it is water"); that is what it is, not what the picture says.
notes: The ads and products are made up (Product A, B, C, D). Read each one aloud and let students picture it, or sketch a quick version on the board. Accept any word or phrase that the picture's details support. The Stretch is the big idea: a picture can suggest something the product cannot really do.

## Warm up: Read a picture
example: Pretend ad: A cat wears a tiny crown and sits on a velvet pillow next to a bowl of Product Z cat food.
  The picture says: Product Z is fancy and special, food for a king.

## Main: Four pretend ads
write: Ad 1: A girl in a soccer uniform races down a field, sweaty and smiling, holding a bottle of Product A water.
  starter: The picture says Product A is ...
  lines: 2
  key: sporty, healthy, for fast, strong kids who play hard.
write: Ad 2: A boy wearing a jacket and tie and big glasses sits at a desk with a laptop, writing in a Product B notebook.
  starter: The picture says Product B is ...
  lines: 2
  key: grown-up, smart, serious and important, like for someone with a real job.
write: Ad 3: A big group of friends laughs together around a picnic table in warm sunshine, passing a box of Product C crackers.
  starter: The picture says Product C is ...
  lines: 2
  key: fun, happy, for sharing; it brings friends together.
write: Ad 4: A kid in a superhero cape leaps high over a fence wearing Product D sneakers.
  starter: The picture says Product D is ...
  lines: 2
  key: powerful, exciting; the sneakers make you jump high like a superhero.

## Stretch: Is it true?
choice: Can Product D sneakers really make you jump like a superhero?
  - Yes, the picture shows it.
  * No, the picture only makes it look that way.
  - Yes, because capes help you fly.
write: Pick one ad. Tell one thing the picture says that the product cannot really do.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: Product C crackers cannot really give you friends; the picture of friends laughing just makes the crackers seem fun.
