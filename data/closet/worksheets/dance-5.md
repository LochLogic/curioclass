# 1. Rondo Dance Chart
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, open floor space
ican: I can chart a rondo dance where a shared A phrase returns between contrasting sections.
directions: Answer the warm up questions about rondo form. Then chart your group's ABACA dance: the shared A phrase, and B and C phrases that contrast with A and with each other. Give the counts and movement qualities for each section.
mistakes: Groups often make B and C feel alike (both slow, both smooth). Each new section needs its own quality. Watch the count math: students forget that A happens three times when they total the dance.
notes: Rondo (ABACA) is a form from music; in dance the A phrase works like a chorus that everyone knows. A good class setup: the whole class learns A together, then small groups create B and C. In performance the whole class dances each A, and only the B group or C group dances its section.

## Warm up: The rondo pattern
example: Rondo form: **A B A C A**. A is the refrain that keeps returning; B and C are different "episodes" in between.
fill:
  - In ABACA, the A section is danced {3} times.
  - A is 8 counts, B is 16 counts and C is 16 counts. The whole dance is {56} counts.
  - If A is 16 counts, B is 8 counts and C is 8 counts, the whole dance is {64} counts.
  - The section danced right before C is {A}.
choice: Which list shows rondo form?
  - A B A
  - A A B B
  * A B A C A
  - A B C D E

## Main: Chart our ABACA dance
table: Section | Counts | Key moves | Movement qualities | Level, pathway or formation
  - A | | | |
  - B | | | |
  - A | same as above | same as above | same as above |
  - C | | | |
  - A | same as above | same as above | same as above |
write: Add up the counts of all five sections. How long is your whole dance?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. The total must include A three times, for example 8 + 16 + 8 + 16 + 8 = 56 counts.

## Explain
write: How is B different from A? Use at least two movement quality words.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer uses quality words such as sharp, smooth, heavy, light, fast, slow, strong or gentle, for example "A is strong and sharp; B is light and floaty with slow turns."
write: How is C different from both A and B?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. C should contrast with both, for example using a new level (low floor work), a new tempo or a new formation that neither A nor B used.

## Stretch
write: The last A could change slightly to show the dance is ending. What one change would you make, and why?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Good ideas: slow the final counts, end in a held group pose, face a new direction, or have everyone finish in unison after B and C groups rejoin.

# 7. Why the Dance Is Danced
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter (optional)
ican: I can sort dances by their purpose using evidence from a description.
directions: Match each purpose to its meaning. Then read each dance description, write its purpose (ceremony, celebration, story or social), and copy the clue words that prove it. Each purpose is used twice.
mistakes: Students confuse ceremony and celebration. A ceremony is a formal ritual with a set order and meaning, like a prayer or an official welcome; a celebration marks a happy occasion like a holiday or new year. Many students write a purpose without clue words: the evidence must come from the description.
notes: Many dances serve more than one purpose (a wedding hora is both a celebration and social), so the descriptions here point clearly to one. The sema is a religious ceremony of the Mevlevi order, and the haka is a Māori tradition with many kinds and purposes; teach both with respect, as living practices, and do not have students imitate them as a game. Show a short video of a few of the dances if you can.

## Warm up: Four purposes
match: Match each purpose to its meaning.
  - ceremony = a formal ritual with a set order, like a prayer or an official welcome
  - celebration = marks a happy occasion, like a holiday or a new year
  - story = acts out a tale with characters and events
  - social = people dance together to have fun and meet others

## Main: Sort the dances
example: **Hora.** At a wedding, guests join hands in a big circle and dance to celebrate the couple. Purpose: celebration. Clue words: "at a wedding," "to celebrate the couple."
table: Dance | Description | Purpose | Clue words
  - Lion dance | At Lunar New Year, two dancers inside one lion costume leap to drums and cymbals to welcome the new year and bring good luck. | {celebration} | {Lunar New Year; welcome the new year}
  - Sema | Members of the Mevlevi order in Turkey turn slowly in white robes as a form of prayer, in a ritual called the sema. | {ceremony} | {a form of prayer; a ritual}
  - Bharatanatyam | In this classical dance from South India, a dancer uses hand gestures and facial expressions to act out tales of gods and heroes. | {story} | {act out tales of gods and heroes}
  - Square dance | In this dance from the United States, four couples form a square while a caller calls out moves, and dancers swap partners and greet their neighbors. | {social} | {swap partners; greet their neighbors}
  - Haka of welcome | In New Zealand, Māori performers do a haka as part of a formal ceremony to welcome guests onto a marae, a meeting ground. | {ceremony} | {formal ceremony to welcome guests}
  - Lindy hop | In the 1930s, young people in Harlem, New York, met at ballrooms like the Savoy to dance the Lindy hop with partners and make friends. | {social} | {dance with partners; make friends}
  - The Nutcracker | In this ballet, dancers act out the tale of a girl whose nutcracker toy comes to life and leads her to a magical land of sweets. | {story} | {act out the tale of a girl}
  - Jarabe tapatío | On Mexican Independence Day, couples in bright costumes dance the jarabe tapatío at fiestas to mariachi music. | {celebration} | {Independence Day; fiestas}

## Stretch
write: Choose one dance above that could have a second purpose. Name the second purpose and explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary, for example "The jarabe tapatío is also social, because couples dance together and flirt playfully at fiestas," or "The lion dance also tells a story when the lion hunts for and 'eats' the lettuce."

# 20. Formations in Three Frames
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils, open floor space, a group of 4 to 6
ican: I can map a group's formations and pathways across a 32-count dance.
directions: Answer the count questions. Then, with your group, map your 32-count idea dance in 3 frames. In each frame, draw an X for each dancer, number the dancers, and draw arrows for the pathways they travel to the next formation.
mistakes: Groups draw the formations but forget the pathways between them; every dancer needs an arrow from where they are to where they go next. Watch for two dancers' arrows that collide: change one pathway or the counts. Students also lose track of stage left and right; the audience is always at the bottom of each frame.
notes: Frames split the 32 counts: frame 1 is counts 1 to 8, frame 2 is counts 9 to 24, frame 3 is counts 25 to 32. Groups can choose a different split if they note it. Have each group walk the pathways slowly before drawing. The idea can come from a poem, a picture, a word or a piece of music.

## Warm up: Counts and formations
fill:
  - Frame 2 starts on count {9} and ends on count {24}, so it lasts {16} counts.
  - 32 counts is {4} sets of 8 counts.
  - A group takes 8 counts to travel from a line to a circle. If each dancer walks one step per count, each dancer takes {8} steps.
choice: Which formation would best show "a group closing in to protect a friend"?
  - a straight line across the stage
  - dancers scattered far apart
  * a tight circle around one dancer
choice: Two dancers' pathways cross at the same count. What should the group do?
  * change one pathway or the timing so they do not collide
  - run faster
  - close their eyes

## Main: Our 3 frames
text: Our dance idea: ____________________________________________
text: **UPSTAGE (back) is at the top of each frame. The audience sits at the bottom.**
draw: Frame 1: counts 1 to 8. Starting formation and pathways. | 2.1
draw: Frame 2: counts 9 to 24. Middle formation and pathways. | 2.1
draw: Frame 3: counts 25 to 32. Ending formation. | 2.1
table: Frame | Formation name | What it shows about our idea
  - 1 | |
  - 2 | |
  - 3 | |
note: Frames are each group's own. Check for an X per dancer, numbers, arrows for every dancer between frames, and a reason in the table that ties each formation to the idea.

## Stretch
write: Which change of formation is the most exciting for the audience to watch? Explain why.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary, for example "The change from frame 2 to frame 3, because the scattered dancers rush into one tight cluster on count 25, like raindrops joining a river."

# 21. Motif Journey Planner
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, open floor space
ican: I can plan how a motif repeats, changes and returns across a dance.
directions: Name your motif and the idea it comes from. Match the ways to vary a motif. Then plan how your motif appears at the beginning, middle and end of your dance, and describe each change.
mistakes: Students often change the motif so much that it is no longer recognizable: keep at least one part of the motif the same in every variation. Others repeat it the same way three times with no change in the middle.
notes: A motif is a short movement idea, often 2 to 4 counts, that a dance is built on. The idea can come from an artwork, a word or a poem studied in this module. Have students teach their motif to a partner, then show only the middle variation: can the partner spot the motif inside it?

## Warm up: Ways to vary a motif
match: Match each way of changing a motif to an example. The motif is: reach up high, then circle the arm down (4 counts).
  - level = do it while kneeling, close to the floor
  - tempo = do it in 2 counts instead of 4
  - size = make the reach tiny, just with one hand
  - direction = face the back wall to do it
  - body part = trace the circle with a foot instead of an arm
  - repetition = do it three times in a row

  - | |
write: Describe your motif in words so a reader could picture it.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong description names body parts, direction and counts, for example "Counts 1-2, both arms sweep up like a wave; counts 3-4, I fold forward and let the arms crash down."

## Main: The journey
table: Part | What the motif does here (repeat, vary or return) | How it changes | Counts
  - Beginning | | |
  - Middle | | |
  - End | | |
write: Why does the motif return at the end? What should the audience feel when they see it again?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer explains that the return ties the dance together and links to the idea, for example "The wave returns, calm and slow, to show the storm is over, so the audience feels peace."

## Stretch
write: Which variation will be hardest for an audience to recognize as your motif? What will you keep the same so they still see it?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the variation and the feature kept the same, such as the shape of the path or the rhythm.

# 33. Dance Review Paragraph
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a review that describes a dance, explains its idea and judges it with criteria.
directions: Sort the review sentences. Then read about the dance "River Run" and write a review paragraph that describes it, explains its idea and judges it with 2 criteria.
mistakes: Students jump straight to "I liked it" without describing. Description comes first and stays neutral (what happened); the judgment comes last and must name a criterion and evidence. Others name criteria but give no example from the dance.
notes: "River Run" is a made-up student dance written for this sheet. After practicing, students can review a class performance or a short video. Good criteria for grade 5: clear movements, movements that show the idea, dancers moving together on the counts, use of levels and space, a strong beginning and ending.

## Warm up: Sort the sentences
sort: Describe, explain or judge?
  - Describe: Six dancers start in a low clump; The dancers travel in a curving line
  - Explain: The curving line shows the river bending; The low start is like a spring under the ground
  - Judge: The ending is strong because every dancer freezes on the same count; The middle is unclear because some dancers lose the counts

## Main: Read and review
passage: River Run
  Six dancers in blue and silver begin in a low clump at stage right, rocking slowly like water bubbling from a spring. One by one they rise and join a curving line that winds across the stage, their arms rippling in a wave from dancer to dancer. In the middle section the music speeds up: the dancers split into two groups that rush past each other in zigzag pathways, like rapids crashing over rocks. Two dancers bump shoulders here and lose the counts for a moment. In the end, the dancers spread wide across the front of the stage and slowly lower their arms to the floor, like a river flowing into the calm sea, and they freeze together on the last count.
write: Describe: What happens in the dance? Give 2 details.
  lines: 2
  starter: In "River Run," ...
  key: In "River Run," six dancers start in a low rocking clump, then join a curving line with rippling arms, rush past each other in zigzags, and end spread wide across the front of the stage.
write: Explain: What is the idea, and how do the moves show it?
  lines: 2
  starter: The dance shows ...
  key: The dance shows the journey of a river from a spring to the sea. The low bubbling start is the spring, the curving line is the river bending, the zigzags are rapids and the wide slow ending is the calm sea.
write: Judge with criterion 1:
  lines: 2
  starter: The dance does / does not ... because ...
  key: For example, "The dance shows its idea clearly, because each section looks like a part of a river, from bubbling spring to calm sea."
write: Judge with criterion 2:
  lines: 2
  starter: The dance does / does not ... because ...
  key: For example, "The dancers do not always stay together on the counts, because two dancers bump and lose the counts in the rapids section, though they all freeze together at the end."

## Stretch
write: Put your sentences together into one review paragraph with a final sentence that sums up your opinion.
  lines: 6
  key: A model: In "River Run," six dancers begin in a low rocking clump, join a curving line with rippling arms, rush past each other in zigzags and end spread across the front of the stage. The dance shows a river's journey from a spring to the sea, and each section matches a part of that journey. The idea is very clear. The dancers mostly move together, but two of them bump and lose the counts in the rapids. Overall, "River Run" is a creative, easy-to-follow dance that would be even stronger with cleaner timing in the middle.
