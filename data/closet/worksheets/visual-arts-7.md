# 1. Proportion and Scale Play
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser, colored pencils
ican: I can change the scale of an object to create an effect and explain what it does to the viewer.
directions: Choose one everyday object (a cup, a shoe, a pencil, an apple). Draw the same object three times in three scenes: at realistic scale, at exaggerated (giant) scale and at miniature (tiny) scale. Then describe the effect of each.
mistakes: Students often draw the object big but forget the scene around it, so nothing shows it is giant. Scale only reads when there is something familiar to compare it with: a person, a door, a tree. Keep the object the same in all three; only its size compared to the scene changes.
notes: Show public-domain examples of scale as meaning: ancient Egyptian wall paintings where the most important person is drawn largest (hierarchical scale), and the illustrations of Gulliver's Travels where Gulliver towers over the tiny people. Proportion is the size of parts compared to each other within one thing; scale is the size of a thing compared to something else.

## Warm up: Words to know
match: Match each word to its meaning.
  - proportion = the size of parts compared to each other inside one object
  - scale = the size of an object compared to the things around it
  - exaggerated scale = much bigger than it would be in real life
  - miniature scale = much smaller than it would be in real life
  - hierarchical scale = the most important figure drawn biggest
choice: In an ancient Egyptian painting, a king is drawn twice as tall as his servants. Why?
  - He was really twice as tall.
  * The artist showed his importance with size.
  - He was closer to the viewer.
  - The artist made a mistake in proportion.

## Main: One object, three scales
text: My object: ____________________
draw: Realistic scale: the object at its normal size next to a person or room | 2
write: Effect: how does this scene feel?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Realistic scale feels ordinary, believable and calm; the viewer barely notices the object.
draw: Exaggerated scale: the object giant in the same kind of scene | 2
write: Effect: how does this scene feel?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A giant object feels surprising, funny, powerful or threatening; it becomes the most important thing in the picture.
draw: Miniature scale: the object tiny in the same kind of scene | 2
write: Effect: how does this scene feel?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A tiny object feels delicate, lost, cute or easy to miss; the viewer has to search for it.

## Stretch
write: Which of your three scenes tells the most interesting story? What clue in the scene makes the viewer understand the object's size?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name a comparison clue, such as a person standing next to a giant apple or a shoe the size of a car, and explain the story it creates.

# 5. Vermeer's The Art of Painting
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler; the teacher shows Vermeer's The Art of Painting (a printout lets students draw the grid right on it)
ican: I can identify balance, emphasis, movement and unity in a painting and support each with evidence.
directions: Sketch the main shapes of the painting in the box and divide your sketch into a 3 by 3 grid, labeled like the example. Then fill in the chart: for each principle, say what you see and which grid squares hold the evidence. Finish with the questions.
mistakes: Students often name a principle without evidence ("it has balance"). Every row needs a thing you can point to and a grid square. Watch for "symmetrical balance": the painting is asymmetrical; the heavy dark curtain on one side is balanced by the lighter, busier space of the studio on the other.
notes: Show Johannes Vermeer's The Art of Painting (about 1666-1668, Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna), public domain. Students should see: a heavy patterned curtain pulled back across the left third with a chair in front of it; a painter seen from behind in a black and white slit doublet, seated at his easel on the right; a young woman posing by the window as Clio, the muse of history, with a laurel wreath, a trumpet and a large book; a big map of the Netherlands on the back wall; a gold chandelier at the top; a black and white tiled floor. Grid squares in the key are approximate; accept nearby squares. Vermeer kept this painting all his life.

## Warm up: Four principles
match: Match each principle to its meaning.
  - balance = the way visual weight is spread so the work feels steady
  - emphasis = the area that catches the eye first
  - movement = the path the viewer's eye travels through the work
  - unity = the sense that all the parts belong together
example: Label your grid like a map: columns A, B, C from left to right, and rows 1, 2, 3 from top to bottom. The top left square is A1; the bottom right square is C3.

## Main: Grid and evidence

  - Balance | {Asymmetrical: the heavy, dark curtain on the left balances the painter, easel and bright wall on the right.} | {A1-A3 and C2-C3}
  - Emphasis | {The young woman in blue, lit by the window, catches the eye; the painter is at work painting her wreath.} | {B2}

choice: What kind of balance does the painting have?
  - symmetrical
  * asymmetrical
  - radial
  - no balance at all

  * pull the viewer in, as if we are peeking into the studio
  - hide the main subject
  - show where the painter lives
  - make the room look smaller
write: How does Vermeer use light to create emphasis? Give one detail.
  lines: 3
  key: Light from the hidden window on the left falls brightest on the young woman's face, her blue robe and the map behind her, while the curtain and the painter's back stay darker, so our eye goes to her first.

## Stretch
write: The painting shows a painter at work, but we never see his face. Why might Vermeer have chosen this, and how does it change what the painting is "about"?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. With his face hidden, the painter becomes any painter, so the work is about the art of painting itself rather than one person; it also lets us stand behind him and share his view of the model.

# 15. Masaccio's Holy Trinity
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler; the teacher shows Masaccio's Holy Trinity (a printout lets students draw on it)
ican: I can find the vanishing point in a Renaissance painting and explain how linear perspective changed what viewers saw.
directions: Read the background. On a printout (or your sketch of the vault), extend the lines of the ceiling squares until they meet. Mark the vanishing point. Then answer the questions about why early viewers found the painting so startling.
mistakes: Students often extend the arch curves instead of the straight edges. Use the straight lines of the coffers (the ceiling squares) that run back into the vault, and the edges of the ledges. Also, the vanishing point is low, not up near the ceiling: it sits at about the eye level of a person standing in the church.
notes: Masaccio painted the Holy Trinity in fresco on a wall of the church of Santa Maria Novella in Florence, in the mid-1420s (about 1425-1427). It is one of the first paintings to use linear perspective, the system Filippo Brunelleschi had recently shown, with a single vanishing point. The vanishing point is at about the level of the ledge where the two donors kneel, near the foot of the cross, roughly the eye level of a standing viewer. In the 1500s, Giorgio Vasari praised the painted vault, saying (in paraphrase) that it looked like a hole had been opened in the wall. A skeleton on a tomb below carries a warning that the viewer will also die one day. Public-domain photos are easy to find.

## Warm up: Background
text: Before the 1400s, many European church paintings showed holy figures against flat gold backgrounds, with little sense of real space. In Florence in the 1420s, the young painter **Masaccio** (1401-1428) painted the **Holy Trinity** on a church wall. He used **linear perspective**: all the lines that go back into space meet at one **vanishing point**, set at the viewer's eye level.
fill:
  - Lines that go back into space meet at the {vanishing point}.
  - In linear perspective, the vanishing point sits on the {horizon line}, at the viewer's eye level.
choice: Where would you expect to find the vanishing point in a painting made for people standing in a church?
  - at the very top of the painting
  * at about the eye level of a standing person
  - at the bottom edge
  - outside the painting, far above it

  - at the head of God the Father

## Stretch
write: The two donors kneel outside the painted chapel, on the same level as the viewer. What message might this placement send to people praying in the church?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. They seem to kneel in our world, between us and the holy figures, so the painting invites viewers to pray as the donors do and connects the real church to the sacred space; the skeleton below adds a reminder about death and faith.

# 21. Renaissance Florence
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how patrons and humanist ideas in Renaissance Florence shaped the art made there.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight the names of patrons. Then answer the questions, using details from the passage.
mistakes: Students often think Renaissance artists simply chose what to paint. Most major works were commissioned: patrons chose the subject, the size and often the materials. Also watch for "humanism" read as "not religious." Humanists were usually religious; they added a new interest in classical learning and human ability.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 350 words). Show public-domain images if you can: Brunelleschi's dome of Florence Cathedral, Ghiberti's Gates of Paradise doors on the Baptistery, Michelangelo's David, and Botticelli's The Birth of Venus. Florence was a republic in name while the Medici held power from about 1434; the details are simplified here.

## Read
passage: Money, Ideas and Art in Florence
  In the 1400s, Florence, Italy, was one of the richest cities in Europe. Its wealth came from banking and from the wool and cloth trade. Rich families and powerful trade groups called guilds wanted the city to look as great as it felt, and they paid for art to make that happen. A person or group who pays for art is called a patron.
  The most famous patrons were the Medici, a family of bankers. Cosimo de' Medici and later his grandson Lorenzo, called "the Magnificent," paid for churches, chapels, paintings and sculpture. They also invited scholars and artists into their homes. A young Michelangelo lived in Lorenzo's household and studied the ancient sculptures in his collection.
  Guilds were patrons too. In 1401 the cloth merchants' guild held a contest to design new bronze doors for the city's Baptistery. Lorenzo Ghiberti won and spent decades on two sets of doors. A famous story says Michelangelo called the second set fit to be the gates of paradise, and that name stuck.
  Patrons paid for more than beauty. A great chapel showed a family's faith, but it also showed its wealth and power. A statue in the town square could carry a message to everyone who walked by. Michelangelo's giant marble David, finished in 1504, showed a young hero who defeated a giant. People in Florence saw themselves in him: a small city standing up to stronger enemies.
  New ideas shaped the art as well. Scholars called humanists studied the writings and art of ancient Greece and Rome. They believed people could learn, reason and create great things. Artists took up those ideas. They studied ancient statues to draw the human body in realistic proportion. Filippo Brunelleschi, who built the huge dome of the cathedral, showed how linear perspective could make a flat painting look deep. Painters like Sandro Botticelli painted stories from Greek and Roman myths, not only Bible stories.
  Money and ideas worked together. Patrons paid the bills, humanists supplied the ideas, and artists turned both into some of the most famous art in the world.

## Questions
choice: What is a patron?
  - an artist who teaches students
  * a person or group who pays for art
  - a scholar who studies Greek writing
  - a building in the town square
fill:
  - The Medici family made its money in {banking}.
  - The contest for the Baptistery doors was held by a {guild} in 1401, and {Ghiberti} won.
choice: Which detail best shows humanist ideas in Renaissance art?
  - Patrons paid for chapels.
  * Artists studied ancient statues to draw the body in realistic proportion.
  - Florence traded wool and cloth.
  - Ghiberti spent decades on the doors.
write: Patrons "paid for more than beauty." Use one example from the passage to explain what else a patron could get from a work of art.
  lines: 3
  key: A family chapel showed the family's faith but also its wealth and power; the David in the town square sent a message that Florence, though small, could stand up to stronger enemies.
write: How did linear perspective connect to humanist ideas about what people can do?
  lines: 3
  key: Humanists believed people could use reason to learn and create; perspective used geometry and careful observation to make flat paintings look deep, showing human skill and reasoning at work.

## Stretch
write: Today, who are the patrons of art in your town or online? How might they shape the art that gets made, the way the Medici and guilds did?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: city governments paying for murals, businesses, museums, schools, crowdfunding supporters, companies paying for ads or game art. Like Renaissance patrons, they choose subjects, size and budget, and the art can carry their message or show their image.

# 25. Making a Woodblock Print in Edo Japan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter; the teacher shows Hokusai's Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave)
ican: I can explain how a Japanese woodblock print was made by a team of specialists.
directions: Read the passage and highlight each person's job. Then look closely at the print your teacher shows and answer the questions, connecting what you see to the person who made it happen.
mistakes: Students often credit the whole print to Hokusai alone. He designed it; carvers cut the blocks and printers made the copies, and a publisher paid for and sold it. Also watch for "Hokusai painted it": the finished print was printed, not painted, and many copies exist.
notes: Show Katsushika Hokusai's Under the Wave off Kanagawa (Kanagawa oki nami ura), known as The Great Wave, from the series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (about 1830-1832). Many museums own impressions; the Metropolitan Museum of Art and others share public-domain images. The passage is original to Curio Class (about 330 words) and simplifies the process. The deep blue in many impressions is Prussian blue, a pigment newly popular in Japan at the time. Students should see three long boats with rowers, a huge clawing wave, a small snow-capped Mount Fuji, and a gray sky that darkens toward the horizon.

## Read
passage: Four Jobs, One Print | numbered
  In Edo, the city we now call Tokyo, colorful woodblock prints were sold in shops like posters and postcards today. They showed actors, beautiful landscapes, travelers and famous places. These prints are called ukiyo-e, "pictures of the floating world." One print could be printed hundreds of times, so ordinary townspeople could afford them.
  Making a print took a team. The publisher started it. The publisher chose the subject, hired the artists, paid for the wood and paper and sold the finished prints. A publisher might plan a whole series, such as views of Mount Fuji, hoping buyers would collect them all.
  The designer drew the picture. A designer like Katsushika Hokusai made a careful ink drawing on thin paper. His name went on the print, and today he is the person we remember.
  The carver turned the drawing into a block. The drawing was pasted face down on a smooth block of cherry wood. The carver cut away all the wood except the lines, which stood up in relief. This destroyed the drawing, so the carving had to be perfect. This first block, the key block, printed the outlines. Then the carver cut a separate block for each color, guided by proofs the designer marked with colors. Expert carvers could cut hair lines finer than a thread.
  The printer made the copies. The printer brushed water-based ink onto a block, laid damp paper on top and rubbed the back with a round flat pad called a baren. Then the next block printed the next color on the same sheet. Small notches cut in the corner and side of every block, called kento, held the paper in the same spot each time so the colors lined up. Printers could also wipe ink onto a block unevenly to make a color fade smoothly from dark to light.
  No one person made the print. It was the work of a business owner, an artist and two kinds of skilled craftspeople working together.

## Questions

  - Made the first ink drawing on thin paper | {designer}
  - Paid for materials and sold the prints | {publisher}
  - Cut the drawing into cherry wood, one block per color | {carver}

  - The wood was very rare.
  * Carving through the pasted drawing destroyed it, so there was no second chance.
  - The publisher checked every line with a ruler.
  - The ink would not stick to rough wood.
choice: What did the kento notches do?
  - They showed the designer's name.
  - They made the colors fade.
  * They kept the paper in the same place for every color block.
  - They held the baren while it dried.
write: Look at the print. Name one detail that shows the carver's skill and one that shows the printer's skill. Explain each.
  lines: 4
  key: Carver: the tiny claw-like fingers of foam and the thin lines inside the wave, cut very finely so they print as sharp white and blue shapes. Printer: the gray sky that fades smoothly from dark to light behind Mount Fuji, made by wiping ink unevenly on the block; also the blues lining up exactly inside the outlines.
write: The passage says, "No one person made the print." Do you agree that Hokusai should get most of the credit? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers weigh both sides: Hokusai's design and idea make the image famous, but the carver's fine cutting and the printer's color work made it real and repeatable, and the publisher made it possible; credit could be shared.

## Stretch
write: Ukiyo-e prints were made in hundreds of copies and sold cheaply. How is that similar to images people share today?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: like posters, prints, magazines or images online, they reached many ordinary people, showed popular subjects (celebrities, travel, landscapes) and were made by teams for a business.

# 35. Mural Design Development
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencils
ican: I can develop a mural theme into designs that use unity and movement across a long wall.
directions: Write your top mural theme. Plan two different full-color designs for a long wall (much wider than it is tall). Each design must hold together with unity and lead the viewer's eye from one end to the other with movement. Then compare them and choose one.
mistakes: Students often draw a row of separate pictures, like a comic strip, with nothing linking them. A long mural needs something that flows the whole length: a river, a road, a ribbon of color, a line of people or a repeated shape. Also watch for too many colors: a limited palette, repeated across the wall, is what makes it feel unified.
notes: Real murals are long: people walk past them, so the design should read from a distance and reward a walk from end to end. Show a public-domain mural photo or the Wall of Respect (Chicago, 1967) as discussion. Design A and B should be truly different (for example, a flowing river versus a rising staircase of shapes), not one design recolored. Students can enlarge the chosen design with a grid later.

  - as many different images as possible
  - a thick black frame around each picture

  - one large circle in the exact center
  - a solid background with no shapes
  * a winding river or road that flows the whole length
  - small pictures scattered at random

## Compare
write: Which design is stronger? Compare their unity and movement, and name one change you will make before the final version.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers compare specific choices: "Design A is stronger because the river carries the eye the whole length and the gold leaves repeat in every section; in Design B the eye stops in the middle. I will add more leaves at the right end so it does not feel empty."

## Stretch
write: People will see the mural from far away and up close. What will they notice from far away, and what details will reward someone who walks right up to it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. From far away: the big shapes, the main colors and the overall flow. Up close: small details such as patterns inside leaves, faces, textures or hidden symbols that make people slow down.
