# 1. Life After High School Activity Map
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can find places to stay active in a community and match them to a person's schedule, budget and interests.
directions: Study the map of Riverton, a pretend city, and the guide to its places. Match each character to the place that fits their schedule, budget and interests best. Then use the map to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students match on interest alone and miss the schedule: Jae wants the weight room, and the rec center opening at 6 a.m. is what makes it work with a 1 p.m. shift. Count blocks along the streets (up, down, left, right), not corner to corner.
notes: Riverton and its people are fictional. Key: Dana, Eastside League; Jae, Northside Rec Center; Omar, River Trail; Rosa, Maple Park. The pool fits no one: it is indoor swimming, and nobody asked for it, which is a good discussion point. Distances: Dana's home (F1) to the league fields (F3) is 2 blocks; Rosa's home (D5) to Maple Park (C3) is 3; Omar's home (E4) to the nearest River Trail square (A4) is 4. Map key: the route runs F1, F2, F3; stars go on the River Trail squares and Maple Park. A follow-up: have students find the real parks, trails and recreation programs near the school using the city or county website.

## The map
text: Each square is one city block. Columns are A to F, rows are 1 to 5. Move only up, down, left or right.
table: Row | A | B | C | D | E | F
  - 1 | River Trail | | Rec Center | | | Dana's home
  - 2 | River Trail | | | Bus stop | |
  - 3 | River Trail | Jae's home | Maple Park | | | League fields
  - 4 | River Trail | | Bus stop | | Omar's home |
  - 5 | River Trail | | Pool | Rosa's home | |
table: Place | What is there | Hours | Cost
  - River Trail | Paved path along the river for walking, running and biking; no cars | Dawn to dusk | Free
  - Maple Park | Outdoor basketball courts, a field and a fitness loop | Dawn to dusk | Free
  - Northside Rec Center | Weight room, indoor track and group classes | 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. every day | $15 a month
  - Community Pool | Indoor lap swimming | 11 a.m. to 7 p.m. | $3 a visit
  - Eastside League (League fields) | Adult softball and volleyball teams for all skill levels | Games Tuesday and Thursday at 7 p.m. | $40 a season

## Main: Match the people
text: **Dana** works 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays and is free Tuesday and Thursday evenings. Dana wants to join a team and meet new people and can pay a small fee. **Jae** works 1 p.m. to 10 p.m. and wants to lift weights indoors all year, even in winter. **Omar** takes college classes, has no money for fees, and loves long bike rides away from traffic. **Rosa** works weekend shifts, is free on weekday afternoons, loves outdoor pickup basketball and does not want to pay fees.
match: Write the letter of the best place for each person.
  - Dana = Eastside League
  - Jae = Northside Rec Center
  - Omar = River Trail
  - Rosa = Maple Park
write: Pick one match and explain the two details that decided it.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Jae, because the rec center has a weight room indoors (all year) and opens at 6 a.m., before Jae's 1 p.m. shift.

## Main: Use the map
fill:
  - Blocks from Dana's home to the League fields: {2}
  - Blocks from Rosa's home to Maple Park: {3}
  - Blocks from Omar's home to the nearest River Trail square: {4}
  - What Jae would pay for a full year at the Rec Center: ${180}
text: On the map, draw the shortest route from Dana's home to the League fields, and draw a star on every place that is free.

## Stretch
write: Imagine you are 19 and living in Riverton. Which two places would you use, and when? Explain how they would fit a busy week.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names two places, gives days and times that fit the hours, mentions cost, and explains why the activities would last (enjoyment, friends, convenience).

# 7. Year-After Graduation Plan
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a 12-month activity plan for life after graduation with goals, seasonal changes and backup plans.
directions: Study Alex's sample plan. Then write your own 12-month plan for the year after graduation: a goal, an activity plan for each season, a backup for each one, and a way to check your progress. Plan for a realistic version of your life next year (work, school, travel, money).
mistakes: Goals are often too vague ("get in shape," "stay active") or tied to appearance; steer students to activity goals they control (minutes, days, events, skills). Many plans have no backup, so the first rainy week or exam week ends the habit. Watch for plans that count only sports teams: most adults stay active through walking, cycling, fitness classes, recreation leagues and active jobs.
notes: Weekly targets follow the US Physical Activity Guidelines for adults (2nd edition, 2018): 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days. Alex is fictional. Plans are personal, but students should not have to share anything private; a student can plan for a realistic version of next year. Model plans vary; a strong plan has a measurable goal, a different main activity or setting for at least two seasons, a specific backup for each season, and checkpoints with a way to adjust.

## Warm up
choice: Which is the strongest goal for the year after graduation?
  - Stay healthy.
  - Get really fit by next summer.
  * Get at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week and strength train twice a week, all 12 months.
  - Join a gym.
choice: Alex plans to bike to work all year. Which is a backup plan?
  - Buy a faster bike.
  - Bike harder in the summer to make up for winter.
  - Stop for the winter and start again in spring.
  * On icy or stormy days, ride the bus and do a 30-minute home workout video after work.
example: **Alex's plan (sample).** Goal: 150 or more active minutes a week and strength twice a week. **Summer:** bike to work (40 min a day) and pickup soccer Saturdays; backup: indoor fitness class on rainy days. **Fall:** college intramural volleyball and campus gym twice a week; backup: 20-minute dorm-room circuit during exam weeks. **Winter:** gym 3 days, weekend snowshoe walks; backup: bus plus home workout when roads are icy. **Spring:** train for a 5 km charity run in May; backup: walk-run plan if a sore knee flares. **Checkpoints:** log minutes weekly; review every 3 months.

## Main: My goal
write: Write one activity goal for the year. Make it measurable (minutes, days, an event or a skill).
  lines: 2
  starter: By the end of my first year after graduation, I will ...
  key: Example: By the end of my first year after graduation, I will average 180 active minutes a week, lift twice a week, and finish a 10 km run.
write: What will your life look like next year (work, school, location, budget), and how does that shape your plan?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: I will work full-time at a warehouse with early shifts and live with roommates on a tight budget, so I need free or cheap options close to home, before or after my shift.

## Main: Season by season
chart: Main activity, where and when | Minutes a week | Backup plan
  labels: Summer (Jun to Aug); Fall (Sep to Nov); Winter (Dec to Feb); Spring (Mar to May)
  height: 0.85
  key: Example: Summer, trail running and a free park yoga class, 180 min, backup: early-morning runs on hot days. Fall, recreation league basketball plus 2 home strength sessions, 200 min, backup: walk 30 min at lunch when work runs late. Winter, community center gym 3 days, 160 min, backup: home workout and stairs when it snows. Spring, train for a 10 km, 220 min, backup: bike instead of run if shins hurt.

## Main: Stay on track
write: List two barriers that could stop you and how you will handle each.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: (1) A new job schedule; I will schedule workouts like shifts in my phone calendar each Sunday. (2) Money; I will use free trails, the park and body-weight workouts if the gym costs too much.
write: How and when will you check your progress? What will you do if you are behind?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: I will log minutes each week and review my plan on the first of each third month (September, December, March, June). If I am behind, I will lower the target for a month, add a workout buddy, or switch to an activity I enjoy more instead of quitting.

## Stretch
write: Who is one person or group that could support your plan, and how?
  lines: 2
  key: Example: A coworker who also wants to run; we can meet at the trail twice a week after our shift and sign up for the spring 10 km together.

# 20. Mentor Session Plan
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan a mentoring session for younger students with a clear goal, an activity, a talk and a close.
directions: Read the model plan. Then use the frames to write a 30-minute plan for a mentoring session with a younger class: a goal, an activity, a short talk and a close. Check that your times add up to 30 minutes.
mistakes: Plans aimed at younger students often have long talks and complex rules; at ages 7 to 10, keep directions short, show instead of tell, and get everyone moving fast. Watch for goals that are about the mentor ("I want to show them my skills") instead of the learners. Mentors also forget the close: a quick review and a positive word are what students remember.
notes: Mentoring sessions should always run with the younger class's teacher present and in charge; mentors never take a child aside alone. Remind mentors to praise effort and specific actions, and to keep the talk to a few minutes. Times in the model: 3 + 15 + 7 + 5 = 30. The talk topics should match the Sportsmanship and Leadership module (taking turns, encouraging teammates, handling losing, playing fair). Model answers vary; a strong plan has one clear learner goal, an activity everyone is active in, a talk with a question kids can answer, and a close that checks the goal.

## Warm up
choice: You are mentoring a class of 8-year-olds. Which way of giving directions works best?
  - Explain every rule before anyone moves.
  * Show the activity with a partner, give two short rules, and start.
  - Hand out a written page of rules to read.
choice: Which is a good learner goal for a mentoring session?
  - I will show the kids how good I am at basketball.
  - The kids will be quiet the whole time.
  * Students will cheer a teammate by name after a mistake at least once in the game.

## Model plan
example: **Goal:** Second graders will say "good try" or "next one" to a teammate after a miss. **Activity (15 min):** Partner beanbag toss to hoops at 3 distances; partners take turns and cheer each try. **Talk (7 min):** Sit in a circle. "How did it feel when your partner cheered after a miss?" Act out a good and a not-so-good cheer. **Close (5 min):** Each child shows a thumbs-up and says one cheer they used; mentors give high-fives. **Opening (3 min):** Names, smile, rules: stay in your space, cheer every try.

## Main: My plan
text: Class I will mentor (grade): ______   Number of students: ______   Space: ______________
write: **Goal.** What will the younger students learn or do? Make it something you can see.
  lines: 2
  starter: By the end, students will ...
  key: By the end, students will take turns fairly in a game by waiting behind the cone and handing the ball to the next player.
write: **Opening.** How will you greet the class and set two simple rules?
  lines: 2
  starter: First, we will ...
  key: First, we will say our names, then I will give two rules: freeze when you hear the whistle, and hand the ball to the next person in line.
write: **Activity.** Describe the activity, the equipment, and how every child stays active.
  lines: 4
  starter: In this activity, ...
  key: In this activity, groups of 4 play relay dribbling around cones with one ball each group. Each child dribbles once, hands the ball to the next child and goes to the back, so lines stay short and everyone moves every minute.
write: **Talk.** Write the question you will ask and what you want them to understand.
  lines: 3
  starter: I will ask, "...
  key: I will ask, "How did you feel when it was your turn and someone handed you the ball nicely?" I want them to see that taking turns fairly makes the game fun for everyone.
write: **Close.** How will you end the session and check the goal?
  lines: 2
  starter: To finish, ...
  key: To finish, each group shows me their fair hand-off, I name one child in each group who waited well, and we do a team cheer.
fill:
  - My times: Opening ____ + Activity ____ + Talk ____ + Close ____ = {30} minutes

## Stretch
write: Name one thing that could go wrong (a child is upset, a game is too hard) and how you will handle it.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: If a child cries after losing the relay, I will crouch to their level, say it is okay to feel upset, let them take a short break near the teacher, and invite them back as the starter for the next race.

# 21. Tournament Results Analysis
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator
ican: I can graph tournament results against sportsmanship ratings and judge whether the data shows a link.
directions: Read the results of the Riverton intramural tournament. Plot one point for each team on the graph (wins across, sportsmanship rating up). Then answer the questions about what the data does and does not show.
mistakes: Students connect the points into a line as if it were a time graph; this is a scatter plot, so points stand alone. Others decide there is a link from one or two teams (the Otters won 5 and were rated highest) instead of looking at all eight. Watch for "sportsmanship causes winning" claims: even a real link would not prove cause.
notes: The teams and data are fictional. In a round robin of 8 teams, each team plays 7 games, so the wins are 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 and 0 (28 games in all). Ratings are the average of the officials' 1 to 5 sportsmanship ratings after each game. Key: highest rating Otters (4.7), lowest Bison (3.0); the top four teams and the bottom four teams both average 3.95, the same as the whole league; the correlation is 0, so there is no link in this data. The key graph joins the points in order of wins only so they are easy to check; students should plot points without a line.

## Data
table: Team | Wins (of 7) | Sportsmanship rating (1 to 5)
  - Hawks | 7 | 4.2
  - Comets | 6 | 3.1
  - Otters | 5 | 4.7
  - Pines | 4 | 3.8
  - Rockets | 3 | 4.5
  - Bison | 2 | 3.0
  - Lynx | 1 | 4.4
  - Tides | 0 | 3.9

## Graph
graph: Wins | 0 7 1 | Sportsmanship rating | 0 5 0.5
  key: 0,3.9 1,4.4 2,3.0 3,4.5 4,3.8 5,4.7 6,3.1 7,4.2

## Analyze
fill:
  - Team with the highest sportsmanship rating: {Otters}
  - Team with the lowest sportsmanship rating: {Bison}
  - Average rating of the top four teams (7, 6, 5 and 4 wins): {3.95}
  - Average rating of the bottom four teams (3, 2, 1 and 0 wins): {3.95}
choice: What does the scatter plot show about wins and sportsmanship in this tournament?
  - Teams that won more had much better sportsmanship.
  - Teams that won more had much worse sportsmanship.
  * There is no clear link: high and low ratings are spread across winning and losing teams.
write: The league director says, "Good sportsmanship makes teams win." Does this data support the claim? Use at least two numbers.
  lines: 4
  key: No. The top four and bottom four teams both average 3.95; the Otters (5 wins) and Rockets (3 wins) are both at 4.5 or higher, while the Comets (6 wins) and Bison (2 wins) are both near 3. Winning and sportsmanship do not move together here, and even if they did, a link would not show that one causes the other.
write: Give one other reason a team might earn a high sportsmanship rating that has nothing to do with winning.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: a captain who sets a respectful tone, a coach who rewards fair play, players who know the officials or the rules well, or a team that values playing for fun.

## Stretch
write: The league wants to reward sportsmanship as much as winning. Propose one change to how the tournament is scored or awarded, and predict one effect.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: add 1 standings point for every game with a sportsmanship rating of 4 or higher; teams would have a reason to stay respectful in close games, though officials' ratings would need to be consistent and fair.

# 33. Coaching Dilemmas
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose and explain a respectful, safe way to handle hard situations when I coach younger players.
directions: You and your classmates are running an after-school sports clinic for 8 to 10 year olds, with a teacher in charge. For each dilemma, choose the best response and explain why it works better than the others.
mistakes: Students pick responses that make the problem disappear fast (bench the showoff, let the scared child sit out) instead of ones that help the child learn. Watch for responses that call a child out in front of the group or that promise things a student coach cannot decide; anything about injuries, parents or playing time goes to the adult in charge.
notes: Key: Lila B, Marcus D, mixed group A, parent C, wrist D, cousin B. The principles behind the key: make it safe to try (softer equipment, smaller steps), correct privately and redirect, adjust tasks so every level is challenged (change space, task, equipment or people), listen and pass adult issues to the adult in charge, never play through a possible injury, and treat every child the same. A student coach should never diagnose an injury or settle a parent's complaint alone. All children in the dilemmas are fictional.

## Dilemmas
choice: Lila, age 9, flinches and turns away every time a volleyball comes toward her.
  - Tell her there is nothing to be scared of and keep running the drill.
  * Switch her to a softer, lighter ball, start with self-tosses and short, close tosses, and praise every attempt.
  - Let her sit out the rest of the clinic.
  - Have the whole group cheer while she tries alone in front of everyone.
write: Why is your choice better than having her sit out?
  lines: 2
  key: Sitting out keeps her afraid and gives her no practice; softer equipment and small steps make the skill feel safe, so she builds confidence and still learns.
choice: Marcus, age 10, is the best player. He keeps the ball to himself and laughs when others miss.
  - Tell him in front of the group that he is being a bad teammate.
  - Ignore it because he makes the scrimmages exciting.
  - Bench him for the whole session.
  * Talk to him privately, name the behavior, and give him a job, like earning points for assists.
write: What makes your choice work for Marcus and for the group?
  lines: 2
  key: A private talk avoids embarrassing him, the clear expectation stops the laughing, and the assist goal turns his skill into helping others, so teammates get more touches.
choice: Your group of 12 ranges from a child who has never dribbled to a travel-team player.
  * Set up stations at 3 levels and let each child pick a challenge, moving up when ready.
  - Teach everyone the same drill at the travel-team level.
  - Split the group into "good" and "beginner" teams by name.
  - Run the drill at the beginner level for everyone.
choice: At pickup, a parent tells you, upset, that her child "barely got to play" in the scrimmage.
  - Explain that her child is not as good as the others.
  - Argue that every child played the same amount.
  * Listen calmly, thank her, and tell her you will bring it to the teacher in charge, then do it.
  - Promise her child will play the most next week.
write: Why should a student coach not settle this alone?
  lines: 2
  key: Playing time and parent concerns are the adult leader's job; the student coach does not have the full picture or the authority, and handing it on calmly keeps trust with the family.
choice: A child falls, holds her wrist, and says it hurts, but she wants to keep playing.
  - Let her play if she can still move her fingers.
  - Tell her to shake it off.
  - Wrap it tightly and send her back in.
  * Have her sit out and get the teacher in charge right away to check it.
choice: Your younger cousin is in the clinic and wants to skip the line and always be your partner.
  - Let it go this time since he is family.
  * Tell him kindly that everyone gets the same turns and partners rotate, and stick to it.
  - Ask another coach to take him so you do not have to deal with it.
  - Let him skip the line but not be your partner.

## Stretch
write: Write a new dilemma you might face as a clinic coach, then write how you would handle it.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: Two kids argue about whether a ball was out. I would stop play, let each child say what they saw, use a quick fair method (replay the point), and remind them that in the clinic we settle calls by replaying, not arguing.
