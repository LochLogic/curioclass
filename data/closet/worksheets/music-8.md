# 1. Dominant Seventh Chords
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can build dominant seventh chords and label the root, third, fifth and seventh.
directions: Build the V7 chord in C, G and F major. Name the notes on the staff, spell each chord, and mark its notes on the keyboard strip.
pairs: chord-explorer
mistakes: The most common error is a major seventh on top (G B D F# in C major). The seventh of V7 always comes from the key itself, a minor 7th above the root. Watch for the missing accidental in D7 (F#, from G major's key signature) and C7 (Bb, from F major's).
notes: V7 = a major triad on scale degree 5 plus a minor 7th above the root. In C major: G7 = G B D F. In G major: D7 = D F# A C. In F major: C7 = C E G Bb. In resolution to I, the third of V7 (the leading tone) rises a half step to the tonic, and the seventh falls a step. The staff block prints natural notes only, so only G7 is shown on the staff; students write D7 and C7 with their accidentals in the table. The keyboard is printed as a table of the 12 keys from C to B (the black keys are the rows named C#, Eb, F#, Ab and Bb; each also has a second name, such as Db for C#); students write R, 3, 5 or 7 in each key the chord uses.

## Warm up: What is V7?

## Stretch: Where does it go?
fill:
  - G7 resolves to the C chord. Its third, B, moves up a half step to {C}.
  - Its seventh, F, moves down a step to {E}.
write: Spell the V7 chord of D major and explain how you found its seventh.
  lines: 3
  key: A7 = A C# E G. A is the 5th note of D major (D E F# G A B C#). Build a major triad on A (A C# E), then add the note a minor 7th (10 half steps) above A, which is G, a note already in D major.

# 7. Pachelbel's Ground Bass
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; a recording of Pachelbel's Canon in D; a keyboard (optional)
ican: I can label a ground bass with Roman numerals and track how melody lines fit its chords.
directions: Name the chord above each bass note of Pachelbel's ground bass and label it with a Roman numeral in D major. Then track three upper lines: tell whether each note is the root, third or fifth of its chord, and find the notes that are not in the chord.
pairs: chord-explorer
mistakes: Students often label every chord major. Use the D major scale pattern: chords on the 3rd and 6th notes (F♯ and B) are minor, so they are iii and vi in lower case. Watch for chord members counted from the bass note instead of the chord root, and for C♯ and F♯ written as C and F: D major has two sharps.
notes: Johann Pachelbel (1653-1706) wrote his Canon in D for three violins over a basso continuo; the 8-note bass repeats under the whole piece while the violins enter one after another, playing the same music in canon, with lines that get busier as the piece goes on. The canon became famous in the late 1960s through recordings. Answers: chords D A Bm F♯m G D G A; I V vi iii IV I IV V. Lines 1 and 2 follow the opening violin statements as they are commonly printed (one note per bass note); line 3 has two notes per bass note. Check lines against your score if you use a different edition. Non-chord notes in line 3: G over A (position 2), E over F♯m (position 4), G over A (position 8); they are passing notes between chord tones.

## Warm up: Chords in D major
fill:
  - D major has two sharps: {F♯} and {C♯}.
  - Chords on the 2nd, 3rd and 6th notes of a major scale are {minor}; chords on the 1st, 4th and 5th are {major}.
  - A triad has a root, a {third} and a {fifth}.

## Main: Map the ground bass

## Main: Track the upper lines
text: Write R (root), 3 (third) or 5 (fifth) for each note of lines 1 and 2.
table: Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8
  - Line 1 note | F♯ | E | D | C♯ | B | A | B | C♯
  - Chord member | {3} | {5} | {3} | {5} | {3} | {5} | {3} | {3}
  - Line 2 note | D | C♯ | B | A | G | F♯ | G | E
  - Chord member | {R} | {3} | {R} | {3} | {R} | {3} | {R} | {5}
  - Line 3 notes | D F♯ | A G | F♯ D | F♯ E | D B | D A | G B | A G
fill:
  - In line 3, the notes that are NOT in their chord are at positions {2}, {4} and {8}.
  - Line 1 moves {down} by step from F♯ to A, then turns back up.
choice: How does the music change from line 1 to line 3?
  - The bass gets faster.
  * The upper line gets busier, from one note to two notes for each bass note.
  - The chords change to minor.
  - The key changes to G major.

## Stretch: Listen
write: Listen to the opening of the canon. Describe how Pachelbel keeps the music interesting when the bass never changes.
  lines: 3
  key: The upper lines change while the bass repeats: the violins enter one at a time in canon, the rhythms get faster (from long notes to quicker ones), and the lines use different chord tones and passing notes over the same chords.

# 20. Shared Breath and Phrase Map
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; instruments or voices
ican: I can mark shared breaths and phrase shapes so all parts of an ensemble move together.
directions: Study the 3-part excerpt (the opening of Beethoven's Ode to Joy theme in D major). Mark where all parts breathe together, map the shape of each phrase, and plan how each part will shorten its held notes so everyone breathes and starts again together.
mistakes: Students often mark breaths wherever they personally run out of air, which splits the ensemble. A shared breath goes at the end of a phrase, and every part takes the time out of its last held note. Watch for breath marks after measure 2 or 6: those split a phrase in the middle. Some students think only the melody needs to breathe; the harmony and bass hold whole notes right up to the phrase end, so they must plan the cut too.
notes: Ode to Joy is the main theme of the last movement of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony (1824), public domain. Here it is set for three parts in D major, 4/4. Phrase 1 is measures 1 to 4 and ends on E over the A chord (a half cadence: it sounds unfinished); phrase 2 is measures 5 to 8 and ends on D over the D chord (it sounds finished). The highest note, A, falls at the end of measure 1 and start of measure 2 (and again in measures 5 and 6), so each phrase grows toward measure 2 and relaxes to its end. A breath mark is a comma (') written above the staff. Staggered breathing (singers or players breathing at different moments, never at a phrase start) lets a long note continue without a gap.

## Warm up: Breath and phrase words
fill:
  - A {phrase} is a musical sentence; it often ends with a long note.
  - A breath mark looks like a {comma} written above the music.
  - When all parts breathe at the same moment, it is a {shared} breath.

## Main: The excerpt
text: Note letters for each measure. A number in parentheses is how many beats a note lasts; other notes get 1 beat.
table: Part | m1 | m2 | m3 | m4 | m5 | m6 | m7 | m8

  - Bass | D(4) | A(4) | D(4) | A(4) | D(4) | A(4) | D(4) | A(2) D(2)

## Main: Map the phrases
fill:
  - Phrase 1 is measures 1 to {4}. Phrase 2 is measures {5} to 8.
  - The highest melody note is {A}. It comes at the end of measure 1 and the start of measure {2}.
  - Phrase 1 ends on {E}, so it sounds unfinished. Phrase 2 ends on {D}, the home note.
draw: Draw a line over measures 1 to 8 that rises where each phrase grows and falls where it relaxes. Mark the peak of each phrase with a star. | 1.0
note: A good map rises from measure 1 to a peak at the start of measure 2, falls through measures 3 and 4, then rises again to a peak at the start of measure 6 and falls to the end. The second phrase can grow a little more, since it brings the music home.
choice: To breathe together after measure 4, what must each part do?
  - The melody breathes; the harmony and bass keep holding.
  * Every part shortens its last note by about an eighth note, breathes, and enters measure 5 together.
  - Every part takes a full extra beat of rest.
  - Only the bass breathes, because it has the longest notes.
choice: A player marks a breath after measure 2. Why is that a problem?
  * It splits phrase 1 in the middle, right after its peak.
  - Measure 2 is too loud to breathe after.
  - No part has a note in measure 3.
  - Breaths can only go at the very end of a piece.

## Stretch: Staggered breathing
write: The director wants the harmony and bass to sound one smooth line from measure 4 into measure 5 with no gap, while the melody still breathes. How can a section of several singers or players do that?
  lines: 3
  key: Use staggered breathing: section members breathe at different moments, never all at once and never right at the start of measure 5, sneaking back in softly so the sound never stops. The melody still takes its shared breath after measure 4.

# 21. Section Leader Task Cards
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors
ican: I can lead my section by giving clear, kind steps to fix a rehearsal problem.
directions: Answer the warm-up questions. Read each task card and write three fix-it steps for it in the chart. Then cut the cards apart and keep them with your steps for sectionals.
mistakes: Students tend to write "tell them to practice" or "tell the director." A section leader's steps should be things the section can try right now, in order, and phrased kindly. Watch for steps that single out one player in front of everyone.
notes: Use these as sectional starters: give each leader one card and five minutes. Good fix-it steps are specific (measure numbers, tempos), start simple, and end with a check ("play it again; did it line up?"). Card 4 can be about any part your ensemble is missing: a student absent, a part not yet learned, or a part cut from the score.

## Warm up: Leading well
choice: Your section keeps dragging in a fast passage. What is the best first step?
  - Tell everyone to try harder.
  * Play the passage together with a metronome at a slower tempo, then speed it up.
  - Skip the passage until the concert.
choice: One player keeps missing an entrance. What is a kind way to help?
  - Point it out to the whole ensemble.
  * Quietly show them the cue (the part to listen for just before the entrance) and mark it in pencil.
  - Play their part for them.

## Main: The task cards
cards: Cut apart after you plan your steps.
  - Tempo dragging: In measures 33 to 40, the section falls behind the conductor and the rest of the band.
  - Missing part: The only player of the harmony part is absent the week before the concert.
  - Out of tune: The section's long unison note at the end of the piece sounds sour.
  - Too loud: The section covers the melody in the soft middle section.
  - Wrong rhythm: Half the section plays the dotted rhythm in measure 12 as two even notes.
chart: Card number | Step 1 | Step 2 | Step 3
  rows: 5
  key: Answers vary. Model steps. Card 3: sing or clap the rhythm at a slow tempo with a metronome; play measures 33 to 40 slowly, then raise the tempo a few clicks at a time; watch the conductor's beat and play it again with the band. Card 4: find who could cover the most important notes; mark the cue notes in their part; rehearse the passage with the new player and check the balance. Card 5: tune to one reference pitch; play the note softly and listen for the beats (wobbles) between players; adjust and hold it together again. Card 6: mark the dynamic in everyone's part; play softer than feels natural while one person listens from the front; check that the melody is clearly heard. Card 7: count and clap the dotted rhythm slowly; play measure 12 alone, then in context; play it together and listen for a long-short pattern.

## Stretch: Feedback
write: A friend in your section keeps rushing. Write what you would say to them, in your own words, so that it helps and does not embarrass them.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer is private, specific and offers help, for example: "Hey, I noticed we both get ahead in the run at measure 20. Want to try it with the metronome at break?"

# 33. Beethoven's Fifth Tempo Graph
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler; optional: recordings of the opening of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony
ican: I can graph and compare tempo data from different performances of the same piece.
directions: Read the data table for four recordings of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony. Graph opening tempo against the length of the first movement, then use the data to compare the performances.
mistakes: Students sometimes mix up tempo and length. Tempo is beats per minute: a bigger number is faster, and a faster tempo usually makes a shorter movement, so the longest recording has the smallest tempo number. Watch for points plotted from the wrong column, and for totals that skip a movement.
notes: The recordings are labeled A to D and the numbers are sample data, modeled on the range of real recordings (first movements from about 6.5 to 8.5 minutes with the exposition repeat). Beethoven's Fifth Symphony in C minor was first performed in Vienna on 22 December 1808. Beethoven later published metronome marks for it: the first movement, Allegro con brio in 2/4, is marked half note = 108, which many conductors find very fast. Answers: totals A 35.6, B 33.7, C 32.3, D 30.2 minutes; closest to 108 is D (2 beats per minute faster); A to D difference 5.4 minutes. Recording E skipped the exposition repeat, so its first movement is shorter even though its tempo is slow. To extend, play the opening 30 seconds of two recordings and have students tap and count the beat.

## Main: Compare the interpretations
fill:
  - The fastest opening tempo is recording {D}; the slowest is recording {A}.
  - The recording closest to Beethoven's marking of 108 is {D}, which is {2} beats per minute faster.
  - The longest and shortest total times differ by {5.4} minutes.
write: Describe the pattern in your graph. What happens to the length of movement 1 as the tempo goes up?
  lines: 2
  key: As the tempo goes up, movement 1 gets shorter: the points slope down from left to right, from 8.2 minutes at 92 to 6.9 minutes at 110.
write: A conductor chose tempo A, far slower than Beethoven's marking. Give one reason a conductor might choose a slower tempo, and one reason to stay close to the marking.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Slower: a large modern orchestra or a big hall can sound heavy and blurred at high speed, or the conductor wants the music to sound grand and weighty. Close to the marking: it is the composer's own instruction, and the fast tempo gives the movement drive and urgency.

## Stretch: An outlier
write: Recording E has an opening tempo of 96, slower than B, but its first movement lasts only 5.9 minutes. What could explain this?
  lines: 2
  key: The conductor probably skipped the exposition repeat (the repeated first section of the movement), so less music was played, even though the tempo is slow.
