# 1. Gestalt: Seeing the Whole
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, black marker or pen, eraser
ican: I can use the Gestalt principles of closure, proximity, similarity and figure-ground in my own designs.
directions: Match each principle to its meaning. Then create a small design for each of the four principles and label it. Explain how a viewer's brain "completes" each one.
mistakes: Students often confuse proximity and similarity: proximity groups things by how close they are, similarity by how alike they look (shape, color, size). For closure, the gaps must be large enough to notice but small enough for the eye to fill in. Figure-ground designs work best in solid black and white.
notes: Gestalt is a German word meaning "form" or "whole"; the principles come from early 1900s German psychologists. Rubin's vase (described by the Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin around 1915) is the classic figure-ground example: a vase or two faces in profile. Many logos use these principles, which makes a good discussion, but keep student designs original.

## Warm up: Match the principle
match: Match each principle to what it does.
  - closure = the eye fills in missing parts to see a complete shape
  - proximity = things placed close together are seen as a group
  - similarity = things that look alike are seen as a group
  - figure-ground = the eye separates an object from its background, and the two can switch
choice: A row of circles has one gap that splits it into a group of 3 and a group of 5. Which principle makes us see two groups?
  - closure
  * proximity
  - similarity
  - figure-ground
choice: A grid of shapes has every other row filled black and the rest left white. We see stripes of rows. Which principle is this?
  - closure
  - proximity
  * similarity
  - figure-ground

## Main: Four small designs
draw: Closure: a shape drawn with gaps that the eye completes | 1.8
draw: Proximity: the same shape repeated, grouped only by spacing | 1.8
draw: Similarity: groups made by alike shapes, sizes or values | 1.8
draw: Figure-ground: a shape that can switch between object and background | 1.8

## Explain
write: Pick your strongest design. What does the viewer's brain do to "finish" it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name the principle and the action: "In my closure design the eye connects the broken lines into a full circle," or "In my figure-ground design the white space between two black profiles becomes a vase."

## Stretch
write: Why do designers use Gestalt principles in logos and signs?
  lines: 3
  key: The brain groups and completes shapes quickly, so a logo can be simple, use fewer lines and still be recognized at a glance; hidden figure-ground shapes also make a design memorable.

# 5. Compositions Across Time
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Raphael's The School of Athens and Cassatt's The Boating Party side by side
ican: I can compare how two artists from different times use the principles of design to organize a composition.
directions: Read the facts about each painting. Look closely at both paintings, then fill in the chart with evidence for each principle. Finish by explaining what the differences show about each artist's goals.
mistakes: Students often write the same thing in both columns ("it has balance"). Push for how: symmetrical or asymmetrical, what is heavy, where the lines lead. Watch for "the Cassatt has no perspective": it does, from a high viewpoint looking down into the boat, with the horizon pushed to the very top.
notes: Show Raphael's The School of Athens (fresco, 1509-1511, in the Stanza della Segnatura of the Vatican Palace) and Mary Cassatt's The Boating Party (oil, 1893-1894, National Gallery of Art, Washington), both public domain. In The School of Athens, Plato and Aristotle stand in the center under the farthest arch, and the vanishing point of the architecture lies between them. In The Boating Party, a dark-clothed man rows from the right with his back to us, facing a woman holding a child; the yellow boat, the oar and his arm point toward them, the sail fills the upper left and the shoreline sits at the very top. Cassatt saw a large exhibition of Japanese prints in Paris in 1890; the high viewpoint, cropping and flat areas of color show that influence. The chart key gives model answers; accept accurate alternatives.

## Warm up: The facts
text: **Raphael** (1483-1520) painted **The School of Athens** as a wall fresco in the Vatican from 1509 to 1511, at the height of the Italian Renaissance. It shows famous thinkers of ancient Greece gathered in a grand building. **Mary Cassatt** (1844-1926), an American who worked in Paris and showed her work with the Impressionists, painted **The Boating Party** in 1893-1894. It shows a man rowing a woman and child in a small sailboat.
choice: Which painting uses symmetrical balance?
  * The School of Athens
  - The Boating Party
  - both
  - neither
choice: In The Boating Party, where is the horizon line?
  - exactly in the middle
  - near the bottom
  * near the very top
  - there is no water

## Main: Principles chart
table: Principle | The School of Athens | The Boating Party
  - Balance | {Symmetrical: groups of figures and matching architecture on both sides of the center.} | {Asymmetrical: the big dark rower on the right balances the lighter woman, child and sail on the left.}
  - Emphasis | {Plato and Aristotle in the center, framed by the far arch against the bright sky.} | {The woman and child, the lightest faces, where the oar, arm and boat lines point.}
  - Movement | {Floor lines, steps and arches pull the eye straight back to the center figures.} | {Curves of the boat and the diagonal oar lead the eye from the rower to the woman and child.}
  - Space and perspective | {Deep one-point perspective; the vanishing point lies between Plato and Aristotle.} | {A high viewpoint looks down into the boat; the water rises like a flat backdrop.}
  - Unity | {Repeated classical arches and calm, balanced groups throughout.} | {Repeated curves and a few strong colors (dark blue, yellow, blue water).}
  - Contrast | {Light open sky behind the center figures, darker groups around them.} | {Dark navy rower against the bright yellow boat; complementary blue and yellow.}
  height: 0.65
write: Raphael's composition feels steady and orderly. Cassatt's feels close and casual. Explain which principles create each feeling.
  lines: 4
  key: Raphael's symmetry, centered emphasis and one-point perspective that leads straight to Plato and Aristotle make everything feel calm, ordered and important. Cassatt's asymmetrical balance, cropped edges, high viewpoint and diagonals put us right in the boat, so the scene feels like an everyday moment we are part of.

## Stretch
write: What do these choices tell you about each artist's goals and time? Use one detail from each painting.
  lines: 4
  key: Raphael aimed to show the ideal of reason and the greatness of ancient thinkers, with perfect Renaissance order and perspective (the vanishing point between Plato and Aristotle). Cassatt aimed to capture a modern, private moment of ordinary life, borrowing the high viewpoint, cropping and flat color of Japanese prints (the rower cut off at the edge, the shoreline at the top).

# 15. Boxes to Buildings
pairs: vanishing-point-studio
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler, eraser
ican: I can combine and cut boxes in one-point perspective to build a complex building.
directions: Answer the warm up questions and draw the three boxes. Then follow the steps to build a building out of boxes: add boxes onto it and cut boxes out of it. Use a ruler and keep every receding line aimed at your one vanishing point.
mistakes: When students add a box on top or to the side, they often give it its own vanishing point by eye. Every receding edge, on every added or cut box, must go to the same vanishing point. Watch the cuts too: a doorway cut into the front face needs depth, so its inside edges also recede toward the vanishing point.
notes: In one-point perspective, the front faces of boxes are true rectangles (parallel to the picture plane), verticals stay vertical, and only the edges going back recede. A box below the horizon shows its top; above the horizon shows its bottom; across the horizon shows neither. The Vanishing Point Studio tool's Draw mode makes boxes that snap to a vanishing point; it is a good demo of adding boxes.

## Warm up: Boxes and the horizon
choice: A box drawn below the horizon line shows which extra face?
  - its bottom
  * its top
  - its back
  - no extra face
choice: A box drawn above the horizon line shows which extra face?
  * its bottom
  - its top
  - its back
  - no extra face
choice: In one-point perspective, what shape is the front face of a box?
  - a trapezoid
  - a triangle
  - a parallelogram
  * a true rectangle
draw: Draw a horizon line and one vanishing point. Draw one box below it, one above it and one crossing it. | 2

## Main: Build a building
example: **Steps**
  1. **Base box:** draw a large box below or across the horizon. Draw its hidden back edges lightly.
  2. **Add:** stack a smaller box on top (an upper floor or tower). Its front face sits on the top of the base box, and its back edges recede to the same vanishing point.
  3. **Add:** attach a wing to one side. Line up its top edge with the base box or make it lower.
  4. **Cut:** cut a doorway or a deep porch into the front face. Draw the opening, then draw its inside edges receding to the vanishing point.
  5. **Cut:** take a notch out of one corner, or cut a step into the roof line.
  6. **Details:** windows on the front face are rectangles; windows on side faces are drawn with edges toward the vanishing point.
  7. Erase hidden lines, then shade one side darker to show form.
draw: My building: at least 2 added boxes and 2 cuts | 4.4
table: Check | Yes or not yet
  - Every receding edge goes to one vanishing point. |
  - Front faces are true rectangles. |
  - I added at least 2 boxes. |
  - I cut at least 2 spaces (doorway, porch, notch or step). |
  - Cuts show depth on their inside edges. |

## Stretch
write: Architects and game designers often start a building as simple boxes. Why is that a useful way to begin?
  lines: 3
  key: Simple boxes set the size, shape and perspective of the whole building quickly and correctly, so it is easy to change the plan; details like windows and doors can then be added without breaking the perspective.

# 21. Four Post-Impressionists
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; the teacher shows one painting by each artist
ican: I can compare the goals, techniques and influence of four Post-Impressionist painters.
directions: Read the artist cards and look at each painting your teacher shows. Complete the chart in your own words. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often treat "Post-Impressionism" as one style. It is a label for very different artists who came after Impressionism and went in different directions. Watch for Seurat's dots described as "random": he placed them carefully using color theory. Gauguin's travels to Tahiti are part of his story; note that his views of Polynesian people as "primitive" are criticized today.
notes: Show these public-domain paintings: Paul Cézanne, Mont Sainte-Victoire (any version, 1880s to 1906) or The Basket of Apples (about 1893); Paul Gauguin, Vision After the Sermon (1888); Georges Seurat, A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884-1886); Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night (1889). The art critic Roger Fry coined the term Post-Impressionism for a London exhibition in 1910. Accept chart answers in students' own words that match the key.

## Read: Artist cards
text: **Paul Cézanne** (1839-1906) built pictures from blocks and patches of color and simple forms, wanting nature to feel solid and lasting. He sometimes showed objects from more than one viewpoint at once.
text: **Paul Gauguin** (1848-1903) painted flat areas of bold, often unnatural color with dark outlines. He wanted to paint ideas, dreams and symbols, not just what the eye sees. He later lived in Tahiti.
text: **Georges Seurat** (1859-1891) studied the science of color. He painted with tiny dots of pure color that blend in the viewer's eye, a method called pointillism.
text: **Vincent van Gogh** (1853-1890) used thick paint, swirling brushstrokes and intense color to show his feelings about what he saw.

## Main: Compare
table: Artist | Goal | Technique | Influenced
  - Cézanne | {make nature look solid and structured} | {patches of color, simple forms, several viewpoints} | {Cubism (Picasso and Braque)}
  - Gauguin | {paint ideas, dreams and symbols} | {flat bold color, dark outlines} | {the Nabis and Fauvism}
  - Seurat | {use color science to build light} | {tiny dots of pure color (pointillism)} | {Neo-Impressionists like Signac; later dot and optical art}
  - Van Gogh | {express emotion} | {thick paint, swirling strokes, intense color} | {Expressionism and Fauvism}

## Questions
choice: Which painter's colors mix in the viewer's eye rather than on the palette?
  - Cézanne
  - Gauguin
  * Seurat
  - Van Gogh
choice: Which painter is most often called a bridge to Cubism?
  * Cézanne
  - Gauguin
  - Seurat
  - Van Gogh
write: All four came after Impressionism. Name one thing they all moved away from, and give an example.
  lines: 3
  key: They moved away from only capturing a quick impression of light. Cézanne wanted lasting structure, Seurat a planned scientific method, Gauguin symbols and ideas, Van Gogh emotion.

## Stretch
write: Which artist's approach would you borrow for a painting of your school? Explain what you would do and why.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name a specific technique (dots, flat color with outlines, blocks of color, swirling strokes) and link it to an effect they want.

# 25. Harlem Renaissance in Context
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter; the teacher shows one Aaron Douglas illustration and one James Van Der Zee photograph from the 1920s
ican: I can connect artworks from the Harlem Renaissance to the Great Migration using evidence from images and a primary source.
directions: Read the background and the source. Study the illustration and the photograph your teacher shows. Fill in the chart, then use the source and both images to explain how the Great Migration shaped the art of Harlem.
mistakes: Students may treat the two artists as making the same kind of art. Douglas made flat, geometric illustrations that turned history and hope into symbols; Van Der Zee made photographs of real Harlem people, often posed in his studio. Both are evidence, of different kinds. Watch also for the idea that the Migration was only about jobs: Locke argues it was also a search for freedom, opportunity and a new identity.
notes: Use an Aaron Douglas illustration from The New Negro (1925) or God's Trombones (1927) (both books are in the public domain in the United States; scans are at HathiTrust and the Internet Archive) and a 1920s Van Der Zee studio portrait or street photo from a museum website (the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Smithsonian hold his work). The source sentences are quoted exactly from Alain Locke's essay "The New Negro" in the 1925 anthology, which uses the language of its time; discuss the word "Negro" as the respectful term used in 1925. About 6 million African Americans left the South between about 1910 and 1970; the first wave, before 1940, built Harlem's Black community. This builds on the grade 5 sheet on Douglas's shapes.

## Background
text: Between about 1910 and 1970, some 6 million African Americans moved from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest and West. This **Great Migration** was a search for work, safety from racist violence and Jim Crow laws, and a better life. Harlem, in New York City, became a center of Black life and culture. In the 1920s, writers, musicians and artists there created the **Harlem Renaissance**. **Aaron Douglas** (1899-1979) moved from Kansas to Harlem in 1925 and made bold illustrations for books and magazines. **James Van Der Zee** (1886-1983) ran a portrait studio in Harlem and photographed its families, churches, parades and businesses for decades.

## Source
source: Alain Locke, "The New Negro," in The New Negro: An Interpretation, edited by Alain Locke (New York: Albert and Charles Boni, 1925)
  [1] In the very process of being transplanted, the Negro is becoming transformed.
  [2] The wash and rush of this human tide on the beach line of the northern city centers is to be explained primarily in terms of a new vision of opportunity, of social and economic freedom, of a spirit to seize, even in the face of an extortionate and heavy toll, a chance for the improvement of conditions.
  [3] In Harlem, Negro life is seizing upon its first chances for group expression and self-determination.
choice: In sentence [2], what does Locke compare the Migration to?
  - a train
  * an ocean tide washing onto a beach
  - a growing tree
  - a city street
choice: According to Locke, what mainly explains the Migration?
  - only the need for factory jobs
  - a government order to move
  * a new vision of opportunity and freedom
  - a wish to leave cities for farms

## Look closely
table: Question | Aaron Douglas illustration | James Van Der Zee photograph
  - What kind of artwork is it? | {a printed illustration, flat and geometric} | {a photograph of real people}
  - What do you see? |  |
  - How are people shown? | {as dark silhouettes in profile, often reaching or moving upward} | {as individuals, often well dressed and carefully posed}
  - What feeling or message? |  |
  height: 0.6
note: Typical answers for the open rows: Douglas: silhouetted figures, rays or circles of light, city skylines, musical instruments or African and Egyptian-inspired shapes; message of hope, history and rising up. Van Der Zee: families, couples or groups in fine clothes, studio backdrops, cars, shops or parades; message of pride, success and dignity in the new city.

## Connect
write: Choose one sentence from the source. Explain how one of the two artworks shows the same idea.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: Sentence [1] says people are "transformed" by moving. Van Der Zee's portraits show Harlem residents as stylish, proud, successful city people, a new image of Black life. Or: Douglas's silhouettes rising toward light and city towers show people moving from the past toward a new future.
write: How are the two artworks different kinds of evidence about Harlem in the 1920s?
  lines: 3
  key: The photograph records real people and places at a moment in time, chosen and posed by the photographer; the illustration is a designed symbol of shared history and hope. One shows what Harlem looked like; the other shows what it meant and dreamed.

## Stretch
write: Locke wrote that in Harlem, "Negro life is seizing upon its first chances for group expression." Why might a new community in a new city produce so much new art?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. People from many places came together, sharing ideas and experiences; magazines, publishers and audiences supported Black artists; and the move itself was a story of change and hope that artists wanted to tell in their own voices.

# 35. Symbols Without Words
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can design a mural that represents a community with visual symbols and no text.
directions: Answer the warm up questions. Choose a community (your school, your town or a group you belong to). Plan 3 visual symbols that stand for it, design a mural that uses all three with no words or letters, and test it on a partner.
mistakes: Students often fall back on symbols that need words to work (a sign, a logo with a name). Ban letters and numbers entirely. Also watch for symbols only one person understands, like an inside joke; a mural symbol should be readable by most people who pass, which is what the partner test checks.
notes: Murals often speak without text: a tree for growth and roots, hands for help and teamwork, a river or road for a journey, a bridge for connection, a sun for hope. Symbols differ across cultures; a symbol that fits one community can confuse another, so encourage students to explain why their community would read it the way they mean. Keep the community general (a school, a town, a club); no symbol should identify a student's family or private life.
