# 1. Objective Summary or Not?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell an objective summary sentence from one with opinions, and rewrite a sentence to remove opinions.
directions: Read the news article. Cut out the 8 summary sentences and sort them: objective (only what the article reports) or opinion-filled (adds the writer's feelings or judgments). Then rewrite 3 opinion-filled sentences so they are objective.
mistakes: Students often call a sentence objective because it contains a fact, even when it also has a loaded word ("grumpy residents"). Have them circle any word that shows a feeling or judgment. Quotes in the article are the speaker's opinions; an objective summary can report them ("Medina said...") without agreeing.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class, and the library, town and people are fictional (about 225 words). Loaded words to point out: smartest, sadly, grumpy, best, amazing, should. Objective rewrites keep the facts and drop the judgment. The glue boxes are short for sentence strips: students can glue the cards in overlapping rows, or glue them on the back under the same headings.

## Read
passage: Library Opens a Tool Shelf
  Last spring, the Maple Hill Public Library added something new next to its books: a shelf of tools. Library card holders can now borrow drills, ladders, sewing machines, garden tools and even a carpet cleaner, the same way they borrow a novel. Items can be checked out for one week.
  The idea came from librarian Rosa Medina, who noticed that many visitors asked for help with home projects. "People kept asking where they could get a tool for one job," Medina said. "Buying a ladder you will use once a year does not make sense for a lot of families."
  The library started with 40 tools donated by a local hardware store and a neighborhood group. By fall, the collection had grown to 112 items, and tools had been checked out more than 900 times. The most popular item was the carpet cleaner, which had a waiting list for most of the summer.
  Not everyone was sure about the plan at first. Some residents worried that tools would come back broken. So far, the library reports that only six items have needed repair. Volunteers check each tool when it is returned.
  Medina hopes to add a class next year where people can learn to use the tools safely. "A library is about sharing knowledge," she said. "Tools are just another kind of knowledge."

## Warm up
text: **Word bank:** objective, opinion, summary, central idea, neutral, loaded word, judgment, reports, states, according to
example: **Opinion-filled:** The library made a brilliant choice to lend tools.
  **Objective:** The library began lending tools to card holders.
choice: Which word makes a sentence opinion-filled?
  - borrowed
  - reported
  * amazing
  - returned

## Main: Sort the summary sentences
sort: Glue each summary sentence under the right heading.
  - Objective: The Maple Hill Public Library began lending tools last spring., The tool collection grew from 40 items to 112 items by fall., Tools were checked out more than 900 times and only six needed repair., The librarian plans to offer a tool safety class next year.
  - Opinion-filled: The library had the smartest idea any library has ever had., Sadly some grumpy residents complained that the tools would break., Everyone should borrow the carpet cleaner because it is the best tool on the shelf., Rosa Medina is an amazing librarian who cares more than anyone in town.

## Main: Make it objective
write: Rewrite: "The library had the smartest idea any library has ever had."
  lines: 2
  starter: Objective: ...
  key: The library started lending tools as well as books.
write: Rewrite: "Sadly some grumpy residents complained that the tools would break."
  lines: 2
  starter: Objective: ...
  key: Some residents worried that tools would come back broken.
write: Rewrite: "Rosa Medina is an amazing librarian who cares more than anyone in town."
  lines: 2
  starter: Objective: ...
  key: Librarian Rosa Medina started the tool shelf after many visitors asked for help with home projects.

# 7. Summarizing and Citing Check
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter if you have one
ican: I can write an objective summary of a passage and cite two pieces of evidence to answer a question.
directions: Read the passage. Choose the best summary sentence, then write your own objective summary. Answer a question with two pieces of cited evidence, and fix a summary sentence that shares an opinion.
mistakes: Students often retell every paragraph in order instead of naming the central idea. An objective summary leaves out the writer's own opinion ("amazing", "I think") and small details like the exact food list. For evidence, watch for two quotes that say the same thing; the second piece should add something new.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 330 words). Sea otters are a classic keystone species: they eat sea urchins, which graze kelp. Hunting for fur nearly wiped otters out; a 1911 international treaty on fur seals also protected them, and kelp returned where otters recovered. Otters eat roughly a quarter of their body weight a day.

## Read
passage: The Otter and the Forest | numbered
  Off the Pacific coast of North America, forests grow under the sea. These forests are made of kelp, a giant seaweed that can grow taller than a ten-story building. Kelp forests shelter hundreds of kinds of fish, crabs and snails. Surprisingly, the health of these forests depends on a small, furry animal: the sea otter.
  Sea otters have no layer of blubber to keep them warm in cold water. Instead, they burn energy quickly and must eat about a quarter of their body weight every day. One of their favorite foods is the sea urchin, a spiny animal shaped like a ball. Otters dive to the sea floor, grab urchins and crack them open while floating on their backs.
  Sea urchins, in turn, eat kelp. When there are enough otters, urchins stay hidden in cracks and eat bits of kelp that drift by. When otters disappear, urchins leave their hiding places and chew through the bottoms of kelp plants. A whole forest can turn into a bare sea floor covered with urchins. Scientists call this an urchin barren.
  This is exactly what happened in the 1700s and 1800s. Hunters killed huge numbers of sea otters for their thick fur, and in many places otters disappeared completely. Urchin numbers exploded, and kelp forests vanished along long stretches of coast. In 1911, several countries signed a treaty that protected sea otters. Slowly, otters returned to some of their old homes, and where they did, the kelp began to grow back.
  Because one animal has such a large effect on its whole community, scientists call the sea otter a keystone species. A keystone is the stone at the top of an arch that holds the other stones in place. Without it, the arch falls. The story of the otter shows that protecting one species can protect an entire underwater forest.

## Warm up
text: **Word bank:** central idea, objective, summary, evidence, cite, paragraph, keystone species, kelp, sea urchin, urchin barren, as a result, according to
choice: Which sentence is the best start to an objective summary?
  - Sea otters are the cutest animals in the ocean, and everyone should love them.
  - Sea otters crack open urchins while floating on their backs.
  * The passage explains how sea otters keep kelp forests healthy by eating sea urchins.
  - I think the hunters in the 1800s should have known better.

## Main: Write the summary
write: Write an objective summary of the passage in 3 or 4 sentences.
  lines: 6
  starter: In "The Otter and the Forest," the author explains that ___. ___. As a result, ___.
  key: In "The Otter and the Forest," the author explains that sea otters protect kelp forests by eating sea urchins, which eat kelp. When hunters killed most otters in the 1700s and 1800s, urchins multiplied and destroyed large kelp forests. After a 1911 treaty protected otters, they returned to some areas and the kelp grew back. As a result, scientists call the sea otter a keystone species.

## Main: Cite two pieces of evidence
write: What happens to a kelp forest when sea otters disappear? Cite two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 6
  starter: One piece of evidence is that "___" (paragraph ___). Another piece of evidence is that "___" (paragraph ___). Together, these show ___.
  key: One piece of evidence is that "when otters disappear, urchins leave their hiding places and chew through the bottoms of kelp plants" (paragraph 3). Another piece of evidence is that after otters were hunted, "urchin numbers exploded, and kelp forests vanished along long stretches of coast" (paragraph 4). Together, these show that without otters, urchins can destroy a kelp forest.

## Stretch: Make it objective
text: **A classmate's summary sentence:** "The terrible hunters almost destroyed the amazing kelp forests, which was so sad."
write: Rewrite the sentence so it is objective.
  lines: 2
  key: Hunting in the 1700s and 1800s removed most sea otters, which led to the loss of many kelp forests.

# 20. Lab Report Peer Review
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can review a lab report for a clear claim, data and reasoning and give helpful suggestions.
directions: Read Riley's lab report. Use the checklist to mark each part Yes or No and say where you looked. Then write two suggestions that would make the report stronger.
mistakes: Students often mark "Yes" because a section exists (there is a conclusion), not because it does the job (the conclusion answers the question). Watch for suggestions that are vague ("add more"). A good suggestion names the part and what to add or change.
notes: Riley is a made-up student. The data follow the real pattern: sugar dissolves faster in warmer water because faster-moving water particles collide with the sugar more often and with more energy. The report's weak spots: a vague claim, no numbers cited, reasoning that repeats the claim, and only one trial per temperature. Its strong spots: a testable hypothesis, units in the table and controlled variables.

## Read the report
example: **Riley's Lab Report: Sugar and Water Temperature**
  **Question:** Does the temperature of water change how fast a sugar cube dissolves?
  **Hypothesis:** If the water is warmer, then the sugar cube will dissolve faster.
  **Procedure:** I filled three cups with 200 mL of water each: cold, room temperature and warm. I dropped one sugar cube into each cup at the same time without stirring and timed how long until no sugar was left. I did this one time.
table: Water temperature (°C) | Time to dissolve (seconds)
  - 10 | 290
  - 22 | 180
  - 45 | 75
example: **Conclusion:** Temperature matters a lot. The warm water was the best. This is because warm water is hot.

## Main: Checklist
text: **Word bank:** claim, evidence, data, reasoning, trial, variable, controlled, units, suggestion, I suggest, consider adding
table: Checklist | Yes or No? | Where I looked or what I noticed
  - The hypothesis can be tested. | {Yes} | {Hypothesis: "If warmer, then faster" can be measured.}
  - The claim clearly answers the question. | {No} | {"Temperature matters a lot" does not say warmer is faster.}
  - The data table has units. | {Yes} | {°C and seconds}
  - The conclusion uses numbers from the data. | {No} | {No times are named in the conclusion.}
  - The reasoning explains why, with science. | {No} | {"Warm water is hot" repeats the idea; no particles.}
  - Other variables were kept the same. | {Yes} | {Same water amount, one cube each, no stirring}
  - There was more than one trial. | {No} | {"I did this one time."}

## Main: Two suggestions
write: Suggestion 1
  lines: 3
  starter: I suggest that you ___ because ___.
  key: I suggest that you rewrite your claim to say that sugar dissolves faster in warmer water and add numbers, like 75 seconds at 45 °C and 290 seconds at 10 °C, because the data are your evidence.
write: Suggestion 2
  lines: 3
  starter: Consider ___ so that ___.
  key: Consider doing three trials at each temperature and averaging them so that one odd result does not change your conclusion. (Also good: explain that warmer water particles move faster and hit the sugar more often.)

## Stretch: Fix the conclusion
write: Rewrite Riley's conclusion as a claim, evidence and reasoning.
  lines: 5
  starter: Claim: ___. Evidence: ___. Reasoning: ___.
  key: Claim: Sugar dissolves faster in warmer water. Evidence: The cube in 45 °C water dissolved in 75 seconds, but the cube in 10 °C water took 290 seconds. Reasoning: Particles in warm water move faster, so they hit the sugar more often and with more energy and pull it apart sooner.

# 21. Lab Reports and Claims Check
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can name variables, hedge a claim and describe what a graph shows.
directions: Read about the experiment and its data. Answer the 6 items. Plot the data on the grid before you describe the graph.
mistakes: Students reverse the variables: the independent variable is the one the scientist changes on purpose (ramp height), the dependent variable is what is measured (distance). Watch for hedged claims that go too far the other way ("Ramps might do anything"). A good hedge still states the pattern.
notes: The data are sample data for this sheet; real classes get similar results with a toy car, a ramp of books and a meter stick. Hedging words: suggest, tend to, may, likely, in this test, for heights up to. Students plot the points; the key line connects them in order.

## The experiment
text: Mateo's group rolled the same toy car down a ramp from 5 different heights. They measured how far the car rolled across the floor after it left the ramp. They used the same car, ramp and floor for every test.
table: Ramp height (cm) | Distance rolled (cm)
  - 10 | 60
  - 20 | 115
  - 30 | 170
  - 40 | 230
  - 50 | 285
text: **Word bank:** independent variable, dependent variable, controlled variable, increase, decrease, trend, steady, suggest, tend to, may, likely, as ___ increases

## Warm up: Variables
fill:
  - The independent variable (what the group changed) is the {ramp height}.
  - The dependent variable (what the group measured) is the {distance rolled}.
choice: Which is a controlled variable in this experiment?
  - The ramp height
  - The distance the car rolled
  * The toy car they used
  - The trend in the data

## Main: Hedge the claim
choice: Which claim is hedged, so it fits the evidence?
  - Higher ramps always make every car go farther.
  * The data suggest that, for heights up to 50 cm, a higher ramp tends to make this car roll farther.
  - Ramp height has nothing to do with distance.
  - Mateo proved that ramps control all motion.
write: Rewrite this claim with hedging language: "A 100 cm ramp will make the car roll 570 cm."
  lines: 2
  starter: Based on the trend, a 100 cm ramp may ...
  key: Based on the trend, a 100 cm ramp may make the car roll about 570 cm, but we did not test heights above 50 cm, so the real distance could be different.

## Main: Graph it and describe it
graph: Ramp height (cm) | 0 50 10 | Distance rolled (cm) | 0 300 50
  key: 10,60 20,115 30,170 40,230 50,285
write: Describe what the graph shows. Use numbers.
  lines: 3
  starter: As the ramp height increased from ___ to ___, the distance ___. The trend is ___.
  key: As the ramp height increased from 10 cm to 50 cm, the distance increased from 60 cm to 285 cm. The trend is steady and close to a straight line: each 10 cm of height added about 55 cm of distance.

## Stretch: Next test
write: What should Mateo's group do to make their claim stronger?
  lines: 2
  starter: They should ___ because ___.
  key: They should run several trials at each height and average them because one roll can be off; they could also test more heights.

# 33. Slang or Standard?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can decide when casual words fit and rewrite them for formal writing.
directions: Each sentence uses a casual phrase and is going somewhere: a text to a friend, a class essay or a job form. Decide if it is OK as is or needs a rewrite. If it needs one, write a formal version.
mistakes: Students sometimes rewrite every sentence, even the text to a friend. Casual language is right for friends; the goal is matching the audience. In formal rewrites, watch for vague replacements ("a lot of stuff" becomes "many things"); push for exact words.
notes: The phrases are common, non-offensive slang. Accept any formal rewrite that keeps the meaning, spells words out and is exact. Items 1 and 4 are texts to friends and stay as they are. Discuss why "no cap" (no lie) would confuse an employer even though a friend understands it.

## Warm up: Where does it fit?
text: **Word bank:** casual, formal, slang, standard English, audience, register, appropriate, rewrite, precise

  * somewhat afraid
  - lowkey nervous
  - freaking out
choice: Where does "gonna" fit best?
  - A job form
  - A class essay
  * A text to a friend
  - A letter to the principal
