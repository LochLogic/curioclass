# 1. From Ritual to Drama: The Festival of Dionysus
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how Greek drama grew out of religious festival, and what the chorus, masks and competition did.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions, citing paragraph numbers as evidence.
mistakes: Students often picture Greek theater as indoor and small, or think the chorus only sang between scenes. The chorus was onstage throughout, in the orchestra, reacting and commenting. Watch for "masks hid the actors because they were shy": the reasons are practical and artistic (few actors, many roles, men playing women, visibility at a distance).
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 520 words). The Thespis story comes from later ancient writers, so it is presented as a tradition, not a proven fact. Sources for teachers: Aristotle's Poetics (on tragedy's origins in the dithyramb and Aeschylus and Sophocles adding actors) and standard histories of Greek theater. The three tragedians' plays each filled one day of the festival.

## Read
passage: From Ritual to Drama: The City Dionysia
  Every spring, when the sailing season opened and visitors could reach Athens by sea, the city held a festival for Dionysus, the god of wine, growth and wild celebration. Athenians called it the City Dionysia, or the Great Dionysia. For several days, ordinary business slowed, and a city of citizens became an audience.
  The festival began as worship. A statue of Dionysus was carried in a torchlit procession and set up near his theater, so the god himself could watch. There were sacrifices and feasting. Long before there were plays, there were choruses: groups of men who sang and danced hymns to Dionysus called dithyrambs. Each tribe of Athens entered a chorus in a dithyramb contest, and the songs told stories of gods and heroes.
  Ancient writers tell a story about how drama grew out of this singing. A performer named Thespis, they said, stepped out of the chorus and spoke as a character, answering the chorus instead of singing with it. Whether or not one man really did this first, the idea captures what happened: a song about a hero became a scene in which someone pretended to be that hero. Today actors are still called thespians.
  By the 400s BCE, the Dionysia was the greatest theater event in the Greek world. Plays were performed in the Theater of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis, an open-air hillside of seats looking down on a round dancing floor called the orchestra. Behind it stood the skene, a building the actors used for changing and as a background. The audience numbered in the thousands.
  The plays were a competition. Months before, a city official chose three tragic playwrights to compete. Each one presented four plays on a single day: three tragedies and a satyr play, a rowdy comedy with a chorus of half-human, half-goat satyrs. Comic playwrights competed too. A wealthy citizen, the choregos, paid to train and costume each chorus, which was both a civic duty and a chance to win honor. Judges chosen by lot voted, and the winners were celebrated and their names recorded.
  The chorus remained the heart of the show. In tragedy it was a group of twelve to fifteen men who sang, danced and spoke together in the orchestra, commenting on the action, asking questions and reacting as ordinary citizens might. The number of individual actors stayed tiny. Aeschylus added a second actor, and Sophocles a third, so that three actors played every speaking role in a tragedy by changing masks.
  Masks made this possible. Made of linen, wood or cork, they covered the whole head, showed a character's age, gender and type, and could be seen from far up the hillside. All performers were men, so masks also let them play women, gods, old kings and young messengers. Masks shifted attention from an actor's own face to the character, the words and the movement of the body.
  Religion, citizenship and art met in that theater. When Athenians watched a tragedy about a proud king or a grieving queen, they were honoring a god, judging a contest and thinking together about questions of power, justice and suffering.

## Stretch
write: The last paragraph says religion, citizenship and art met in the theater. Choose one modern event that also mixes community, ritual and performance. How is it like the Dionysia, and how is it different?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: a parade, a graduation, a holiday concert or a sports opening ceremony. A strong answer names a shared feature (a whole community gathers, ritual opening, competition or honoring) and a real difference (no god being honored, performers are not competing, the audience is not judging).

directions: The graph is a side view of the Globe (solid: walls and roof; dashes: stage, pillar and heavens; dots: gallery floors), cut through the middle from the back wall of the stage to the far side of the yard. mistakes: Students often picture the Globe as a modern indoor theater with the whole audience seated facing a picture-frame stage. Standing in the yard cost a penny, and a gallery seat cost more. In Hamlet, the Ghost calls out from under the stage, which the cellar below the trap made possible.

## How the parts served the plays
choice: The stage thrust out into the yard, with audience on three sides. What did this make possible?
  - Changing big painted scenery between scenes
  * Actors speaking soliloquies close to the audience, almost like talking to them
  - Hiding the actors from the audience
  - Performing only at night
write: How did the trapdoor and the space under the stage help a play like Hamlet?
  lines: 2
  key: Actors could appear or disappear through the floor, for ghosts, spirits or graves; in Hamlet the Ghost speaks from under the stage, which made him seem to come from below the earth.
write: The yard and the galleries held different audiences. Who was in each, and how might an actor play to both?
  lines: 3
  key: The cheapest tickets (a penny) let people stand in the yard right around the stage; people who paid more sat in the three levels of galleries. An actor could play close and direct to the groundlings, then lift voice and gestures up to the galleries, using the whole round space.
write: The heavens roofed part of the stage. Give one practical job and one storytelling job it had.
  lines: 2
  key: Practical: it kept rain off the actors and costumes and could hide machinery or a space above. Storytelling: painted with sky and stars, it stood for the heavens over the action, while the cellar below stood for the underworld.

## Stretch
write: Plays at the Globe were lit by daylight. How would daylight change the way actors and audience related, compared with a dark modern theater?
  lines: 3
  key: The actors could see every audience member, and the audience could see each other, so performances were shared and lively: actors spoke to the crowd, and the crowd reacted aloud. Night and darkness had to be shown through words and props such as torches, not lighting.

# 20. Reading a Light Plot
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can read a light plot and its legend to find instrument types, positions, colors and channels.
directions: The plot below is written as a table, laid out like the drawing: the upstage position is at the top and the audience is at the bottom. Each cell is one instrument. Read the legend, then answer the 8 questions.
mistakes: Students often confuse unit numbers with channel numbers. The unit number says which instrument on a position; the channel is the control number the board operator uses, and several units can share one channel. Watch for stage right and stage left reversed: on a plot the audience is at the bottom, so stage right is on the left of the page.
notes: Gel numbers are from Rosco's color list: R02 Bastard Amber (a warm pale amber), R27 Medium Red, R33 No Color Pink, R60 No Color Blue (a cool pale blue), R80 Primary Blue. N/C means no color. On a real plot each instrument is drawn as a symbol with its unit number, channel (often in a circle) and color written next to it; this sheet puts the same information in table cells. Count for question 1: 4 units on each of 3 positions.

# 21. Where Is the Light Coming From?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose a motivated light source for a scene and describe its angle, color and intensity.
directions: A motivated light source is the light that seems to come from something in the world of the play: a window, a lamp, a fire, the moon. For each scene, name the source and describe its angle, color and intensity. Use the example.
mistakes: Students often name a stage instrument ("a spotlight") instead of the source in the story. Watch for angle answers like "from above" for every scene: low sun, a candle on a table and a streetlight all come from very different angles. Color is often left as "white"; push for warm or cool and a reason.
notes: Angle words to accept: high, low, steep, from the side (side light), from behind (back light), from below. Intensity: low, medium, high, or a percentage. The key gives short model answers; accept any answer that is believable for the scene. Fuller versions: the campfire and the TV flicker; the desk lamp and the streetlight each make a small pool of light; the moon comes from the side or behind, fairly high; the setting sun fades as the scene goes on; the streetlight may read cool or sodium orange. Stage lighting does not have to copy the source exactly: it suggests it, so the actors' faces can still be seen.

## Example
example: A classroom at 8 a.m. on a sunny day: **Source:** windows on the stage left wall. **Angle:** from the side, fairly low. **Color:** warm pale yellow. **Intensity:** high.

## Stretch
write: Choose scene 1 or scene 4. The audience still needs to see the actors' faces clearly. What extra light would you add that does not ruin the effect? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: for the campfire, a low-level warm front light from both sides (about 30 to 40 percent) in the same orange color, so faces read but the fire still seems to be the source; for sunset, a soft warm fill from the opposite side so the far side of each face is not black.

# 33. Vocal Markup
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, your monologue
ican: I can mark a monologue for operative words, pauses, rate changes and breath points and use the marks in performance.
directions: Learn the symbol key. Mark the warm-up lines, then copy 8 lines of your monologue with space between them and mark them. Read them aloud using your marks.
mistakes: Students often underline almost every word, which means none of them stand out. Aim for one or two operative words per line: the words that carry new information or contrast. Watch for breath marks placed mid-phrase; breathe at punctuation and thought changes so the sense of the line is not broken.
notes: Operative words are the words that carry the thought: new information, contrasts and key images. In Juliet's line, "wherefore" means "why", not "where": she asks why Romeo has to be a Montague. The warm-up lines are the opening of Mark Antony's funeral speech in Julius Caesar (Act 3, Scene 2) and are in the public domain. The key's markups are models; accept any marking that the student can justify.

## Symbol key
example: **underline** = operative word (shown here in bold) | **|** = short pause | **||** = long pause | **V** = breath | **>>** = speed up | **<<** = slow down
example: Juliet (Romeo and Juliet, Act 2, Scene 2): O **Romeo**, Romeo, | **wherefore** art thou Romeo? || V << Deny thy **father** | and refuse thy **name**; V

(Operative words: bury and praise are a contrast; evil and good, lives and interred are contrasts.)
