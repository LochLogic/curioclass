# 1. What Makes a Source Reliable?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can judge how reliable a source is by asking who made it, why, with what evidence, when, and whether others agree.
directions: Read the passage and highlight the five questions it teaches. Then answer the questions. When a question asks you to find a sentence, copy it exactly.
mistakes: Students often treat the first or most confident result as the most reliable. Others think one red flag (an old date) makes a source useless; the passage says older sources can still be useful for some topics. Push students to weigh all five questions together, the way Jaden does.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 300 words). The goldfish facts are checked: the "three-second memory" is a popular myth, and experiments have shown goldfish can learn tasks, such as pushing a lever or responding to a sound for food, and remember them for weeks or months. The university page and websites in the story are made up. The five questions match common source-evaluation checklists used in school libraries (authority, purpose, evidence, currency, corroboration).

## Read
passage: Can You Trust It?
  Jaden wanted to know if goldfish really have a three-second memory. In two minutes, he found three websites. One said yes. One said no. One said goldfish can remember things for months. How could he decide which one to trust?
  Librarians suggest asking a few questions about any source. First, who made it? A page written by a scientist who studies fish has more authority on this question than a page with no author at all. If you cannot tell who made a source, be careful.
  Second, why was it made? Some sources are made to teach. Others are made to sell something, to entertain or to persuade. A pet store page that wants you to buy a bigger tank might stretch the facts to make a sale.
  Third, what is the evidence? A reliable source shows how it knows. It might describe an experiment, link to a study or quote an expert by name. A source that says "everyone knows" is giving you an opinion, not evidence.
  Fourth, when was it made? For some topics, such as the history of ancient Egypt, an older source can still be useful. For topics that change quickly, such as medicine or technology, newer information is usually better.
  Finally, do other good sources agree? Jaden found a university page describing experiments in which goldfish learned to push a lever to get food and still remembered the trick weeks later. Two science museum websites reported similar results. The "three-second" page had no author, no date and no evidence. Jaden had his answer: the three-second memory is a myth.
  No single question can prove that a source is perfect. A famous author can make a mistake, and a new website can be excellent. But asking all five questions together helps you decide how much to trust what you read.

  lines: 2

  - What did people in ancient Egypt eat?
  - When was the Great Wall of China started?
  - What did the first American flag look like?
  * What is the best treatment for a common illness?

  lines: 3

## Stretch
write: The passage says, "A famous author can make a mistake, and a new website can be excellent." Why does the author include this sentence? What should a researcher do because of it?
  lines: 3
  key: To warn that no single clue proves a source good or bad; a researcher should ask all five questions together and check other sources instead of trusting fame or rejecting something just because it is new.

# 21. Question Growth Tree
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can grow new research questions from one starting question and choose which ones to follow.
directions: Write one starting question on the trunk line. Grow six branch questions from it, each asking about a different part of the topic. Then star the two branches you most want to follow, and explain your choice.
mistakes: Students often write branches that are the trunk question reworded ("Why do cats purr?" and "What makes cats purr?"). Each branch should open a new direction: who, when, where, how, what if, or a comparison. Others star the easiest branch; ask which branch they are most curious about and could find sources for.
notes: The example tree is a model for the warm up; the key shows sample branches for it. Good branches can be answered with research (not only opinion) and are smaller than the trunk. A branch worth following is one the student is curious about and can find sources for. This sheet pairs well with a class "rabbit hole" notebook where students keep their trees.

## Warm up: Read a tree
example: Trunk: Why do cats purr?
  Branch (how): How do cats make the purring sound?
  Branch (when): Do kittens purr from the day they are born?
  Branch (compare): Do lions and tigers purr too?
choice: Which question is a good new branch for the trunk "Why do cats purr?"
  - What makes cats purr?
  - Do you like cats?
  - Why do cats purr so much?
  * Can a cat's purr help it heal?
write: Why is "What makes cats purr?" a weak branch?
  lines: 2
  key: It asks the same thing as the trunk in different words, so it does not grow the research in a new direction.

## Main: Grow your tree
write: My trunk question (pick one or write your own: Why do birds migrate? How are tunnels built under rivers? Why do we dream?):
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Example: Why do birds migrate?
chart: # | My branch question (try: who, when, where, how, compare, what if) | Star two
  rows: 6
  key: Sample for "Why do birds migrate?": 1 Who studies bird migration, and how? 2 When in the year do birds leave and return? 3 Where do hummingbirds spend the winter? 4 How do birds find their way? (starred) 5 How is bird migration like whale migration? 6 What if winters get warmer: will some birds stop migrating? (starred)
draw: Draw your tree: the trunk question at the bottom, six branches with a short label each, and stars on the two you will follow. | 3

## Stretch: Choose a path
write: Which starred branch will you follow first? Explain why it is worth following and where you could look for answers.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample for "Why do birds migrate?": I will follow "How do birds find their way?" because it surprises me that they travel thousands of miles, and I can look in bird books in the 598s and on a bird research website.
