# 1. Secondary Dominants
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, staff paper or a keyboard (optional)
ican: I can spell secondary dominants, find their raised tone, and resolve them to their target chords.
directions: Study the worked example. Then spell V/V and V/vi in four major keys, name the raised (chromatic) tone in each, and spell the chord it resolves to.
mistakes: Students often spell V/V from the key's own notes (in C, D F A instead of D F# A). A secondary dominant is always a major chord (or a dominant seventh), so it usually needs an accidental the key does not have. Watch for F major's V/V: its raised tone is B natural, raised from the key's Bb, so it has no sharp at all.
notes: To find V/x: take the root of the target chord x, go up a perfect 5th, and build a major triad there. The raised tone is the third of the secondary dominant: it is the leading tone of the target and resolves up by half step to the target's root. Answers: C major V/V = D F# A (F#) to G B D; V/vi = E G# B (G#) to A C E. G major V/V = A C# E (C#) to D F# A; V/vi = B D# F# (D#) to E G B. F major V/V = G B D (B natural) to C E G; V/vi = A C# E (C#) to D F A. D major V/V = E G# B (G#) to A C# E; V/vi = F# A# C# (A#) to B D F#.

## Warm up: How it works
example: In C major, ii is D minor (D F A). V/ii: go up a perfect 5th from D to A, build a major triad: A C# E. The raised tone is C#, which leads up a half step to D.
fill:
  - A secondary dominant is the {V} chord of a chord other than I.
  - Its root is a perfect {5th} above the root of its target chord.
  - Its third is the {leading} tone of the target and resolves up by a {half} step.

  * V/vi raises G to G#, making a major chord that pulls to A minor.
  - V/vi has a different root.

## Stretch: Add the seventh
write: Spell V7/V in C major. Explain how its raised tone and its seventh resolve when it moves to V.
  lines: 4
  key: V7/V = D F# A C. When it moves to V (G B D), the raised tone F# rises a half step to G, and the seventh, C, falls a half step to B.

# 7. Chromatic Chord Listening Lab
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; a piano or keyboard for the teacher
ican: I can hear where chromatic chords occur, check my ears against the score, and name each chromatic chord.
directions: Fold the page so Part B is hidden. Part A: listen to each excerpt twice and write the measures where you hear a chromatic chord (a chord with a note outside the key), or "none." Part B: unfold, check the score, and label each chromatic chord. Part C: tally your accuracy.
mistakes: In minor keys, students mark V (E or E7 in A minor, with G#) as chromatic; the raised leading tone is normal in minor, so count V as diatonic. Watch for students who mark every loud or surprising moment: ask them to sing the bass line and hear which chord broke the key. The augmented sixth often sounds like a dominant seventh until it resolves outward to the octave.
notes: Play each progression twice at a moderate tempo, one chord per measure, block chords in a comfortable middle register, and say the measure numbers aloud ("1, 2, 3...") the first time only. Chromatic notes to listen for: C# (A7), Ab (F minor), Bb (Neapolitan in A minor), D# (German sixth), G# (E7 in C) and F# (D7). There are 6 chromatic chords in all: excerpt 6 holds two, excerpt 3 holds none (a deliberate check on false alarms). Voicings: Bb/D means a Bb major triad with D in the bass; the German sixth is F A C D# resolving to E major.

write: In excerpt 6, the chromatic notes are G# and F#. Explain where each one wants to go and why.
  lines: 3

# 20. Era Comparison Essay Planner
minutes: 40
materials: pencil; scores or recordings of the two works you choose
ican: I can plan a compare-and-contrast essay with a clear thesis, three points and evidence from scores or recordings.
directions: Choose one Romantic work and one twentieth-century work you have studied (or use Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique and Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring). Write a thesis, plan three points of comparison, and attach specific evidence from the score or recording to each point.
mistakes: Students list facts about each work in two separate halves and never compare. Every point needs both works side by side and a "so what." Watch for evidence that is just a label ("it is dissonant"): ask for the measure, the instrument, the moment or the timing in the recording.
notes: Model facts (Berlioz and Stravinsky): Symphonie fantastique (1830) follows a written program about an artist obsessed with a beloved, represented by a recurring melody, the idée fixe, which is transformed in each of the five movements; in the finale it becomes a shrill dance tune on the E-flat clarinet. Its harmony is tonal and its meters are regular. The Rite of Spring (1913) builds on short, repeated folk-like fragments (the opening bassoon melody comes from a Lithuanian folk song collection), a stamping, accented dissonant chord in "Augurs of Spring" (often described as E-flat seventh on top of F-flat major), and meters that change almost every measure in the "Sacrificial Dance." Both use a very large orchestra and tell a story: one through a program, the other through a ballet scenario. Use measure numbers from the class scores or timings from the class recordings.

## Warm up: What makes a thesis
choice: Which is the strongest thesis for a compare-and-contrast essay?
  - Berlioz and Stravinsky were both important composers.
  - The Rite of Spring is a ballet and the Symphonie fantastique is a symphony.
  * Both works tell a story with a huge orchestra, but Berlioz unifies his story with a transformed melody while Stravinsky drives his with rhythm and clashing harmony.
choice: Which piece of evidence is most specific?
  - The Rite of Spring is very rhythmic.
  * In the "Sacrificial Dance," the meter changes almost every measure.
  - Stravinsky liked unusual sounds.

## Stretch
write: Write the opening two sentences of your essay: a hook and your thesis.
  lines: 4
  key: Model: In 1830 a symphony told a story through one obsessive melody; in 1913 a ballet told a story through pounding rhythm and a chord that clashed with itself. # 21. History Checkpoint 11
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can identify works, styles and traits from Romantic music through early jazz and explain why styles changed.
directions: Place seven works and events on the timeline, match styles to their traits, identify the style of each description, and explain one cause of a style change.
mistakes: Students blur Impressionism and Romanticism because both can be lush; point to Debussy's floating chords and unresolved harmony against Romantic goal-directed climaxes. Watch for ragtime called jazz: ragtime is composed and written out, early jazz is built on improvisation.
notes: Dates: Berlioz, Symphonie fantastique, premiered 1830; Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, premiered 1865; Dvořák, Symphony No. 9 "From the New World," premiered in New York 1893; Debussy, Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun, premiered 1894; Scott Joplin, Maple Leaf Rag, published 1899; Stravinsky, The Rite of Spring, premiered 1913; the first jazz recordings were made in 1917; Louis Armstrong's Hot Five began recording in 1925. The "New World" Symphony appears in the questions, not on the timeline, because it sits a year from Debussy's Faun and the letters would overlap. Accept timeline letters within about ten years. Causes of change to accept with evidence: new instruments and larger orchestras, nationalism, recording technology spreading styles quickly, the Great Migration bringing Black musicians and New Orleans jazz north, reaction against earlier styles.

## Stretch: Why did styles change?
write: Explain one cause of a style change between 1820 and 1930. Name the cause, the change it led to, and at least one work or date from this sheet as evidence.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. For example: "Recording technology changed how music spread. Before records, a style traveled through sheet music and live performance; after the first jazz recordings in 1917, listeners across the country could hear New Orleans-style improvisation, and by 1925 Louis Armstrong's Hot Five records made the improvised solo the center of jazz." Other strong causes: nationalism (Dvořák's "New World" Symphony drawing on folk and spiritual idioms), reaction against Romantic excess (Debussy, Stravinsky), or larger orchestras and new instruments making program music like the Symphonie fantastique possible.

# 33. Practice Strategy Experiment
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, metronome, a timer, your recital music
ican: I can test practice strategies like a scientist and use data to choose the strategy that works best for me.
directions: Pick 3 short passages (2 to 4 measures each) that are hard for you, all at a similar level. Test one strategy on each: chunking, rhythm variation and slow practice. Before and after 8 minutes of the strategy, play the passage 5 times at the same target tempo and count the clean tries. Then compare your results.
mistakes: Students change the target tempo between the before and after tests, which ruins the comparison; the test tempo stays fixed. Watch for passages of very different difficulty, which make the strategies hard to compare. Some count "almost clean" tries: decide before you start what counts as a mistake (any wrong note, rhythm or a stop).
notes: Strategies: chunking means practicing a small unit (a beat or a measure) until it is clean, then linking units, often with the last note of one chunk as the first of the next. Rhythm variation means practicing an even passage in dotted, reversed-dotted or grouped rhythms, then returning to the even version. Slow practice means playing well below tempo with full attention and raising the metronome step by step. Results vary by player and passage; the point is the method. Warm up: 2 of 5 to 4 of 5 is 40% to 80%, a change of 40 percentage points; 3 of 5 to 4 of 5 is 60% to 80%, a change of 20 points.
