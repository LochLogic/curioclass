# 1. Inside a Vape
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how a vape works and what is in the aerosol it makes.
directions: Match each vape part to its job. Draw and label a simple vape diagram. Then complete the aerosol chart with one health effect for each ingredient.
mistakes: Many students believe vapes make "just water vapor." They make an aerosol: tiny particles and chemicals that are breathed deep into the lungs. Students may also think nicotine-free labels can be trusted; CDC reports that some products labeled nicotine-free have been found to contain nicotine.
notes: Facts follow the CDC and the U.S. Surgeon General's report on e-cigarette use among youth. Show a plain parts diagram of an e-cigarette on the board (or sketch one: a long body with the battery at the bottom, the coil in the middle and the pod and mouthpiece on top) for students to copy. Use no brand names and no product photos. Keep the focus on facts and on staying vape-free; if a student says they or someone they know vapes, follow up privately and share school supports.

## Warm up: How it works
text: A vape (also called an e-cigarette) uses a **battery** to heat a metal **coil**. The coil heats a liquid from the **pod** or tank and turns it into an **aerosol**: a cloud of tiny particles that the user breathes in through the **mouthpiece**.

## Main: Parts and jobs
match: Match each part to its job.
  - Battery = Powers the device and sends electricity to the coil
  - Heating coil = Gets hot and turns the liquid into aerosol
  - Pod or tank = Holds the e-liquid
  - Mouthpiece = Where the aerosol is breathed in
draw: Draw a simple vape from the board. Label the battery, heating coil, pod and mouthpiece. | 2.2

## Main: What is in the aerosol?
table: Ingredient | What it is | One health effect
  - Nicotine | The addictive drug found in tobacco | {Highly addictive; harms the developing teen brain (attention, learning, mood and impulse control)}
  - Flavorings | Chemicals that make it taste like candy or fruit | {Some, like diacetyl, are linked to serious lung disease}
  - Metals | Tiny bits of nickel, tin and lead from the coil | {Breathed deep into the lungs, where they can do harm}
  - Solvents | Liquids (propylene glycol, glycerin) that carry the nicotine and flavor | {When heated they can form harmful chemicals that irritate the lungs and airways}
choice: Which statement about vape aerosol is true?
  - It is just harmless water vapor
  * It contains nicotine, ultrafine particles and harmful chemicals
  - It is safe because it has a fruit flavor

## Stretch
write: A friend says, "Vaping is fine because it's not smoking." Use two facts from this sheet to answer them.
  lines: 4
  key: Vapes usually contain nicotine, which is highly addictive and harms the developing brain. The aerosol also carries metals and chemicals like diacetyl into the lungs. Not burning tobacco does not make it safe.

# 7. What Pushes, What Pulls
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can sort the influences on a choice about substance use and plan how to use the helpful ones.
directions: Read about Nia's choice and the 8 influences on it. Decide if each influence is internal (from inside Nia) or external (from outside her), and positive (pushes her toward a healthy choice) or negative (pulls her toward a risky one). Write each number in the right box of the map.
mistakes: Students often call every feeling negative. Wanting to stay strong for swim team is a feeling, and it is positive. Watch for confusion about stress (number 4): the stress comes from inside Nia, so it is internal, even though tests caused it.
notes: Nia and her friends are characters made up for Curio Class. Facts follow the CDC: most e-cigarettes contain nicotine, which is highly addictive and can harm the parts of the teen brain that control attention, learning and mood; the brain keeps developing until about age 25. Many teens believe vaping relaxes them, but nicotine withdrawal between uses can make stress and anxiety feel worse. Keep the talk on Nia's influences, not on students' own families or friends.

## Warm up: Nia's choice
text: Nia is 12. At a sleepover, her friend Kayla pulls out a vape with a sweet mango flavor and asks, "Want to try it? Everybody does it." Nia has to decide what to do.
fill:
  - An influence that comes from inside a person is {internal}.
  - An influence that comes from people, places or media around a person is {external}.

## Main: Eight influences
text: **1.** Kayla tried vaping last month and says it is "no big deal." **2.** Nia's older brother told her how hard it was for him to quit vaping. **3.** Nia is curious what it feels like. **4.** Nia is stressed about tests and heard vaping helps you relax. **5.** Videos online show teens vaping with bright, fruity flavors. **6.** Nia wants to keep her lungs strong for swim team. **7.** Nia is proud that she makes her own choices instead of following the crowd. **8.** Nia's family has a clear rule against vaping.
table: | Positive (pushes toward a healthy choice) | Negative (pulls toward a risky choice)
  height: 0.9
  - **Internal** (inside Nia) | {6, 7} | {3, 4}
  - **External** (outside Nia) | {2, 8} | {1, 5}

## Think about it
choice: Which influence is internal and negative?
  - Number 8: the family rule
  * Number 3: curiosity
  - Number 6: swim team
choice: Influence 4 says vaping helps you relax. What is true?
  * Nicotine withdrawal between uses can make stress and anxiety feel worse
  - Vaping is a proven way to treat stress
  - Vapes do not contain anything that affects the brain
write: Which positive influence do you think could help Nia the most right now? Explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Number 6. Swim team is something Nia cares about, so remembering that vaping can harm her lungs and breathing gives her a strong reason to say no right there at the sleepover.

## Main: Turn a pull into a push
write: Pick one negative influence. Explain how Nia could weaken it or answer it.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Number 4, stress. Nia could use healthy ways to relax, like a walk, music or deep breathing, and ask a teacher for help studying. Number 1: she could say, "No thanks, it's not for me," and change the subject.

## Stretch
write: Write what Nia could say to Kayla. Use at least one of her positive influences.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: "No thanks. I've got a swim meet and I need my lungs. Want to start the movie?"

# 20. What Should They Do? Choice Cards
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose healthy ways to handle stress, untangle thinking traps and know when to get help.
directions: Read each choice card. Circle the best choice of the three. Then pick two cards and explain why your choice is the best one.
mistakes: Students may pick "keep the secret" on card 7 because a friend asked. Make it clear that a friend's safety comes before a promise: tell a trusted adult right away. On thinking trap cards, watch for answers that swap a negative thought for a fake-positive one ("I'm the best at everything"); the goal is a fair, realistic thought.
notes: Characters are made up for Curio Class. Thinking traps (all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, catastrophizing, labeling) come from cognitive behavioral therapy. Card 4 shows signs that last two weeks or more, a common sign that it is time to talk to an adult or doctor. Card 7: if a student is worried about someone right now, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline takes calls and texts at 988; share your school's counselor and process too.

## Warm up: Thinking traps
match: Match each thinking trap to an example.
  - All-or-nothing = "If it's not perfect, it's a total failure."
  - Mind reading = "She didn't wave. She must hate me."
  - Catastrophizing = "One bad grade means my whole future is ruined."
  - Labeling = "I made one mistake, so I'm a failure."

## Main: Eight choice cards
choice: **Card 1, stress.** Jalen has three tests next week and his heart races when he thinks about them. What should he do?
  * Make a study plan with small parts each day and take short breaks
  - Stay up all night before each test
  - Stop thinking about the tests until the night before
choice: **Card 2, all-or-nothing.** Priya plays one wrong note at the band concert and says, "I ruined the whole concert." What helps most?
  - Quit band so it never happens again
  * Notice the trap and think, "One note was off; most of the concert went well"
  - Tell everyone it was another player's fault
choice: **Card 3, mind reading.** Omar's friend has not texted back all day. Omar thinks, "He's mad at me." What should Omar do?
  - Send ten texts in a row
  - Stop talking to his friend
  * Remember there could be other reasons, and ask him tomorrow
choice: **Card 4, getting help.** Sofia has felt sad and hopeless for over two weeks and has stopped enjoying soccer. What should she do?
  * Tell a trusted adult, like a parent, school counselor or doctor
  - Wait a few more months to see if it goes away
  - Keep busy so nobody notices
choice: **Card 5, stress.** Ana's hands shake right before her class presentation. What can she do in the next minute?
  - Run out of the room
  * Take slow breaths, in for 4 counts and out for 4 counts
  - Drink an energy drink
choice: **Card 6, catastrophizing.** Ben gets a C on a quiz and thinks, "I'll fail the class and never get into college." What helps most?
  - Hide the quiz from everyone
  - Decide that school does not matter
  * Replace the thought with a realistic one and ask the teacher how to improve
choice: **Card 7, helping a friend.** Kai's friend says she sometimes thinks about hurting herself and asks him to keep it a secret. What should Kai do?
  - Keep the secret because she trusted him
  - Tell her to cheer up and change the subject
  * Tell a trusted adult right away, even though she asked him not to
choice: **Card 8, labeling.** Mia forgets her homework and says, "I'm such a failure." What should she tell herself instead?
  * "I forgot it this time. I'll set a reminder tonight."
  - "I'll never remember anything."
  - "Homework doesn't matter anyway."

## Explain two
write: Card number ____ : Why is your choice better than the other two?
  lines: 3
  key: Example, card 3: Omar does not know why his friend has not texted. Asking checks the facts instead of guessing. Sending ten texts or giving up on the friendship could hurt it over something that may not be true.
write: Card number ____ : Why is your choice better than the other two?
  lines: 3
  key: Example, card 7: The friend's safety matters more than the secret. A trusted adult can get her the help she needs. Changing the subject or keeping quiet leaves her alone with a serious problem.

## Stretch
write: Write your own choice card about stress or a thinking trap for a made-up character. Give the best choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: "Leo misses the bus and thinks, 'This whole day is ruined.' Best choice: 'One thing went wrong. I'll ask for a ride and start fresh in first period.'"

# 21. Qualities of a Good Friend
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can describe what makes a healthy friendship and support my ideas with examples.
directions: Read the story. Rank the ten friendship qualities from most important (1) to least important (10) for you. Then explain your top three with examples from the story.
mistakes: Students sometimes rank "fun" or "popular" highly and then cannot find story evidence. Every top-three choice needs an example of a character's action. Watch for students who equate "keeps every secret" with loyalty: a good friend gets help from a trusted adult when a secret means someone could get hurt.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 300 words). The ranking is personal, so there is no single right order; grade the evidence. Story examples: honest (Jonah tells Ruby the truth about the poster), good listener (Jonah asks and waits after the lost game), includes others (Ruby invites Theo), loyal (Ruby defends Jonah), forgiving (Jonah accepts her apology), keeps safe secrets and gets help (Ruby tells the counselor).

## Read
passage: The Poster Project
  Ruby and Jonah had been friends since second grade, but sixth grade was testing them. On Monday, their teacher assigned partner posters about the water cycle. Ruby asked Jonah to be her partner before anyone else could.
  Ruby spent Tuesday night drawing a giant raincloud. On Wednesday, she showed Jonah. "Be honest," she said. "Is it good?" Jonah looked for a while. "The colors are great," he said, "but the arrows go the wrong way. Evaporation goes up." Ruby frowned, then laughed and fixed it.
  On Thursday, a new student named Theo stood alone at lunch holding his tray. Ruby waved him over. "Sit with us," she said. Jonah slid his backpack off the seat.
  Friday was the soccer game. Jonah missed an easy goal, and the team lost. On the bus, a boy said Jonah had cost them the game. Ruby turned around. "He played the whole game. Leave him alone." Later, Jonah was quiet. Ruby asked, "Do you want to talk about it?" and then she just listened.
  The next week, Ruby forgot to bring her half of the poster on the day it was due. Jonah was upset. Ruby said, "I'm sorry. I let you down. I'll stay in at recess to finish it." Jonah thought about it. "Okay," he said. "Let's finish it together."
  That Friday, Theo told Ruby something that worried her. An older kid had been threatening him online, and he made her promise not to tell. Ruby thought about it all night. The next morning, she went with Theo to see the school counselor. "Some secrets aren't safe to keep," she told him. Theo was nervous, but he was also relieved.

# 33. Private or Okay to Share?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can decide which information is private and which is okay to share online.
directions: Cut out the 10 cards. Glue each one under Keep private or Okay to share. Then explain your choice for 3 of the cards.
mistakes: Students often put "pet's name" in Okay to share. Pet names are a common password and security question answer, so it is safer kept private. Watch for "school name" as okay: with a first name, it can tell a stranger where to find someone.
notes: Advice follows the Federal Trade Commission's guidance for kids online. "Okay to share" still means sharing with care, on accounts a parent or guardian knows about, and never with a stranger who asks for more. Avoid having students list their own real information as examples.

## Warm up
choice: Why is it risky to share where you go to school with someone you only know online?
  - Schools do not like being named
  * A stranger could use it to find out where you are each day
  - It makes your profile picture look worse
choice: Which of these is the best password?
  - The name of your pet
  - Your birthday
  * A long phrase of random words with numbers and symbols

## Main: Sort the cards
sort: Private or okay to share?
  - Keep private: your birthday (day, month and year); your home address; your password; your school's name; your pet's name; your phone number
  - Okay to share: your favorite song; your favorite food; a drawing of a dragon you made; a book you liked this year

## Main: Explain three
table: Card | Private or okay? | Why
  height: 0.6
  - Your pet's name | {Private} | {Pet names are often used in passwords and security questions, so someone could use it to get into an account.}
  - Your birthday | {Private} | {With your name, a full birth date can be used to pretend to be you or to answer security questions.}
  - Your favorite song | {Okay to share} | {It does not tell anyone who you are, where you live or how to get into your accounts.}

## Stretch
write: A player in a game chat writes, "What school do you go to? Maybe we're neighbors!" Write a safe reply, or explain what you would do instead.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: "I don't share that online. Want to play another round?" Or do not answer, and tell a trusted adult if they keep asking. Block or report if they push.
