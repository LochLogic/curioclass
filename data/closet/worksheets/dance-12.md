# 1. Three Theories of Dance
minutes: 55
materials: pencil, highlighter, a video clip or live performance of one dance chosen by the teacher
ican: I can apply expression, formalist and cultural context theories to interpret a dance.
directions: Read the three short passages. Answer the questions about each theory. Then apply all three theories to one dance and decide which theory reveals the most about it.
mistakes: Students treat the theories as right and wrong answers. Each is a lens: it shows some things clearly and hides others. Formalism is often misread as "the dance has no meaning"; it means the meaning is found in the form itself (line, shape, structure, rhythm). Cultural context is not just "where it is from": it asks who made the dance, for whom, and what it does in that community.
notes: The passages are original summaries written for Curio Class. Martha Graham's Lamentation (1930) works well as the shared dance: a solo in which the dancer sits on a bench inside a tube of stretch fabric and pushes against it, often read as an image of grief. Joann Kealiinohomoku's essay "An Anthropologist Looks at Ballet as a Form of Ethnic Dance" (1970) is the classic source for the third passage; Clive Bell's idea of "significant form" (1914) comes from visual art. Students may instead analyze a dance from earlier in the unit.

## Read
passage: Three Ways to Understand a Dance
  **Expression theory.** For many people, a dance matters because it communicates feeling. Expression theory holds that a choreographer begins with an inner experience, such as grief, joy or anger, and shapes movement until the audience can sense that experience too. Supporters point out that watching a dancer collapse or soar can make our own bodies respond; the American critic John Martin called this response "metakinesis," the idea that movement carries feeling from the dancer to the watcher. Under this theory, a dance succeeds when its emotion is clear and honest, and the best question to ask is, "What does this dance make me feel, and how does it do that?" Critics of the theory note that a dancer can perform sorrow without feeling it, and that some powerful dances do not seem to be about emotion at all.
  **Formalism.** Formalist theory moves attention from the artist's feelings to the work itself. The art critic Clive Bell argued that visual art moves us through "significant form," the arrangement of lines, shapes and colors, and formalists in dance apply the same idea to bodies in space and time. A formalist looks at line, shape, symmetry, rhythm, repetition, contrast and structure. A plotless ballet in which dancers form and dissolve geometric patterns, or a modern work built from chance procedures, may have no story or stated emotion, yet a formalist can describe exactly why it is satisfying to watch. The key question is, "How is this dance built, and how do its parts work together?" Critics answer that formalism can ignore what a dance means to the people who made it.
  **Cultural context theory.** The third approach argues that no dance can be fully understood apart from the community, history and purpose that shaped it. In 1970, the anthropologist and dance scholar Joann Kealiinohomoku published an essay arguing that ballet is itself a form of ethnic dance, reflecting the values and history of the European cultures that created it. Her point was that every dance, not only dances from places unfamiliar to Western audiences, carries its culture's ideas about the body, gender, beauty and community. A cultural context reading asks who made the dance, who performs and watches it, where and when it happens, and what it does for that community: does it celebrate, teach, worship, protest or entertain? Critics warn that context can crowd out close looking at the movement, but supporters reply that without context we may misread what we see.
  Most critics today combine these lenses. A single dance can express feeling, display beautiful form and carry cultural meaning all at once, and each theory helps us notice something the others miss.

## Questions
choice: According to expression theory, a dance succeeds when
  * its emotion reaches the audience clearly
  - its geometric patterns are perfectly symmetrical
  - it is performed in the place where it began

  - expression
  * formalism
  - cultural context
choice: Why did Kealiinohomoku call ballet a form of ethnic dance?
  - Ballet borrowed steps from many different countries.
  * Every dance, including ballet, reflects the culture that created it.
  - Ballet was first performed outside Europe.
write: What does "metakinesis" mean, in your own words?
  lines: 2
  key: The idea, from critic John Martin, that watching movement makes the viewer's own body sense or respond to it, so feeling travels from the dancer to the audience.

## Apply: one dance, three lenses
text: Dance I am analyzing: ______________________________ Choreographer and year: __________________
text: Rows in order: expression, formalism, cultural context.
chart: Theory | What this lens notices in the dance (2 specific details) | The question it answers best
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary by dance. Expression: the feeling and how movement makes the viewer sense it ("What does it make me feel, and how?"). Formalism: line, shape, rhythm, repetition and structure ("How is it built?"). Cultural context: who made it, for whom, when, and what it does for that community ("What does it mean to the people who made it?").
write: Which theory reveals the most about this dance? Defend your choice with evidence, and name one thing that theory misses.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. Model for Lamentation: Expression reveals the most, because the dancer straining against the fabric makes grief visible and the viewer feels the pull in their own body. Formalism adds the sculptural diagonals the fabric makes and the stillness of the seated position; cultural context adds that Graham made it in 1930 as American modern dance broke away from ballet. Expression misses how carefully the shapes are designed, which formalism explains.

# 7. Peer Review Edit
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, a colored pen, a partner's draft dance review
ican: I can edit a dance review for evidence, fairness and clarity, and name my own writing habits.
directions: Diagnose each sentence of the practice review. Then use the checklist to edit your partner's review, writing notes they can act on. Finish by reflecting on your own habits as a writer.
mistakes: Peer editors either praise everything ("Great job!") or rewrite the whole review in their own voice. Notes should name the problem and point to a fix while leaving the writer's ideas in place. Watch the line between a harsh judgment and an unfair one: a reviewer can dislike a piece strongly and still be fair if the claim is about the work and backed by evidence.
notes: The three criteria: evidence (each judgment is supported by something observable), fairness (the review judges the work, not the person, and by standards that fit the work's style and purpose), clarity (a general reader can follow every sentence). Pairs should swap drafts written in the earlier review lessons. Model one note aloud before students begin, for example "Paragraph 2: 'The ending is powerful' needs evidence. What did you see in the last 30 seconds?"

## Warm up: Diagnose the practice review
text: A classmate wrote this review of Piece A, an invented student dance. Name the main problem in each sentence: **evidence**, **fairness**, **clarity**, or **none** if the sentence is strong.
table: Sentence from the review | Main problem
  - 1. Piece A is the worst dance I have ever seen, and the choreographer clearly has no talent. | {fairness}
  - 2. In the opening, six dancers roll slowly downstage in a staggered line, so the stage looks like waves breaking on a beach. | {none}
  - 3. The dance was kind of a vibe thing with stuff happening at different times. | {clarity}
  - 4. The middle section is weak. | {evidence}
  - 5. It is not a real dance, because there are no ballet steps in it. | {fairness}
  - 6. The music, which was good, and the lights, sort of, were when the part with the thing happened. | {clarity}
  - 7. The final image is haunting: one dancer stands alone in a narrow strip of blue light while the others lie still behind her. | {none}
write: Rewrite sentence 4 so it has evidence. You may invent details that could be true of a dance.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "The middle section loses energy: the same four-count arm phrase repeats eight times at one tempo, and the dancers stay in a single straight line, so nothing changes for almost a minute."
write: Rewrite sentence 1 so it stays negative but is fair.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "Piece A has a striking opening, but the piece loses its way after the first minute and never recovers the energy of its rolling entrance." The rewrite judges the work, not the person.

## Reflect on my own writing
write: Which of the three criteria (evidence, fairness, clarity) is hardest for you as a writer? Give an example from one of your own reviews.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong reflection is honest and specific, quoting or describing a real sentence the student wrote and naming the habit behind it, such as stacking adjectives instead of describing movement.
write: Name one habit you will change in your next review and how you will check for it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "I write a judgment and move on. Before I turn in a review, I will underline every judgment and draw an arrow to its evidence; any judgment with no arrow gets evidence or gets cut."

# 20. Dancer Track Maps
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, colored pencils (one color per dancer), your issue piece and cast list
ican: I can map each dancer's track through my choreography so every entrance, exit and key moment is planned.
directions: Label the nine stage areas. Then map four dancers' tracks through your issue piece, one stage diagram per dancer: mark where they enter, number their key moments in order, draw their pathways, and mark where they exit. Fill in the track summary and check for traffic problems.
mistakes: Students mix up stage left and right. Stage directions are from the performer's point of view facing the audience, so stage right is on the audience's left. Watch for tracks that end with a dancer stranded on stage with no planned exit, and for two dancers whose paths cross on the same counts.
notes: Map key: E = entrance (with the wing or side it comes from), X = exit, circled numbers = key moments in order, solid line = traveling pathway, dotted line = a slow walk or transition. Upstage is drawn at the top of each box and the audience at the bottom. For larger casts, groups can copy the box on blank paper or map pairs together. A track is the full path one performer follows from first entrance to last exit; stage managers and swing performers in professional companies learn whole shows by their tracks.

## Warm up: Stage areas
text: Upstage is at the top. The audience sits below the bottom row. Write the abbreviation for each area (US = upstage, DS = downstage, SR = stage right, SL = stage left, C = center).
table: Audience's left side | Middle | Audience's right side
  height: 0.38
  - {USR} | {USC} | {USL}
  - {SR} | {C} | {SL}
  - {DSR} | {DSC} | {DSL}
choice: A dancer is told to exit "down right." Facing the audience, which way does she go?
  - Toward the back of the stage, on her left
  * Toward the audience, on her right
  - Toward the audience, on her left

## Main: Four tracks
text: E = entrance, X = exit, circled numbers = key moments, solid line = traveling, dotted line = walking or a transition. Upstage at the top of each box.
draw: Dancer 1: ______________ | 1.5
draw: Dancer 2: ______________ | 1.5
draw: Dancer 3: ______________ | 1.5
draw: Dancer 4: ______________ | 1.5
chart: Dancer | Enters (where, on what count) | Key moments (what happens at each number) | Exits (where, on what count)
  rows: 4
  height: 0.75
  key: Answers vary. A complete summary gives each dancer an entrance with a side and a count, key moments numbered in the same order as the map, and a planned exit (or a final position held to the end). The maps and the chart should match.

## Check the traffic
write: Find a moment where two dancers' tracks cross. When does it happen, and how do you keep them safe and clear?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "Dancers 2 and 4 both cross center around count 1 of phrase 9; dancer 2 now crosses upstage of dancer 4 and waits one count, so they pass without a collision."

## Stretch
write: Which dancer's track carries the most meaning for your issue? Explain how the path itself tells part of the story.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer connects pathway and meaning, for example "Dancer 3 starts isolated in the upstage left corner and moves in straight lines toward the group, reaching center only at the end, which shows her being let in."

# 21. Eight-Rehearsal Schedule and Log
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, a calendar, your issue piece and cast
ican: I can plan and track a rehearsal process for my choreography and adjust it based on progress.
directions: Answer the planning questions. Fill in the schedule with a date and goal for each of the 8 rehearsals. After each rehearsal, log the sections you completed and what you adjusted. Reflect at the end.
mistakes: Students set the same goal ("work on dance") for every rehearsal. Each goal should name a section and an outcome ("teach section B, counts 1-32"). Leaving no time to clean or run the whole piece is the most common planning error: protect the last two rehearsals for full runs.
notes: Allow 30 minutes to plan, then about 5 minutes after each rehearsal to log it. This log follows a group's issue piece across about four weeks. Check logs after rehearsals 2, 5 and 8. A good plan sets new material in the first half, cleaning and transitions in the middle, and full run-throughs at the end, with at least one rehearsal planned as a buffer for falling behind.

## Warm up: Plan backward
fill:
  - 8 rehearsals of 45 minutes give {360} minutes, or {6} hours, of rehearsal time.
  - The piece has 4 sections. If you set one new section per rehearsal starting with rehearsal 1, all sections are set by rehearsal {4}.
  - Rehearsals 7 and 8 are full runs. That leaves rehearsals {5} and {6} for cleaning and transitions.
  - A 3-minute piece run 4 times in one rehearsal takes {12} minutes of dancing.

## Main: Schedule and log
text: One row per rehearsal, 1 to 8. Fill in the date and goal now; fill in the last two columns after each rehearsal.
chart: Rehearsal and date | Goal (section and outcome) | Sections completed | Adjustments made
  rows: 8
  key: Answers vary. A sound plan sets new sections in rehearsals 1 to 4, cleans and works transitions in 5 and 6, and runs the whole piece in 7 and 8; goals name a section and an outcome, and adjustments are specific.

## Reflect
write: Which rehearsal fell furthest behind its goal? What did you adjust in the schedule because of it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the rehearsal, the cause (absences, a section that was too hard, transitions) and a concrete change, such as cutting 8 counts, merging two goals or using the buffer rehearsal.
write: Name one change you made to the choreography itself during the process. Did it make the piece communicate your issue more clearly?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer describes a specific change (a new formation, a simplified phrase, a different ending) and links it to the issue, for example "We changed the ending from a line to a circle so the audience sees the group finally including the dancer who was isolated."

## Stretch
write: If you had 4 rehearsals instead of 8, what would you cut, and what would you protect?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers cut or simplify sections and keep at least one full run-through, protecting the ideas that carry the issue (the key formation or motif).

# 33. Risk Assessment Matrix
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, your site-specific performance plan
ican: I can assess the risks of a site-specific performance and plan a response to each one.
directions: Practice scoring risks in the warm up. Then rate the 8 risks for your own site from 1 to 3 for likelihood and impact, multiply to get a score, place each one on the matrix, and write a response. Answer the questions.
mistakes: Students rate everything high or everything low. Push for honest ratings grounded in the site: noise from a busy road is likely there, but rare in a quiet courtyard. Watch for "accept" used as a way to skip planning; accepting a risk still means saying what you will do if it happens. Responses should be concrete actions, not "be careful."
notes: Score = likelihood x impact, each rated 1 (low) to 3 (high), so scores run from 1 to 9. Scores of 6 or 9 need a plan before the show can go on; 3 or 4 need a plan; 1 or 2 can be accepted with a note. The four standard responses: avoid (change the plan so the risk cannot happen), reduce (make it less likely or less harmful), share (move part of it to someone else, such as the site manager or school nurse), accept (live with it and know what you will do). Have the plan checked by an adult responsible for the site.

## Warm up: Score it
fill:
  - Likelihood 3, impact 2: score {6}
  - Likelihood 1, impact 3: score {3}
  - Likelihood 2, impact 2: score {4}
  - The highest possible score is {9}, and the lowest is {1}.
choice: A dancer could slip on wet stone steps. The group moves that phrase to a flat, dry area of the site. Which response is this?
  * Avoid
  - Share
  - Accept
choice: The group asks the school nurse to attend the performance with a first aid kit. Which response is this?
  - Avoid
  * Share
  - Accept
