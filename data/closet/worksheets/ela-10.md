# 1. Cultural Lens: The Necklace
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can explain how a story's cultural setting shapes a character's choices, citing the text.
directions: After reading "The Necklace" by Guy de Maupassant (1884), read the background and the seven passages. Chart three of Mathilde's choices and the class expectation behind each one, then explain how her world shapes her, citing at least three passages.
mistakes: Students often judge Mathilde only as vain. The task asks why her choices make sense in her world: a woman's status depended on her husband's rank, and appearance was how rank was read. Also watch for the idea that she loses the necklace "because she is poor": her fear of being seen in poor clothes (passage 4) and of being taken for a thief (passage 5) drive the choices that cost her ten years.
notes: Maupassant published the story in French as "La Parure" in 1884. The passages follow the anonymous English translation in Project Gutenberg's Complete Original Short Stories of Guy de Maupassant (eBook #3090), where it is titled "The Diamond Necklace," with curly quotation marks made straight. Class anthologies use other translations, so wording may differ; have students cite the passage numbers here. Paste is glass made to look like gemstones.

## Background
text: In Paris in the 1880s, a woman's place in society depended mostly on her family's money and her husband's rank. A dowry, the money a bride's family gave at marriage, helped decide whom she could marry. Clerks in government offices earned a modest living. At an official ball, guests judged each other by clothing and jewelry, and to look poor among the rich was a public embarrassment.

## Read: Seven passages
source: Guy de Maupassant, "The Necklace" ("La Parure," 1884), anonymous English translation
  [1] "She had no dowry, no expectations, no way of being known, understood, loved, married by any rich and distinguished man; so she let herself be married to a little clerk of the Ministry of Public Instruction."
  [2] "Mathilde suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born to enjoy all delicacies and all luxuries."
  [3] "No; there's nothing more humiliating than to look poor among other women who are rich."
  [4] "He threw over her shoulders the wraps he had brought, the modest wraps of common life, the poverty of which contrasted with the elegance of the ball dress. She felt this and wished to escape so as not to be remarked by the other women, who were enveloping themselves in costly furs."
  [5] "She did not open the case, as her friend had so much feared. If she had detected the substitution, what would she have thought, what would she have said? Would she not have taken Madame Loisel for a thief?"
  [6] "Thereafter Madame Loisel knew the horrible existence of the needy. She bore her part, however, with sudden heroism. That dreadful debt must be paid. She would pay it."
  [7] "Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste! It was worth at most only five hundred francs!"

## Warm up
choice: According to passage 1, why did Mathilde marry a clerk?
  - She loved him more than any rich man.
  * Without a dowry or connections, she had no way to marry a rich man.
  - Her friend Madame Forestier chose him for her.
  - She wanted a quiet life.
choice: In passage 4, why does Mathilde hurry away from the ball?
  - She is tired and wants to sleep.
  - She has lost the necklace and wants to search for it.
  * She is ashamed for the rich women to see her plain wraps.
  - Her husband is angry with her.

## Main: Choices and class expectations
chart: Mathilde's choice | The class expectation behind it | Passage number
  rows: 3
  key: Refuses the invitation unless she has a gown and jewels, then borrows the necklace: looking poor among the rich is humiliating [3]. Rushes out in her modest wraps instead of waiting for a cab: she must not be "remarked" in clothes of "common life" [4]. Replaces the necklace in secret instead of confessing: she fears being taken "for a thief" [5]. Also accept: married a clerk because without a dowry she had no other choice [1].
write: How does passage 1 explain the root of Mathilde's unhappiness? What does it suggest about the choices women of her time had?
  lines: 4
  key: Her lack of a dowry and connections, not her own failings, decided her marriage: she "let herself be married to a little clerk." It suggests that women's futures depended on money and marriage, so she feels she was born into the wrong class.
write: Explain how class expectations lead the Loisels to replace the necklace instead of telling the truth. Cite at least two passages.
  lines: 5
  key: To admit the loss would expose them as people who could not be trusted with fine things; passage 5 shows the fear that Madame Forestier would take her "for a thief." Mathilde has already shown she cannot bear to look poor [3], [4], so they borrow and sink into debt rather than face that shame.
write: Passage 6 says Mathilde bore her new life "with sudden heroism." How does the story's ending (passage 7) change the meaning of her ten years of work?
  lines: 5
  key: She works heroically to protect her honor, but the necklace was "paste," worth only five hundred francs. Her sacrifice was for an appearance, the same thing she valued too much at the start, so the ending exposes how empty the class standards were that ruled her choices.

## Stretch: The writer's view
write: Does Maupassant blame Mathilde, her society, or both? Make a claim and support it with three passages.
  lines: 7
  key: Strong answers argue for both. Society: passage 1 shows a world where a woman without a dowry has "no way" upward, and passages 3 and 4 show that looking poor brings humiliation. Mathilde: passage 2 shows she feels "born to enjoy all delicacies," and her pride keeps her from confessing [5]. The paste necklace [7] mocks a society that cares only for appearances and a heroine who believed in them.
# 23. Peer Review: Style and Syntax
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, two colors of pen
ican: I can revise writing for varied syntax, precise verbs and concise wording, and give a peer useful feedback.
directions: Read the sample paragraph a student wrote about Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart." Score it with the rubric, mark its style problems, and suggest three revisions.
mistakes: Students often change content instead of style, or call any short sentence "choppy." Keep the student's ideas and fix only how they are expressed. Weak verbs are not wrong; they are vague (gets, has, makes, does, is). Swap them only when a precise verb says more. Cutting words that carry meaning ("pale blue") is not concision.
notes: The sample paragraph is original to Curio Class, written to include planted problems. Sentences 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 all open with "The narrator"; weak verb phrases are in sentences 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8; wordiness is in sentences 1, 4, 7 and 10. Accept any revision that keeps the meaning and solves the problem.

## Read the sample
passage: Sample: Guilt in "The Tell-Tale Heart"
  **(1)** The narrator in "The Tell-Tale Heart" is a person who says over and over again that he is not mad. **(2)** The narrator is very nervous, and the narrator is very proud of how careful he is. **(3)** The narrator gets more and more upset when he hears a beating sound. **(4)** Due to the fact that he thinks he is clever, he does a lot of planning before the crime. **(5)** The narrator has a fear of the old man's pale blue eye. **(6)** The narrator makes the decision to get rid of the eye forever. **(7)** In my personal opinion, I think that Poe is showing that guilt is something that cannot be hidden. **(8)** At the end of the story, the sound gets louder and louder until the narrator confesses. **(9)** Poe uses the heartbeat to show guilt. **(10)** It is clear that the narrator is not as calm as he says he is, and this is what makes the story frightening.

  lines: 3
