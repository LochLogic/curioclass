# 1. Cultural Lens: The Necklace
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can explain how a story's cultural setting shapes a character's choices, citing the text.
directions: After reading "The Necklace" by Guy de Maupassant (1884), read the background and the seven passages. Chart three of Mathilde's choices and the class expectation behind each one, then explain how her world shapes her, citing at least three passages.
mistakes: Students often judge Mathilde only as vain. The task asks why her choices make sense in her world: a woman's status depended on her husband's rank, and appearance was how rank was read. Also watch for the idea that she loses the necklace "because she is poor": her fear of being seen in poor clothes (passage 4) and of being taken for a thief (passage 5) drive the choices that cost her ten years.
notes: Maupassant published the story in French as "La Parure" in 1884. The passages follow the anonymous English translation in Project Gutenberg's Complete Original Short Stories of Guy de Maupassant (eBook #3090), where it is titled "The Diamond Necklace," with curly quotation marks made straight. Class anthologies use other translations, so wording may differ; have students cite the passage numbers here. Paste is glass made to look like gemstones.

## Background
text: In Paris in the 1880s, a woman's place in society depended mostly on her family's money and her husband's rank. A dowry, the money a bride's family gave at marriage, helped decide whom she could marry. Clerks in government offices earned a modest living. At an official ball, guests judged each other by clothing and jewelry, and to look poor among the rich was a public embarrassment.

## Read: Seven passages
source: Guy de Maupassant, "The Necklace" ("La Parure," 1884), anonymous English translation
  [1] "She had no dowry, no expectations, no way of being known, understood, loved, married by any rich and distinguished man; so she let herself be married to a little clerk of the Ministry of Public Instruction."
  [2] "Mathilde suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born to enjoy all delicacies and all luxuries."
  [3] "No; there's nothing more humiliating than to look poor among other women who are rich."
  [4] "He threw over her shoulders the wraps he had brought, the modest wraps of common life, the poverty of which contrasted with the elegance of the ball dress. She felt this and wished to escape so as not to be remarked by the other women, who were enveloping themselves in costly furs."
  [5] "She did not open the case, as her friend had so much feared. If she had detected the substitution, what would she have thought, what would she have said? Would she not have taken Madame Loisel for a thief?"
  [6] "Thereafter Madame Loisel knew the horrible existence of the needy. She bore her part, however, with sudden heroism. That dreadful debt must be paid. She would pay it."
  [7] "Oh, my poor Mathilde! Why, my necklace was paste! It was worth at most only five hundred francs!"

## Warm up
choice: According to passage 1, why did Mathilde marry a clerk?
  - She loved him more than any rich man.
  * Without a dowry or connections, she had no way to marry a rich man.
  - Her friend Madame Forestier chose him for her.
  - She wanted a quiet life.
choice: In passage 4, why does Mathilde hurry away from the ball?
  - She is tired and wants to sleep.
  - She has lost the necklace and wants to search for it.
  * She is ashamed for the rich women to see her plain wraps.
  - Her husband is angry with her.

## Main: Choices and class expectations
chart: Mathilde's choice | The class expectation behind it | Passage number
  rows: 3
  key: Refuses the invitation unless she has a gown and jewels, then borrows the necklace: looking poor among the rich is humiliating [3]. Rushes out in her modest wraps instead of waiting for a cab: she must not be "remarked" in clothes of "common life" [4]. Replaces the necklace in secret instead of confessing: she fears being taken "for a thief" [5]. Also accept: married a clerk because without a dowry she had no other choice [1].
write: How does passage 1 explain the root of Mathilde's unhappiness? What does it suggest about the choices women of her time had?
  lines: 4
  key: Her lack of a dowry and connections, not her own failings, decided her marriage: she "let herself be married to a little clerk." It suggests that women's futures depended on money and marriage, so she feels she was born into the wrong class.
write: Explain how class expectations lead the Loisels to replace the necklace instead of telling the truth. Cite at least two passages.
  lines: 5
  key: To admit the loss would expose them as people who could not be trusted with fine things; passage 5 shows the fear that Madame Forestier would take her "for a thief." Mathilde has already shown she cannot bear to look poor [3], [4], so they borrow and sink into debt rather than face that shame.
write: Passage 6 says Mathilde bore her new life "with sudden heroism." How does the story's ending (passage 7) change the meaning of her ten years of work?
  lines: 5
  key: She works heroically to protect her honor, but the necklace was "paste," worth only five hundred francs. Her sacrifice was for an appearance, the same thing she valued too much at the start, so the ending exposes how empty the class standards were that ruled her choices.

# 6. Death as a Gentleman Caller
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can analyze how an extended metaphor shapes a poem's tone.
directions: Read Emily Dickinson's poem twice. Map the extended metaphor of Death as a polite caller taking the speaker on a carriage ride, then explain how it creates the poem's calm tone.
mistakes: Students often read the poem as frightening because its subject is death. Ask them to find a single word of fear or struggle: there is none. Watch also for students who stop the metaphor after stanza 1; it runs to the end, from the carriage to the "house" (the grave) to "eternity."
notes: The poem follows its first printing in Poems by Emily Dickinson (1890), edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson, where it was titled "The Chariot" (Project Gutenberg eBook #12242). Dickinson wrote it around 1863. Her manuscript, printed in later editions, has her dashes and capitals and one more stanza, after the setting sun, in which the sun passes them, the dew grows cold, and the speaker notices her thin gown. Classes using that version can add it to the map. "Cornice" may need glossing: it is the decorative top edge of a roof.

## Read: The poem
source: Emily Dickinson, "Because I could not stop for Death" (written about 1863; first printed 1890 as "The Chariot"); stanza numbers added
  [1] Because I could not stop for Death,
  He kindly stopped for me;
  The carriage held but just ourselves
  And Immortality.
  [2] We slowly drove, he knew no haste,
  And I had put away
  My labor, and my leisure too,
  For his civility.
  [3] We passed the school where children played,
  Their lessons scarcely done;
  We passed the fields of gazing grain,
  We passed the setting sun.
  [4] We paused before a house that seemed
  A swelling of the ground;
  The roof was scarcely visible,
  The cornice but a mound.
  [5] Since then 't is centuries; but each
  Feels shorter than the day
  I first surmised the horses' heads
  Were toward eternity.

## Warm up
choice: In the poem's metaphor, Death is pictured as
  - a thief who breaks in at night
  * a polite gentleman who calls for the speaker in his carriage
  - a soldier who defeats the speaker in battle
  - a storm that sweeps the speaker away
choice: Which word best describes the poem's tone?
  - terrified
  - bitter
  - joking
  * calm

## Main: Map the extended metaphor
table: Detail in the poem | What it stands for in the journey from life to death
  - "He kindly stopped for me" | {Death arrives on his own, like a courteous suitor calling}
  - "The carriage" | {the hearse, or the passage out of life}
  - "Immortality" rides along | {the promise of life after death, like a chaperone}
  - "the school where children played" | {childhood, the start of life}
  - "the fields of gazing grain" | {adulthood or ripeness, the middle of life}
  - "the setting sun" | {old age and the end of life}
  - "a house that seemed / A swelling of the ground" | {the grave}
  - "the horses' heads / Were toward eternity" | {the journey goes on into the afterlife}
write: List three words or phrases from stanzas 1 and 2 that make Death seem gentle. Explain how they shape the tone.
  lines: 3
  key: "kindly," "slowly drove, he knew no haste," "civility." Death behaves like a patient, well-mannered gentleman, so the speaker trusts him and goes along quietly; the tone stays calm instead of fearful.
write: Stanza 3 repeats "We passed." What does the repetition add to the metaphor and the mood?
  lines: 3
  key: The repetition makes the scenes go by like views from a slow carriage window, one stage of life after another. Its steady rhythm keeps the mood quiet and even as the speaker watches her life pass.
write: The last stanza says centuries feel "shorter than the day" the speaker realized where the carriage was going. What does this ending reveal, and is the tone still calm?
  lines: 4
  key: The speaker has been dead for centuries and is speaking from eternity, where time no longer matters; only the moment of understanding stands out. The tone stays calm, even a little wondering, because she has accepted the journey; the realization is quiet, not a cry of fear.

## Stretch: Your own metaphor
write: Dickinson makes death a gentleman caller. Choose another hard idea (failure, change, growing up) and describe it in two sentences as a visitor with manners. What tone does your choice create?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: Change arrives without knocking, already wearing my coat, and asks politely whether I am ready to go. The tone is wry but accepting, since Change is pushy yet courteous.

# 17. Structured Debate Prep
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can prepare claims, evidence, rebuttals and responses for a structured debate.
directions: Your class is debating this resolution: "Our school should go cashless." Your teacher will assign your side. Read the evidence cards, then plan three claims with evidence, predict two rebuttals from the other side, and write your responses.
mistakes: Students often predict weak rebuttals they can easily knock down. A good prediction is the other side's strongest point. Watch also for claims without evidence and for responses that only repeat the original claim; a response must answer the rebuttal directly (concede, then outweigh; or show the evidence is wrong or limited).
notes: Cards 1, 3 and 4 describe a made-up school and are invented for practice. Card 2 is from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's 2021 National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households, which found that 4.5 percent of U.S. households had no checking or savings account; check the latest survey for newer figures. The key shows a model for each side. Structured formats vary; this plan fits most formats with constructive speeches and rebuttals.

## Evidence cards
cards: Evidence cards
  plain: yes
  - **Card 1 (made-up school data):** At Lakeview High, cash payments at lunch took about 20 seconds each and card or account payments about 8 seconds, so lines moved faster without cash.
  - **Card 2 (FDIC survey, 2021):** About 4.5 percent of U.S. households had no bank account, so some families pay mostly in cash.
  - **Card 3 (made-up school data):** Lakeview's student store lost $340 in cash last year to counting errors and one theft.
  - **Card 4 (made-up counselor's statement):** "When every purchase goes through an account, the school keeps a record of what each student buys. Families should know who sees it."

## Warm up
choice: Which is the strongest response to a rebuttal?
  - Repeating your claim more loudly
  - Saying the other side has not done research
  * Admitting what is true in the rebuttal, then showing why your side still matters more
  - Changing the subject to a new claim
choice: Card 2 would most help which side?
  - The side for going cashless
  * The side against going cashless
  - Neither side, because it is about households, not schools
  - Both sides equally

## Main: Plan your case
text: My side (circle one): **For** going cashless / **Against** going cashless
chart: Claim | Evidence (card number or your own research)
  labels: Claim 1; Claim 2; Claim 3
  height: 0.75
  key: For: 1. Cashless lunch lines are faster, so students get more time to eat (Card 1). 2. Cash can be lost or stolen; accounts are safer and easier to track (Card 3). 3. Families can load money and see balances, so payments are simpler to manage (own research). Against: 1. Some families do not have bank accounts and pay in cash; a cashless school shuts them out (Card 2). 2. Accounts create records of student purchases, which raises privacy concerns (Card 4). 3. Cash teaches students to handle and count real money (own research).
chart: Rebuttal the other side will likely make | My response
  labels: Rebuttal 1; Rebuttal 2
  height: 0.9
  key: For side: Rebuttal 1: Families without bank accounts are shut out (Card 2). Response: The school can accept cash at the front office to load accounts, so no one is excluded while lines still move faster. Rebuttal 2: Accounts track what students buy (Card 4). Response: The school can limit records to balances and publish who sees them; the safety of no lost cash outweighs this. Against side: Rebuttal 1: Lines are faster without cash (Card 1). Response: Twelve seconds per student matters less than every family being able to pay; a separate cash line keeps most lines fast. Rebuttal 2: Cash gets lost (Card 3). Response: $340 a year is a small, fixable loss with better counting, not a reason to exclude families.

## Stretch: Weigh it
write: Write the one sentence you would say in your closing to explain why your side's best point outweighs the other side's best point.
  lines: 2
  key: For: Even granting the access concern, which a cash loading desk solves, faster lines and no lost cash help every student every day. Against: A few seconds saved in line cannot outweigh a policy that leaves some families unable to pay for their children's lunch.

# 23. Peer Review: Style and Syntax
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, two colors of pen
ican: I can revise writing for varied syntax, precise verbs and concise wording, and give a peer useful feedback.
directions: Read the sample paragraph a student wrote about Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart." Score it with the rubric, mark its style problems, and suggest three revisions.
mistakes: Students often change content instead of style, or call any short sentence "choppy." Keep the student's ideas and fix only how they are expressed. Weak verbs are not wrong; they are vague (gets, has, makes, does, is). Swap them only when a precise verb says more. Cutting words that carry meaning ("pale blue") is not concision.
notes: The sample paragraph is original to Curio Class, written to include planted problems. Sentences 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 all open with "The narrator"; weak verb phrases are in sentences 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8; wordiness is in sentences 1, 4, 7 and 10. Accept any revision that keeps the meaning and solves the problem.

## Read the sample
passage: Sample: Guilt in "The Tell-Tale Heart"
  **(1)** The narrator in "The Tell-Tale Heart" is a person who says over and over again that he is not mad. **(2)** The narrator is very nervous, and the narrator is very proud of how careful he is. **(3)** The narrator gets more and more upset when he hears a beating sound. **(4)** Due to the fact that he thinks he is clever, he does a lot of planning before the crime. **(5)** The narrator has a fear of the old man's pale blue eye. **(6)** The narrator makes the decision to get rid of the eye forever. **(7)** In my personal opinion, I think that Poe is showing that guilt is something that cannot be hidden. **(8)** At the end of the story, the sound gets louder and louder until the narrator confesses. **(9)** Poe uses the heartbeat to show guilt. **(10)** It is clear that the narrator is not as calm as he says he is, and this is what makes the story frightening.

table: Criterion | Strong (4) | Developing (2) | Score

  - Precise verbs | Verbs are specific and vivid | Relies on is, has, gets, makes, does | {2}

## Main: Mark the problems
fill:
  - The sentences that open with the same two words: {1, 2, 3, 5, 6}
  - Sentence 5, weak verb phrase "has a fear of": replace with {fears}
  - Sentence 6, weak verb phrase "makes the decision": replace with {decides}
  - Sentence 4, weak verb phrase "does a lot of planning": replace with {plans carefully}
  - Sentence 3, weak verb "gets more and more upset": replace with {grows frantic}
  - Sentence 8, weak verb "gets louder and louder": replace with {swells, pounds}
  - Sentence 4, wordy phrase "Due to the fact that": replace with {Because}
  - Sentence 7, wordy phrase "In my personal opinion, I think that": {cut it}
  - Sentence 1, wordy phrase "is a person who says over and over again": replace with {insists}

## Suggest three revisions
write: Revision 1: Combine sentences 2 and 3 into one varied sentence with precise verbs.
  lines: 3
  key: Nervous yet proud of his caution, the narrator grows frantic when he hears a beating sound.
write: Revision 2: Rewrite sentences 5 and 6 so they do not both begin with "The narrator" and use stronger verbs.
  lines: 3
  key: Haunted by the old man's pale blue eye, the narrator decides to destroy it forever.
write: Revision 3: Rewrite sentence 10 concisely.
  lines: 2
  key: The narrator's calm is a lie, and that gap makes the story frightening.

## Stretch: Feedback note
write: Write a two-sentence note to the writer: one specific strength and one specific next step.
  lines: 3
  key: Strength: your claim in sentence 9 is clear and connects the heartbeat to guilt. Next step: vary how your sentences begin and swap verbs like "gets" and "has" for precise ones so your analysis sounds as confident as your ideas.

# 28. Eyewitness to the 1906 Earthquake
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can trace how a central idea develops and is refined across a text.
directions: Read the excerpt from Jack London's article for Collier's magazine, published May 5, 1906, about two weeks after the San Francisco earthquake and fire. Track what each paragraph adds to the central idea, then write the central idea in your own words.
mistakes: Students often name a topic ("the earthquake") instead of an idea about it. Others stop at destruction and miss how London develops it: the earthquake breaks the city's systems, the fire finishes the city, yet the people stay calm and kind, and the city plans to rebuild. Point out that the article is mostly about the fire, which did far more damage than the shaking.
notes: The excerpt follows the text of "The Story of an Eyewitness" as transcribed by the Museum of the City of San Francisco (sfmuseum.org), with a few obvious typing errors corrected (missing periods; "calmly" for "calming"); [...] marks a cut. Collier's paid London 25 cents a word for the article. London's figures are an eyewitness's impressions written days after the fire; later historians estimate that roughly 3,000 people died and more than half the city's population lost their homes. "Nabobs" are very rich people; "conflagration" is a huge fire.

## Read: The excerpt
passage: from "The Story of an Eyewitness," by Jack London (1906) | numbered
  The earthquake shook down in San Francisco hundreds of thousands of dollars' worth of walls and chimneys. But the conflagration that followed burned up hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of property. There is no estimating within hundreds of millions the actual damage wrought. Not in history has a modern imperial city been so completely destroyed. San Francisco is gone. Nothing remains of it but memories and a fringe of dwelling-houses on its outskirts. Its industrial section is wiped out. Its business section is wiped out. Its social and residential section is wiped out. [...]
  On Wednesday morning at a quarter past five came the earthquake. A minute later the flames were leaping upward. In a dozen different quarters south of Market Street, in the working-class ghetto, and in the factories, fires started. There was no opposing the flames. There was no organization, no communication. All the cunning adjustments of a twentieth century city had been smashed by the earthquake. The streets were humped into ridges and depressions, and piled with the debris of fallen walls. The steel rails were twisted into perpendicular and horizontal angles. The telephone and telegraph systems were disrupted. And the great water-mains had burst. All the shrewd contrivances and safeguards of man had been thrown out of gear by thirty seconds' twitching of the earth-crust.
  Remarkable as it may seem, Wednesday night while the whole city crashed and roared into ruin, was a quiet night. There were no crowds. There was no shouting and yelling. There was no hysteria, no disorder. I passed Wednesday night in the path of the advancing flames, and in all those terrible hours I saw not one woman who wept, not one man who was excited, not one person who was in the slightest degree panic stricken.
  Before the flames, throughout the night, fled tens of thousands of homeless ones. Some were wrapped in blankets. Others carried bundles of bedding and dear household treasures. [...] Yet everybody was gracious. The most perfect courtesy obtained. Never in all San Francisco's history, were her people so kind and courteous as on this night of terror.
  At one o'clock in the morning I walked down through the same section [the heart of the city]. Everything still stood intact. There was no fire. And yet there was a change. A rain of ashes was falling. The watchmen at the doors were gone. The police had been withdrawn. There were no firemen, no fire-engines, no men fighting with dynamite. The district had been absolutely abandoned. I stood at the corner of Kearny and Market, in the very innermost heart of San Francisco. Kearny Street was deserted. Half a dozen blocks away it was burning on both sides. The street was a wall of flame. And against this wall of flame, silhouetted sharply, were two United States cavalrymen sitting their horses, calmly watching. That was all. Not another person was in sight. In the intact heart of the city two troopers sat their horses and watched.
  San Francisco, at the present time, is like the crater of a volcano, around which are camped tens of thousands of refugees. [...] The Government has the situation in hand, and, thanks to the immediate relief given by the whole United States, there is not the slightest possibility of a famine. The bankers and business men have already set about making preparations to rebuild San Francisco.

## Warm up
choice: Which sentence in paragraph 1 states the main claim most directly?
  - "There is no estimating within hundreds of millions the actual damage wrought."
  * "San Francisco is gone."
  - "Its industrial section is wiped out."
  - "Its business section is wiped out."

## Main: Trace the central idea
chart: What this paragraph adds to the central idea | Key words or sentence
  labels: Paragraph 1; Paragraph 2; Paragraph 3; Paragraph 4; Paragraph 5; Paragraph 6
  height: 0.45
  key: 1: The scale: a whole modern city destroyed, mostly by fire ("San Francisco is gone"; "wiped out" repeated). 2: Why: thirty seconds of shaking broke every system a modern city relies on, so nothing could stop the fire ("All the shrewd contrivances and safeguards of man"). 3: A turn: in the middle of ruin the people stay calm ("not one person ... panic stricken"). 4: The people are also kind ("Never ... so kind and courteous"). 5: The city's heart is abandoned to the fire; only two calm cavalrymen watch, a single image of helplessness and composure ("two troopers sat their horses and watched"). 6: The aftermath: the city is a "crater," but relief has arrived and rebuilding has already begun.
write: Paragraph 2 repeats "There was no." What does this repetition show, and how does it connect to the last sentence of the paragraph?
  lines: 2
  key: The repeated "no" lists one failed system after another, showing that nothing stood between the city and the fire. It builds to the last sentence, which says all of "man's" safeguards were undone by thirty seconds of shaking: human cleverness is small next to nature.
write: Paragraph 3 begins "Remarkable as it may seem." How does this phrase refine the central idea?
  lines: 2
  key: It signals a surprise and a turn. After two paragraphs of total destruction, London adds that the people met it calmly, so the central idea becomes not just that the city was destroyed but that its people kept their order and dignity while it happened.
write: Write the central idea of the excerpt in one or two sentences.
  lines: 3
  key: The earthquake and fire wiped out a great modern city and proved every human safeguard helpless against nature, yet the people met the disaster with calm and courtesy, and the city was already preparing to rebuild.

# 39. Six-Panel Storyboard First
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can plan the pacing of a narrative scene before I draft it.
directions: Choose a made-up scene from the list or invent your own. Sketch it in six panels (stick figures are fine), then mark each panel FAST, SLOW or PAUSE and note how you will write it. Draft only after the storyboard is done.
mistakes: Students often mark every panel SLOW, or draw six panels that each cover a different day. A scene happens in one place over a short time; the storyboard should show one moment stretched or squeezed. Save SLOW for the most important moment (usually panel 4 or 5) and use FAST to move through set-up.
notes: Pacing tools for the notes line: FAST uses summary, short sentences and quick actions; SLOW uses sensory detail, small movements and a character's thoughts (slow motion); PAUSE stops the action for a memory, a reflection or a single line of dialogue. The model key uses scene A.
