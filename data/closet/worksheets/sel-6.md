# 1. Conflict or Not?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell when a situation is a conflict and name what kind of conflict it is.
directions: Read each scenario. Write yes if it is a conflict and no if it is not. For each conflict, write its type: resources, values or misunderstanding. If it is not a conflict, write none.
mistakes: Students call any problem a conflict. A flat tire or a nervous feeling is a problem, but a conflict needs people whose needs, wants or ideas clash. Watch scenario 4: two people wanting the same thing is not a conflict once they have agreed.
notes: Definitions used here. **Conflict:** a clash between people over needs, wants or ideas. **Resources:** both sides want something limited (space, time, money, things). **Values:** the sides disagree about what is right or most important. **Misunderstanding:** one side got the wrong idea about what the other meant or did. Some real conflicts mix types; if a student argues a second type with evidence, give credit.

## Warm up
example: Two friends both want the last seat on the bus by the window. Conflict? **yes**. Type: **resources** (one seat, two people).

## Main: Sort the scenarios
table: # | Scenario | Conflict? | Type
  - 1 | Ava and Jun both want the one free library computer for the whole period. | {yes} | {resources       }
  - 2 | Leo's bike gets a flat tire on the way to school. | {no } | {none            }
  - 3 | Sam thinks Tia ignored him in the hall on purpose, but Tia never saw him. | {yes} | {misunderstanding}
  - 4 | Rina and Cole both want the last slice of pizza, so they happily agree to split it. | {no } | {none            }
  - 5 | The chess club and the drama club both want the gym on Thursday afternoon. | {yes} | {resources       }
  - 6 | Maya thinks every player should get equal time in games; Dev thinks the best players should play the most. | {yes} | {values          }
  - 7 | Eli forgets his lunch at home. | {no } | {none            }
  - 8 | Nora texts "fine." Jada reads it as angry, but Nora was just busy. | {yes} | {misunderstanding}
  - 9 | Kim feels nervous before her piano recital. | {no } | {none            }
  - 10 | Lucas thinks the class should tell the teacher who broke a window; Ana thinks they should stay quiet to protect a friend. | {yes} | {values          }

## Stretch
write: Choose one conflict from the table. What is one good first step to resolve it?
  lines: 3
  starter: In scenario ... a good first step would be ...
  key: Answers vary. For example: In scenario 8, Jada could ask Nora, "Are you upset with me?" before reacting. In scenario 5, the clubs could ask the office to split the time or take turns by week.

# 7. The Group Project Topic Fight
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can find the causes of a conflict and suggest solutions that work for everyone.
directions: Read the story. Highlight the moments that made the conflict worse in one color, or underline them, and the moments that helped in another way (a box or a second color). Then answer the six questions.
mistakes: Students often name the topic itself as the cause ("they wanted different inventions"). Different ideas are normal; the conflict grew because of how the group handled them: Zoe started without asking, Marco insulted her idea, and Aisha's idea was never heard. Watch for students who blame one character for everything; each person added to the problem and each could help fix it.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. Conflict styles used in this unit: avoid, accommodate, compete, compromise, collaborate. Zoe and Marco both compete at first, Aisha avoids, and Ben's suggestion moves the group toward collaborating. The final topic is a true win-win because it includes everyone's idea rather than splitting the difference.

## Read
passage: The Topic Fight | numbered
  Ms. Rivera gave each group one week to choose an invention that changed the world and make a presentation about it. Zoe, Marco, Aisha and Ben sat down together on Monday.
  "I already started on the printing press," Zoe said, pulling out three pages of notes. "I worked on it all weekend."
  Marco frowned. "You picked without us? And the printing press is boring. Nobody cares about old books. Let's do video games."
  "Video games didn't change the world," Zoe snapped. "They're just for fun." Her voice got louder. "At least I actually did some work."
  Aisha opened her mouth to say she had an idea about the telephone, then closed it again. She stared at her notebook. Ben looked at the clock. They had already wasted twenty minutes, and the topic was due Wednesday.
  On Tuesday, nobody sat together. Zoe told a friend in another group that Marco was lazy. Marco told Ben that Zoe was bossy.
  Finally, Ben spoke up at lunch. "This is getting us nowhere. What if each of us says why we like our idea, and nobody interrupts?" Zoe said the printing press let ideas spread fast for the first time. Marco said video games connect players around the world. Then Ben turned to Aisha. "You haven't said anything yet." Quietly, she explained that the telephone let people talk across long distances instantly.
  Marco blinked. "Wait. All of those are about how people share ideas and stay connected." Zoe slowly smiled. "We could do a timeline: printing press, telephone, internet and games. Each of us takes one part." By Wednesday morning, they turned in their topic: "Inventions That Connected the World."

## Questions
write: Name two actions that started or worsened the conflict. Give the paragraph number for each.
  lines: 3
  key: Any two: Zoe chose the topic and started without asking the group (2); Marco called her idea boring (3); Zoe said video games were "just for fun" and that she "actually did some work" (4); Zoe and Marco called each other names to other people (6).
choice: Which conflict style did Aisha use in paragraph 5?
  - Compete
  * Avoid
  - Compromise
  - Collaborate
write: What did Aisha's silence cost the group? Why does it matter in a conflict when one person stays quiet?
  lines: 3
  key: Her telephone idea went unheard, and it turned out to be the link that brought the group together. When someone stays quiet, the group misses ideas and the person may feel left out, so the solution does not work for everyone.
choice: What was the turning point of the conflict?
  - Zoe showed her notes from the weekend.
  - The group stopped sitting together.
  - Ms. Rivera picked a topic for them.
  * Ben asked each person to explain their idea without interruptions.
choice: Why is the final topic a win-win rather than a compromise?
  * It includes every member's idea instead of each person giving something up.
  - Zoe got her topic and everyone else gave in.
  - They split the difference by choosing a fifth invention.
  - The teacher was happy with it.
write: Paragraph 6 shows the conflict spreading to other people. What should Zoe and Marco do now to repair the damage?
  lines: 3
  key: Each should apologize to the other for the name-calling and set the record straight with the friends they talked to ("I was upset; Marco isn't lazy"). Next time they should bring the problem to each other, not to other people.

## Stretch
write: Rewrite Marco's line in paragraph 3 so that it shares his concern without starting a fight.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "I wish we had picked together, but thanks for starting. I was hoping we could do something more modern, like video games. Could we hear everyone's ideas before we decide?"

# 20. Conflict Resolution Review
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can analyze a conflict, name the styles people use, and plan an I-statement, a win-win solution and a real apology.
directions: Read the scenario. Then complete each step: name each character's conflict style, write an I-statement, plan a win-win solution, and write an apology.
mistakes: Students often write a "you" sentence dressed up as an I-statement ("I feel that you are selfish"). An I-statement names the speaker's own feeling, the specific action and its effect. For the win-win, watch for plans where one side simply gives in; check that the plan meets both needs. Apologies often skip the fix; a real apology says sorry, names what you did, and offers a repair.
notes: The scenario is original to Curio Class. Styles in the key: Jordan avoids at first (stops talking to Sam and complains to others), Sam competes ("I need them more"), and Ms. Okafor's request moves them toward collaborating. Several win-win plans work; the key gives one. Students can be graded on a 2-point scale per step: complete and fits the scenario (2), partly (1), missing (0), for 8 points total.

## Read
passage: The Mural Brushes
  Jordan and Sam were chosen to paint the school's hallway mural together. The art teacher, Ms. Okafor, gave them one shared box of supplies, including three good fine-tipped brushes for detail work.
  After the first week, Jordan noticed that the fine brushes were missing every morning. Sam had been taking them home each night to practice his lettering. When Jordan wanted to paint the small flowers in her section, she had to use a thick, worn brush, and the petals came out blotchy.
  Jordan didn't say anything to Sam. Instead, she started painting on the opposite end of the wall and stopped eating lunch with him. She told two friends, "Sam is hogging everything. He thinks the mural is all his."
  On Thursday, one of the friends repeated this to Sam. Sam found Jordan in the hallway. "Why are you talking about me behind my back?" he said. "I need those brushes more than you do. My lettering is the hardest part of the whole mural!"
  Jordan crossed her arms. "Fine. Keep them. I'll just paint the big shapes."
  Ms. Okafor overheard. "The mural is due in two weeks, and it has to look like one piece," she said. "I need you two to figure this out together by Monday."

## Step 1: Name the styles
table: Character | Style (avoid, accommodate, compete, compromise, collaborate) | Evidence from the scenario
  height: 0.6
  - Jordan, at first | {avoid} | {said nothing to Sam, moved away, complained to friends}
  - Sam, in the hallway | {compete} | {"I need those brushes more than you do"}
  - Jordan, "Fine. Keep them." | {accommodate} | {gives in, but is still upset (crossed arms)}

## Step 2: I-statement
write: Write an I-statement Jordan could say to Sam: I feel ... when ... because ... I need ...
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: I feel frustrated when the fine brushes go home with you every night, because my flowers come out blotchy with the worn brush. I need a fair way for us both to use them.

## Step 3: Win-win plan
chart: Jordan's need | Sam's need | Our plan that meets both
  rows: 1
  height: 1.2
  key: Jordan needs fine brushes during class for her flowers. Sam needs to practice lettering at home. Plan: the fine brushes stay at school during mural time, one is set aside for each of them, and Sam practices at home with a borrowed or inexpensive practice brush, or they use a sign-out sheet so the third brush can go home on alternate nights.

## Step 4: Apologies
write: Write the apology Jordan could give Sam for talking behind his back. Include sorry, what she did, and a fix.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "Sam, I'm sorry I told people you were hogging everything instead of talking to you. That wasn't fair. I'll tell them I was upset and that we're working it out."
write: Write the apology Sam could give Jordan.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "I'm sorry I took the fine brushes home every night without asking. I didn't think about your flowers. From now on they stay at school, and we'll use a sign-out sheet."

# 21. Five Steps to a Decision
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can use five steps to make a responsible decision.
directions: Read about Jordan's choice. Then use the five steps to help Jordan decide: define the decision, list the options, weigh the consequences, choose, and reflect.
mistakes: Students skip straight to "choose" and pick what they would want. Push them to use Jordan's facts. In the consequences chart, watch for only pros or only cons; each option should have both.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. There is no single right choice: either option can be a responsible decision if the reasons come from the facts and Jordan's goals. Grade the steps, not the pick. Step 5 asks how Jordan could check the choice later, so it is written as a plan.

## Read
passage: Club or Team?
  Jordan is in sixth grade at Lakeview Middle School. This fall, two after-school activities meet at the same time: the robotics club and the cross-country team. Both meet on Tuesdays and Thursdays from 3:15 to 4:45. Jordan can only pick one.
  Jordan has wanted to join robotics since fourth grade, after watching a robot competition at the library. The club builds a robot for a regional contest in February. Jordan's best friend, Sam, already signed up. The club costs 20 dollars for parts, and Jordan has saved 15 dollars from helping a neighbor rake leaves.
  Cross-country is free. The coach, Ms. Patel, told Jordan, "You have good endurance. I'd love to have you." Running helps Jordan feel calm when school gets stressful, and Jordan likes being outside after sitting in class all day. The team has meets on some Saturdays, which is when Jordan usually visits a grandparent.
  Jordan also has a math grade that slipped to a C last quarter and wants to bring it up. Both activities end early enough to leave time for homework, but cross-country meets can run long.
  Sign-ups close on Friday. Jordan has made a list in a notebook twice, crossed it out both times, and still feels stuck.

## Step 1: Define
write: What decision does Jordan need to make, and by when?
  lines: 2
  key: Jordan must choose either the robotics club or the cross-country team, because they meet at the same time, and must decide by Friday.

## Step 2: Options
fill:
  - Option A: {the robotics club}
  - Option B: {the cross-country team}

## Step 3: Consequences
table: Option | Possible good results | Possible problems
  - Robotics club | {follows a long-time interest; time with Sam; build for a February contest} | {costs 20 dollars and Jordan has 15; less time outside and moving}
  - Cross-country | {free; coach wants Jordan; running helps Jordan feel calm; time outside} | {Saturday meets clash with grandparent visits; meets can run long}

## Step 4: Choose
write: Which option should Jordan choose? Give two reasons from the facts.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: Robotics, because Jordan has wanted it for years and only needs 5 more dollars, which could come from one more job; Jordan could still run on weekends for calm. Or: Cross-country, because it is free, helps Jordan handle stress and the coach values Jordan; Saturday visits could move to Sunday.

## Step 5: Reflect
write: In December, how could Jordan check whether this was a good decision? Name two things to look at.
  lines: 3
  key: Jordan could check whether they enjoy the activity and look forward to it, whether the math grade is improving, whether stress is lower, and whether the money or Saturday plans worked out.

## Stretch
write: Jordan's friend says, "Just pick robotics because I'm in it." Is that a responsible reason on its own? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Not on its own. A friend is a fair reason to weigh, but a responsible decision looks at all the consequences: cost, Jordan's interests, stress, schoolwork and time.

# 33. How Students Decide: Survey Graph
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can read survey data about how students make decisions and write conclusions supported by evidence.
directions: Read the bar graph from a made-up survey of 100 sixth graders who were asked, "When you have a big choice to make, what do you usually do first?" Answer the questions, then write three conclusions with evidence from the graph.
mistakes: Students often read the bar number (1 to 5) instead of the height. Others write conclusions the graph cannot support, such as "asking a friend is the best way to decide"; the survey shows what students do, not what works best. Each conclusion needs a number from the graph as evidence.
notes: The survey and its numbers are made up for practice. Counts: ask a friend 28, ask a trusted adult 24, go with my gut 26, make a pros and cons list 14, search online 8; total 100, so each count is also a percent. Connect to the five-step decision model from sheet 21: only 14 students start with a plan that weighs options.

## Stretch

  lines: 2
  key: Sample: "Think of a big choice you made this year. Which steps did you use, and how happy are you now with the choice?"
