# 1. Neighborhood Food Access Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can map food access in a community and propose ways to improve it.
directions: Study the map of pretend Riverside. Color the grocery store, corner stores and bus stops using the key. Measure each home's walking distance to the grocery store, mark the low-access homes, and propose two fixes.
mistakes: Students often count diagonal steps. People walk along streets, so count squares across plus squares up or down. Watch Park Row: it is exactly 1 mile away, and the rule is more than 1 mile, so it is not low access. Some students count the corner store as a grocery; corner stores usually carry few fresh foods and charge more.
notes: Riverside is made up for Curio Class. The low-access rule follows the USDA Food Access Research Atlas: in a city, a home more than 1 mile from a supermarket or large grocery store is low access (10 miles in rural areas). Real fixes communities use: new bus routes or stops, mobile markets, healthy corner store programs that stock fresh produce, farmers markets that accept SNAP, community gardens, grocery delivery. Avoid blaming people for where they shop; the point is how a neighborhood is built.

## Propose fixes
write: Fix 1: Describe a change, mark it on the map, and explain which homes it helps.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: add a bus route along row H and up column 2 with a stop near the grocery store, so River Court can ride to G. Any fix tied to specific homes and the map earns credit.
write: Fix 2: Describe a different kind of change and explain who it helps.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: a healthy corner store program that stocks fresh fruit and vegetables at the corner stores in E6 and G8, helping Elm Street Homes and River Court; or a weekly mobile market at River Court.

## Stretch
write: River Court has the worst access. Which of your fixes would help it most, and what might stop the fix from working?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: a mobile market helps most because it comes to River Court, but prices, hours or funding could limit it. Strong answers name a real barrier (cost, schedule, funding, safety).

# 7. Rebalance Sam's Week
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can revise a week of eating, sleep and activity to meet health guidelines and explain each change.
directions: Read Sam's week and the guidelines. Do the math to see how far Sam is from each guideline. Then plan realistic changes and explain why each one helps.
mistakes: Students often rewrite Sam's whole life at once ("run 2 hours a day, never eat fast food"). Strong revisions are specific and realistic, change one habit at a time, and keep things Sam enjoys. Watch for the sleep math: the guideline is a range, so use 8 hours as the minimum when finding the gap.
notes: Sam is a character made up for Curio Class. Guidelines: sleep 8 to 10 hours a night for ages 13 to 18 (American Academy of Sleep Medicine); at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity every day for ages 6 to 17, with muscle and bone strengthening at least 3 days a week (Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans); added sugars under 10% of daily calories, which is 50 grams on a 2,000-calorie day, and half of each plate fruits and vegetables (Dietary Guidelines for Americans, MyPlate). A 12-ounce regular cola has about 39 grams of added sugar. The 2,000-calorie figure is a reference on food labels, not a target for any person; keep the talk on Sam, not on students' own bodies or weight.

## Warm up: Sam's week
text: Sam is 17. **Sleep:** goes to bed at 1:00 a.m. and wakes at 7:00 a.m. on school nights; sleeps until noon on weekends. **Activity:** walks 20 minutes with a friend twice a week; plays video games about 4 hours a day. **Eating:** skips breakfast; lunch is chips and a soda from the vending machine; dinner is fast food (a burger and fries) most nights; eats fruit about once a week and few vegetables. **Drinks:** about 3 cans of regular soda a day (12 ounces, about 39 grams of added sugar each).
text: **Guidelines for teens:** Sleep 8 to 10 hours a night. Move at least 60 minutes every day, with muscle and bone strengthening at least 3 days a week. Keep added sugars under 50 grams a day (on a 2,000-calorie day). Fill half of each plate with fruits and vegetables.

## Main: Find the gaps
fill:
  - On school nights Sam sleeps {6} hours, which is {2} hours short of the 8-hour minimum each night.
  - Over 5 school nights, Sam misses at least {10} hours of sleep.
  - Sam is active {40} minutes a week. The guideline is {420} minutes a week, so Sam is {380} minutes short.
  - Sam's 3 sodas have about {117} grams of added sugar, which is {67} grams over the 50-gram limit.

## Main: Revise the week
table: Area | What Sam does now | One realistic change | Why it helps
  height: 0.75
  - Sleep | 1:00 a.m. to 7:00 a.m. | |
  - Activity | 40 minutes a week | |
  - Breakfast | skips it | |
  - Lunch and drinks | chips and soda; 3 sodas a day | |
  - Dinner | fast food most nights | |
note: Model changes: Sleep: lights out by 11:00 p.m. with screens off at 10:30 (8 hours), because sleep helps memory, mood and focus. Activity: walk or bike 30 minutes every day and join a pickup game or the gym 3 days a week with strength moves, building up over a few weeks; this meets the 60-minute goal and builds muscle and bone. Breakfast: a quick option like a banana and peanut butter toast or yogurt, for energy and focus in the morning. Lunch and drinks: pack a sandwich and fruit; swap sodas for water, keeping at most one soda a day (39 grams), under the 50-gram limit. Dinner: cook at home or choose grilled options with a vegetable side most nights, so half the plate is fruits and vegetables.

## Think about it
choice: Sam wants to cut sugar but loves soda. Which plan is most likely to last?
  - Never drink soda again starting today
  * Cut to one soda a day this month, with water or sparkling water the rest of the time
  - Switch to sports drinks
choice: Sam says, "I'll catch up on sleep on the weekend." What is true?
  - Weekend sleep fully makes up for the week
  - Sleeping until noon has no effect on Monday
  * Sleeping in helps a little but shifts the body clock, making it harder to fall asleep Sunday night
write: Which of Sam's changes do you think will help the most, and why? Connect it to at least one other area.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Sleep. With 8 hours, Sam will have more energy to be active and is less likely to crave sugary drinks and snacks to stay awake, so sleep supports the activity and eating changes too.

## Stretch
write: Write a 4-week plan that adds Sam's changes a little at a time. What comes first, and why?
  lines: 4
  key: Example: Week 1: screens off at 10:30 and lights out by 11:00. Week 2: cut to two sodas, then one, and eat breakfast. Week 3: walk 30 minutes a day. Week 4: add strength work 3 days a week and a vegetable with dinner. Sleep first, because more energy makes the other changes easier.

# 20. Two Studies, One Question
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can compare two health studies and explain which gives stronger evidence and why.
directions: Read the two pretend study summaries. Both ask the same question. Fill in the comparison chart, then decide which study gives stronger evidence and explain why.
mistakes: Students often pick the study with the more exciting result or the bigger percentage. Steer them to design: random assignment, a control group, sample size, how the outcome was measured, and who paid. Watch for "correlation means cause": Study A shows breakfast eaters scored higher, but something else (like more sleep or more support at home) could explain both.
notes: Both studies are made up for Curio Class to teach how to judge evidence; they are not real research and should not be cited as findings. In real research on school breakfast, results have been mixed: many studies find better attendance and fewer hunger-related problems, and effects on test scores vary. Key ideas: a randomized controlled trial (RCT) can show cause; an observational study shows a link; larger, more varied samples generalize better; self-reports are less reliable than records; funding by a company that profits from the result is a possible conflict of interest; peer review and replication add trust.

## Warm up: Words to know
match: Match each term to its meaning.
  - Control group = The group that does not get the program, for comparison
  - Random assignment = Using chance to decide who gets the program
  - Sample size = How many people are in the study
  - Conflict of interest = The funder or author could gain from a certain result
  - Self-report = People describe their own behavior, which may not be accurate

## Read: Two pretend studies
source: Pretend Study A, summary (made up for this sheet)
  Question: Does eating breakfast improve teens' test scores? Researchers gave a survey to 40 students at one high school. Students wrote down whether they "usually eat breakfast." Breakfast eaters had average test scores 12 points higher than students who skipped breakfast. The study was paid for by a cereal company and posted on the company's website. Conclusion: "Breakfast boosts test scores!"
source: Pretend Study B, summary (made up for this sheet)
  Question: Does eating breakfast improve teens' test scores? Researchers worked with 1,200 students at 12 high schools in cities and rural towns. Schools were randomly assigned to start a free breakfast program or keep things the same for one school year. Researchers used school records for test scores and attendance. Scores rose by 2 points more in breakfast schools than in the others, a small difference, and attendance improved. The study was paid for by a government research grant and published in a peer-reviewed journal. Conclusion: "Breakfast programs may slightly improve scores and improve attendance."

## Main: Compare the studies
table: Feature | Study A | Study B
  - Number of students | {40} | {1,200}
  - Number of schools | {1} | {12}
  - How the groups were chosen | {students chose for themselves (observational)} | {schools randomly assigned}
  - Control group? | {no true control group} | {yes, schools without the program}
  - How the data were collected | {self-report survey about breakfast} | {school records for scores and attendance}
  - Who paid for it | {a cereal company} | {a government research grant}
  - Where it was published | {the company's website} | {a peer-reviewed journal}
  - Result | {12 points higher} | {2 points higher, plus better attendance}

## Decide
choice: Which study gives stronger evidence that breakfast causes higher scores?
  - Study A, because its result is bigger
  * Study B, because it used random assignment, a large sample and school records
  - Neither, because they disagree
choice: Why might Study A's 12-point difference not be caused by breakfast?
  * Students who eat breakfast may also sleep more or have other advantages that raise scores
  - Breakfast has no effect on the brain
  - 40 students is too many to study
write: Explain which study is stronger and why, in 3 to 4 sentences. Use at least three features from the chart.
  lines: 4
  key: Study B is stronger. It randomly assigned 12 schools and 1,200 students, so the groups were alike except for the breakfast program, which lets it test cause. It used school records instead of self-reports, and it was funded by a government grant and peer reviewed. Study A used only 40 self-reporting students at one school and was paid for by a company that sells cereal, so its bigger result is less trustworthy.

## Stretch
write: Study B's result was small. Does that mean breakfast programs are not worth it? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Not necessarily. A small, real effect across many students can still matter, and attendance also improved. Other benefits, like less hunger, are not measured by test scores. More studies in other places would help confirm it.

# 21. Spotting Graph Spin
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can find the misleading features in health graphs and redraw a graph honestly.
directions: Read the five graph descriptions (your teacher may sketch them on the board). Match each graph to its spin. Then explain what an honest reader should conclude, and redraw Graph 5 honestly on the grid.
mistakes: Students often accept a dramatic picture without reading the axis labels. Always read the starting value of the y-axis and the spacing of the x-axis first. For Graph 5, the evenly spaced years squeeze 10-year gaps and 1-year gaps into the same width, so the line looks like it flattens. For the hand sanitizer graph, watch for "the line went down, so it worked": flu cases fall every spring.
notes: All graphs and data are made up for Curio Class. If you can, sketch each graph on the board as described so students see the spin. For the redraw, the key shows the honest line with years spaced to scale and the y-axis starting at 0; the recent rise is as steep as or steeper than the early one, so helmet use is not leveling off.

## Read: Five graphs
text: **Graph 1.** A bar graph for a made-up energy drink shows a "tiredness score" of 52 before and 48 after. The y-axis starts at 46, so the "after" bar looks less than half as tall.
text: **Graph 2.** A line graph shows flu cases in Pretend County from February to April only, falling fast. The title says, "Cases dropped after our hand sanitizer launched in February!"
text: **Graph 3.** A bar graph titled "Sports injuries DOUBLED!" shows 2 injuries at one school last year and 4 this year.
text: **Graph 4.** A picture graph shows one water bottle for Town A and a bottle twice as tall and twice as wide for Town B, which drinks twice as much water.
text: **Graph 5.** A line graph shows the percent of kids in Pretend City who wear bike helmets: 40% in 2000, 45% in 2010, 50% in 2020, 52% in 2022 and 54% in 2023. The five years are spaced evenly across the x-axis, and the title says, "Helmet use is leveling off. Time to end the helmet program."

## Main: Find the spin
match: Match each graph to its misleading feature.
  - Graph 1 = The y-axis does not start at zero, so a small difference looks huge
  - Graph 2 = Cherry-picked dates that leave out the normal seasonal pattern
  - Graph 3 = Tiny numbers make a change sound dramatic
  - Graph 4 = A picture grown in two directions looks four times bigger
  - Graph 5 = Uneven time gaps spaced evenly make recent change look slower than it is
write: Graph 1: How big was the real change in the tiredness score? What should an honest reader conclude?
  lines: 2
  key: 52 to 48 is a drop of 4 points, about 8%. The drink made a small difference at most, and we don't know who measured the score or how.
write: Graph 2: What other information would you need before believing the sanitizer worked?
  lines: 2
  key: Data from earlier years showing flu always falls in spring, and a comparison with a similar place without the sanitizer. Flu cases drop every spring, so the timing alone proves nothing.
write: Graph 3: Why is "DOUBLED!" misleading here?
  lines: 2
  key: Going from 2 to 4 injuries at one school is a change of only 2 injuries, which could easily be chance; with numbers this small, "doubled" sounds bigger than it is.

  key: 2000,40 2010,45 2020,50 2022,52 2023,54

## Stretch
write: On your honest graph, how does the change from 2020 to 2023 compare with the change from 2000 to 2010? What does the honest graph show?
  lines: 3
  key: 2000 to 2010 rose 5 points in 10 years (0.5 per year); 2020 to 2023 rose 4 points in 3 years (about 1.3 per year). So helmet use is rising faster lately. The evenly spaced graph made it look like it was leveling off; the honest graph shows it is still climbing, so the claim to end the program is not supported.

# 33. Opioids and the Body
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how opioids act on the brain and breathing and recognize the signs of an overdose.
directions: Read how opioids affect the body. Match each part of the body to what opioids do there and label the diagram. Then mark which signs point to an overdose and plan the response.
mistakes: Students often think an overdose looks like someone falling asleep normally. Snoring or gurgling sounds and being unable to wake up are danger signs, not rest. Watch for "let them sleep it off" and "put them in a cold shower": call 911, give naloxone if available, and keep them breathing. Some students believe naloxone is only for people who use drugs on purpose; it helps anyone with an opioid overdose, including an accidental one, and it does no harm to someone who has not taken opioids.
notes: Facts follow the CDC, NIDA and SAMHSA. Opioids attach to opioid receptors in the brain, spinal cord and gut; in the brainstem they slow breathing, which is what makes an overdose deadly. Fentanyl is about 50 times stronger than heroin and 100 times stronger than morphine (CDC), and it is found in many fake pills that look like real prescription pills. Signs of overdose: pinpoint pupils, cannot be woken or is limp, slow, weak or no breathing, choking or gurgling sounds, and blue, gray or pale lips and fingertips. Naloxone is sold over the counter as a nasal spray in the U.S. (FDA approval, 2023). Most states have Good Samaritan laws that give some legal protection to people who call 911 for an overdose; check your state's law. If a student shares a personal or family situation, follow up privately and offer the counselor.

## Read: How opioids work
text: Opioids include prescription pain medicines like oxycodone and morphine, and illegal drugs like heroin and illegally made fentanyl. They attach to **opioid receptors** in the brain and spinal cord. There they block pain signals and cause a rush of **dopamine**, which can bring a strong feeling of pleasure and drives people to use again. Opioids also act on the **brainstem**, the part of the brain that controls breathing. A large dose slows breathing so much that the body does not get enough oxygen. That is an **overdose**, and it can cause brain damage or death within minutes.

## Main: What happens where?
match: Match each part of the body to what opioids do there.
  - Brain's pain pathways and spinal cord = Pain signals are blocked
  - Brain's reward system = A rush of dopamine brings pleasure and drives repeated use
  - Brainstem = Breathing slows; too much can stop it
  - Lungs and blood = Less oxygen reaches the brain and organs
  - Eyes = Pupils shrink to tiny points
  - Stomach and intestines = Digestion slows, causing nausea and constipation
draw: Draw a simple outline of a head and upper body. Label the brain, brainstem, spinal cord, lungs and eyes, and write one effect next to each. | 2.6
note: Check placement: the brainstem sits at the base of the brain, connecting it to the spinal cord at the back of the neck; the lungs fill the chest on both sides. Effects as in the matching key.

## Main: Is it an overdose sign?
table: Sign | Overdose sign? (yes or no)
  - Tiny, pinpoint pupils | {yes}
  - Slow, weak or stopped breathing | {yes}
  - Cannot be woken up, even when shouted at | {yes}
  - Talking quickly and laughing | {no}
  - Gurgling or deep snoring sounds | {yes}
  - Blue, gray or pale lips and fingertips | {yes}
  - Red face and sweating after exercise | {no}
  - Body is limp | {yes}

## Respond
text: **The steps:** call 911, give naloxone if it is available, try to keep the person breathing, lay them on their side if they are breathing, and stay until help arrives.
choice: You find a friend who will not wake up and is making gurgling sounds. What should you do first?
  - Let them sleep it off
  - Put them in a cold shower
  * Call 911 and give naloxone if there is some
choice: Why are fake pills bought from friends or online so dangerous?
  - They are always weaker than real pills
  * They can contain fentanyl, which is so strong that a tiny amount can cause an overdose
  - They only cause stomachaches

## Stretch
write: Why might a person who stopped using opioids for a while be at higher risk of overdose if they use again?
  lines: 3
  key: Their tolerance drops while they are not using. If they take the same amount they used before, it is now too much for their body, and breathing can slow or stop.
