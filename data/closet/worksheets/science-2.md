# 1. Material Detectives
minutes: 30
materials: for each group: a metal spoon, a kitchen sponge, a rock, a paper clip, a pipe cleaner, a glass marble, a satin ribbon, a wooden block; pencil
ican: I can test and describe materials by their properties.
directions: Do three tests on each object. Squeeze it: hard or soft? Try to bend it gently: bendy or stiff? Rub it with a finger: rough or smooth? Put an X in one box of each pair.
mistakes: Students may call the paper clip stiff because it is hard. Hard and bendy can go together: a paper clip does not squish, but it bends. Remind them to bend gently and never force an object that does not bend.
notes: The key shows typical results for common versions of these objects. A rough rock or a smooth river stone, or a sponge with a smooth side, can change the answer: accept any result that matches the object the group tested. Collect the marbles at the end.

  - Something that changes shape when you gently bend it is {bendy}.
  - Something that feels bumpy or scratchy is {rough}.

# 5. Which Paper Holds the Most?
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can read a bar graph to find which material works best.
directions: (Read aloud.) A class made 4 bridges, each from a different kind of paper. They added pennies one at a time until each bridge bent. Read the bar graph. Answer the questions.
mistakes: Some students read the bridge number (1 to 4) as the number of pennies. Show them how to slide a finger from the top of a bar across to the numbers on the side. On "how many more," students may add (6 + 20) instead of finding the difference.
notes: This is one class's made-up but realistic data; your own bridges will hold different numbers depending on the paper and the gap. To run the test: lay each paper strip across a 15 cm gap between two stacks of books, and set pennies in the middle one at a time. Thicker, stiffer paper holds more. The bars go up by twos, so each line on the side is 2 pennies.

## Warm up: Read the graph
text: **Bridge 1** tissue paper. **Bridge 2** notebook paper. **Bridge 3** construction paper. **Bridge 4** cardstock (thin cardboard).
graph: Bridge number | 0 5 1 | Pennies held before bending | 0 24 2
  minor: off
  line: 0.7,0 0.7,2 1.3,2 1.3,0 1.7,0 1.7,6 2.3,6 2.3,0 2.7,0 2.7,12 3.3,12 3.3,0 3.7,0 3.7,20 4.3,20 4.3,0
fill:
  - The tissue paper bridge held {2} pennies.
  - The construction paper bridge held {12} pennies.

## Main: Answer the questions
choice: Which paper held the most pennies?
  - tissue paper
  - notebook paper
  - construction paper
  * cardstock
choice: Which paper held the fewest pennies?
  * tissue paper
  - notebook paper
  - cardstock
fill:
  - How many more pennies did cardstock hold than notebook paper? {14} more
choice: Look at the 4 bars. What pattern do you see?
  - Thin papers held the most pennies.
  * Thicker, stiffer papers held more pennies.
  - All the papers held the same number.
write: You want to build a bridge for a toy car. Which paper would you pick? Tell why, using the graph.
  lines: 2
  key: Cardstock, because its bar is the tallest: it held 20 pennies, the most of any paper.

## Stretch: Make it stronger
write: A student folds the notebook paper back and forth like a fan before making the bridge. Do you think it will hold more or fewer pennies? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: More. Folding makes the paper stiffer, so it bends less. (Folded paper bridges really do hold many more pennies.)

# 16. Fair Test for Beans
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons; for the class test: 4 clear cups with the same soil, 8 bean seeds, water, a sunny window, a dark cupboard, a measuring spoon, masking tape for labels
ican: I can plan a fair test to find out if plants need water and light.
directions: (Read aloud.) We will plant beans in 4 cups. Each cup gets something different. Plan the test, then draw what you think each cup will look like in 2 weeks.
mistakes: Many students predict that a bean in the dark cannot sprout at all. Beans sprout using food stored in the seed, so the dark cup often sprouts and grows tall, thin and pale yellow; it is the plant that needs light, not the sprouting seed. Some also think the dry cup will grow a little: with no water at all, a bean will not sprout.
notes: Plant 2 beans per cup, about 2 cm deep. Cups A and B get 2 spoonfuls of water every other day; C and D get none. A and C sit in the sunny window; B and D go in a dark cupboard (check it is not near a heater). Typical result after 2 weeks: A, a green plant with leaves; B, a tall, thin, pale yellow plant that soon droops; C and D, no sprouts. The key shows typical results; accept any prediction with a reason. Use dried pinto or lima beans from the store; remind students not to eat seeds used in class.

## Warm up: What does a plant need?
choice: What will be different from cup to cup?
  - the kind of seed
  * the water and the light
  - the size of the cup
fill:
  - To be fair, every cup gets the same kind of {soil}.
  - To be fair, every cup gets the same kind of {seed}.

## Main: Plan the 4 cups
table: Cup | Water? | Light? | Where it goes
  - A | yes | yes | {sunny window}
  - B | yes | no | {dark cupboard}
  - C | no | yes | {sunny window}
  - D | no | no | {dark cupboard}
text: Draw what you think each cup will look like in 2 weeks.
table: Cup A: water and light | Cup B: water only | Cup C: light only | Cup D: neither
  height: 1.6
  - | | |
note: Typical results: A, a green plant with leaves; B, a tall, thin, pale yellow plant; C, no sprout (the bean stays dry); D, no sprout.
choice: Which cup do you think will grow the best plant?
  * Cup A
  - Cup C
  - Cup D
write: Which cup do you think will not sprout at all? Tell why.
  lines: 2
  key: Cups C and D, because they get no water, and a seed needs water to sprout. Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Stretch: Test it
write: Why do we put 2 beans in each cup instead of just 1?
  lines: 2
  key: In case one bean does not sprout. Two beans make the test fairer, so one bad seed does not change the result.

# 22. Plant Needs Check-In
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell what plants need to grow and how seeds and bees help plants.
directions: Circle what a plant needs. Match each seed to how it travels. Then explain a bee's job.
mistakes: Some students say plants need food from us, like people do. Plants need water and light to grow, and they make their own food. Students also think bees visit flowers to help them: bees visit to get food, and moving pollen happens as they go.
notes: Read the picture words aloud for students who need it. Plants also need air: accept it if a student adds it. The four seeds are real: dandelion seeds have fluffy parachutes, burdock burrs have tiny hooks, coconuts float, and squirrels bury acorns and forget some.

## Warm up: What does a plant need?
picword:
  - sun | sunlight
  - faucet | water
  - ball | a ball
  - teddy-bear | a teddy bear
  - sock | a sock
  - book | a book
write: Circle the two pictures above that show what a plant needs to grow.
  lines: 0
  key: Circle sunlight and water.

## Main: Seeds on the move
match: Match each seed to how it travels.
  - dandelion seed with a fluffy top = The wind blows it far away.
  - burr with tiny hooks = It sticks to an animal's fur.
  - coconut = It floats across the water.
  - acorn = A squirrel buries it and forgets it.
choice: Why is it good for a seed to travel away from its parent plant?
  - It gets to see new places.
  * It gets more space, light and water to grow.
  - It can grow bigger seeds.

## Stretch: A bee's job
write: Explain a bee's job for a flower in one sentence. Use the word pollen.
  lines: 2
  starter: A bee helps a flower by ...
  key: A bee carries pollen from one flower to another, which helps the plant make seeds.

# 26. One Tree, Many Neighbors
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell how one plant can be a home for many kinds of living things.
pairs: habitat-hunt
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen as your teacher reads the story. Read along. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students may say the tree only gives a home. Push for food and water too: the flowers give nectar and the plants on the branches hold water. Some think bromeliads are part of the tree; they are separate plants that grow on its branches.
notes: Kapok trees grow in rainforests in Central and South America and in Africa. They rise above most of the other trees. Harpy eagles often nest in tall kapok trees, bats drink nectar from the flowers at night (and carry pollen as they go), and small frogs lay eggs in the water caught by bromeliads. Show a photo of a kapok tree if you can.

## Read
passage: The Kapok Tree
  High in the rainforest stands a kapok tree. It is taller than almost every tree around it. This one tree is a home for many living things.
  At the very top, a big eagle builds a nest of sticks. Monkeys run along the high branches.
  Small plants grow on the branches too. Rain fills their leaves like tiny cups. Little frogs lay their eggs in these pools.
  At night, bats visit the tree's flowers to drink sweet nectar. Ants march up and down the bark.
  One tree gives food, water and a safe home to many neighbors.

## Main: Answer the questions
choice: What is the story mostly about?
  - how to climb a tall tree
  * one tree that is a home for many living things
  - why bats fly at night
choice: Where does the eagle build its nest?
  - in the small plants
  - on the ground
  * at the top of the tree
choice: What do the bats get from the tree?
  * sweet nectar from the flowers
  - water from the leaves
  - a nest of sticks
write: Where do the little frogs lay their eggs?
  lines: 1
  key: In the pools of rainwater inside the leaves of the small plants on the branches.
write: Name two things the tree gives its neighbors.
  lines: 2
  key: Any two: food (nectar), water (in the small plants), a safe home or nest, a place to run and climb.

## Stretch: Draw it
draw: Draw the kapok tree. Draw and label 3 of its neighbors in the right places: top, branches, trunk. | 2.4

# 37. Bird's-Eye Landform Map
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayons (blue, green, brown); the class model landscape (sand or clay in a tray, or the one described below)
ican: I can draw a map that shows landforms and bodies of water from above.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look straight down at the model, like a bird flying over it. Make a symbol for each landform in the key. Then draw the map.
mistakes: Many students draw a side view, with the hill as a bump and the trees standing up. Have them stand and look straight down at the model: a hill from above is a closed shape, and a lake is a round blob. Watch that the river is drawn as a long, thin line that connects to the lake.
notes: Build a simple model in a tray: a clay or sand hill at one end, a blue paper lake, a blue yarn river running from the hill to the lake, and flat sand for the plain. If you have no model, read this one aloud: "A tall hill is in the top left corner. A river starts at the hill and winds down to a round lake in the bottom right corner. The rest of the land is a flat plain." A common map symbol for a hill is a set of rings, one inside the other. Any clear, consistent symbols are fine.

## Warm up: Bird's-eye view
choice: A bird's-eye view shows things as they look from ...
  - the side
  * straight above
  - under the ground
choice: From above, what shape would a round lake be?
  - a long, thin line
  - a pointy triangle
  * a round shape
fill:
  - A long, thin body of water that flows is a {river}.
  - Wide, flat land is a {plain}.

## Main: Make the key

  height: 0.7

  - lake |
  - river |
  - plain |
note: Typical symbols: hill, rings one inside the other or brown bumps; lake, a blue round shape; river, a blue wavy line; plain, green flat ground or short grass marks.

## Map it
draw: My bird's-eye map. Use the symbols from your key. | 3.4
note: A good map shows all four landforms from above, uses the key's symbols, and has the river connected to the lake.

## Stretch: Tell about it
write: Water flows downhill. Where on your map does the river start, and where does it go?
  lines: 2
  key: Typical: The river starts at the hill and flows down across the plain into the lake.
