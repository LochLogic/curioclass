# 1. Macronutrients for Performance
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how carbohydrates, protein and fat fuel different kinds of physical activity.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight the main fuel for each kind of activity. Then answer the six questions, using details from the passage.
mistakes: Students often believe protein is the main fuel for sports. Protein mostly builds and repairs; carbohydrate and fat supply nearly all the energy. Watch for "fat is bad for athletes": fat is the main fuel at rest and during easy, long activity.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 580 words). Facts follow mainstream sports nutrition guidance (Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, American Academy of Pediatrics): carbohydrate is stored as glycogen and powers high-intensity work; fat fuels rest and low-to-moderate activity; protein supports repair and is a minor fuel; most teens get enough protein from regular meals, and supplements are rarely needed. The sheet uses no calorie numbers by design; keep it on fuel and timing, not on any student's own eating or body.

## Read
passage: Three Athletes, Three Fuels
  It is a Saturday in spring, and three teammates are using their bodies in very different ways. Dani runs the 400-meter dash, Ezra runs the 5-kilometer cross-country race, and Mai is spending the morning lifting weights for track and field throwing events. All three run on the same three macronutrients: carbohydrates, fat and protein. But how their muscles use those fuels depends on how hard and how long they work.
  Muscles cannot use food directly. They run on a molecule called ATP, and they only keep a tiny amount on hand. For the first few seconds of an all-out burst, like Dani's start out of the blocks, muscles use a small quick-energy store. After that, they have to make more ATP from food fuels, and the harder the effort, the more they rely on carbohydrate.
  Carbohydrates from foods like rice, bread, pasta, fruit and potatoes are broken down into glucose. The body stores extra glucose as glycogen in the muscles and the liver. Glycogen is the fastest fuel to turn into energy, which is why it powers hard efforts like sprinting, jumping and the fast parts of a soccer game. Dani's 400 meters lasts under a minute, and nearly all of it is fueled by glycogen already sitting in her leg muscles. The catch is that glycogen stores are limited. During long, hard exercise they can run low, and an athlete may suddenly feel heavy and slow. Runners call this "hitting the wall."
  Fat is the body's largest energy store, by far. Even a lean athlete carries enough stored fat to fuel many hours of activity. Fat is the main fuel when the body is resting and during easy, steady activity like walking or a slow jog. But turning fat into energy takes more steps and more oxygen, so it is too slow to power an all-out sprint by itself. In Ezra's race, his body burns a mix: fat supplies a steady share of the energy while glycogen covers the harder parts, such as the hills and the final kick. Training helps endurance athletes burn more fat at a given pace, which saves glycogen for when they need it most.
  Protein, from foods like eggs, beans, fish, chicken, dairy and tofu, plays a different role. Its main job is building and repairing tissue, including the tiny tears that strength training causes in muscle fibers. Mai's workout does not burn much protein for energy; instead, the protein she eats over the next day helps her muscles rebuild stronger. Protein becomes a bigger fuel source only when glycogen is very low, which is one reason athletes are told not to skip meals.
  Timing matters too. A meal with carbohydrate a few hours before a long event helps fill glycogen stores. During activity that lasts more than about an hour, a sports drink or a small snack with carbohydrate can help. After hard exercise, a meal or snack with both carbohydrate and protein refills glycogen and supports muscle repair. Water matters through it all: even mild dehydration can make exercise feel harder.
  What about protein powders and supplements? Most teens, including athletes, already get enough protein from regular meals and snacks. Health experts recommend food first, and they suggest talking with a doctor or a registered dietitian before trying any supplement.
  Three athletes, three kinds of effort, and one lesson: no single macronutrient is the "best." Each has a job, and a balanced plate gives the body the right fuel at the right time.

# 7. Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can find food safety mistakes and name the step that prevents foodborne illness.
directions: Study the four steps. Read each kitchen scenario. In the chart, write the mistake and the food safety step that would prevent illness. Each step is used twice.
mistakes: Students often think rinsing raw chicken makes it cleaner. It does not kill germs and splashes them onto the sink and counters, so FoodSafety.gov says not to wash raw meat or poultry. Watch for "it looked done" in scenario 3: color is not a safe test; only a food thermometer shows a safe temperature.
notes: Facts follow FoodSafety.gov and the USDA: wash hands with soap for 20 seconds; refrigerator at 40 °F or below; poultry and reheated leftovers to 165 °F; ground beef to 160 °F; refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours (1 hour above 90 °F); thaw in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave, never on the counter. Scenario 6 could also be fixed by boiling the used marinade before using it on cooked food; accept Cook with that explanation. Characters are made up for Curio Class.

## Warm up: The four steps
match: Match each step to what it means.
  - Clean = Wash hands, surfaces and produce often
  - Separate = Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs away from ready-to-eat food
  - Cook = Heat food to a safe inside temperature, checked with a thermometer
  - Chill = Refrigerate food quickly and thaw it safely
fill:

  - Chicken is safe when it reaches {165} °F inside.

# 21. Quackery Red Flags
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use red flags to judge whether a health product claim is likely valid or quackery.
directions: Read the red-flag checklist. For each pretend product claim, write the numbers of any red flags you see, then mark it likely valid or quackery. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students sometimes mark every claim with a number or a doctor in it as valid. Look for evidence and limits: valid claims are specific, modest and match what trusted health agencies say. "Natural" is not the same as safe.
notes: The products are made up for Curio Class, though claims 2, 5 and 7 match real, mainstream guidance (American Academy of Dermatology on sunscreen; CDC on yearly flu vaccine for everyone 6 months and older; American Dental Association on fluoride toothpaste). Quackery means promoting health products or treatments that do not work or are unproven, usually for profit. The FTC and FDA warn about the same red flags.

## Warm up: The red-flag checklist
text: **1.** Cures many unrelated problems. **2.** Quick or guaranteed results. **3.** A "secret," "ancient" or "miracle" formula doctors don't want you to know. **4.** Only personal stories as proof, no studies. **5.** "All natural, so no side effects." **6.** Pressure to buy now: limited time, act fast.

## Main: Judge the claims
table: Pretend product claim | Red flags | Likely valid or quackery?
  - 1. Product A, pills: "Cures acne, anxiety and arthritis in 3 days. Guaranteed!" | {1, 2} | {quackery}
  - 2. Sunshield SPF 30: "Broad-spectrum sunscreen. Helps prevent sunburn when reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming." | {none} | {valid}
  - 3. Product C, foot pads: "The ancient secret that pulls toxins out through your feet overnight. Only 2 hours left at this price!" | {2, 3, 6} | {quackery}
  - 4. Product D, herbal tea: "100% natural herbs, so there are no side effects. Thousands of happy customers say it fixed their sleep." | {4, 5} | {quackery}
  - 5. Yearly Flu Vaccine: "Recommended every year for everyone 6 months and older to lower the risk of flu and serious illness." | {none} | {valid}
  - 6. Product F, a shake: "Grow 3 inches taller in 30 days! Doctors don't want you to know about this miracle." | {2, 3} | {quackery}
  - 7. Fluoride Toothpaste: "Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste helps prevent cavities." | {none} | {valid}
  - 8. Product H, a bracelet: "Magnetic energy cures headaches, back pain and the flu. Order in the next 10 minutes for a free second bracelet!" | {1, 6} | {quackery}

## Think about it
write: Claim 5 and claim 6 both mention doctors or experts in some way. Why is one valid and one quackery?
  lines: 3
  key: Claim 5 matches what public health agencies like the CDC recommend, makes a modest claim ("lower the risk") and has strong evidence. Claim 6 promises a quick, impossible result and says doctors are hiding a "miracle," with no evidence; height is set mainly by genes and growth plates.
write: Why is "all natural" (red flag 5) not proof that a product is safe?
  lines: 2
  key: Many natural substances can cause side effects, allergies or interactions with medicines; anything strong enough to change the body can have side effects. "Natural" is a marketing word, not evidence.

## Stretch
write: Pick one quackery claim. Rewrite it as an honest, valid claim, or explain why it cannot be made honest.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: claim 3 cannot be made honest because foot pads do not pull toxins from the body; the liver and kidneys do that. Or claim 4: "A warm, caffeine-free tea some people find relaxing at bedtime. Ask a doctor if you take medicines."

# 33. Nicotine Dependence Cycle
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain the nicotine dependence cycle and where withdrawal and cravings appear in it.
directions: Read about how nicotine works. Put the six stages of the cycle in order on the line. Then draw the cycle, mark where withdrawal (W) and cravings (C) appear, and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think a vape or cigarette relieves stress. What it mostly relieves is the withdrawal caused by the last dose, so the "relief" is part of the cycle. Watch for the idea that dependence takes years: teens can show signs of dependence after a short time, even with occasional use.
notes: Facts follow the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), the CDC and the U.S. Surgeon General. Nicotine reaches the brain within seconds of being inhaled and triggers the release of dopamine; its effects fade within a few hours. Withdrawal can include irritability, anxiety, trouble concentrating, restlessness and strong urges to use; it is usually worst in the first few days and eases over a few weeks, though cravings can come back with triggers for much longer. Free help: 1-800-QUIT-NOW and smokefree.gov (including SmokefreeTeen). If students mention someone at home who smokes or vapes, keep the talk free of blame.

## Read: How nicotine hooks the brain
text: When someone vapes or smokes, nicotine reaches the brain within seconds. It makes the brain release **dopamine**, a chemical that brings a quick feeling of pleasure or relief. Within a few hours, the nicotine level drops. The brain, which has adjusted to having nicotine, now feels off: the person may be irritable, anxious or unable to focus. This is **withdrawal**. Soon comes a strong urge to use again, called a **craving**. Using again brings quick relief, and the cycle starts over. Over time the brain needs more nicotine to feel the same, which is called **tolerance**.

## Main: Put the cycle in order
timeline: 1 6 1
  - 1 | Uses nicotine (vapes or smokes)
  - 2 | Nicotine reaches the brain in seconds; dopamine brings pleasure or relief
  - 3 | Within a few hours, the nicotine level in the body drops
  - 4 | Feels irritable, anxious, restless and cannot focus
  - 5 | Feels a strong urge to use nicotine again
  - 6 | Uses again to feel relief, and the cycle repeats
draw: Draw the six stages as a circle with arrows. Write W next to the stage where withdrawal appears and C next to the stage where cravings appear. | 2.6
note: Withdrawal (W) is stage 4 (irritable, anxious, cannot focus). Cravings (C) are stage 5 (strong urge to use again). Stage 6 leads back to stage 1.

## Stretch

  lines: 3
  key: Example: at stage 5, cravings. The person could use a plan for urges (wait 10 minutes, drink water, take a walk, text a friend), get support from a doctor or a quit program like 1-800-QUIT-NOW, and avoid triggers. Not starting at stage 1 is the surest way.
