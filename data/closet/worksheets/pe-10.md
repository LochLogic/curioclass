# 1. Target Heart Rate Formula
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can use the heart rate reserve formula to find a target heart rate zone.
directions: Study the example. Then find the target zone, 60% to 80% of heart rate reserve, for each person. Round to the nearest whole beat per minute.
mistakes: The most common error is multiplying the maximum heart rate by the percentage and forgetting the resting rate. In this formula the percentage is taken of the reserve (max minus resting), and then the resting rate is added back. Students also forget to subtract the age from 220 first.
notes: This is the heart rate reserve (Karvonen) method. 220 minus age is only an estimate of maximum heart rate; real maximums can differ by 10 or more beats per minute, so the zone is a guide, not a rule. All people on the sheet are sample people; students do not need to use or share their own heart rates. Key: Jordan 204, 140, 148 to 176; Ms. Ruiz 180, 110, 136 to 158; Mr. Okafor 160, 100, 120 to 140; Kai 195, 120, 147 to 171.

## Warm up: The formula
example: **Step 1.** Maximum heart rate (MHR) = 220 - age
  **Step 2.** Heart rate reserve (HRR) = MHR - resting heart rate
  **Step 3.** Target heart rate = (HRR x percent) + resting heart rate
  Sam is 15 with a resting heart rate of 65. MHR = 220 - 15 = 205. HRR = 205 - 65 = 140. At 60%: 140 x 0.6 = 84, and 84 + 65 = **149**. At 80%: 140 x 0.8 = 112, and 112 + 65 = **177**. Sam's zone is 149 to 177 beats per minute.
fill:
  - The maximum heart rate for a 30-year-old: {190}
  - The heart rate reserve for MHR 190 and resting rate 70: {120}

## Main: Find each zone
table: Person | Age | Resting HR | Max HR | HRR | 60% target | 80% target
  - Jordan | 16 | 64 | {204} | {140} | {148} | {176}
  - Ms. Ruiz | 40 | 70 | {180} | {110} | {136} | {158}
  - Mr. Okafor | 60 | 60 | {160} | {100} | {120} | {140}
  - Kai | 25 | 75 | {195} | {120} | {147} | {171}
write: Show your work for one person.
  lines: 4
  key: Example, Ms. Ruiz: 220 - 40 = 180; 180 - 70 = 110; 110 x 0.6 = 66, 66 + 70 = 136; 110 x 0.8 = 88, 88 + 70 = 158.
write: Look at the four zones. As people get older, what happens to the zone, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: The zone gets lower, because the estimated maximum heart rate (220 - age) drops as age goes up, so the reserve gets smaller.

## Stretch
fill: During a workout, Jordan counts his pulse for 10 seconds. To be at the bottom of his zone (148), he should count about {25} beats. (Divide by 6 and round.)
write: Jordan's friend is also 16 but has a resting heart rate of 84. Find the friend's zone and explain why it differs from Jordan's.
  lines: 4
  key: MHR 204; HRR 204 - 84 = 120; 60%: 72 + 84 = 156; 80%: 96 + 84 = 180. The zone is 156 to 180, higher at both ends than Jordan's 148 to 176, because the formula adds the higher resting rate back in; the reserve is smaller (120 instead of 140), so the zone is also narrower.

# 7. Injury Prevention Checklist
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can spot the injury risk in a training plan and write a fix that prevents it.
directions: Read the worked example. For each of the 8 scenarios, write the letter of the main injury risk from the risk list, then write a specific fix in the table. Use each letter once.
mistakes: Students write fixes that are too vague ("be careful," "warm up more") instead of specific actions with an amount or a time. Scenario 5 (Ana, 7 days a week) is sometimes marked as too much too soon; the plan did not jump suddenly, it never stops, so the risk is no rest and recovery. Watch for "push through it" thinking in scenario 8: sharp or worsening pain is a stop sign, not a toughness test.
notes: Key: 1 B, 2 A, 3 E, 4 C, 5 D, 6 H, 7 F, 8 G. The 10 percent figure is a common rule of thumb for raising weekly running distance, not an exact law; the point is gradual increases. Youth sports guidance commonly recommends at least one or two days off a week from organized sport. Shoe life varies by shoe and runner; "worn soles and lost cushioning" is the check, not a set date. Accept other accurate fixes; the best ones name an amount, a time or a person to tell.

## Warm up
example: **Dee** joins a new interval class and goes 6 days in her first week. Her calves are sore and tight. **Risk:** B, too much too soon. **Fix:** Start with 2 or 3 classes a week with a rest day between, and add one class only after 2 or 3 weeks feel easy.
choice: Which is a sign to stop and get help instead of pushing through?
  - Mild muscle soreness the day after a new workout
  - Breathing hard during a sprint
  * Sharp pain in one spot that gets worse as you keep going
choice: Why do training plans add work gradually?
  - So workouts are easier and take less time
  * Muscles, bones and tendons need time to adapt to new stress, and they adapt more slowly than fitness improves.
  - Because the body cannot get fitter after age 16

## Main: Name the risk
text: **Risk list:** A Skipped warm-up. B Too much too soon. C Poor technique. D Not enough rest and recovery. E Wrong or worn-out equipment. F Heat and dehydration. G Training through pain. H Unbalanced training.
fill:
  - Mia ran about 10 km a week all fall, then signed up for a race and ran 25 km the next week. Risk: {B}
  - Leo walks straight from the bus to the weight room and does heavy squats as his first exercise. Risk: {A}
  - Sara runs in the same shoes she has used for three years; the soles are worn flat on one side. Risk: {E}
  - Ty's knees cave inward every time he lands from a jump at volleyball practice. Risk: {C}
  - Ana plays on two soccer teams and has a practice or game 7 days a week, with no break for months. Risk: {D}
  - Jake trains only his chest and arms (bench press and curls) and never his back or legs. Risk: {H}
  - On a 34 degree Celsius afternoon, Kim practices for 90 minutes and drinks nothing until it ends. Risk: {F}
  - Noah feels sharp pain in one shin on every run but keeps running "to toughen it up." Risk: {G}

## Main: Write the fix
table: # | Prevention fix (specific: what, how much, when)
  height: 0.5
  - 1 | {Build up gradually: add about 10 percent a week over several weeks before the race.}
  - 2 | {Do 5 to 10 minutes of light cardio and dynamic moves, then lighter warm-up sets before heavy squats.}
  - 3 | {Replace the worn shoes with a pair that fits running, and check soles and cushioning regularly.}
  - 4 | {Practice landing with knees over toes and hips back, starting with low jumps and a coach watching.}
  - 5 | {Take at least one or two days off each week and a break between seasons.}
  - 6 | {Balance the plan: add back (pulling) and leg exercises so every muscle group is trained.}
  - 7 | {Drink water before, during (every 15 to 20 minutes) and after, take shade breaks, or practice in a cooler hour.}
  - 8 | {Stop running, tell a coach or athletic trainer, and get checked if the pain continues.}

## Stretch
write: Write a 4-item checklist an athlete could read before every workout to prevent at least four of the risks above.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: 1. Did I warm up for 5 to 10 minutes with dynamic moves? 2. Is today's workout only a small step up from last week? 3. Do I have water, and are my shoes and gear in good shape? 4. Do I have any sharp or worsening pain I should report before I start?

# 20. Leadership Observation
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, clipboard, a class game to watch
ican: I can rate a leader's behaviors on a rubric and back each rating with evidence.
directions: Read the rubric. Practice by rating Priya, a captain in a written game summary. Then watch one captain during a real class game, rate each behavior from 1 to 4, and write what you saw or heard that earned the rating.
mistakes: Students rate the whole person ("she's a great leader, 4s everywhere") instead of each behavior on its own; ask for one piece of evidence per row before a number goes in. Others count skill as leadership: scoring a lot is not on the rubric. Watch for evidence that is opinion ("he was negative") instead of an action or a quote.
notes: Practice key for Priya, with accepted ranges: Sets the tone 4 (team huddle, "have fun and talk on every ball"); Communicates clearly 4 (calls on most plays, sets the back row); Encourages after mistakes 1 (said nothing to Josh after two missed serves); Includes everyone 1 (took Josh's serve and skipped his turn); Handles conflict and calls 4 (walked the team back, asked the referee calmly); Leads by example 3 (dove for every ball, but broke the serving rotation rule). Accept a rating 1 point off if the evidence supports it. For the live game, assign each observer one captain, and remind them to watch without coaching or calling out during play.

## The rubric
table: Behavior | 4: Strong | 2: Developing
  - Sets the tone | Positive energy and body language before and during play | Mood depends on the score
  - Communicates clearly | Specific calls and directions most plays ("Mine!", "Shift left") | Talks some, often vague or late
  - Encourages after mistakes | Quick support every time a teammate errs | Notices some mistakes, silent on others
  - Includes everyone | Gets every player involved; quieter players get turns | Relies on a few players
  - Handles conflict and calls | Calms teammates; asks officials calmly | Stays out of it or gets drawn in
  - Leads by example | Full effort and follows the rules all game | Effort or rule-following comes and goes
text: A 3 is between Strong and Developing. A 1 means the behavior was missing or the opposite happened.

## Warm up: Practice with Priya
text: **Priya, captain of the Green team, class volleyball.** Before the game she huddled the team: "Let's have fun and talk on every ball." She called "Mine!" and "Out!" on most plays and told the back row where to stand. When Josh missed two serves in a row, she said nothing; on his next turn she served for him, skipping his turn. When the other team argued a call, Priya walked her teammates back to their spots and calmly asked the referee to explain. She dove for every ball, even when Green was down by 8.

write: Write feedback for the captain: one glow (a strength with evidence) and one grow (a goal with a specific action to try next game).

# 21. Role Clarity Chart
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can match team roles to people's strengths and list what each role does.
directions: Match each role to its main job. Read the team roster and assign each player the role that fits their strengths. Then list each role's tasks before, during and after a game.
mistakes: Students often treat motivator and encourager as the same role. The motivator lifts the whole team's energy and keeps the goal in front of everyone; the encourager supports one teammate at a time, especially after a mistake. The captain is not the boss of every task: a clear chart spreads the work.
notes: The roster is fictional so no one in your class is labeled. To use the chart with a real class team, let students choose roles for themselves and rotate them during the unit so everyone tries leading. Key roster: Priya captain, Sam motivator, Mateo organizer, Ana encourager. Chart key, sample tasks (accept any reasonable tasks that fit the role): Captain: before, leads the warm-up and the coin toss; during, calls plays and speaks with officials; after, leads the handshake. Motivator: before, leads a team cheer and states the goal; during, keeps energy up on the bench; after, names what the team did well. Organizer: before, sets out equipment and the lineup; during, runs substitutions; after, collects equipment. Encourager: before, checks on nervous teammates; during, gives specific praise after mistakes; after, thanks each teammate for one thing.

## Warm up: Match the role
match: Write the letter of each role's main job.
  - Captain = Leads the team, makes quick game decisions and speaks for the team with officials
  - Motivator = Raises the whole team's energy and reminds everyone of the team goal
  - Organizer = Handles equipment, the lineup and the substitution rotation
  - Encourager = Supports teammates one at a time, especially after a mistake

## Main: Assign the roles
table: Player | Strength | Role
  - Priya | Stays calm under pressure and makes fast decisions in a game | {Captain}
  - Sam | Starts team cheers and gets everyone fired up before the game | {Motivator}
  - Mateo | Keeps lists, remembers whose turn it is and never forgets the pinnies | {Organizer}
  - Ana | Notices when a teammate is down and gives a quiet pep talk | {Encourager}
table: Role | Before the game | During the game | After the game
  - Captain | | |
  - Motivator | | |
  - Organizer | | |
  - Encourager | | |
write: Choose one role from your chart and explain why the team plays better when someone clearly owns that role.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: With an organizer, equipment and the rotation are ready before the whistle, so no time is wasted and everyone gets fair playing time; when nobody owns the job, it gets forgotten or several people argue about it.

## Stretch
write: Two players both want to be captain. Write a fair way to settle it that keeps the team strong.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: rotate the captain each game or each half; make one captain and one co-captain with different jobs; let the team vote on the qualities a captain needs first, then choose.

# 33. Distance Data
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator, a measuring tape and softballs for the stretch
ican: I can graph throwing data before and after a coaching cue and use the numbers to explain the change.
directions: Riley threw a softball 8 times, then got one cue and threw 8 more. Plot every throw on the graph, find the center and spread of each set, and explain the change. In the stretch, collect the same data for your partner.
mistakes: Students compare only the best throw before with the best throw after; one throw is not the pattern. Have them use the mean or median of each set. Some plot the after throws starting again at 1 so they land on top of the before throws; the after set is throws 9 to 16. Watch for "the cue caused all of it": the after throws also came later in the session, after more practice.
notes: Riley is a fictional student. The cue, "Step toward the target with your opposite foot," is a standard overhand throwing cue; before it, Riley stepped with the throwing-side foot. Before: 18, 21, 17, 20, 19, 22, 18, 21 (sum 156, mean 19.5, median 19.5, range 22 - 17 = 5). After: 23, 22, 25, 24, 26, 23, 25, 24 (sum 192, mean 24, median 24, range 26 - 22 = 4). Change in mean 4.5 m, which is 4.5 / 19.5 = about 23 percent. The worst after throw (22) equals the best before throw (22). For the stretch, measure throws in meters on a flat field with a clear landing zone, and have throwers go one at a time on a signal.
