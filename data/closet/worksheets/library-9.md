# 1. What Makes a Question Enduring?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell enduring questions apart from factual and opinion questions and explain what makes a question enduring.
directions: Read the three definitions. Cut out the 12 question cards and sort them into enduring, factual and opinion. Glue them down once you are sure. Then explain what makes three of the enduring questions last.
mistakes: Students often file any "big-sounding" question as enduring, including opinion questions like "Is summer better than winter?" Ask two tests: Have people argued about it for centuries? Does the answer matter for how people live together? Others call "What causes the seasons?" enduring because it sounds deep; science has a settled, checkable answer, so it is factual.
notes: An enduring question has no single correct answer, has been asked across many times and cultures, and matters for how people live (heroism, fairness, duty, freedom). A factual question has one checkable answer. An opinion question is a matter of personal taste or a small choice; it can be argued, but it does not carry lasting public importance. A few cards invite debate (for example, "Should our class get a hamster or a fish?" is a choice, not an enduring question); accept a different placement only with a reason that uses the definitions.

## Warm up: Three kinds of questions
example: **Enduring:** has no single right answer, has been argued for centuries, and matters for how people live together. Example: What makes a law fair?
  **Factual:** has one answer you can look up and check. Example: How many US states are there?
  **Opinion:** a matter of personal taste or a small choice. Example: Which ice cream flavor is best?

## Main: Sort the cards
sort: Glue each question under its kind.
  - Enduring: What makes a person a hero?, Is it ever right to break a rule?, What do we owe people we will never meet?, How should a community share what it has?
  - Factual: When did people first land on the Moon?, What causes the seasons on Earth?, Who wrote the first draft of the Declaration of Independence?, How many people live in Canada?
  - Opinion: Which pizza topping is the best?, Is summer better than winter?, Which sport is the most fun to watch?, Should our class get a hamster or a fish?

## Explain three
write: Pick an enduring question. What makes it last? Name people or times who might answer it differently.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "What makes a person a hero?" Ancient Greek epics praised strength in battle, while many people today honor nurses, firefighters or people who speak out; each age answers differently, and the answer shapes who a society honors.
write: Pick a second enduring question and explain why it has no single right answer.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "Is it ever right to break a rule?" It depends on the rule and the reason; people who broke unjust laws in protest are admired by many, yet a society also needs people to follow laws, so thoughtful people disagree.
write: Pick a third enduring question. How could it connect to something happening today?
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: "What do we owe people we will never meet?" It connects to debates about helping other countries after disasters or protecting the climate for future generations.

## Stretch: Turn it
write: Turn the factual question "When did people first land on the Moon?" into an enduring question.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Should humans spend money exploring space while there are problems on Earth? / Why do people feel the need to explore the unknown?

# 7. Read a Database Record
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can read an academic database record to judge a source from its abstract, subject terms, peer review status and citation details.
directions: Your research question is "Would starting high school later help students sleep and learn?" Read the three database records. Highlight the abstract's main finding in each. Then use the records to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often judge a record by its title alone; the abstract tells what the source actually found and how. Others assume anything in a database is peer reviewed; databases also hold magazine and newspaper articles, so check the record's source type. Some copy the record's subject terms without seeing that they are a ready-made list of better search words.
notes: All three records, journals, authors and findings are made up for practice, though they reflect real research trends on adolescent sleep (many studies link later school start times with more sleep for teens). DOIs beginning 10.5555 are reserved for examples and lead nowhere. Peer reviewed means other experts checked the article before the journal published it. Record 3 is relevant but only partly: it is about phones at night, not start times, though its subject terms point toward the question.

## The records
source: Record 1
  **Title:** Later Bells, More Sleep: A Two-Year Study of High School Start Times. **Authors:** Okafor, Grace; Lindqvist, Erik. **Source:** *Journal of Youth Sleep Research*, vol. 14, no. 2, 2024, pp. 101-118. **Source type:** academic journal. **Peer reviewed:** yes. **DOI:** 10.5555/jysr.2024.0142
  **Abstract:** Two high schools moved their start time from 7:30 to 8:30 a.m. Students wore sleep trackers before and after the change. Average sleep rose by 34 minutes a night, and first-period attendance improved. A nearby school that kept its start time showed no change. **Subject terms:** school start times; adolescent sleep; attendance; academic achievement
source: Record 2
  **Title:** Should School Start Later? Parents Weigh In. **Author:** Banks, Tara. **Source:** *Family Life Weekly*, 3 Sept. 2025, p. 22. **Source type:** magazine. **Peer reviewed:** no.
  **Abstract:** Six parents share their views on a proposal to start their district's high school later, including worries about bus schedules, sports practices and after-school jobs. **Subject terms:** school schedules; parents; opinion
source: Record 3
  **Title:** Screens After Dark: Phone Use and Teen Sleep. **Authors:** Reyes, Daniel; Cho, Min-ji; Patel, Asha. **Source:** *Adolescent Health Quarterly*, vol. 9, no. 4, 2023, pp. 55-70. **Source type:** academic journal. **Peer reviewed:** yes. **DOI:** 10.5555/ahq.2023.0094
  **Abstract:** A survey of 1,200 students aged 14 to 18 found that those who used phones in bed slept 40 minutes less on school nights than those who did not. The authors suggest that school start times and screen habits both affect teen sleep. **Subject terms:** adolescent sleep; smartphones; screen time; sleep hygiene

# 20. Citation and Copyright Check
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can write an MLA citation, explain a Creative Commons license, and fix plagiarism with a paraphrase and a citation.
directions: Part 1: write one MLA works cited entry from the source facts. Part 2: decode a Creative Commons license and decide whether two uses follow it. Part 3: find the plagiarism in a student sentence and fix it.
mistakes: In Part 1, students often list the second author last name first too; in MLA only the first author is inverted. In Part 2, students often think "NC" means no one may use the work; it means no commercial use. In Part 3, students often change a word or two and call it a paraphrase. A real paraphrase uses new structure and wording, and it still needs a citation.
notes: MLA 9th edition, journal article with two authors: Last, First, and First Last. "Title." *Journal*, vol., no., year, pp. pages. https://doi.org/... MLA recommends a DOI when one exists. The source, authors, journal and photo are made up; DOIs beginning 10.5555 are reserved for examples. Creative Commons licenses: BY = credit the creator; NC = noncommercial uses only; SA = share alike (new work made from it must use the same license); ND = no changes. CC licenses also ask users to link the license and to say if they made changes.

## Part 1: Write the citation
table: Fact | Detail
  - Authors | Maria Santos and Kevin Liu
  - Article title | Rivers Under the City: Mapping Buried Streams
  - Journal | Urban Water Studies
  - Volume, issue | volume 7, issue 3
  - Year, pages | 2024, pages 88-104
  - DOI | 10.5555/uws.2024.0733
write: Write the works cited entry. Underline the journal title (it is in italics when typed).
  lines: 3
  key: Santos, Maria, and Kevin Liu. "Rivers Under the City: Mapping Buried Streams." *Urban Water Studies*, vol. 7, no. 3, 2024, pp. 88-104, https://doi.org/10.5555/uws.2024.0733.

  lines: 1

## Part 2: Decode a license
text: A photo of a city creek is posted online with this license: **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**.
table: Part | What it means
  - BY | {you must credit the creator}
  - NC | {noncommercial uses only; you cannot use it to make money}
  - SA | {anything you make from it must be shared under the same license}
choice: You crop the photo for your free class website, credit the photographer, note that you cropped it, link the license, and share your page under CC BY-NC-SA. Does this follow the license?
  * Yes
  - No, because cropping is never allowed
  - No, because a website is commercial
choice: A local business wants to print the photo on postcards to sell. Does the license allow it?
  - Yes, if the business credits the photographer
  * No, not without separate permission, because selling postcards is commercial
  - Yes, because the photo is online

## Part 3: Fix the plagiarism
source: Original, from Santos and Liu, page 95
  "When cities bury streams in pipes, the water moves faster and floods more often downstream, which is why some cities now dig the streams back up."
text: **Student sentence:** When cities bury streams in pipes, water moves faster and floods more downstream, so some cities dig streams back up.
write: What is wrong with the student sentence?
  lines: 2
  key: It copies the source's words and sentence structure almost exactly, with only a few words dropped, and it gives no citation, so it presents the authors' words and idea as the student's own.
write: Rewrite it as a true paraphrase with an in-text citation.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: Piping a stream underground speeds up its flow and makes floods worse farther along its path, so some cities have begun uncovering their buried streams (Santos and Liu 95).

# 21. Source Portfolio Tracker
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, your research sources
ican: I can track my sources by author, type, viewpoint and credibility so I can see the balance of my research.
directions: Read the two sample source descriptions and fill in their rows. Then set up your own tracker for 8 sources on your research question. Rate each source's credibility from 1 (low) to 4 (high) and give a reason.
mistakes: Students often write the website name as the author, or rate credibility by how much they agree with a source. Credibility depends on the author's expertise, evidence and purpose, not on agreement. Others leave the viewpoint blank for news or reference sources; even a neutral source has a viewpoint (for example, "neutral overview").
notes: The two sample sources are made up. Credibility scale used here: 4 = expert author, clear evidence, little conflict of interest; 3 = reliable but limited (for example, a good news article that summarizes research); 2 = some problems (unclear author, one-sided, little evidence); 1 = serious problems (no author, no evidence, or a strong conflict of interest). A balanced portfolio usually mixes source types (books, articles, data, interviews, primary sources) and viewpoints.

## Warm up: Two sample sources
text: **Sample A:** A 2025 report, "Phones in the Classroom," by Dr. Rita Shah, an education researcher at a state university. It surveys 2,000 students and teachers and finds mixed effects of phone bans. Funded by a state education grant.
text: **Sample B:** A 2026 blog post, "Ban Every Phone Now!", by a parent who runs a website selling locking phone pouches to schools. It tells stories from three classrooms and links to no studies.

# 33. Fair Use Four Factors
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can apply the four fair use factors to a school use of copyrighted work and decide whether it is likely fair use.
directions: Read the four factors. For each case, decide whether each factor favors fair use or weighs against it, then give your verdict: likely fair use or likely not. Fair use is decided case by case, so explain your thinking.
mistakes: Students often treat "for school" as an automatic pass. Educational purpose helps the first factor, but copying a whole work that is made to be bought (Case 2) still weighs heavily against fair use. Others count factors like votes; courts weigh them together, and factors 1 and 4 usually matter most. Giving credit is good practice, but it does not make a use fair.
notes: The four factors come from US copyright law (17 U.S.C. section 107): (1) the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial or nonprofit educational; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the work as a whole; (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the work. Courts also ask whether a use is transformative (adds new meaning or purpose). The cases are made up. Verdicts: 1 likely fair, 2 likely not, 3 likely not, 4 likely fair. This sheet is a teaching tool, not legal advice. Accept "mixed" for a factor when the student's reason is sound.

## Warm up: The four factors
example: **1. Purpose:** Is the use for teaching, comment, criticism or news? Does it transform the work? Or is it commercial copying?
  **2. Nature:** Is the work factual (favors fair use) or highly creative (weighs against)?
  **3. Amount:** How much was used, and was it the heart of the work?
  **4. Market effect:** Could the use replace a sale or a license the owner would earn?
choice: Which factor asks whether your use could take away sales from the owner?
  - Purpose
  - Nature
  - Amount
  * Market effect

## The cases
text: **Case 1.** In a literary analysis essay, a student quotes three short passages from a recent novel to support her argument about its theme.
text: **Case 2.** A school club photocopies an entire workbook for every member each year instead of buying copies.
text: **Case 3.** The school posts a sports highlight video on a public channel that earns ad money. A popular song plays in full in the background.
text: **Case 4.** In a class presentation on media bias, a student shows screenshots of two news websites' headlines about the same event and analyzes their word choices.

## Main: Weigh the factors
table: Factor | Case 1 | Case 2 | Case 3 | Case 4
  - 1. Purpose | {favors: commentary, education} | {mixed: education but plain copying} | {against: commercial, not about the song} | {favors: criticism, education}
  - 2. Nature | {against: creative novel} | {against: made to be bought and used up} | {against: creative song} | {favors: factual news}
  - 3. Amount | {favors: short passages} | {against: the whole work} | {against: the whole song} | {favors: headlines only}
  - 4. Market effect | {favors: no lost sales} | {against: replaces buying} | {against: skips a license} | {favors: no lost sales}
  - Verdict | {likely fair use} | {likely not} | {likely not} | {likely fair use}
  height: 0.6

## Explain
write: In Case 1, the nature factor weighs against fair use. Why is the use still likely fair?
  lines: 3
  key: The other factors, especially purpose and market effect, favor it: the student comments on the novel, uses only short passages, and no one would skip buying the book because of her essay. Courts usually give the nature factor the least weight.
write: How could the school change Case 3 so it is no longer a copyright problem?
  lines: 3
  key: Use music that is licensed for this use, royalty-free or in the public domain (or with a Creative Commons license that allows it), or get permission from the copyright owners.

## Stretch
write: A student says, "I gave credit, so it is fair use." Respond using the four factors.
  lines: 3
  key: Credit is good practice but is not one of the four factors. A use is fair only if purpose, nature, amount and market effect together support it; copying a whole work for free can still harm its market, credited or not.
