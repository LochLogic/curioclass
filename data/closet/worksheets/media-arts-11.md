# 1. Mise-en-Scène in Caligari
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; the teacher shows stills from The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920, public domain)
ican: I can analyze how setting, costume, lighting and blocking create meaning in a film.
directions: Your teacher will show four stills from The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920). Answer the warm up questions. For each still, write what you see and its effect on the viewer. Then write a short critique of how the film's mise-en-scène shows a disturbed mind.
mistakes: Students often describe what is in a still without saying what it does to the viewer. Every chart row needs an effect: unease, menace, confinement. Blocking means where actors stand and move in the frame, not the plot. Students may call the sets "unrealistic" as a flaw; the distortion is deliberate.
notes: The film was directed by Robert Wiene, with sets designed by Hermann Warm, Walter Reimann and Walter Röhrig, and is a landmark of German Expressionism. It is in the public domain in the United States; stills and full prints are available from public archives. Suggested stills: (1) the town: crooked houses, slanted roofs and windows, a twisting street; (2) Dr. Caligari at the fair presenting Cesare standing in his cabinet; (3) Cesare edging along a wall at night; (4) Cesare carrying Jane across the rooftops. In many scenes, shadows and streaks of light are painted onto the sets. Preview stills before class; the film has frightening moments but no graphic content in these scenes.

## Warm up: Terms
match: Write the letter next to each element of mise-en-scène.
  - Setting = The place: sets, buildings, furniture, props
  - Costume = What actors wear, including makeup and hair
  - Lighting = Where light and shadow fall, and how hard or soft they are
  - Blocking = Where actors stand and how they move in the frame
choice: In many scenes of Caligari, the shadows and streaks of light are:
  - Made by real lamps on set
  * Painted directly onto the sets
  - Added later with computers
  - Caused by damage to the film
choice: Expressionist sets like Caligari's are designed to show:
  - A real town as accurately as possible
  * A troubled inner world, more than a realistic place
  - The cheapest way to build a set
  - A science fiction future

## Main: Analyze the stills
example: For each still, write what you see and its effect on the viewer. **Model, still 1 (the town), setting:** buildings lean at sharp angles, windows are jagged triangles, the street twists. **Effect:** the town feels unstable and threatening, as if the world itself is wrong.
write: Still 2 (the fair), **costume:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Caligari wears a top hat, cloak and round glasses and carries a cane; Cesare wears a dark, tight bodysuit with a pale face and heavy dark eye makeup. Effect: both look strange and unnatural; Caligari seems like a showman hiding something, Cesare like a living shadow.
write: Still 3 (along the wall), **blocking:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Cesare moves stiffly, pressed flat against the wall, arms close to his body, sliding sideways. Effect: he seems part of the set, inhuman and creeping; the viewer senses danger before anything happens.
write: Still 4 (the rooftops), **lighting:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Harsh contrast between bright and dark areas, with light and shadow shapes painted onto the set rather than cast by lamps. Effect: the night feels unreal and dreamlike, like a nightmare, and the figures stand out as dark shapes.
write: Any still, your choice of element: what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific detail in the still and a specific effect on the viewer.

## Main: Critique
write: In a paragraph, argue how the film's mise-en-scène shows a disturbed or unreliable view of the world. Use at least three details from the stills, each from a different element.
  lines: 9
  key: A strong paragraph makes a claim (the whole world of the film looks the way a troubled mind would see it) and supports it with details from different elements: slanted, jagged sets and painted shadows (setting and lighting) make the town feel unreal and threatening; Cesare's dark, tight costume and heavy eye makeup and Caligari's top hat, cloak and round glasses (costume) make them look strange and menacing; Cesare's stiff, sideways movement pressed along the wall (blocking) echoes the angular sets, so the actors seem part of the distorted world. The best answers explain the effect of each detail, not just list it.

## Stretch
write: Choose one element of Caligari's style and describe how you would use it in a modern 1-minute short film. What would it make the audience feel?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: paint hard shadows onto cardboard walls of a school hallway set so a student's anxiety before a test feels physical; the audience shares the character's dread.

# 7. Motif Tracker
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; a short film chosen by the teacher for Part 2
ican: I can track a visual motif across a film and explain how its meaning changes.
directions: Answer the warm up. Read the scene list for the short film The Blue Thermos and track the thermos through every scene. Then track a color or object motif in the short film your class watches.
mistakes: Students list where the object appears but not how it is shown. Push for framing, focus, light and position: a motif's meaning often lives in how the camera treats it. Students also give the motif one fixed meaning; the most interesting motifs change as the character changes.
notes: The Blue Thermos is a made-up film written for this sheet. For Part 2, choose a short film (under 20 minutes) with a clear repeated color or object, and preview it. Students can track during a second viewing; pausing at each appearance helps. A motif is a repeated element that builds meaning across a work; a symbol can appear once.

## Warm up
choice: What makes something a motif rather than just a prop?
  - It is expensive
  - It appears in the first shot
  * It repeats across the film and builds meaning each time
  - It is the main character's favorite thing
choice: Which choice best shows that a filmmaker wants us to notice an object?
  - It sits in the background, out of focus, every time
  * It is framed in close-up, in sharp focus, with light falling on it
  - It is the same color as the walls
  - It is never shown on screen

## Main: The Blue Thermos
table: Scene | What happens and how the thermos is shown
  - 1. Kitchen, dawn | Grandpa fills a blue thermos with tea and hands it to Noor before school. Close-up, centered, warm light from the window.
  - 2. School bus | Classmates laugh at the old thermos. Noor pushes it deep into her backpack; we see a sliver of blue at the edge of the frame, in shadow.
  - 3. Hospital waiting room | Noor sits alone holding the empty thermos. Wide shot, cold white light; the blue thermos is the only strong color in the frame.
  - 4. Grandpa's kitchen, weeks later | The thermos sits on the counter, out of focus in the background, while Noor's mother talks on the phone.
  - 5. Kitchen, dawn | Noor learns to make Grandpa's tea and fills the thermos. The same close-up as scene 1, but now the hands are hers.
  - 6. School bus | Noor sets the thermos on the seat beside her, in plain view and in sunlight. A classmate asks about it, and she pours him a cup.
chart: How the camera treats it (framing, focus, light, position) | What it means here
  labels: Scene 1; Scene 2; Scene 3; Scene 4; Scene 5; Scene 6
  height: 0.55
  key: 1: centered close-up, warm light; Grandpa's care and their bond. 2: hidden at the frame's edge, in shadow; Noor's embarrassment, hiding her family. 3: the only color in a cold wide shot; fear and loneliness, Grandpa's absence. 4: out of focus in the background; grief, a loss no one is talking about. 5: the same close-up as scene 1 with her hands; Noor carries on Grandpa's care. 6: in plain view, in sunlight, shared; pride and connection, his care passed on to others.
write: How does the motif's meaning change from scene 1 to scene 6? What does that change say about Noor?
  lines: 3
  key: It moves from something Grandpa gives her, to something she hides, to a reminder of loss, to something she makes and proudly shares. The change shows Noor growing from embarrassment and grief to pride in her grandfather and taking his role of caring for others.

## Part 2: Track a motif in our film
write: Film title and the motif you will track (a color or an object):
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; the motif should repeat at least four times in the film.
chart: Time or scene | How the camera treats it | What it means here
  rows: 5
  height: 0.55
  key: Answers vary. Each row names a specific moment, describes framing, focus, light or position, and gives a meaning tied to the story at that point.

## Stretch
write: Find the moment where the motif's meaning shifts the most. Explain what changes on screen and in the story at that moment.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one moment, contrasts how the motif is shown before and after (for example, from hidden to centered, or from warm to cold light), and links the shift to a turning point for a character.

# 20. Accessibility Audit
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can audit designs for contrast, type size and color-blind readability and recommend fixes.
directions: Read the audit guidelines. Answer the warm up. Audit each of the three designs on all three criteria, then rank the fixes and redesign one part.
mistakes: Students give a design a pass because it "looks fine to me." The audit is against guidelines, not taste. Students also miss that color-blind readability is about meaning carried by color alone: a red and green chart with a color-only legend fails even if the colors are bright, while a red warning that also says "WARNING" in words passes.
notes: The three designs are made up. Contrast minimums follow WCAG 2 level AA (4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text); the ratios on the sheet were computed from the hex colors. The type size guideline is a class rule for print, not a single official standard. Color vision deficiency affects roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women in many populations, most often red and green. If students did the Contrast Check sheet in grade 10, they can verify the ratios themselves.

## Warm up
example: **Our audit guidelines.** **Contrast:** at least 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (about 18 point or more). **Type size (class rule for print):** body text at least 12 point; chart labels at least 10 point; signs read from across a room at least 24 point. **Color-blind readability:** never use color alone to carry meaning; add words, labels, patterns or shapes.
choice: Which is the most common kind of color vision deficiency?
  - Seeing no color at all
  * Trouble telling red from green
  - Trouble telling black from white
  - Seeing everything as blue
choice: A map shows bus routes in red and green with no labels. What is the best fix?
  - Make the red brighter
  - Remove the map
  * Label each route line with its number or name, and use different line patterns
  - Print it in black and white only

## Main: Three designs
table: Design | What it looks like
  - 1. Spring Concert poster | Headline "Spring Concert" in 60-point black on white (21:1). Date, time and place in 11-point gray #999999 on white (2.8:1). No color coding.
  - 2. Survey results bar chart | "Yes" bars are green and "No" bars are red; the legend shows only a green square and a red square. Bar labels in 8-point black on white (21:1). Title in 16 point.
  - 3. Bus stop sign | Route names in 28-point white on navy #003366 (12.6:1). Each route line on the small map is a different color and is also labeled with its number. A "Last bus 11 p.m." notice in 14-point white on orange #FFA500 (2.0:1).
chart: Contrast | Type size | Color-blind readability | Most important fix
  labels: Design 1; Design 2; Design 3
  height: 1.1
  key: Design 1: contrast fails for the 11-point gray details (2.8:1, needs 4.5:1), the headline passes; type size fails for details (11 point, under 12); color-blind passes (no color coding); fix: make the date, time and place dark gray or black and at least 12 point, since that is the information people need. Design 2: contrast passes (21:1); type size fails for 8-point labels (under 10); color-blind fails (red and green carry the meaning alone); fix: label the bars "Yes" and "No" directly or add patterns, and enlarge labels to at least 10 point. Design 3: contrast passes for route names but fails for the notice (2.0:1, below even the 3:1 large-text minimum); type size passes for route names but the 14-point notice is small for a sign read from a distance; color-blind passes (routes are numbered); fix: change the notice to near-black text on orange (8.8:1) or white on navy, and make it larger.

## Main: Rank and redesign
write: Rank the three most important fixes across all designs, from most to least urgent. Explain your top choice.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong ranking puts fixes that block essential information first, such as the concert's date, time and place (2.8:1 at 11 point) or the last-bus notice (2.0:1), because people who cannot read them miss the event or the bus; the chart's color-only legend is close behind because the meaning is lost entirely for many readers.
draw: Redesign one failing part (the concert details, the chart legend or the last-bus notice). Label your colors, sizes and any added words or patterns. | 2

## Stretch
write: A classmate says, "Accessibility rules make designs boring." Respond with a specific example from this audit.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer argues that the fixes keep the style while helping everyone, such as near-black text on the same bright orange keeping the brand color while making the notice readable, or direct labels on the chart making it faster for every reader, not only people with color vision deficiency.

# 21. School Wayfinding
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can design a sign system that helps people find their way.
directions: Answer the questions about wayfinding. Design a simple icon for each destination. Then plan the signs at three decision points in a school and sketch one directional sign.
mistakes: Students often put too much on one sign. A directional sign should list only the destinations reachable from that point, a few at most, with one clear arrow each. Icons get too detailed: a good icon reads at a glance and from far away. Arrows should point the way people walk (up for straight ahead), and the same style must repeat everywhere.
notes: Wayfinding signs come in a few kinds: identification (marks a place, like a room sign), directional (arrows to places), informational or orientation (maps, "you are here") and regulatory (rules, like "staff only"). In the United States, permanent room identification signs must include raised letters and braille under ADA rules. Students can test their icons by showing them to a classmate with no labels and asking what each means.

## Warm up
match: Write the letter next to each kind of sign.
  - Identification sign = Marks a place, such as "Room 114" beside a door
  - Directional sign = Points the way with arrows, such as "Gym" with an arrow
  - Orientation sign = A map with "You are here"
  - Regulatory sign = States a rule, such as "Staff only"
choice: Where should directional signs go?
  - Only at the front door
  * At decision points, where people must choose a direction
  - On every wall, every few feet
  - Only inside classrooms
choice: Which feature helps people who are blind or have low vision use room signs?
  - Small, pale lettering
  * Raised letters and braille, with strong contrast
  - Signs placed near the ceiling
  - Only icons and no text
choice: Why should one arrow style repeat on every sign?
  * People learn the system once and read it faster everywhere
  - It is cheaper to buy one arrow
  - Different arrows look more exciting
  - Arrows are not needed if icons are used

## Main: Icons
text: Draw one simple icon for each destination: office, library, gym, cafeteria, nurse, restrooms. Use the same line weight and style for all six.
draw: Six icons | 2.2

## Main: Plan three decision points
table: Decision point | Destinations listed on the sign | Arrow for each
  - Main entrance | |
  - Where the main hall meets the gym hall | |
  - Top of the stairs | |

## Main: Sketch one directional sign
draw: Directional sign for one decision point: destinations, icons and arrows | 2.4

## Stretch
write: A visitor arrives who does not read English. How does your system still help them? What would you add?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer points to icons and arrows that work without words, a consistent color for each area, a map at the entrance, and adds translations of key words used in the school community or room numbers that follow a clear order.

# 33. Story Structure Board
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, scissors, glue
ican: I can arrange documentary sequences into acts and identify the turning points.
directions: Read the summary of the documentary Signal Strong. Cut out the 10 sequence cards and glue each under its act, in story order. Then identify the turning points and test one change to the structure.
mistakes: Students put the letter about the building sale in Act 2 because it starts the trouble. In a three-act structure it is the turning point that ends Act 1: it launches the main problem. Students also confuse the midpoint (a shift in direction, often new hope or a new approach) with the climax (the decisive moment the film builds to).
notes: Signal Strong and everyone in it are made up. Documentary editors often build this kind of board with index cards on a wall; the same method works for students' own films, with one card per sequence. Some editors open on a dramatic moment and then flash back; the stretch tests that choice. Accept a reasonable alternate placement if the student explains it, such as the fundraiser planning in late Act 1.

## Warm up
text: **Signal Strong** follows a teen-run community radio station over one school year. Its founder, Ms. Ortega, has run it from a rented building for 20 years. When the building is sold, the station has 90 days to move or shut down.
choice: In a three-act structure, what does the turning point at the end of Act 1 do?
  - Ends the film
  * Launches the main problem or goal that drives the middle of the film
  - Introduces the credits
  - Repeats the opening scene

## Main: Build the board
sort: Glue each sequence under its act, in story order from top to bottom.
  - Act 1 (setup): Cold open: teen hosts start the 6 a.m. show; Meet Ms. Ortega and the station's 20-year history; A letter arrives: the building is sold and the station must leave in 90 days
  - Act 2 (rising trouble): Hosts search for a new space and three landlords say no; The team plans a fundraiser concert; A listener calls in to say the station got her through a hard winter and donations pour in; The old transmitter fails during the move and the station goes silent
  - Act 3 (resolution): The public library offers its basement; The first broadcast from the new studio; The youngest host trains new volunteers
note: Story order within the acts: Act 1: cold open, meet Ms. Ortega, the letter. Act 2: the search, the fundraiser plan, the listener's call, the transmitter failure. Act 3: the library's offer, the first broadcast, training new volunteers.

## Main: Turning points
choice: Which sequence is the midpoint, where the story shifts direction?
  - The cold open
  - The library's offer
  - The first broadcast
  * The listener's call that brings in donations
choice: Which sequence is the low point just before Act 3?
  - The fundraiser plan
  * The transmitter failure that leaves the station silent
  - Meeting Ms. Ortega
  - Training new volunteers
choice: Which sequence is the climax?
  * The first broadcast from the new studio
  - The letter about the building
  - The search for a new space
  - The cold open
write: Why does the film end with the youngest host training new volunteers instead of ending on the first broadcast?
  lines: 3
  key: The epilogue answers the bigger question behind the move: will the station last? Showing new volunteers being trained suggests the station's future is safe and its mission continues, so the ending is about the community, not just one successful broadcast.

## Stretch
write: An editor wants to open the film on the transmitter failing (dead air, panicked hosts), then flash back to the start of the year. What does this structure gain, and what does it risk?
  lines: 4
  key: It gains an immediate hook and tension: viewers know a crisis is coming and watch to see how the station got there. It risks giving away the low point, making the midpoint's hope feel less surprising, and confusing viewers about time unless the flashback is clearly marked.
