# 1. Mise-en-Scène in Caligari
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; the teacher shows stills from The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920, public domain)
ican: I can analyze how setting, costume, lighting and blocking create meaning in a film.
directions: Your teacher will show four stills from The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920). Answer the warm up questions. For each still, write what you see and its effect on the viewer. Then write a short critique of how the film's mise-en-scène shows a disturbed mind.
mistakes: Students often describe what is in a still without saying what it does to the viewer. Every chart row needs an effect: unease, menace, confinement. Blocking means where actors stand and move in the frame, not the plot. Students may call the sets "unrealistic" as a flaw; the distortion is deliberate.
notes: The film was directed by Robert Wiene, with sets designed by Hermann Warm, Walter Reimann and Walter Röhrig, and is a landmark of German Expressionism. It is in the public domain in the United States; stills and full prints are available from public archives. Suggested stills: (1) the town: crooked houses, slanted roofs and windows, a twisting street; (2) Dr. Caligari at the fair presenting Cesare standing in his cabinet; (3) Cesare edging along a wall at night; (4) Cesare carrying Jane across the rooftops. In many scenes, shadows and streaks of light are painted onto the sets. Preview stills before class; the film has frightening moments but no graphic content in these scenes.

## Warm up: Terms
match: Write the letter next to each element of mise-en-scène.
  - Setting = The place: sets, buildings, furniture, props
  - Costume = What actors wear, including makeup and hair
  - Lighting = Where light and shadow fall, and how hard or soft they are
  - Blocking = Where actors stand and how they move in the frame
choice: In many scenes of Caligari, the shadows and streaks of light are:
  - Made by real lamps on set
  * Painted directly onto the sets
  - Added later with computers
  - Caused by damage to the film
choice: Expressionist sets like Caligari's are designed to show:
  - A real town as accurately as possible
  * A troubled inner world, more than a realistic place
  - The cheapest way to build a set
  - A science fiction future

## Main: Analyze the stills
example: For each still, write what you see and its effect on the viewer. **Model, still 1 (the town), setting:** buildings lean at sharp angles, windows are jagged triangles, the street twists. **Effect:** the town feels unstable and threatening, as if the world itself is wrong.
write: Still 2 (the fair), **costume:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Caligari wears a top hat, cloak and round glasses and carries a cane; Cesare wears a dark, tight bodysuit with a pale face and heavy dark eye makeup. Effect: both look strange and unnatural; Caligari seems like a showman hiding something, Cesare like a living shadow.
write: Still 3 (along the wall), **blocking:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Cesare moves stiffly, pressed flat against the wall, arms close to his body, sliding sideways. Effect: he seems part of the set, inhuman and creeping; the viewer senses danger before anything happens.
write: Still 4 (the rooftops), **lighting:** what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Harsh contrast between bright and dark areas, with light and shadow shapes painted onto the set rather than cast by lamps. Effect: the night feels unreal and dreamlike, like a nightmare, and the figures stand out as dark shapes.
write: Any still, your choice of element: what do you see, and what is the effect?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific detail in the still and a specific effect on the viewer.

## Main: Critique
write: In a paragraph, argue how the film's mise-en-scène shows a disturbed or unreliable view of the world. Use at least three details from the stills, each from a different element.
  lines: 9
  key: A strong paragraph makes a claim (the whole world of the film looks the way a troubled mind would see it) and supports it with details from different elements: slanted, jagged sets and painted shadows (setting and lighting) make the town feel unreal and threatening; Cesare's dark, tight costume and heavy eye makeup and Caligari's top hat, cloak and round glasses (costume) make them look strange and menacing; Cesare's stiff, sideways movement pressed along the wall (blocking) echoes the angular sets, so the actors seem part of the distorted world. The best answers explain the effect of each detail, not just list it.

## Stretch
write: Choose one element of Caligari's style and describe how you would use it in a modern 1-minute short film. What would it make the audience feel?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: paint hard shadows onto cardboard walls of a school hallway set so a student's anxiety before a test feels physical; the audience shares the character's dread.

# 21. School Wayfinding
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can design a sign system that helps people find their way.
directions: Answer the questions about wayfinding. Design a simple icon for each destination. Then plan the signs at three decision points in a school and sketch one directional sign.
mistakes: Students often put too much on one sign. A directional sign should list only the destinations reachable from that point, a few at most, with one clear arrow each. Icons get too detailed: a good icon reads at a glance and from far away. Arrows should point the way people walk (up for straight ahead), and the same style must repeat everywhere.
notes: Wayfinding signs come in a few kinds: identification (marks a place, like a room sign), directional (arrows to places), informational or orientation (maps, "you are here") and regulatory (rules, like "staff only"). In the United States, permanent room identification signs must include raised letters and braille under ADA rules. Students can test their icons by showing them to a classmate with no labels and asking what each means.

## Warm up
match: Write the letter next to each kind of sign.
  - Identification sign = Marks a place, such as "Room 114" beside a door
  - Directional sign = Points the way with arrows, such as "Gym" with an arrow
  - Orientation sign = A map with "You are here"
  - Regulatory sign = States a rule, such as "Staff only"
choice: Where should directional signs go?
  - Only at the front door
  * At decision points, where people must choose a direction
  - On every wall, every few feet
  - Only inside classrooms
choice: Which feature helps people who are blind or have low vision use room signs?
  - Small, pale lettering
  * Raised letters and braille, with strong contrast
  - Signs placed near the ceiling
  - Only icons and no text
choice: Why should one arrow style repeat on every sign?
  * People learn the system once and read it faster everywhere
  - It is cheaper to buy one arrow
  - Different arrows look more exciting
  - Arrows are not needed if icons are used

## Stretch
write: A visitor arrives who does not read English. How does your system still help them? What would you add?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer points to icons and arrows that work without words, a consistent color for each area, a map at the entrance, and adds translations of key words used in the school community or room numbers that follow a clear order.
