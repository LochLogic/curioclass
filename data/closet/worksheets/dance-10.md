# 1. Graham, Horton, Cunningham
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, video clips or a demonstration of each technique chosen by the teacher
ican: I can compare three modern dance techniques by their principles, signature moves and use of the floor.
directions: Match each choreographer to a principle of their technique. Then complete the comparison chart and answer the questions, using what you see in class demonstrations or video clips.
mistakes: Students mix up Graham and Horton because both use strong, dramatic shapes. Graham starts from the breath and the torso (contraction and release); Horton builds long lines and strength through the whole body (flat backs, lateral T, hinges). Cunningham is often described only as "random": chance decided the order of material, but the movement itself was precise and highly trained.
notes: Key facts: Martha Graham (1894-1991) built her technique on contraction and release, rooted in the breath; her class begins seated on the floor, and Graham falls take the body to the floor and back up. Lester Horton (1906-1953) worked in Los Angeles; his technique uses flat backs, the lateral T, deep lunges and hinges to build strength and flexibility, and it is still taught by the Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater, since Ailey trained with Horton. Merce Cunningham (1919-2009), who danced with Graham's company before founding his own, used chance procedures to order material, made dances and music separately (often with John Cage), treated every point on stage as equally important, and built movement from the spine's curve, arch, tilt and twist; his work stays mostly upright.

## Main: Comparison chart
chart: Choreographer | Core principle | Signature moves | Use of the floor | Relationship to music
  rows: 3
  key: Graham: contraction and release from the breath; spirals and Graham falls; class begins seated on the floor, falls go to the floor and back up; music usually commissioned to support the drama. Horton: strength and long lines through the whole body; flat backs, lateral T, hinges, deep lunges, fortifications; some floor work for strengthening, mostly standing; music varied. Cunningham: movement for its own sake, ordered by chance; curve, arch, tilt and twist of the spine, quick footwork, any point on stage can be the focus; mostly upright with little floor work; dance and music made independently and joined in performance.
write: Which of the three techniques puts the most emphasis on the floor? Use evidence from the chart.
  lines: 3
  key: Graham: class begins seated on the floor with breathing and contraction exercises, and Graham falls take dancers to the floor and back up.

## Analyze
write: Graham's technique has been called dramatic and expressive. How do contraction and release help a dancer show emotion?
  lines: 3
  key: The contraction curls the torso inward from the pelvis, like a sob, a gasp or a blow to the body, and the release opens it again; because the movement starts in the center of the body with the breath, it reads as an inner feeling made visible.
write: Cunningham treated movement itself as the subject of his dances, rather than a story. How does chance support that approach?
  lines: 3
  key: Chance (tossing coins or dice to decide order, direction or timing) keeps the choreographer's personal story or emotion from shaping the dance, so the audience looks at the movement for its own sake and makes its own meaning.

## Stretch
write: Choose one technique to warm up a cast for a dance about strength and endurance. Defend your choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a technique and links specific features to the dance, for example Horton, because its flat backs, lunges and fortification exercises build the full-body strength and stamina the piece needs.

# 21. Relationships on Stage
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils, open floor space
ican: I can map how dancers relate to each other on stage to communicate an issue.
directions: Choose two or three dancers from your issue piece. Map 5 moments on the stage diagrams (X for each dancer, an arrow for the way they face, a dotted line for where they travel). Name the relationship in each moment and explain what it shows about your issue.
mistakes: Students show relationships only with distance (close or far). Push them to use facing, levels, timing and touch-free contact such as mirroring or shadowing. "Support" should not mean lifting: in this unit it means one dancer framing, steadying or echoing another.
notes: Relationship words to post: lead and follow, mirror, shadow, oppose (counter), support, question and answer, meet and part. Support moments must stay safe: no lifts or weight-bearing without trained instruction; use hand-to-hand counterbalances only if they have been taught. Have pairs perform each moment while the class guesses the relationship before students draw.

## Warm up: Name the relationship
table: What you see | Relationship (lead, follow, oppose, support)
  - Dancer A starts a phrase; B copies it one count later, right behind A. | {follow}
  - A and B face each other at opposite ends of a diagonal and push toward each other without touching. | {oppose}
  - A sinks to a low level; B kneels beside A and slowly helps A rise by offering a steadying hand. | {support}
  - A changes direction and the group turns to go where A goes. | {lead}

  key: Answers vary; for example "Two dancers move in opposite directions on a diagonal, showing people who resist the change."

  lines: 1

  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the relationship (for example mirror or unison) and how it resolves the issue.

## Stretch
write: Which moment uses facing or level most powerfully? Explain how it changes the meaning compared with simply standing near each other.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the moment and the choice, for example "In moment 3, the dancers face away from each other at different levels, so even though they are close, they seem disconnected."
