# 1. Graham, Horton, Cunningham
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, video clips or a demonstration of each technique chosen by the teacher
ican: I can compare three modern dance techniques by their principles, signature moves and use of the floor.
directions: Match each choreographer to a principle of their technique. Then complete the comparison chart and answer the questions, using what you see in class demonstrations or video clips.
mistakes: Students mix up Graham and Horton because both use strong, dramatic shapes. Graham starts from the breath and the torso (contraction and release); Horton builds long lines and strength through the whole body (flat backs, lateral T, hinges). Cunningham is often described only as "random": chance decided the order of material, but the movement itself was precise and highly trained.
notes: Key facts: Martha Graham (1894-1991) built her technique on contraction and release, rooted in the breath; her class begins seated on the floor, and Graham falls take the body to the floor and back up. Lester Horton (1906-1953) worked in Los Angeles; his technique uses flat backs, the lateral T, deep lunges and hinges to build strength and flexibility, and it is still taught by the Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater, since Ailey trained with Horton. Merce Cunningham (1919-2009), who danced with Graham's company before founding his own, used chance procedures to order material, made dances and music separately (often with John Cage), treated every point on stage as equally important, and built movement from the spine's curve, arch, tilt and twist; his work stays mostly upright.

## Warm up: Who said what?
match: Match each choreographer to a principle of their technique.
  - Martha Graham = contraction and release, driven by the breath
  - Lester Horton = flat backs, lateral T and hinges that build long lines and strength
  - Merce Cunningham = chance procedures, with dance and music made independently
choice: Which technique usually begins class seated on the floor?
  * Graham
  - Horton
  - Cunningham
choice: Which choreographer collaborated with composer John Cage and let chance decide the order of movements?
  - Graham
  - Horton
  * Cunningham
choice: A dancer tips the whole straight body sideways, balancing on one leg with arms and leg extended in a long line. This shape, the lateral T, comes from
  - Graham
  * Horton
  - Cunningham

## Main: Comparison chart
chart: Choreographer | Core principle | Signature moves | Use of the floor | Relationship to music
  rows: 3
  key: Graham: contraction and release from the breath; spirals and Graham falls; class begins seated on the floor, falls go to the floor and back up; music usually commissioned to support the drama. Horton: strength and long lines through the whole body; flat backs, lateral T, hinges, deep lunges, fortifications; some floor work for strengthening, mostly standing; music varied. Cunningham: movement for its own sake, ordered by chance; curve, arch, tilt and twist of the spine, quick footwork, any point on stage can be the focus; mostly upright with little floor work; dance and music made independently and joined in performance.
write: Which of the three techniques puts the most emphasis on the floor? Use evidence from the chart.
  lines: 3
  key: Graham: class begins seated on the floor with breathing and contraction exercises, and Graham falls take dancers to the floor and back up.

## Analyze
write: Graham's technique has been called dramatic and expressive. How do contraction and release help a dancer show emotion?
  lines: 3
  key: The contraction curls the torso inward from the pelvis, like a sob, a gasp or a blow to the body, and the release opens it again; because the movement starts in the center of the body with the breath, it reads as an inner feeling made visible.
write: Cunningham treated movement itself as the subject of his dances, rather than a story. How does chance support that approach?
  lines: 3
  key: Chance (tossing coins or dice to decide order, direction or timing) keeps the choreographer's personal story or emotion from shaping the dance, so the audience looks at the movement for its own sake and makes its own meaning.

## Stretch
write: Choose one technique to warm up a cast for a dance about strength and endurance. Defend your choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a technique and links specific features to the dance, for example Horton, because its flat backs, lunges and fortification exercises build the full-body strength and stamina the piece needs.

# 7. Judgment Plus Evidence
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, a live or recorded dance performance chosen by the teacher
ican: I can support each judgment in a dance review with specific evidence from the performance.
directions: Sort the warm-up statements into judgments and evidence. Study the model. Then watch the performance, fill in the planning chart with one judgment and two pieces of evidence for each area, and write your review.
mistakes: Students offer a second judgment as "evidence" ("It was powerful because it was moving"). Evidence names something an audience member could point to: a step, a count, a formation, a level, a costume, a sound, a light change. Watch for evidence that is true but does not support the judgment it sits under.
notes: A judgment is an opinion about quality or effect; evidence is an observable detail. Watch the performance twice if you can: once for an overall response, once to collect evidence. A dance from earlier in the module works well, or any 3 to 5 minute piece. The model is written about an imagined piece so it does not give away answers for the class performance.

## Warm up: Judgment or evidence?
table: Statement | Judgment or evidence?
  - The ending felt hopeful. | {judgment}
  - In the last 8 counts, all five dancers rise from the floor and reach toward the upstage light. | {evidence}
  - The partnering section was the strongest part of the dance. | {judgment}
  - The two soloists repeat the same arm gesture, one count apart, three times. | {evidence}
  - The costumes were distracting. | {judgment}
  - The music drops out completely for about 10 seconds while the dancers hold a group shape. | {evidence}

## Model
example: **Judgment:** The opening builds tension well.
  **Evidence 1:** The dancers enter one at a time from the same downstage-left corner, so the stage fills slowly.
  **Evidence 2:** Each dancer freezes in a low crouch until the last one arrives, and the cello holds one long note the whole time.
  **Why it works:** The slow build and the held note make the audience wait for something to break.

choice: Which piece of evidence best supports the judgment "The duet shows two people who cannot agree"?
  - The duet is about two minutes long.
  * The dancers keep turning their backs as soon as the other reaches out.
  - The duet was my favorite part of the piece.
choice: A reviewer writes, "The lighting was beautiful because it looked really nice." What is wrong with this support?
  - It describes the lighting in too much detail.
  - It is about lighting instead of dancing.
  * It repeats the judgment instead of naming something the audience could see.

## Main: Plan your review
text: Performance: ______________________________ Choreographer or company: ____________________
chart: My judgment | Evidence 1 (what I saw or heard) | Evidence 2 (what I saw or heard)
  labels: Choreography; Performance (the dancers); Music, costumes or light
  height: 1.0
  key: Answers vary. Each row should hold one clear judgment and two specific, observable details (counts, steps, formations, levels, sounds, light changes) that support that judgment, not new opinions.
write: Write your review. Give three paragraphs, one for each row of the chart: start each with your judgment, then give both pieces of evidence and explain how they support it.
  lines: 14
  starter: In [title], [choreographer] ...
  key: Answers vary. A strong review states each judgment in its own paragraph, follows it with two specific pieces of evidence from the performance, and explains in a sentence how the evidence supports the judgment. It uses dance vocabulary (levels, formations, dynamics, unison, canon) and avoids "I liked it" statements without support.

## Stretch
write: Choose your weakest piece of evidence. Rewrite it so it is more specific.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong revision adds where, when or how: for example "The dancers moved fast" becomes "In the middle section, the dancers sprint across the diagonal in pairs, landing every jump on count 1."

# 20. Persuasive Arc Outline
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, your group's issue and any movement material you have made so far
ican: I can outline a dance's arc so each stage moves the audience toward my message.
directions: Match each stage of a persuasive arc to what it does. Study the model row. Then outline your own dance: for each stage, write what the audience should understand and the movement evidence that will show it.
mistakes: Students describe the issue in words ("people waste water") instead of naming movement. Every box in the evidence column should hold something a viewer could see: a motif, a formation, a level, a dynamic, a relationship, a use of space. Watch for a "turn" that is just the end of the tension; the turn is a change in direction, the moment something shifts.
notes: The four stages here (setup, tension, turn, resolution) follow a classic story arc and work for most issue pieces. A persuasive dance does not need words: the arc itself argues by showing a problem, making the audience feel its cost, and then showing a change. Groups who want an open ending can make the resolution a question, but it should still be a deliberate choice. Keep issues school appropriate and approved in advance.

## Warm up: Stages of the arc
match: Match each stage to what it does.
  - Setup = shows the world and the people before the problem grows
  - Tension = makes the problem bigger and lets the audience feel its cost
  - Turn = the moment something changes direction
  - Resolution = shows the new state of things, or leaves the audience with a clear question
choice: In a dance about phone use, dancers stop moving in unison and each one turns away to face a different direction. Which stage is this most likely to be?
  - Setup
  * Tension
  - Resolution

## Model row
example: **Issue:** litter in a city park. **Stage:** Turn.
  **The audience should understand:** One person's choice can change what the group does.
  **Movement evidence:** One dancer stops, picks up an imagined object with a slow, sustained reach, and holds it overhead. The others freeze, then one at a time copy the reach in canon.

## Main: My arc
text: Our issue: ________________________________________________
text: The message we want the audience to leave with: ______________________________
chart: What the audience should understand | Movement evidence (motif, formation, level, dynamics, relationship) | About how long
  labels: Setup; Tension; Turn; Resolution
  height: 1.15
  key: Answers vary. A strong outline gives one clear idea per stage and names specific movement for each: for example a smooth unison circle in the setup, broken formations and sharp dynamics in the tension, a single dancer's change that others answer in the turn, and a new formation or a return of the opening motif changed in the resolution. Timings should add up to the length of the piece.
write: Which movement idea returns, changed, from the setup to the resolution? How does the change show your message?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one motif or formation and the change, for example "The opening circle returns at the end, but now it is wider and includes the dancer who was left out, showing the group has changed."

## Stretch
write: Imagine an audience member who disagrees with your message. Which stage of your arc is most likely to change their mind, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers point to the tension or the turn, because seeing the cost of a problem or watching a character choose differently can persuade without lecturing, and they name the specific movement that does the work.

# 21. Relationships on Stage
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils, open floor space
ican: I can map how dancers relate to each other on stage to communicate an issue.
directions: Choose two or three dancers from your issue piece. Map 5 moments on the stage diagrams (X for each dancer, an arrow for the way they face, a dotted line for where they travel). Name the relationship in each moment and explain what it shows about your issue.
mistakes: Students show relationships only with distance (close or far). Push them to use facing, levels, timing and touch-free contact such as mirroring or shadowing. "Support" should not mean lifting: in this unit it means one dancer framing, steadying or echoing another.
notes: Relationship words to post: lead and follow, mirror, shadow, oppose (counter), support, question and answer, meet and part. Support moments must stay safe: no lifts or weight-bearing without trained instruction; use hand-to-hand counterbalances only if they have been taught. Have pairs perform each moment while the class guesses the relationship before students draw.

## Warm up: Name the relationship
table: What you see | Relationship (lead, follow, oppose, support)
  - Dancer A starts a phrase; B copies it one count later, right behind A. | {follow}
  - A and B face each other at opposite ends of a diagonal and push toward each other without touching. | {oppose}
  - A sinks to a low level; B kneels beside A and slowly helps A rise by offering a steadying hand. | {support}
  - A changes direction and the group turns to go where A goes. | {lead}

## Main: Map 5 moments
text: My issue: ________________________________________
draw: Moment 1: Lead. Upstage at the top. | 1.25
write: What does moment 1 show about your issue?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer ties the leader to the issue, for example "One student starts a recycling habit and the others notice."
draw: Moment 2: Follow | 1.25
write: What does moment 2 show?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; for example "The others copy her one count late, showing change spreading slowly."
draw: Moment 3: Oppose | 1.25
write: What does moment 3 show?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; for example "Two dancers move in opposite directions on a diagonal, showing people who resist the change."
draw: Moment 4: Support | 1.25
write: What does moment 4 show?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; for example "One dancer steadies another who is at a low level, showing help from a friend."
draw: Moment 5: My choice | 1.25
write: What does moment 5 show?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the relationship (for example mirror or unison) and how it resolves the issue.

## Stretch
write: Which moment uses facing or level most powerfully? Explain how it changes the meaning compared with simply standing near each other.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the moment and the choice, for example "In moment 3, the dancers face away from each other at different levels, so even though they are close, they seem disconnected."

# 33. Dancing with Architecture
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, your site, three short phrases you already know (8 to 16 counts each)
ican: I can adapt movement phrases to the architecture of a site and explain how the changes affect meaning.
directions: First list what each feature offers a dancer. Then adapt three phrases you know: one to stairs, one to a column, one to a window. Record the original, the adapted version and what changed. Answer the questions.
mistakes: Students simply perform the studio phrase next to the feature. An adaptation changes the movement because of the feature: levels on stairs, hiding and revealing around a column, framing through a window. No climbing on railings, ledges or window sills, and no hanging from columns.
notes: Get permission to use the site and check every surface for safety before students work: dry steps, no traffic or doors opening into the space, and room for an audience to watch safely. Have students mark through phrases at half speed on stairs first. The four movement elements used in the chart are space, time, energy (dynamics) and relationship. If your school has no columns or large windows, any repeating vertical shape (posts, pillars, a row of trees) or any frame (a doorway, an archway) works.

## Warm up: What does the feature offer?
table: Feature | One movement possibility it offers | One safety rule
  - Stairs | {levels: dancers on different steps, or a phrase that travels up or down one step per count} | {stay on the steps, never on the railing; mark it slowly first}
  - Column | {hide and reveal: disappear behind it and appear on the other side; trace its height with a reach} | {no climbing or hanging; keep hands light}
  - Window | {framing: the audience sees only part of the body; dance inside or outside the frame} | {no sitting or standing on the sill}

## Main: Adapt three phrases
chart: Original phrase (counts and main moves) | Adapted at the site | What changed (space, time, energy, relationship)
  labels: Phrase 1: stairs; Phrase 2: column; Phrase 3: window
  height: 1.35
  key: Answers vary. Strong rows describe the original in counts and moves, then the adapted version precisely (for example "the 8-count arm sequence now travels down one step on each odd count, ending seated on the bottom step"), and name which element changed and how.
write: Which adaptation changed the meaning of the phrase the most? Explain what the audience understands now that it did not before.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Phrase 2: in the studio it was a simple turn, but when I disappear behind the column on count 4 and reappear on count 6, it reads as hiding and coming back, as if I am unsure about being seen.
write: Where will the audience stand or sit to see all three adaptations? What will they miss from that spot?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific viewing spot, explains what it shows well (for example the full staircase), and admits a trade-off, such as the window phrase being seen only from outside.

## Stretch
write: Choose one feature and describe how your phrase would change if the audience watched from above, such as from a balcony or upper window.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers notice that from above, floor patterns, pathways and group shapes become clearer while levels and facing are harder to read, and they change the phrase to use those strengths.
