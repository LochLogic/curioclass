# 1. First Kitchen Setup
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan a safe, practical kitchen and balanced meals for living on my own.
directions: Read about Jordan's new apartment. Plan 10 kitchen tools, 15 pantry staples and 3 easy balanced meals. Then answer the food safety questions.
mistakes: Students often list gadgets (a waffle maker, an air fryer) before basics. Ask, "Could Jordan cook most meals without this?" Watch for meals with no vegetables or fruit, and for raw meat and vegetables sharing one cutting board without washing.
notes: Jordan is a story character. Balanced meals follow MyPlate (USDA): half the plate fruits and vegetables, about a quarter grains (half of them whole), about a quarter protein, plus dairy or a calcium-rich food. Food safety numbers follow FoodSafety.gov and the USDA: refrigerator at 40 °F or below, freezer at 0 °F, chicken and turkey cooked to 165 °F, ground beef to 160 °F, leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours (1 hour if it is above 90 °F). The sheet uses no calorie numbers by design. Pairs well with a budget lesson in Personal Finance.

## Warm up: Meet Jordan
text: Jordan is 19 and just moved into a small apartment with a two-burner stove, an oven, a fridge and very little money left over. Jordan works days and wants to cook simple meals at home most nights instead of buying takeout.

Strong reasons tie each tool to many meals or to safety.

Look for a mix across all four columns and foods that keep well or are frozen.

# 7. School Meals Policy Argument
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can write an evidence-based argument about a school nutrition policy, including a counterargument.
directions: Read the policy question and the two sources. Decide your position. Use the frames to plan and write your argument: a claim, evidence from both sources, your reasoning, a counterargument and a rebuttal.
mistakes: Students often drop in evidence without explaining it ("Source 1 says 40%."). Each piece of evidence needs a sentence that connects it to the claim. Watch for counterarguments that are too weak to be worth answering; the strongest arguments take on the other side's best point. Both positions can earn full credit.
notes: The district and both sources are made up for Curio Class, so students argue from the same evidence; the numbers are realistic but not real data. Real context: since 2022, several states, starting with California and Maine, have passed laws to offer free school meals to all students, and many schools in high-need areas offer free meals to all through the federal Community Eligibility Provision. Rubric (4 points each): clear claim; evidence from both sources, cited; reasoning that connects evidence to the claim; fair counterargument with a rebuttal; clear, organized writing.

## Warm up: The question
text: **Policy question:** Should the Lakeview School District give free breakfast and lunch to every student, whatever their family's income, instead of only to students whose families qualify?
source: Source 1: Pretend report from the Lakeview District Nutrition Office (made up for this sheet)
  In a two-year pilot, 4 Lakeview schools served free meals to all students. Students eating school lunch rose from 52% to 81%. Nurse visits for headaches and stomachaches before lunch fell by 18%. Counselors reported that students no longer avoided the lunch line because of "free lunch" labels. Paperwork for meal applications dropped, freeing about 300 staff hours a year.
source: Source 2: Pretend letter from the Lakeview Taxpayers' Group (made up for this sheet)
  Free meals for every student would cost the district about $1.9 million a year more than the current program, even after federal money. That is roughly the cost of 20 teachers. Many families who can afford lunch would get it for free. The district could instead make applications easier and reach more of the families who qualify but have not signed up, at a much lower cost.

## Main: Plan your argument
write: **Claim.** State your position in one sentence.
  lines: 2
  starter: The Lakeview School District should ...
  key: The Lakeview School District should give free breakfast and lunch to every student. (Or: should keep income-based meals and make it easier for eligible families to sign up.)
write: **Evidence 1** from Source 1 or 2. Quote or paraphrase it and cite the source.
  lines: 2
  starter: According to ...
  key: According to the district's nutrition office, students eating school lunch rose from 52% to 81% in the pilot schools (Source 1).
write: **Evidence 2** from the other source.
  lines: 2
  starter: In addition, ...
  key: In addition, the Taxpayers' Group points out that the program would cost about $1.9 million more a year (Source 2), but Source 1 shows some of that is offset by about 300 staff hours saved on paperwork.
write: **Reasoning.** Explain how your evidence supports your claim.
  lines: 3
  starter: This matters because ...
  key: This matters because students who eat lunch are less hungry, which may explain fewer nurse visits, and they can focus in class. Removing labels means no student skips a meal out of embarrassment.
write: **Counterargument.** State the strongest point on the other side, fairly.
  lines: 2
  starter: Some people argue that ...
  key: Some people argue that the $1.9 million cost, about 20 teachers, is too much, especially when many families can afford to pay.
write: **Rebuttal.** Answer the counterargument.
  lines: 3
  starter: However, ...
  key: However, a hungry student cannot learn well even with more teachers, and income-based programs miss families who qualify but do not apply or are embarrassed to. The district could look for state funding, as several states have done, to cover the cost.

## Write it
write: Write your argument as one or two paragraphs, using your plan. End with a concluding sentence.
  lines: 12
  key: Model paragraph: The Lakeview School District should give free breakfast and lunch to every student. In the district's own pilot, the share of students eating lunch rose from 52% to 81%, and nurse visits for hunger-type complaints fell 18% (Source 1). When every student eats for free, nobody is labeled, so students who need meals actually eat them. Some argue that the extra $1.9 million a year, about the cost of 20 teachers, is too much (Source 2). However, teachers cannot teach hungry students well, and the pilot saved about 300 staff hours of paperwork. Lakeview should adopt free meals for all and seek state funding to cover the cost, because fed students are ready to learn.

# 20. Patient Privacy Rights
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain my rights under federal health privacy rules, including who can see my records and how to request them.
directions: Read the summary of federal health privacy rights. Highlight each right. Then answer the six questions, citing paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students often believe HIPAA covers every organization that has health information. It applies to most health care providers, health plans and their business partners, but not to employers, most schools, life insurers or most health apps. Watch for "my doctor can never share anything without my permission": providers can share for treatment, payment and certain public health and legal reasons.
notes: The passage is a paraphrased summary written for Curio Class, based on the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) consumer page "Your Rights Under HIPAA"; it is not a quotation, so check the current HHS page (hhs.gov/hipaa) before teaching. Providers generally must respond to a records request within 30 days and may extend once by up to 30 more days. Rules for minors' records vary by state and by type of care (for example, some reproductive and mental health care); in general a parent or guardian acts for a minor until 18. School health records kept by a school are usually covered by FERPA, not HIPAA.

## Read
passage: Your Health Privacy Rights: A Summary | numbered
  A federal law called HIPAA, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, includes a Privacy Rule that protects your health information. The rule sets limits on who can look at and receive your health records, and it gives you rights over that information. These rules apply to most doctors, nurses, clinics, hospitals, pharmacies and health insurance plans, and to companies that work for them.
  Some organizations that may have health information about you do not have to follow the Privacy Rule. These include employers, life insurance companies, most schools and school districts, many law enforcement agencies, and most health and fitness apps.
  You have the right to see and get a copy of your health records. You usually ask the provider or health plan in writing. In most cases, you must get your copy within 30 days, and you can be charged a reasonable fee for the cost of copying and mailing.
  You have the right to ask your provider to correct information in your records that you think is wrong or missing. You also have the right to get a notice that explains how your information may be used and shared, and to get a list of certain times your information was shared.
  Your information can be used and shared without your written permission for some purposes: to treat you and coordinate your care, to bill and get paid for your care, to protect public health, such as reporting certain diseases, and to make certain reports required by law. It can be shared with family or friends who are involved in your care unless you object.
  Your provider cannot give your information to your employer, or use it to market or sell products, without your written permission. You can ask to be contacted at a different address or phone number, and you can ask your provider to limit what it shares.
  If you believe your rights were violated, you can file a complaint with your provider or health plan, or with the Office for Civil Rights at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

## Questions
choice: Which organization must follow the HIPAA Privacy Rule?
  - A company where you work part-time
  * A hospital that treated you
  - A fitness app on your phone
choice: How long does a provider usually have to give you a copy of your records?
  - 24 hours
  - 1 year
  * 30 days
write: Maya, 19, wants a copy of her records from her doctor. Explain how she should ask and what it may cost. Cite the paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: Paragraph 3: she should ask the doctor's office in writing. She should usually get the copy within 30 days and may be charged a reasonable fee for copying and mailing.
write: Name two reasons a doctor can share your information without your written permission. Cite the paragraph.
  lines: 2
  key: Paragraph 5: to treat you or coordinate your care, to bill for your care, to protect public health (such as reporting certain diseases), or to make reports required by law.
choice: Leo's manager calls his clinic to ask why Leo missed work. What should the clinic do?
  * Refuse to share Leo's information without his written permission
  - Share it, because employers pay for health insurance
  - Share it, as long as Leo is over 18
write: Leo thinks a pharmacy shared his information wrongly. What can he do? Cite the paragraph.
  lines: 2
  key: Paragraph 7: he can file a complaint with the pharmacy or his health plan, or with the Office for Civil Rights at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

## Stretch
write: A popular sleep-tracking app collects data about your heart rate and sleep. Using paragraph 2, explain why your data in that app may have less protection than your doctor's records, and one thing you could do about it.
  lines: 3
  key: Most health and fitness apps are not covered by the HIPAA Privacy Rule, so its limits on sharing may not apply. Read the app's privacy policy and settings, limit what it collects or shares, and choose apps that do not sell data.

# 21. Appointment Prep Sheet
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can prepare for a health care visit and make sure I understand the plan.
directions: Read about Rosa's upcoming appointment. Use the frames to help her describe her symptoms, plan her questions and repeat back the doctor's instructions.
mistakes: Students often write vague symptom descriptions ("my head hurts a lot"). Push for when it started, how often, how bad on a 0 to 10 scale, and what makes it better or worse. Watch for students who skip the teach-back; repeating the plan in your own words is how patients catch misunderstandings.
notes: Rosa is a story character; do not ask students about their own health. The teach-back method (repeat back what you heard in your own words) is recommended by AHRQ, the federal Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. The three core questions in Part 2 follow common patient-safety guidance: what is my main problem, what do I need to do, and why is it important. At 18, a patient can schedule and attend visits on their own; a young adult can still bring a trusted person.

## Warm up: Meet Rosa
text: Rosa is 18 and making her first doctor's appointment on her own. For three weeks she has had headaches most afternoons, usually after school and work. They feel like a tight band around her forehead, about 6 out of 10. Water and rest help a little. She has been sleeping about 5 hours a night and drinks two energy drinks a day. She takes no medicines except an over-the-counter pain reliever a few times a week. She is allergic to penicillin.

## Part 1: Describe the symptoms
write: When did it start, and how often does it happen?
  lines: 2
  starter: The headaches started ...
  key: The headaches started about three weeks ago and happen most afternoons.
write: Where is it, what does it feel like, and how bad is it from 0 to 10?
  lines: 2
  starter: It feels like ...
  key: It feels like a tight band around my forehead, about a 6 out of 10.
write: What makes it better or worse, and what has she tried?
  lines: 2
  starter: It gets better when ...
  key: Water and rest help a little. It comes after school and work. I take an over-the-counter pain reliever a few times a week.
write: What other facts should Rosa share, even if she is not sure they matter?
  lines: 2
  key: She sleeps about 5 hours a night, drinks two energy drinks a day, is allergic to penicillin, and takes a pain reliever a few times a week.

## Part 2: Questions to ask
write: Write three questions Rosa should ask before she leaves.
  lines: 4
  starter: 1. What is my main problem? 2. ...
  key: What is my main problem? What do I need to do? Why is it important for me to do this? Others: Could my sleep or caffeine be causing this? When should I come back or call? What signs mean I should get care right away?
choice: What should Rosa bring to the appointment?
  - Nothing; the doctor will have everything
  * Her ID, insurance card if she has one, a list of medicines and allergies, and her symptom notes
  - Only a friend to do the talking

## Part 3: Repeat it back
text: The doctor says: "These sound like tension headaches. Aim for 8 to 10 hours of sleep, cut back to no more than one energy drink a day and then stop, and drink water through the day. Take the pain reliever no more than 2 days a week. Keep a headache diary for 4 weeks and come back then. If you get the worst headache of your life, a stiff neck with fever, or trouble seeing or speaking, go to the emergency room."
write: Write Rosa's teach-back: repeat the plan in her own words.
  lines: 4
  starter: So what I'm hearing is ...
  key: So what I'm hearing is these are probably tension headaches. I should sleep 8 to 10 hours, cut back to one energy drink a day and then stop, drink water, take the pain reliever only 2 days a week or less, keep a headache diary for 4 weeks, and come back. If I get the worst headache of my life, a stiff neck with fever, or trouble seeing or speaking, I go to the ER.

## Stretch
write: Why does the teach-back help Rosa more than just nodding and saying "okay"?
  lines: 3
  key: Saying the plan in her own words lets the doctor catch anything she misunderstood (like how often to take the pain reliever), helps her remember it, and gives her a chance to ask questions before she leaves.

# 33. Comparing Cannabis Policies
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can compare state cannabis laws and evaluate how well they protect young people and public safety.
directions: Read the summaries of two pretend states' cannabis laws. Fill in the comparison chart, then answer the six questions.
mistakes: Students often read "legal for adults" as "safe" or "legal for everyone." In every state with legal adult use, the age is 21, and driving while impaired by cannabis is illegal in all states. Watch for confusion between medical and adult use: State B allows only medical use with a card.
notes: State A and State B are made up for Curio Class so the sheet stays accurate as real laws change; their rules are modeled on common features of real state laws. Real facts: every U.S. state that allows adult use sets the age at 21; driving while impaired by cannabis is illegal in every state; cannabis has been illegal under federal law, and its federal status was under review in 2024 and 2025, so check the current status before teaching. THC is the main chemical in cannabis that causes a high; today's products can be much stronger than in past decades, and high-potency use is linked to a higher risk of psychosis and of cannabis use disorder, especially for teens (NIDA). Students may have strong views or family experience; keep the discussion on the policies.

## Read: Two pretend states
source: Pretend summary: State A cannabis law (made up for this sheet)
  Adults 21 and older may buy and have up to 1 ounce of cannabis for personal use from licensed stores. Driving while impaired is illegal; a blood test showing 5 nanograms of THC per milliliter or more counts as impaired. Edibles are limited to 10 mg of THC per serving and 100 mg per package; there is no limit on the THC in flower or concentrates. Packages must be child-resistant, may not show cartoons, and stores may not advertise within 1,000 feet of a school. 25% of cannabis tax money funds youth prevention programs.
source: Pretend summary: State B cannabis law (made up for this sheet)
  Cannabis is legal only for medical use. Patients 18 and older need a doctor's recommendation and a state card; patients under 18 also need a parent or guardian as caregiver and a second doctor's approval. Drivers under 21 may have no THC at all in their blood (zero tolerance); for drivers 21 and older, an officer's evidence of impairment is used. Products may not be more than 30% THC. All cannabis advertising is banned. People under 21 who have cannabis without a card pay a civil fine and must complete a drug education course.

## Main: Compare the laws
table: Feature | State A | State B
  - Who may legally use it | {adults 21 and older (adult use)} | {medical patients with a card only}
  - Youngest legal user | {21} | {under 18, with a caregiver and two doctors}
  - Driving rule | {5 ng/mL of THC or more counts as impaired} | {zero THC for drivers under 21; evidence of impairment for 21+}
  - Potency limits | {10 mg per edible serving, 100 mg per package; none on flower or concentrates} | {no product over 30% THC}
  - Advertising | {no ads within 1,000 feet of schools; no cartoons} | {all ads banned}
  - Other youth protections | {child-resistant packages; 25% of taxes to youth prevention} | {fine plus drug education course for under-21 possession}

## Questions
choice: Which state allows a 25-year-old without a medical card to buy cannabis legally?
  * State A
  - State B
  - Both states
  - Neither state
choice: A 19-year-old in State B drives with a small amount of THC in their blood but seems unimpaired. What does the law say?
  - It is legal because they seem fine
  - It is legal because they are over 18
  * It is illegal because drivers under 21 may have no THC at all
choice: Which State A rule leaves a gap in limiting potency?
  - The limit of 10 mg per edible serving
  * There is no limit on THC in flower or concentrates
  - Packages must be child-resistant
write: Which state's advertising rule protects teens more? Explain why advertising matters for youth.
  lines: 3
  key: State B, which bans all advertising. Ads can make a product look normal, fun or safe and increase young people's interest, and State A's 1,000-foot rule still lets teens see ads elsewhere and online.
write: Explain one strength of each state's approach to youth protection.
  lines: 3
  key: State A: child-resistant packaging, no cartoons and 25% of taxes for youth prevention programs. State B: zero tolerance for young drivers, a full advertising ban and education instead of a criminal record for under-21 possession.
write: If you were advising State A's lawmakers, what one change would you recommend to better protect young people? Use evidence from the sheet.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Set a THC limit on flower and concentrates, because high-potency products are linked to a higher risk of psychosis and cannabis use disorder in teens, and State A's only potency limit is on edibles. Other strong answers: a full ad ban like State B's, or a zero-tolerance driving rule for under 21.

## Stretch
write: Some people argue that legal stores reduce teen use because they check IDs, while others argue legal sales make cannabis seem normal. What kind of evidence would you need to decide?
  lines: 3
  key: Data on teen use from the same state before and after the law, compared with similar states that did not change their laws, from large, repeated surveys run by neutral sources (such as the CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey or Monitoring the Future), across several years.
