# 1. Tug-of-War Arrows
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can draw forces as arrows and tell if they are balanced or unbalanced.
directions: Read each scene. In the box, make a quick stick-figure sketch of the scene. Draw an arrow for each push or pull: the arrow points the way the force goes, and a stronger force gets a longer arrow. Then fill in the blanks.
mistakes: Students often draw arrows the same length for every force, or point a pull arrow toward the person pulling from the wrong side. Check: equal forces, equal arrows. In scene 5, students forget the table pushes up on the book; it is not moving, so something must balance its weight.
notes: Use one square of the ruler (1 cm) for each unit of strength so arrow lengths can be checked: a force of 3 is a 3 cm arrow. Balanced forces do not change an object's motion; unbalanced forces make it start, stop, speed up, slow down or turn.

## Warm up
example: Two dogs pull a rope. Each pulls with a strength of 3, in opposite directions.
  Draw: an arrow 3 cm long pointing left, and one 3 cm long pointing right. Balanced: the rope does not move.
fill:
  - When forces on an object are equal and opposite, they are {balanced}.
  - When one force is stronger, the forces are {unbalanced} and the object's motion changes.

## Main: Six scenes
text: **Scene 1.** Tug-of-war: the Red team pulls left with a strength of 4. The Blue team pulls right with a strength of 4.
draw: Scene 1 | 1.2
fill: Balanced or unbalanced? {balanced}  Which way does the rope move? {it does not move}
text: **Scene 2.** Tug-of-war: the Red team pulls left with a strength of 6. The Blue team pulls right with a strength of 3.
draw: Scene 2 | 1.2
fill: Balanced or unbalanced? {unbalanced}  Which way does the rope move? {left, toward Red}
text: **Scene 3.** Two friends push a heavy box from opposite sides. Each pushes with a strength of 3.
draw: Scene 3 | 1.2
fill: Balanced or unbalanced? {balanced}  Does the box move? {no}
text: **Scene 4.** Two friends push a box from the same side, to the right. Each pushes with a strength of 3.
draw: Scene 4 | 1.2
fill: Balanced or unbalanced? {unbalanced}  Which way does the box move? {right}
text: **Scene 5.** A book sits still on a table. Its weight pulls down with a strength of 2.
draw: Scene 5: draw the pull down and the table's push up | 1.2
fill: How strong is the table's push up? {2}  Balanced or unbalanced? {balanced}
text: **Scene 6.** A wagon: Maya pulls right with a strength of 5. Her little brother pulls left with a strength of 2.
draw: Scene 6 | 1.2
fill: Balanced or unbalanced? {unbalanced}  Which way does the wagon move? {right, toward Maya}

## Stretch
write: In scene 2, how could the Blue team make the forces balanced? Give the strength they would need.
  lines: 2
  key: Pull harder, with a total strength of 6 (add 3 more), so both teams pull with 6 in opposite directions.

# 5. Pendulum Swing Graph
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can use patterns in motion data to predict how an object will move next.
directions: Our class timed pendulums with different string lengths. Each group counted how many full swings (over and back) a washer made in 30 seconds. Find the middle value for each length, graph it, and use the pattern to predict.
mistakes: Students often count every pass as a swing; a full swing is over and back to where it started. On the graph, some plot the string length on the side axis; length goes along the bottom. When predicting, watch for students who keep subtracting the same amount: the drop gets smaller as the string gets longer, so the curve flattens out.
notes: The table shows typical class data for a metal washer on a string, pulled a short way to the side and let go. If your class collected its own data, cross out these numbers and use yours. Real pendulums follow this pattern closely: the length of the string, not how heavy the washer is, sets how fast it swings. From the physics, a 120 cm pendulum makes about 13 to 14 full swings in 30 seconds.

## Warm up: Find the middle value
text: The middle value is the number in the middle when you put the three in order.
table: String length (cm) | Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 3 | Middle value
  - 20 | 34 | 33 | 32 | {33}
  - 40 | 24 | 23 | 24 | {24}
  - 60 | 19 | 20 | 19 | {19}
  - 80 | 17 | 16 | 17 | {17}
  - 100 | 15 | 15 | 14 | {15}

## Main: Graph the data
text: Plot a dot for each middle value. Then connect the dots with a smooth line.
graph: String length (cm) | 0 120 20 | Full swings in 30 seconds | 0 40 5
  key: 20,33 40,24 60,19 80,17 100,15
choice: What pattern do you see?
  - The longer the string, the more swings.
  * The longer the string, the fewer swings.
  - The swings stay the same for every string.
fill:
  - From 20 cm to 40 cm, the swings dropped by {9}.
  - From 80 cm to 100 cm, the swings dropped by {2}.
choice: Look at your two answers above. How does the line change as the string gets longer?
  - It drops faster and faster.
  - It goes back up.
  * It drops more slowly and starts to flatten out.

## Predict
write: Predict how many full swings a 120 cm pendulum would make in 30 seconds. Use the pattern to explain.
  lines: 2
  key: About 13 or 14. The swings keep going down as the string gets longer, but only by a little each time, so a bit fewer than 15.
write: A pendulum made 28 swings in 30 seconds. About how long was its string? How do you know?
  lines: 2
  key: About 30 cm (between 20 cm and 40 cm), because 28 is between 33 and 24 on the graph.

## Stretch: Change the weight
write: Sam taped a second washer on, so the pendulum was heavier, but kept the string 60 cm long. Sam counted 19 swings again. What does this tell you about what controls the swing?
  lines: 2
  key: The weight did not change the number of swings; the string length is what controls how fast a pendulum swings.

# 16. Bean in a Bag Journal
minutes: 15
materials: a clear zip-top bag, a paper towel, 3 dried beans (pinto or lima), water, tape, a sunny window, pencil, ruler
ican: I can observe and record how a bean plant changes as it begins its life cycle.
directions: Wet the paper towel so it is damp, not dripping. Fold it into the bag. Push 3 beans between the towel and the bag, about halfway down. Seal the bag and tape it to a window. Every other day, write the date, draw the best bean, and tell what is new.
mistakes: Students often expect the shoot to come out first. The root comes first and grows down; the shoot comes later and grows up. Some draw what they think a bean plant looks like instead of what they see: remind them to draw only what is in the bag today, and to measure the root with a ruler.
notes: Setting up takes about 15 minutes; after that, each journal entry takes about 5 minutes, every other day for 2 weeks. Typical timeline in a warm room: day 1 to 2, the beans swell; day 2 to 4, the seed coat splits and a root pokes out and turns down; day 4 to 6, the root grows longer and fuzzy root hairs appear; day 5 to 8, the shoot hooks up and the seed opens; day 8 to 14, the first green leaves open. A cold window slows it down. Add a spoonful of water if the towel dries out. If a bag gets moldy, throw it away without opening it. Remind students not to eat the beans.

## Warm up: Predict
choice: Which part do you think will come out of the bean first?
  * the root
  - the leaves
  - a flower
write: Which way do you think the root will grow? Why?
  lines: 1
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason. (Roots grow down, toward the pull of gravity.)

## Main: My bean journal
table: Day | Date | My drawing of the best bean | What is new? | Root (mm)
  height: 0.9
  - 1 | | | |
  - 3 | | | |
  - 5 | | | |
  - 7 | | | |
  - 9 | | | |
  - 11 | | | |
  - 14 | | | |
note: Typical entries: day 1, bean is bigger and the skin is wrinkly; day 3, the seed coat split and a small white root came out; day 5, the root is longer, pointing down, with fuzzy hairs; day 7, a curved shoot is growing up; days 9 to 14, green leaves open at the top. Root lengths vary.

## Look back
write: Put the stages in order: shoot grows up, root comes out, bean swells, leaves open.
  lines: 1
  key: Bean swells, root comes out, shoot grows up, leaves open.
fill:
  - The root grew {down} and the shoot grew {up}.
  - The fuzzy hairs on the root help it take in {water}.
write: Why does the root come out before the leaves? How does this help the young plant?
  lines: 2
  key: The root holds the plant in place and takes in water, which the plant needs before it can grow a shoot and leaves.

## Stretch: Turn it over
write: After the root is 3 cm long, turn the bag upside down for 2 days. Predict what the root will do. Then check.
  lines: 2
  key: The root tip bends and grows down again, because roots grow toward the pull of gravity.

# 22. Group Jobs Chart
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how living in a group helps animals survive.
directions: Read about four animal groups. Fill in the chart: what the group does, and how it helps the animals survive. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often copy a fact without saying how it helps ("bees make honey"). Push for the survival link: honey is stored food for winter. Some think one ant or bee is the boss that gives orders; a queen lays eggs but does not tell workers what to do.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). A strong chart row names a job and its payoff for survival: finding food, staying safe, raising young, or staying warm.

## Read
passage: Better Together
  Some animals live alone, but many live in groups. A group can do things that one animal could never do by itself.
  Ants live in colonies. Worker ants split up the jobs. Some find food and carry it home. Some care for the young ants. Others guard the nest and fight off enemies.
  Honeybees live in a hive with thousands of other bees. Worker bees collect nectar and pollen from flowers and turn nectar into honey. The honey is stored food for winter. In cold weather, the bees huddle in a tight ball and shiver their wing muscles to stay warm. The queen lays the eggs.
  Many small fish swim together in a school. When a hungry predator comes, the fish turn and flash all at once. It is hard for the predator to pick out one fish to chase. With so many eyes, someone in the school is likely to spot danger fast.
  Elephants live in herds of mothers, aunts, sisters and calves. The oldest female leads the herd. She remembers where to find water, even in a dry year. When lions come near, the adults stand in a ring around the calves to keep them safe.

## Chart
chart: Animal group | What the group does: its jobs and actions | How this helps the animals survive
  rows: 4
  key: Ants: workers share jobs: find food, care for young, guard the nest; more food is gathered and the nest and young stay safe. Honeybees: collect nectar and pollen, make and store honey, huddle and shiver in winter; stored food and warmth help the hive live through winter. Fish school: swim and turn together; a predator has trouble picking one fish, and many eyes spot danger. Elephant herd: the oldest female leads to water, adults ring the calves; the herd finds water in dry times and calves are protected.

## Questions
choice: Why is it hard for a predator to catch a fish in a school?
  - The fish in a school are bigger.
  * It is hard to pick out one fish to chase.
  - The fish hide in the sand.
choice: Why does it help the herd to have an old female leader?
  - She is the biggest elephant.
  * She remembers where to find water.
  - She can run the fastest.

## Stretch
write: Living in a group also has a cost. Name one problem a group might have that an animal living alone would not.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: the animals must share food, a sickness can spread quickly, or a big group is easier for predators to notice.

# 26. Kitten Litter Trait Tally
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can use data to show that young animals have traits like their family but are not all the same.
directions: Read the table about this litter of 8 kittens. Make a tally mark for each trait. Count the totals, then graph fur color.
mistakes: Students sometimes tally a kitten twice for the same trait or skip one. Have them check that every trait group adds up to 8. On the graph, watch for bars that start above zero or are not lined up with the number of the color.
notes: All 8 kittens have the same mother and were born in the same litter, yet they look different: that is variation. The table stands in for a picture of the litter; if you can, show a photo of a real mixed litter. The kittens are 12 weeks old on purpose: all kittens are born with blue eyes, and their adult eye color shows up when they are about 6 to 8 weeks old.

## Warm up: Meet the kittens
table: Kitten | Fur color | Eye color | Tail
  - 1 | orange | green | long
  - 2 | black | yellow | long
  - 3 | orange | yellow | short
  - 4 | gray | green | long
  - 5 | black | yellow | long
  - 6 | orange | green | long
  - 7 | white | blue | long
  - 8 | black | green | short

## Main: Tally the traits
table: Trait | Variation | Tally | Total
  - Fur color | orange | | {3}
  - Fur color | black | | {3}
  - Fur color | gray | | {1}
  - Fur color | white | | {1}

  - Eye color | yellow | | {3}
  - Eye color | blue | | {1}

## Think about it
choice: Which eye color is most common in this litter?
  - yellow
  - blue
  * green
choice: Which two fur colors are tied for the most?
  * orange and black
  - gray and white
  - black and white
write: These kittens are brothers and sisters from one litter. Why do you think they do not all look the same?
  lines: 2
  key: Each kitten gets a different mix of traits from its parents, so kittens in one family can look different from each other. This is called variation.

## Stretch: Check the totals
write: Add the totals for fur color. Then add the totals for tail. What do you notice, and why does it make sense?
  lines: 2
  key: Both add up to 8 (3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8 and 6 + 2 = 8). It makes sense because every kitten has exactly one fur color and one tail, and there are 8 kittens.

# 37. Town Weather Table
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can graph weather data and describe the pattern I see.
directions: This table shows the high temperature and the rain for every day of April in the town of Pine Hollow. Plot each day's high temperature as a dot, and connect the dots. Then add up the rain and describe the patterns.
mistakes: Students may plot the rain numbers on the temperature graph; only the highs go on the graph. Some read across the wrong row of the table: have them put a ruler under the row they are reading. When adding the rain, students often forget the days with 0.
notes: Pine Hollow is a made-up town, but the pattern is real for many places in the northern half of the United States in April: the highs climb as spring goes on, and rainy days are cooler, because clouds block sunlight. 25 mm is about 1 inch. The graph's side axis goes up by 4, with a light line at every 2 degrees, so odd temperatures fall halfway between lines.

  - 4 | 53 | 0 | 14 | 55 | 0 | 24 | 63 | 5
  - 5 | 55 | 0 | 15 | 58 | 0 | 25 | 60 | 10

  - The coolest day was day {2}, with a high of {46} °F.
  - It rained on {11} days this month.

## Describe the pattern
choice: What happened to the high temperatures from the start of April to the end?
  - They went down.
  * They went up, with some dips.
  - They stayed the same.
choice: Look at the rainy days. How were their highs?
  * cooler than the day before
  - warmer than the day before
  - always exactly 50 °F
write: Describe the pattern in Pine Hollow's April weather in two sentences. Use numbers from the table.
  lines: 3
  key: The highs got warmer during the month, from the 40s at the start (46 °F on day 2) to the 60s at the end (69 °F on day 29). Every time it rained, the high dropped a few degrees, then it warmed up again.

## Stretch: Predict
write: Pine Hollow has a picnic planned for May 1. Use the pattern to predict the high temperature. Will it be warmer or cooler than April 1?
  lines: 2
  key: About 66 to 70 °F, much warmer than April 1 (48 °F), because the highs kept climbing all month. Accept a prediction that follows the pattern.
