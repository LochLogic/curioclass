# 1. Shakuntala and Sanskrit Drama
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify the conventions of classical Sanskrit drama in a scene from Kalidasa's Shakuntala.
directions: Read the background and the four adapted passages from Shakuntala. Mark the moments where the play talks about itself or its audience. Then answer the questions, citing the passage numbers.
mistakes: Students often treat the stage director in the prologue as a character in the story. He is a member of the acting company speaking to the audience before the story begins; he steps into the story only through his final line, which hands the audience over to the king. Watch for students who read the jester as a villain or a fool: the vidushaka is the king's close friend, and his complaints about food and comfort are the joke.
notes: The four passages are adapted and condensed by Curio Class from the public-domain English translation by Arthur W. Ryder (1912), not quoted; check against the translation (available from public-domain libraries) if you want students to read Ryder's own words. Kalidasa is usually dated to about the 4th or 5th century CE. Conventions to discuss: the opening benediction (a blessing, called the nandi); the prologue led by the sutradhara (the stage director or manager of the company); the vidushaka (jester), a Brahmin friend of the king who speaks Prakrit, the everyday language, rather than Sanskrit; mixed verse and prose; and the expectation of a happy ending, since Sanskrit drama avoids tragedy. The Natyashastra, an ancient Sanskrit treatise on performance, describes these conventions.

## Background
text: Shakuntala (full title: The Recognition of Shakuntala) was written in Sanskrit by the poet Kalidasa in India about 1,600 years ago. King Dushyanta, out hunting, meets Shakuntala in a forest hermitage, and they fall in love. A curse makes the king forget her, and a lost ring finally brings back his memory. The play is still performed and taught around the world.

## Read: Four passages
source: Kalidasa, Shakuntala, adapted by Curio Class from the translation by Arthur W. Ryder (1912)
  [1] (The Benediction.) May the god Shiva bless everyone gathered here. He shows himself in eight forms that we can see: in water, the first thing created; in the fire of the sacrifice; in the priest who offers it; in the sun and moon that measure time; in the sky that carries sound; in the earth that holds every seed; and in the air that all living things breathe.
  [2] (The Prologue. Enter the STAGE DIRECTOR and an ACTRESS.) STAGE DIRECTOR: Enough of that. Tonight our audience is wise and well educated, and we will perform a new play by Kalidasa, called Shakuntala and the Ring of Recognition. Every actor must do his best. ACTRESS: You have trained us so well, sir, that nothing will go wrong. STAGE DIRECTOR: Then sing us a song to delight the audience. Sing about the summer, the season we are enjoying now. (The ACTRESS sings of summer days, cool baths and sweet-smelling flowers that women wear behind their ears.)
  [3] STAGE DIRECTOR: Well sung! Look, the whole audience sits still as a painting, held by your song. Your music carried me away, just as King Dushyanta here is carried away by the swift deer he is chasing. (They go out. The play begins with the KING in his chariot, hunting.)
  [4] (Act Two. Enter the JESTER, the king's friend Madhavya, groaning.) JESTER: Oh, I am worn out from being friends with a king who loves hunting! "There goes a deer!" "There goes a boar!" All day we run from forest to forest in the summer heat. We drink stream water, warm and tasting of leaves. We eat roasted meat at any hour, never a proper meal. And at night, I cannot even sleep, because the hunters go out before dawn shouting and blowing horns. I will just stand here leaning on my stick, as if I were lame, and maybe the king will let me rest.

## Warm up
fill: The play was written in the language called {Sanskrit}.

match: Match each convention to its passage.
  - A blessing that opens the performance = Passage 1
  - A member of the company speaks to the audience about the play = Passage 2
  - The prologue hands the audience into the story = Passage 3
  - A comic friend of the king complains about his comfort = Passage 4

## Main
write: In passage 2, what does the stage director tell the audience about the play and about them? Why might a playwright open this way?
  lines: 3
  key: He names the new play and its author and calls the audience wise and well educated. Flattering the audience wins them over, sets expectations and frames the play as a performance by a company, a convention of Sanskrit drama.
write: In passage 3, how does the stage director's last line connect the prologue to the story?
  lines: 3
  key: He compares being carried away by the song to King Dushyanta being carried away by the deer, so the audience's attention moves from the actress straight to the king hunting: the prologue's last line introduces the first scene.
choice: What makes the jester in passage 4 funny?
  - He is cruel to the animals.
  * He complains about food, heat and sleep, small comforts, while his friend is a king on a grand hunt.
  - He plots to take the king's throne.
  - He sings a long, sad song.
write: The jester pretends to be lame so the king will let him rest. What does this show about the jester's role in the play?
  lines: 3
  key: He is a comic schemer and the king's close, joking friend; he brings humor and everyday human wants into a play about kings and love, and he can tease the king in a way no one else can.
write: Compare the prologue in passages 2 and 3 to the way plays usually begin today.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Today plays usually begin straight in the story, or with a curtain speech about phones and exits; this prologue is part of the play, names the author, flatters the audience and performs a song before smoothly handing over to the story.

## Stretch
write: Sanskrit drama almost never ends in tragedy. Using the background, predict how the story of Shakuntala ends, and explain how the convention shapes the audience's experience.
  lines: 4
  key: The king's memory returns, and Shakuntala and the king are reunited (with their son). Because the audience expects a happy ending, they can enjoy the sorrow of separation without dread; the pleasure is in how the reunion happens, a feeling (rasa) the play builds toward.

# 21. Sunset in Five Cues
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a sequence of light cues that moves a scene through time.
directions: The scene: two old friends sit on a front porch from late afternoon until after dark. The scene runs 12 minutes. Write 5 cues that move the light from afternoon to night. For each cue, give when it starts, what the audience sees, the levels and the fade time.
mistakes: Students often make each cue a sudden jump. Sunsets are slow: long fade times (60 seconds or more) let the change happen without the audience noticing each cue. Watch for the scene going dark too early; the actors still need light on their faces at night, from a motivated source such as the porch lamp or the moon.
notes: Levels are percentages (0 to 100). Model cues are in the key; accept any sequence that moves logically from bright, warm and high to dim, cool and low, keeps faces visible, and uses a motivated night source. A practical is a light the actors can switch on in the scene, such as the porch lamp; give it a quick fade (snap or 1 to 2 seconds) because a lamp turns on at once.

## Example
example: **Cue 1, 0:00, afternoon:** sun through the trees from stage right, high and warm (pale amber) at 80%; porch area at 70%; sky backdrop pale blue at 60%. Fade: 5 seconds (preset as lights come up).

## Main: Your 5 cues
table: Cue | Starts at | What the audience sees | Levels | Fade time

  - 2 | {3:00} | {late afternoon, sun lower and more amber} | {sun 70, area 65, sky 60 warmer} | {60 sec}
  - 3 | {6:00} | {sunset, low orange sun, pink and orange sky} | {sun 50, area 55, sky 70} | {90 sec}
  - 4 | {9:00} | {dusk, sun gone, blue sky; a friend switches on the porch lamp} | {sun 0, area 40 cool, lamp 60, sky 40} | {120 sec, lamp 2 sec}
  - 5 | {11:30} | {night, moonlight and the lamp} | {moon 30, lamp 70, area 25, sky 15} | {60 sec}
write: Why should the fade times in cues 2 to 4 be long?
  lines: 2
  key: A real sunset changes slowly; long fades let the light drift without the audience noticing each change, so it feels like time passing rather than a lighting effect.

## Stretch
write: In your cue 4, a character turns on the porch lamp. Why is it useful to tie a cue to an action in the scene?
  lines: 3
  key: The action motivates the change, so it feels natural; the stage manager can call the cue exactly on the actor's move; and the brighter lamp gives the actors a believable source of face light as the sky goes dark.
