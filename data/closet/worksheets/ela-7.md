# 1. Setting Shapes Character: The Call of the Wild
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how a change in setting changes a character, citing the text.
directions: Read the background and the four excerpts from Chapter 1 of Jack London's The Call of the Wild (1903). Fill in the chart comparing Buck in each setting, then answer the questions with quotations and excerpt numbers.
mistakes: Students may say Buck is simply "broken" by the club. London says the opposite: "he was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken." Buck learns a new rule and adapts; his pride survives. Watch for answers that describe the setting without connecting it to how Buck thinks or acts.
notes: The excerpts follow Project Gutenberg eBook #215 word for word, with curly quotation marks made straight. Chapter 1 shows a dog being beaten with a club; the sheet summarizes that scene rather than quoting it, but preview it before students read the full chapter. Glossary: demesne, an estate; unwonted, unusual; primitive, early and basic, ruled by force; latent, hidden but present; uncowed, not frightened into giving up.

## Background
text: In 1897, gold is found in the Klondike in far northern Canada, and thousands of people rush north. They need strong dogs to pull sleds. Buck, a large, powerful dog, lives a comfortable life in California. One night a gardener's helper named Manuel, who needs money to pay gambling debts, secretly sells him. Buck is shipped north by train in a crate, and in a yard in Seattle a man in a red sweater beats him with a club until he stops attacking.

## Read: Four excerpts
source: Jack London, The Call of the Wild (1903), Chapter 1, "Into the Primitive"
  [1] "Buck lived at a big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley. Judge Miller's place, it was called."
  [2] "And over this great demesne Buck ruled. Here he was born, and here he had lived the four years of his life. [...] But Buck was neither house-dog nor kennel-dog. The whole realm was his."
  [3] "Buck had accepted the rope with quiet dignity. To be sure, it was an unwonted performance: but he had learned to trust in men he knew, and to give them credit for a wisdom that outreached his own."
  [4] "He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken. He saw, once for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club. He had learned the lesson, and in all his after life he never forgot it. That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway. The facts of life took on a fiercer aspect; and while he faced that aspect uncowed, he faced it with all the latent cunning of his nature aroused."

## Warm up
choice: In excerpts 1 and 2, what is Buck's place in his world?
  - He is a working dog who pulls sleds.
  * He rules the whole estate, like a king.
  - He is a stray who lives on the street.
  - He is one of many kennel dogs nobody notices.
choice: In excerpt 3, why does Buck accept the rope calmly?
  - He is too tired to fight.
  - He has been trained to wear a rope every day.
  * He trusts the men he knows and believes they are wiser than he is.
  - He wants to go on a trip.

## Main: Buck in two settings
chart: Judge Miller's place (California) | The yard of the man in the red sweater (Seattle)
  rows: 3
  key: Judge Miller's place: warm, safe, rich and "sun-kissed"; Buck rules it, is proud and trusting, and expects respect; "The whole realm was his" [2]; he trusts "men he knew" [3]. The yard: harsh, ruled by force; Buck learns he "stood no chance against a man with a club" [4]; he becomes watchful and cunning, but stays proud: "beaten [...] but [...] not broken" [4].
write: How does the setting in excerpts 1 and 2 shape the kind of dog Buck is at the start? Quote the text.
  lines: 3
  key: Because he was born and raised on a large, comfortable estate where "Buck ruled" and "The whole realm was his" [2], he is proud and confident and has never been challenged.
write: Excerpt 3 says Buck trusted "men he knew." How does that trust, learned at Judge Miller's place, put him in danger?
  lines: 3
  key: He has only known kind people, so he lets Manuel tie the rope on him "with quiet dignity" [3]. His trust in humans, which made sense at home, lets him be sold and kidnapped.
write: According to excerpt 4, what lesson does the new setting teach Buck? Use two quotations.
  lines: 4
  key: He learns that "he stood no chance against a man with a club" and that he now lives under "the reign of primitive law" [4], where force rules. He obeys the club but is "not broken."
write: Explain the phrase "he met the introduction halfway." What does it show about how Buck responds to change?
  lines: 3
  key: Buck does not just suffer the new rules; he adapts to them. He accepts the lesson and begins to use "the latent cunning of his nature," so the harsh setting brings out a wilder, smarter side of him.

## Stretch
write: The chapter is called "Into the Primitive." Predict how the frozen North might keep changing Buck. Support your prediction with a word or phrase from excerpt 4.
  lines: 4
  key: As the setting gets harsher, Buck will rely more on instinct and "cunning" than on trust in people, becoming wilder and fiercer; "The facts of life took on a fiercer aspect" suggests each new place will make him tougher.
# 25. Several Pieces of Evidence
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can support an analysis of a nonfiction text with several pieces of evidence.
directions: Read the article. Answer each question in a few sentences, citing at least two pieces of evidence for each one. Put quotations in quotation marks and add the paragraph number.
mistakes: Students often stop at one quotation, or cite two pieces that say the same thing. Push for evidence from different paragraphs or different kinds (a number, an event, a quotation). Watch for evidence that is copied but not explained: each piece needs a "this shows" sentence.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 330 words). Facts to know: the Pacific Railway Act was signed July 1, 1862; the Central Pacific built east from Sacramento and the Union Pacific west from Omaha; the lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869. Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's workforce; a widely reproduced photograph of the celebration does not show them.

## Read
passage: Joining the Coasts: The First Transcontinental Railroad
  **[1]** In the 1850s, crossing the United States was slow and dangerous. A trip by wagon from Missouri to California could take four to six months. In 1862, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. It gave two companies land and money to build a railroad across the West.
  **[2]** The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east. Almost at once, it ran into the Sierra Nevada mountains. Workers blasted tunnels through solid granite, sometimes moving only a few inches in a day. In winter, snow piled so high that crews dug tunnels under it to reach their work.
  **[3]** The company could not find enough workers, so it hired immigrants from China. Within a few years, Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's crews. They did the most dangerous jobs, including handling explosives, and they were paid less than white workers. Many died in accidents and avalanches.
  **[4]** The Union Pacific built west from Omaha, Nebraska, across the open plains. Its crews included Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans from both armies. They laid track quickly over flat land, but the railroad crossed land that belonged to Native nations such as the Lakota and Cheyenne, who fought to defend it.
  **[5]** On May 10, 1869, the two lines met at Promontory Summit in Utah Territory. Leaders tapped a golden spike into the last tie, and a telegraph sent one word across the country: "DONE."
  **[6]** The railroad changed the nation. A trip across the country now took about a week. Goods, mail and people moved quickly between the coasts, and new towns grew along the tracks. But the railroad also brought settlers and hunters onto Native lands, and the great buffalo herds that Plains nations depended on were nearly wiped out within twenty years. The famous photograph of the celebration shows smiling railroad men, but the Chinese workers who built much of the line are not in it.

## Warm up
choice: Which piece of evidence best shows that building through the Sierra Nevada was difficult?
  - "The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east."
  * "Workers blasted tunnels through solid granite, sometimes moving only a few inches in a day."
  - "They laid track quickly over flat land."
  - "A trip across the country now took about a week."

## Main: Analyze with several pieces of evidence
write: Why was the railroad so important to the country? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: It made travel far faster: a wagon trip "could take four to six months" [1], but afterward "A trip across the country now took about a week" [6]. Goods, mail and people moved quickly, and "new towns grew along the tracks" [6].
write: How does the author show that Chinese workers were essential to the railroad but treated unfairly? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: Essential: "Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's crews" and did "the most dangerous jobs" [3]. Unfair: they "were paid less than white workers" [3], and they "are not in" the famous photograph [6].
write: Compare the challenges the two companies faced. Cite evidence from at least two paragraphs.
  lines: 4
  key: The Central Pacific faced the mountains: granite tunnels and deep snow [2]. The Union Pacific "laid track quickly over flat land" [4], but crossed land that "belonged to Native nations," who "fought to defend it" [4].
write: The author ends with the photograph instead of the golden spike. What point does this ending make? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: The ending shows the celebration left out people who paid a high price: the Chinese workers who "built much of the line are not in it" [6], and Plains nations lost the buffalo they "depended on" [6]. The author wants readers to see both the achievement and its costs.

## Stretch
write: Some readers call the railroad a triumph; others call it a story of great costs. Which view does the article support better? Cite at least one piece of evidence for each side before you decide.
  lines: 5
  key: Triumph: tunnels through "solid granite" [2] and a trip that "now took about a week" [6]. Costs: Chinese workers "paid less" and many "died in accidents and avalanches" [3]; buffalo herds "nearly wiped out" [6]. Strong answers note that the article gives both, and that its final sentence leans toward the costs being forgotten.
