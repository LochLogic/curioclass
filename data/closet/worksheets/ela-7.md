# 1. Setting Shapes Character: The Call of the Wild
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how a change in setting changes a character, citing the text.
directions: Read the background and the four excerpts from Chapter 1 of Jack London's The Call of the Wild (1903). Fill in the chart comparing Buck in each setting, then answer the questions with quotations and excerpt numbers.
mistakes: Students may say Buck is simply "broken" by the club. London says the opposite: "he was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken." Buck learns a new rule and adapts; his pride survives. Watch for answers that describe the setting without connecting it to how Buck thinks or acts.
notes: The excerpts follow Project Gutenberg eBook #215 word for word, with curly quotation marks made straight. Chapter 1 shows a dog being beaten with a club; the sheet summarizes that scene rather than quoting it, but preview it before students read the full chapter. Glossary: demesne, an estate; unwonted, unusual; primitive, early and basic, ruled by force; latent, hidden but present; uncowed, not frightened into giving up.

## Background
text: In 1897, gold is found in the Klondike in far northern Canada, and thousands of people rush north. They need strong dogs to pull sleds. Buck, a large, powerful dog, lives a comfortable life in California. One night a gardener's helper named Manuel, who needs money to pay gambling debts, secretly sells him. Buck is shipped north by train in a crate, and in a yard in Seattle a man in a red sweater beats him with a club until he stops attacking.

## Read: Four excerpts
source: Jack London, The Call of the Wild (1903), Chapter 1, "Into the Primitive"
  [1] "Buck lived at a big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley. Judge Miller's place, it was called."
  [2] "And over this great demesne Buck ruled. Here he was born, and here he had lived the four years of his life. [...] But Buck was neither house-dog nor kennel-dog. The whole realm was his."
  [3] "Buck had accepted the rope with quiet dignity. To be sure, it was an unwonted performance: but he had learned to trust in men he knew, and to give them credit for a wisdom that outreached his own."
  [4] "He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken. He saw, once for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club. He had learned the lesson, and in all his after life he never forgot it. That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway. The facts of life took on a fiercer aspect; and while he faced that aspect uncowed, he faced it with all the latent cunning of his nature aroused."

## Warm up
choice: In excerpts 1 and 2, what is Buck's place in his world?
  - He is a working dog who pulls sleds.
  * He rules the whole estate, like a king.
  - He is a stray who lives on the street.
  - He is one of many kennel dogs nobody notices.
choice: In excerpt 3, why does Buck accept the rope calmly?
  - He is too tired to fight.
  - He has been trained to wear a rope every day.
  * He trusts the men he knows and believes they are wiser than he is.
  - He wants to go on a trip.

## Main: Buck in two settings
chart: Judge Miller's place (California) | The yard of the man in the red sweater (Seattle)
  rows: 3
  key: Judge Miller's place: warm, safe, rich and "sun-kissed"; Buck rules it, is proud and trusting, and expects respect; "The whole realm was his" [2]; he trusts "men he knew" [3]. The yard: harsh, ruled by force; Buck learns he "stood no chance against a man with a club" [4]; he becomes watchful and cunning, but stays proud: "beaten [...] but [...] not broken" [4].
write: How does the setting in excerpts 1 and 2 shape the kind of dog Buck is at the start? Quote the text.
  lines: 3
  key: Because he was born and raised on a large, comfortable estate where "Buck ruled" and "The whole realm was his" [2], he is proud and confident and has never been challenged.
write: Excerpt 3 says Buck trusted "men he knew." How does that trust, learned at Judge Miller's place, put him in danger?
  lines: 3
  key: He has only known kind people, so he lets Manuel tie the rope on him "with quiet dignity" [3]. His trust in humans, which made sense at home, lets him be sold and kidnapped.
write: According to excerpt 4, what lesson does the new setting teach Buck? Use two quotations.
  lines: 4
  key: He learns that "he stood no chance against a man with a club" and that he now lives under "the reign of primitive law" [4], where force rules. He obeys the club but is "not broken."
write: Explain the phrase "he met the introduction halfway." What does it show about how Buck responds to change?
  lines: 3
  key: Buck does not just suffer the new rules; he adapts to them. He accepts the lesson and begins to use "the latent cunning of his nature," so the harsh setting brings out a wilder, smarter side of him.

## Stretch
write: The chapter is called "Into the Primitive." Predict how the frozen North might keep changing Buck. Support your prediction with a word or phrase from excerpt 4.
  lines: 4
  key: As the setting gets harsher, Buck will rely more on instinct and "cunning" than on trust in people, becoming wilder and fiercer; "The facts of life took on a fiercer aspect" suggests each new place will make him tougher.
# 6. The Highwayman: Sound Devices
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, two colors of highlighter
ican: I can identify repetition, rhyme, alliteration and rhythm in a poem and explain the effect of each.
directions: Read the four stanzas from Alfred Noyes's ballad "The Highwayman" aloud, quietly, so you can hear them. Mark repetition in one color and alliteration in another. Then fill in the rhyme scheme, complete the chart and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students may mark any repeated letter as alliteration ("the ... moon"); alliteration is the same beginning sound in nearby stressed words (ghostly galleon, clattered and clashed). They may also call "riding / riding" a rhyme; it is repetition of the same word. For effect, push past "it sounds good": what does the sound make the reader feel or picture?
notes: "The Highwayman" was first published in 1906 and is in the public domain. The stanzas follow Alfred Noyes, Collected Poems, Volume One (1913), Project Gutenberg eBook #30501, except that the dashes after "riding" in stanza 1 are printed as commas to match Curio Class style. Glossary: galleon, a large sailing ship; claret, dark red; breeches, short trousers; rapier hilt, the handle of a thin sword; ostler, a stable worker; plaiting, braiding; priming, the gunpowder that fires a musket. The poem ends with the deaths of Bess and the highwayman; preview it before assigning the whole ballad.

## Read
source: Part One, stanza 1
  The wind was a torrent of darkness among the gusty trees,
  The moon was a ghostly galleon tossed upon cloudy seas,
  The road was a ribbon of moonlight over the purple moor,
  And the highwayman came riding,
  Riding, riding,
  The highwayman came riding, up to the old inn-door.
source: Part One, stanza 2
  He'd a French cocked-hat on his forehead, a bunch of lace at his chin,
  A coat of the claret velvet, and breeches of brown doe-skin;
  They fitted with never a wrinkle: his boots were up to the thigh!
  And he rode with a jewelled twinkle,
  His pistol butts a-twinkle,
  His rapier hilt a-twinkle, under the jewelled sky.
source: Part One, stanza 3
  Over the cobbles he clattered and clashed in the dark inn-yard,
  And he tapped with his whip on the shutters, but all was locked and barred;
  He whistled a tune to the window, and who should be waiting there
  But the landlord's black-eyed daughter,
  Bess, the landlord's daughter,
  Plaiting a dark red love-knot into her long black hair.
text: *Later in the poem, King George's soldiers come to the inn to trap the highwayman. They tie Bess up at her window with a musket beside her, and they wait. Then she hears him coming.*
source: Part Two, stanza 6. Alfred Noyes, "The Highwayman" (1906), from Collected Poems, Volume One (1913)
  *Tlot-tlot; tlot-tlot!* Had they heard it? The horse-hoofs ringing clear;
  *Tlot-tlot, tlot-tlot*, in the distance? Were they deaf that they did not hear?
  Down the ribbon of moonlight, over the brow of the hill,
  The highwayman came riding,
  Riding, riding!
  The red-coats looked to their priming! She stood up, straight and still!

## Warm up: Rhyme scheme of stanza 1
text: Give the first line A. Lines that rhyme get the same letter. Lines 4 and 5 end with the same word, so they share a letter.
fill:
  - trees {A}   seas {A}   moor {B}   riding {C}   riding {C}   inn-door {B}
  - Stanza 2 follows the same pattern. Its line 3 ends in "thigh," which rhymes with "sky," the last word of line {6}.
choice: Which phrase from stanza 1 uses alliteration?
  - among the gusty trees
  * a ghostly galleon
  - up to the old inn-door
  - over the purple moor
choice: In Part Two, stanza 6, "Tlot-tlot" is an example of which sound device?
  - Rhyme
  - Metaphor
  - Alliteration
  * Onomatopoeia

## Main: Sound devices and their effects
chart: Example from the poem (stanza) | Effect on the reader
  labels: Repetition; Rhyme; Alliteration; Rhythm
  height: 0.75
  key: Repetition: "riding, / Riding, riding" (1, and again in Part Two, 6), or "a-twinkle" (2), or "the landlord's daughter" (3); it imitates steady hoofbeats and makes us wait for the rider. Rhyme: trees/seas, moor/inn-door (1); it ties the long lines together like a song and makes the ballad easy to remember. Alliteration: "ghostly galleon" (1), "breeches of brown" (2), "clattered and clashed" (3); the hard c sounds echo hooves on cobblestones. Rhythm: the long lines with a strong, rolling beat ("The highwayman came riding, up to the old inn-door") sound like a galloping horse and push the story forward.
write: Read stanza 3, line 1 aloud. How do its sounds help you hear the scene?
  lines: 3
  key: The repeated hard c and cl sounds in "cobbles," "clattered" and "clashed" sound like iron horseshoes striking stones, so the reader hears the highwayman arrive noisily in the dark yard.
write: Part Two, stanza 6 repeats "The highwayman came riding, / Riding, riding" from stanza 1. How is the effect different the second time?
  lines: 3
  key: In stanza 1 the repetition is exciting, like the start of an adventure. In Part Two the same words fill the reader with dread, because we know the soldiers are waiting to ambush him. The repeated hoofbeats now sound like a countdown.

# 18. Misplaced Modifier Fix-Up
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional)
ican: I can find and fix misplaced and dangling modifiers.
directions: A misplaced modifier sits next to the wrong word. A dangling modifier has no word in the sentence to describe at all. For each sentence, write M (misplaced) or D (dangling), then rewrite it so it says what the writer meant. Last, draw what two of the sentences actually say.
mistakes: Students often fix a dangling modifier by moving words around without adding the missing doer: "The phone rang while brushing my teeth" is still dangling. The fix needs "I": "While I was brushing my teeth, the phone rang." Sentence 8 is the trickiest: "almost ate" means she nearly ate but did not; "ate almost" means she ate most of it.
notes: The key gives one fix for each; accept any rewrite that puts the modifier next to the word it describes or adds the missing doer. Drawing the literal meaning is the fun part and the best check: if the drawing is silly, the sentence needs fixing.

## Warm up
example: Misplaced: "I saw a deer riding the bus to school." (The deer is riding the bus!) Fixed: **Riding the bus to school, I saw a deer.**
choice: Which sentence has a dangling modifier?
  - Covered in mud, the dog shook itself off.
  * Walking into the gym, the music was loud.
  - Maria found a coin shining on the sidewalk.
  - Tired from the hike, we rested by the lake.

## Main: Find and fix
table: Sentence | M or D | Rewrite it
  - The waiter served a hamburger to the man covered in ketchup. | {M} | {The waiter served the man a hamburger covered in ketchup.}
  - Barking loudly, the mail carrier ran from the dog. | {M} | {The mail carrier ran from the loudly barking dog.}
  - While brushing my teeth, the phone rang. | {D} | {While I was brushing my teeth, the phone rang.}
  - We bought a puppy for my cousin with floppy ears. | {M} | {We bought my cousin a puppy with floppy ears.}
  - After finishing my homework, the dog needed a walk. | {D} | {After I finished my homework, the dog needed a walk.}
  - Hanging in the closet, Dad found his old jacket. | {M} | {Dad found his old jacket hanging in the closet.}
  - To win the race, the shoes had to be laced tight. | {D} | {To win the race, Ava had to lace her shoes tight.}
  - She almost ate the whole pizza. | {M} | {She ate almost the whole pizza.}
  height: 0.55

## Draw what it really says
draw: Sentence #____ as written | 1.6
draw: Sentence #____ as written | 1.6
note: Good choices to draw: the man covered in ketchup (1), the mail carrier barking (2), the cousin with floppy ears (4), Dad hanging in the closet (6).

## Stretch
write: Write your own funny sentence with a misplaced modifier. Then fix it.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: The teacher handed cookies to the students on paper plates. Fixed: The teacher handed the students cookies on paper plates.
# 25. Several Pieces of Evidence
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can support an analysis of a nonfiction text with several pieces of evidence.
directions: Read the article. Answer each question in a few sentences, citing at least two pieces of evidence for each one. Put quotations in quotation marks and add the paragraph number.
mistakes: Students often stop at one quotation, or cite two pieces that say the same thing. Push for evidence from different paragraphs or different kinds (a number, an event, a quotation). Watch for evidence that is copied but not explained: each piece needs a "this shows" sentence.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 330 words). Facts to know: the Pacific Railway Act was signed July 1, 1862; the Central Pacific built east from Sacramento and the Union Pacific west from Omaha; the lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869. Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's workforce; a widely reproduced photograph of the celebration does not show them.

## Read
passage: Joining the Coasts: The First Transcontinental Railroad
  **[1]** In the 1850s, crossing the United States was slow and dangerous. A trip by wagon from Missouri to California could take four to six months. In 1862, during the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. It gave two companies land and money to build a railroad across the West.
  **[2]** The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east. Almost at once, it ran into the Sierra Nevada mountains. Workers blasted tunnels through solid granite, sometimes moving only a few inches in a day. In winter, snow piled so high that crews dug tunnels under it to reach their work.
  **[3]** The company could not find enough workers, so it hired immigrants from China. Within a few years, Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's crews. They did the most dangerous jobs, including handling explosives, and they were paid less than white workers. Many died in accidents and avalanches.
  **[4]** The Union Pacific built west from Omaha, Nebraska, across the open plains. Its crews included Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans from both armies. They laid track quickly over flat land, but the railroad crossed land that belonged to Native nations such as the Lakota and Cheyenne, who fought to defend it.
  **[5]** On May 10, 1869, the two lines met at Promontory Summit in Utah Territory. Leaders tapped a golden spike into the last tie, and a telegraph sent one word across the country: "DONE."
  **[6]** The railroad changed the nation. A trip across the country now took about a week. Goods, mail and people moved quickly between the coasts, and new towns grew along the tracks. But the railroad also brought settlers and hunters onto Native lands, and the great buffalo herds that Plains nations depended on were nearly wiped out within twenty years. The famous photograph of the celebration shows smiling railroad men, but the Chinese workers who built much of the line are not in it.

## Warm up
choice: Which piece of evidence best shows that building through the Sierra Nevada was difficult?
  - "The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east."
  * "Workers blasted tunnels through solid granite, sometimes moving only a few inches in a day."
  - "They laid track quickly over flat land."
  - "A trip across the country now took about a week."

## Main: Analyze with several pieces of evidence
write: Why was the railroad so important to the country? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: It made travel far faster: a wagon trip "could take four to six months" [1], but afterward "A trip across the country now took about a week" [6]. Goods, mail and people moved quickly, and "new towns grew along the tracks" [6].
write: How does the author show that Chinese workers were essential to the railroad but treated unfairly? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: Essential: "Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's crews" and did "the most dangerous jobs" [3]. Unfair: they "were paid less than white workers" [3], and they "are not in" the famous photograph [6].
write: Compare the challenges the two companies faced. Cite evidence from at least two paragraphs.
  lines: 4
  key: The Central Pacific faced the mountains: granite tunnels and deep snow [2]. The Union Pacific "laid track quickly over flat land" [4], but crossed land that "belonged to Native nations," who "fought to defend it" [4].
write: The author ends with the photograph instead of the golden spike. What point does this ending make? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: The ending shows the celebration left out people who paid a high price: the Chinese workers who "built much of the line are not in it" [6], and Plains nations lost the buffalo they "depended on" [6]. The author wants readers to see both the achievement and its costs.

## Stretch
write: Some readers call the railroad a triumph; others call it a story of great costs. Which view does the article support better? Cite at least one piece of evidence for each side before you decide.
  lines: 5
  key: Triumph: tunnels through "solid granite" [2] and a trip that "now took about a week" [6]. Costs: Chinese workers "paid less" and many "died in accidents and avalanches" [3]; buffalo herds "nearly wiped out" [6]. Strong answers note that the article gives both, and that its final sentence leans toward the costs being forgotten.
# 30. Evaluate the Speaker's Reasoning
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can judge whether a speaker's reasoning is sound and whether the evidence is relevant and sufficient.
directions: Listen as your teacher reads a student's speech to the school board, twice. The first time, just listen. The second time, take notes. Then use the reasons listed below to evaluate the speech: is each piece of evidence relevant (connected to the claim) and sufficient (enough to prove it)?
mistakes: Students tend to judge whether they agree with the claim (longer lunch sounds great) instead of whether the reasoning holds up. Remind them a weak argument can have a good conclusion. Watch for "everyone agrees" marked as strong evidence: popularity does not prove a claim (the bandwagon fallacy).
notes: The speech is original to Curio Class; the student, school and data are made up. It mixes two well-supported reasons with three flawed ones: a hasty generalization from one friend, a bandwagon appeal and an irrelevant point. Reason 2 is solid but not airtight (students at the end of the line might throw away food for other reasons); strong students will notice that.

## Listen
note: Speech to read aloud, twice. "Good morning. My name is Jordan, and I am asking the school board to make lunch 30 minutes long instead of 20. First, students do not have enough time to eat. Last month, our student council timed the lunch line every day for two weeks. On average, students waited 9 minutes to get their food. That leaves just 11 minutes to eat. Second, rushed students throw away more food. On those same days, the council weighed the food thrown away after each lunch period. Students at the end of the line threw away about twice as much food as students at the front. Third, my best friend got a stomachache last week because he ate too fast. Rushing makes kids sick. Also, everyone I know agrees that lunch is too short, and if everyone thinks so, it must be true. And honestly, the cafeteria walls are a boring gray, which makes lunch even worse. Ten more minutes would mean less waste, calmer students and a better afternoon of learning. Please vote yes. Thank you."
write: Notes: What is the speaker's main claim?
  lines: 1
  key: Lunch should be 30 minutes long instead of 20.
lines: 3

## Main: Evaluate each reason
table: The speaker's reason | Relevant? | Sufficient? | Sound or flawed, and why
  - 1. Students wait 9 minutes in line, leaving 11 to eat. | {yes} | {yes} | {Sound: specific data from two weeks of timing.}
  - 2. Students at the end of the line throw away twice as much food. | {yes} | {yes} | {Sound: measured data, though other causes are possible.}
  - 3. My best friend got a stomachache from eating fast. | {yes} | {no} | {Flawed: one example cannot prove that rushing makes kids sick.}
  - 4. Everyone I know agrees lunch is too short. | {no} | {no} | {Flawed: bandwagon; popularity does not prove a claim.}
  - 5. The cafeteria walls are a boring gray. | {no} | {no} | {Flawed: wall color has nothing to do with lunch length.}
  height: 0.55
choice: Which reason is the strongest support for the claim?
  * Reason 1: the timed lunch line
  - Reason 3: the friend's stomachache
  - Reason 4: everyone agrees
  - Reason 5: the gray walls
choice: The speaker ends by promising "calmer students and a better afternoon of learning." What is the problem with this?
  - It is too short.
  - It repeats reason 1.
  * No evidence in the speech supports it.
  - It is not about lunch.

## Stretch
write: Overall, is the speaker's reasoning sound? Give your verdict, then suggest one piece of evidence that would make the speech stronger.
  lines: 4
  key: Partly. Reasons 1 and 2 use real, relevant data, but reasons 3 to 5 are weak or off topic and hurt the speaker's credibility. Stronger evidence: a survey of the school nurse's visits for stomachaches, or data from a nearby school that lengthened lunch and measured less waste.
# 42. Two-Perspective Scene
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can write the same scene from two characters' perspectives, showing how their thoughts and details differ.
directions: Read the situation. Plan what each character sees, thinks, knows and wants. Then write the scene twice, once from each character's perspective in first person. Same events, different minds.
mistakes: The most common problem is two versions that are nearly identical with the names swapped. Each version should notice different details (Dev notices the ball's path; Mrs. Patel notices the glass on her kitchen floor) and hold different private knowledge. Watch also for characters who know things they could not know, such as Mrs. Patel reading Dev's thoughts.
notes: The situation is original to Curio Class. The planning chart deliberately gives each character a secret: Dev knows he was showing off; Mrs. Patel knows she has been meaning to replace that cracked window for months. Strong pieces let those secrets shape each narrator's feelings.

## Warm up: The situation
example: On a Saturday afternoon, Dev, age 12, is practicing pitches against the fence in his backyard while two friends watch. He throws a fastball too high. It sails over the fence and through the kitchen window of his neighbor, Mrs. Patel. She is at the sink, washing dishes. A minute later, she walks outside holding the baseball.
  Dev's secret: he threw extra hard to show off for his friends. Mrs. Patel's secret: that window already had a crack, and she has been meaning to replace it for months.
choice: Which detail could only appear in Mrs. Patel's version?
  - The ball sailing over the fence
  - His friends' faces when the glass breaks
  * Glass landing in her soapy dishwater
  - The weight of the ball in Dev's hand before he throws

## Plan: Two minds
chart: Dev | Mrs. Patel
  labels: What I see and hear; What I think and feel; What I know that the other does not; What I want
  height: 0.7
  key: Dev: sees the ball rising too high, hears the crash and his friends gasp; feels panic and embarrassment; knows he was showing off; wants to not get in trouble and to look brave. Mrs. Patel: sees glass in the sink and a baseball by her feet; startled, then a little relieved; knows the window was already cracked; wants the boy to be honest and wants the window fixed at last.

## Write: Two perspectives
write: Dev's perspective (first person)
  starter: I wound up for my fastest pitch yet, ...
  lines: 7
  key: Model: I wound up for my fastest pitch yet, mostly because Kai and Lucas were watching. The ball left my fingers too high. I watched it climb over the fence, and then came the sound: a crash like a dropped tray. Nobody breathed. When Mrs. Patel came out holding my baseball, my face burned. I could run inside, or I could tell the truth. "That was me," I said. "I was showing off."
write: Mrs. Patel's perspective (first person)
  starter: I was rinsing the last plate when ...
  lines: 7
  key: Model: I was rinsing the last plate when the window exploded and something thumped onto the floor. Glass glittered in my dishwater. A baseball sat by my slippers. My heart pounded, and then I looked at the frame. That crack had been there since spring; I had only been waiting for a reason. I picked up the ball and walked outside, where a red-faced boy was already stepping forward.

## Stretch
write: How does each character's secret change the way they feel about the same moment?
  lines: 3
  key: Dev's secret makes him feel guilty, since the accident is partly his fault for showing off. Mrs. Patel's secret softens her reaction: the window was already cracked, so she is more surprised than angry.
