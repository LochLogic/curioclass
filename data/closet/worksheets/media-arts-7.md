# 1. Shallow and Deep Focus
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser, a blending stump or tissue (optional)
ican: I can show depth of field in a sketch and choose it to guide the viewer's eye.
directions: Read about depth of field. Answer the warm up questions. Then sketch the same scene three times: shallow, medium and deep focus. Draw sharp areas with crisp, dark lines; draw blurred areas with soft shading, smudged edges and no small details. Fill in the chart and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often blur the subject instead of the background in the shallow frame. In shallow focus the subject is sharp and everything far behind it melts. Others "blur" by drawing lightly but keep every detail: blur means losing detail (no leaves, no window frames), not just lighter lines. The scene must stay the same in all three frames; only the focus changes.
notes: Depth of field is how much of the picture, front to back, looks sharp. A wider lens opening (a smaller f-number like f/2), standing closer to the subject and zooming in all make it shallower. A phone's "portrait" mode imitates shallow focus. Smudging graphite with a finger or tissue makes convincing blur.

## Warm up
example: **Shallow focus:** only the subject is sharp; the background is a soft blur. **Deep focus:** almost everything, near and far, is sharp. **Medium:** the subject and things near it are sharp; the far background is a little soft.
choice: In shallow focus, what is sharp?
  - Everything in the picture
  * Only the subject, or a thin slice near it
  - Only the background
  - Nothing
choice: Which choice makes depth of field shallower?
  * A wider lens opening, like f/2
  - A smaller lens opening, like f/16
  - Standing farther from the subject
  - Zooming out to a wider view
choice: A filmmaker wants viewers to notice a person in the far background while the hero talks up front. Which focus works best?
  - Shallow focus
  * Deep focus
  - Everything blurred
  - It does not matter

## Main: One scene, three focus settings
text: Scene: a cat sits on a windowsill. Outside the window, a tree and a house across the street.
draw: 1. Shallow focus | 1.8
draw: 2. Medium focus | 1.8
draw: 3. Deep focus | 1.8
table: Version | What is sharp | What is blurred
  - Shallow | {the cat} | {the windowsill edges, the tree and the house}
  - Medium | {the cat and the windowsill} | {the far tree and house, a little}
  - Deep | {the cat, the window, the tree and the house} | {nothing, or almost nothing}

## Think about it
write: Which version makes the cat feel most important? Explain how the blur does that.
  lines: 2
  key: The shallow version, because the background blur removes distracting detail, so the eye goes straight to the one sharp thing, the cat.
write: When would a storyteller choose the deep focus version instead?
  lines: 2
  key: When the setting or something far away matters to the story, for example to show the neighbor arriving outside, or to let viewers notice several things at once.

## Stretch
write: In a "focus pull" the sharp area moves during a shot. Plan one: what is sharp at the start, what is sharp at the end, and what story moment does it show?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: start sharp on the cat, then shift focus to a dog appearing outside the window; it shows the cat noticing a threat.

# 7. Lewis Hine's Photographs
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; projector to show the two photographs from the Library of Congress
ican: I can analyze how a documentary photographer uses composition and captions to send a message.
directions: Read about Lewis Hine. Look closely at each photograph as your teacher shows it, and read Hine's own caption. Answer the questions about composition and message, then judge how the photo and caption work together.
mistakes: Students describe what is in the photo but not where or how it is placed. Push for composition words: foreground, scale, lines, angle, light, crop. Students may also treat the captions as neutral labels; Hine wrote them as evidence and argument for a cause, and both photo and caption were chosen to persuade.
notes: Both photographs are public domain (Library of Congress, National Child Labor Committee collection, "No known restrictions on publication"). Search loc.gov for the item numbers in the citations and preview both before class. The captions are quoted from the Library of Congress records. Hine (1874 to 1940) was a teacher who became the photographer for the National Child Labor Committee in 1908. To get into mills and mines he sometimes posed as a fire inspector, a postcard seller, a Bible salesman or an industrial photographer. His photographs supported the campaign that led to child labor limits, and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set national rules. Composition answers in the key say what to look for; confirm them against the projected photo. Keep the focus on the children's working conditions and the photographer's choices; this is not a sheet for role-play.

## Warm up: The photographer
text: Lewis Hine was a teacher who believed photographs could change laws. From 1908 he traveled the country for the National Child Labor Committee, a group working to end child labor. Mill and mine owners did not want him there, so he sometimes got in by saying he was there to photograph the machines. He often wrote down a child's name, age and work, and sometimes their height.
choice: Why did Hine write captions with names, heights and dates?
  - To decorate the photographs
  * To make the photos evidence that real children were doing this work
  - Because every photo needs a title by law
  - To sell the photos to the mill owners
choice: Who was Hine's main audience?
  - The mill and mine owners
  - Other photographers
  * Voters, reformers and lawmakers who could change the laws
  - The children in the photos

## Main: Photograph 1
source: Lewis W. Hine, photograph and caption for the National Child Labor Committee, Lancaster, South Carolina, November 30, 1908. Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, item 2018674894.
  "Sadie Pfeifer, 48 inches high, has worked half a year. One of the many small children at work in Lancaster Cotton Mills."
write: Where is Sadie in the frame, and how big is she compared with the machinery?
  lines: 2
  key: Look for: Sadie stands close to the camera beside a spinning machine; she is small next to it, and the machine is much bigger than she is. Her size compared with the machine is the point of the picture.
write: Find a line that leads your eye. Where does it go, and what does it make you feel about the mill?
  lines: 2
  key: Look for: the long row of the spinning machine running back into the distance. It makes the machinery seem endless and the mill huge, and it suggests many more children like her further down the row.
write: How does the caption add to the photo? Find two facts and one phrase that argues.
  lines: 3
  key: Facts: her name, her height (48 inches) and that she has worked half a year. The phrase "one of the many small children" turns one girl into evidence of a widespread problem.

## Main: Photograph 2
source: Lewis W. Hine, photograph and caption for the National Child Labor Committee, South Pittston, Pennsylvania, January 1911. Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, item 2018676220.
  "A view of Ewen Breaker of the Pa. [Pennsylvania] Coal Co. The dust was so dense at times as to obscure the view. This dust penetrates the utmost recesses of the boy's lungs. (See labels 1927 to 1930 for names of some of these.) A kind of slave driver sometimes stands over the boys, prodding or kicking them into obedience."
text: Breaker boys sat above chutes of coal and picked out rock and slate by hand.
write: Describe the composition: where the boys are placed, the angle of the camera, and the light or air in the photo.
  lines: 3
  key: Look for: many boys in rows filling the frame, bent over the chutes; a camera position that looks across or down on the rows; dim, dusty air that softens the background. The rows and the crowding show how many boys work there.
write: What does the caption tell you that the photo cannot show by itself?
  lines: 2
  key: The dust gets deep into the boys' lungs (a health harm you cannot see), the dust was sometimes too thick to see through, and an adult sometimes stood over the boys and pushed or kicked them to keep them working.

## Stretch
write: Hine sometimes said he was there to photograph machines, and he wrote captions to persuade. Does that make his photographs less trustworthy as historical evidence? Explain your thinking.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer notes that Hine had a clear purpose and chose angles and words to persuade, so historians should know his point of view; but his photos record real children, places and dates, his captions include checkable facts, and many other records confirm child labor. Purpose affects how we read a source, not whether it is useful.

# 20. Animation Check
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can use keyframes, cycles and exposure sheets to plan animation timing.
directions: Answer the 8 review items about keyframes, cycles and exposure sheets. Use the exposure sheet for items 6 to 8. Show your work in the margin.
mistakes: Students count the keyframes themselves as inbetweens; between frame 1 and frame 9 there are 7 frames, not 8 or 9. On an exposure sheet, an empty drawing box means the drawing above is still on screen (held), not that the screen is blank.
notes: Animating "on ones" means a new drawing on every frame; "on twos" means each drawing is held for 2 frames. An exposure sheet (also called an X-sheet or dope sheet) is a frame-by-frame chart that tells the animator and camera which drawing appears on each frame and lines up the dialogue. Real sheets also have columns for layers and camera moves.

## Warm up: Keyframes
choice: What is a keyframe?
  - Any frame that has a sound on it
  * A main drawing that marks an important pose or the start or end of a move
  - The last frame of every film
  - A frame that is drawn in color
fill:
  - Keyframes are at frame 1 and frame 9. Animated on ones, there are {7} inbetween frames.
  - The same move animated on twos has drawings on frames 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9. That is {3} inbetween drawings.

## Main: Cycles
choice: In a walk cycle, why must the last drawing lead smoothly into the first?
  - So the walk ends with a stop
  - So the animator can skip the keyframes
  - So the character changes size
  * So the cycle can repeat over and over without a jump
fill:
  - A walk cycle has 12 drawings, shot on twos at 24 fps. One cycle lasts {24} frames, or {1} second. In a 6-second shot the cycle plays {6} times.

## Main: Read the exposure sheet
text: Mia looks up and says "Hi!" An empty drawing box means the drawing above it is held.
table: Frame | Dialogue | Drawing
  - 1 | | A1
  - 2 | |
  - 3 | |
  - 4 | |
  - 5 | "H | A2
  - 6 | -i!" |
  - 7 | | A3
  - 8 | |
  - 9 | |
  - 10 | |
  - 11 | |
  - 12 | |
fill:
  - Drawing A1 is on screen for {4} frames.
  - At 24 fps, drawing A3 is on screen for {1/4} of a second.
choice: Why does the exposure sheet have a dialogue column next to the drawing column?
  * So the mouth drawings line up with the sounds on the exact frames
  - So the actor knows when to stop recording
  - So the drawings can be done in any order
  - So the film has subtitles

## Stretch
write: Plan a 1-second jump at 24 fps with 3 keyframes. Name each key pose and the frame it goes on, and say whether you would animate it on ones or on twos and why.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: key 1 crouch on frame 1, key 2 top of the jump on frame 12, key 3 landing on frame 24; on twos to save drawings for a simple move, or on ones for a fast, smooth action. A strong answer places the keys in order across 24 frames and gives a reason for the choice.

# 21. Story World Map
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional)
ican: I can plan a story world and choose which medium tells each part of it.
directions: Read about the story world. Answer the questions about transmedia storytelling. Draw a map of the world with its places. Then complete the chart: for each place or character, tell what happens there and which medium tells that part of the story, and why.
mistakes: Students often use every medium to retell the same events. In transmedia storytelling, each medium adds something new: a podcast might tell the backstory, a comic a side character's adventure. Check that each chart row has a reason tied to what that medium does best. Maps sometimes lose their labels: every place needs a name.
notes: The world of Brindle is original to Curio Class; students may invent their own world instead. Accept any well-reasoned media choices. A good extension is to have groups each make one piece (a comic page, a 1-minute podcast script, a poster) for the same world.

## Read the story world
example: **The Floating Market of Brindle:** Brindle is a town built on rafts tied together on a wide, slow river. **Places:** the Lantern Bridge, the Fish Market raft, the Old Lighthouse, the Reed Maze, Grandma Sol's Repair Shop. **Characters:** Pim, a 12-year-old map maker; Grandma Sol, who fixes anything; Captain Ferro, who wants to cut the rafts loose and sell them; Wick, a talking heron who knows every channel.

## Warm up
choice: In transmedia storytelling, each medium should:
  - Tell exactly the same events as the others
  * Add a new part of the story, so the world grows across media
  - Use only words
  - Be made by one person
choice: Which medium is best for exploring a place step by step, choosing where to go?
  - A poster
  * A game or a printed choose-your-path book
  - A song
  - A single photo
choice: Wick the heron tells legends of the river at night. Which medium fits that best?
  - A street sign
  * A podcast or audio story
  - A spreadsheet
  - A logo

## Main: Map the world
draw: Map of Brindle: draw and label all five places, the river and the routes between them | 3.6

## Main: Which medium tells which part?
chart: Place or character | What happens here | Medium | Why this medium
  rows: 6
  key: Model rows: Reed Maze | Pim gets lost and finds a hidden channel | choose-your-path book | readers make the choices Pim makes. Wick | legends of the river | podcast | voices and water sounds set the mood at night. Captain Ferro | his plan to sell the rafts | short video | music and close-ups build suspense. Lantern Bridge | the town festival | poster | one bold image invites people in. Grandma Sol's shop | how she fixed the lighthouse lamp | comic | panels show each step of the repair.

## Stretch
write: Your audience can only follow one medium. Which part of the world would they miss most? How could you hint at it in the medium they do follow?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific missing part (Wick's legends) and a hint (a character in the video hums the legend or mentions the heron), so the main story still makes sense alone.

# 33. Fair Use Four Factors
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can apply the four fair use factors to decide whether a remix is likely to be fair use.
directions: Match each factor to the question it asks. Read the four remix scenarios. Fill in the grid with how each factor leans, then decide whether each use is likely fair use.
mistakes: Students think giving credit makes a use fair; credit is good practice but is not one of the four factors. Others think "nonprofit" or "for school" settles it; purpose is only one factor, and courts weigh all four together. Students also treat the factors as a score to add up; a single strong factor, like replacing sales of the original, can outweigh others.
notes: The four factors come from Section 107 of the U.S. Copyright Act: the purpose and character of the use (including whether it is commercial or nonprofit educational), the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and the effect on the potential market for or value of the work. Courts decide fair use case by case, so the key gives likely outcomes, not legal advice. A parody that comments on the original work itself can be fair use even when it is commercial (Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, 1994). All scenarios and works are made up. Fair use is a U.S. idea; other countries have different rules.

## Warm up: The four factors
match: Write the letter of the question each factor asks.
  - Purpose and character of the use = Is it for teaching, news, comment or parody, or to make money? Does it add something new?
  - Nature of the original work = Is the original mostly factual or highly creative? Was it published?
  - Amount used = How much was taken, and was it the most important part?
  - Effect on the market = Could the new use replace sales of the original or hurt its value?
choice: A student credits the artist under a video that uses the artist's whole song. What does the credit do for fair use?
  - It makes the use fair automatically
  * It is polite and good practice, but it is not one of the four factors
  - It makes the use illegal
  - It transfers the copyright to the student

## Main: Four remixes
example: **A.** For a class project, a student makes a 3-minute video review of a new movie, using three 5-second clips to point out the camera work. **B.** A dance channel uses an entire hit song under its dance video and sells the video as a paid download. **C.** A comedy group rewrites the words of a famous pop song to make fun of the song itself, using its melody, and sells tickets to the show. **D.** A T-shirt company prints a photographer's whole photo of a sunset on shirts without asking.
chart: Purpose | Nature | Amount | Market effect | Likely fair use?
  labels: A. Movie review; B. Dance download; C. Song parody; D. T-shirt photo
  height: 0.85
  key: A: purpose favors (comment, education, adds analysis); nature leans against (a creative movie); amount favors (three short clips); market favors (a review does not replace the movie); likely fair use. B: purpose against (commercial, adds little to the song); nature against (creative song); amount against (the entire song); market against (the download competes with the song and its licensing); likely not fair use. C: purpose favors (parody that comments on the song itself), even though tickets are sold; nature against (creative song); amount leans either way (takes the recognizable melody, but parody needs to call up the original); market favors (a parody does not replace the song); likely fair use, but a court would look closely. D: purpose against (commercial decoration, adds nothing); nature against (a creative photo); amount against (the whole photo); market against (it takes the photographer's licensing income); likely not fair use.

## Main: Decide
choice: Which scenario is the strongest case for fair use?
  * A, because it uses short clips to comment on the movie and does not replace it
  - B, because dance is creative
  - D, because the photo is of nature
  - All four are equally fair
choice: Which factor hurts scenario D the most?
  - Nature of the original work
  - Purpose, because the photo is old
  * Effect on the market: shirts take income the photographer could earn by licensing the photo
  - None; D is fair use

## Stretch
write: Change scenario B so that it is more likely to be fair use. Explain which factors your change improves.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: the channel makes a free video that uses only a few seconds of the song while a dancer explains its rhythm and teaches the steps. This improves purpose (education, commentary, not selling), amount (a short part, not the whole song) and market effect (it does not replace the song). Getting a license from the rights holder avoids the fair use question altogether.
