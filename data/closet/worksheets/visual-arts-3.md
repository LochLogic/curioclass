# 1. Build a 12-Color Wheel
minutes: 40
materials: crayons, colored pencils or paint in red, yellow and blue (plus white paper), pencil, a round lid or compass to trace
pairs: paint-mixer
ican: I can build a 12-part color wheel and label primary, secondary and tertiary colors.
directions: Fill in the chart first. Then trace a circle in the box, divide it into 12 parts like a clock face, and color each part. Put yellow at 12 o'clock. Label every part with its color name and P (primary), S (secondary) or T (tertiary).
mistakes: Students often put the secondary colors in the wrong gap (green between red and yellow). Check that each secondary sits halfway between the two primaries that make it. Tertiary names always say the primary first: red-orange, not orange-red.
notes: This wheel uses the red, yellow and blue primaries that students mix with paint and crayons. If you paint, mix each secondary from equal parts of its two primaries, then each tertiary from a primary plus the secondary next to it. The Paint Mixer tool shows the same wheel if you want to check colors on screen.

## Warm up: Three kinds of colors
example: **Primary** colors cannot be mixed from other colors: red, yellow, blue. **Secondary** colors mix two primaries: orange, green, violet. **Tertiary** colors mix a primary with the secondary next to it: yellow-orange.
fill:
  - red + yellow = {orange}
  - yellow + blue = {green}
  - blue + red = {violet}

## Main: Plan the wheel
table: Clock position | Color | P, S or T
  - 12 | yellow | {P}
  - 1 | {yellow-orange} | {T}
  - 2 | {orange} | {S}
  - 3 | {red-orange} | {T}
  - 4 | red | {P}
  - 5 | {red-violet} | {T}
  - 6 | {violet} | {S}
  - 7 | {blue-violet} | {T}
  - 8 | blue | {P}
  - 9 | {blue-green} | {T}
  - 10 | {green} | {S}
  - 11 | {yellow-green} | {T}
draw: My 12-color wheel (yellow at the top) | 3.6

## Check your wheel
choice: Which two colors mix to make red-violet?
  - red and blue
  * red and violet
  - violet and blue
  - red and orange
fill:
  - The color straight across the wheel from yellow is {violet}.
  - The color straight across the wheel from orange is {blue}.
  - The color straight across the wheel from red is {green}.

## Stretch
write: Colors straight across from each other are called complementary colors. Look at your three answers above. What kind of color is always across from a primary?
  lines: 2
  key: A secondary color. Each primary sits across from the secondary made of the other two primaries (yellow and violet, red and green, blue and orange).

# 21. Hokusai's Mount Fuji
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; the teacher shows the three prints on a screen or as printouts
ican: I can describe how an artist made the same subject look big or small and explain why.
directions: Your teacher will show three prints from Hokusai's series Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji. Find Mount Fuji in each one. Fill in the chart, then answer the questions.
mistakes: In The Great Wave, many students first think the small peak is another wave. Point out its snowy top: the wave curls around it. Students may say Fuji is "far away" without saying how they can tell; push for clues: it is small, low in the picture, behind other things.
notes: Show these three prints (all public domain, with high-quality images from museum open-access collections such as The Metropolitan Museum of Art): Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave), Fine Wind, Clear Morning (often called Red Fuji), and Fujimigahara in Owari Province (a barrel maker working inside a huge barrel, with Fuji tiny in the distance through it). Katsushika Hokusai made the series as color woodblock prints in about 1830 to 1832. This sheet has no pictures, so students need the images in front of them the whole time.

## Warm up: Meet the artist
fill:
  - Hokusai was an artist from {Japan}.
  - Mount Fuji is a {mountain} (it is also a volcano).
  - These pictures are woodblock {prints}: carved wood blocks were inked and pressed onto paper.

## Main: Find Fuji
table: Print | Where is Fuji in the picture? | Big or small? | How did Hokusai make it look that way?
  - The Great Wave | {low in the picture, framed by the curling wave} | {small} | {the huge wave fills the front, so Fuji looks far away}
  - Fine Wind, Clear Morning | {fills most of the picture} | {big} | {nothing else is in front; the mountain almost touches the edges}
  - Fujimigahara in Owari Province | {inside the round barrel, far away} | {small} | {the giant barrel is close; Fuji peeks through it}

## Think about it
choice: In The Great Wave, what is the biggest thing in the picture?
  - Mount Fuji
  * the wave
  - the boats
  - the sky
choice: Why do faraway things look small in a picture?
  * Things look smaller the farther away they are from us.
  - Faraway things are always small in real life.
  - The artist ran out of room.
write: Fuji is the subject of the whole series. Why do you think Hokusai sometimes made it tiny?
  lines: 3
  key: To surprise us and make us look for it; to show Fuji is always there in everyday life while people work; to make the wave or the barrel feel huge and powerful next to it. Accept any reason tied to the prints.

## Stretch
draw: Draw your own view of a mountain. Make it tiny by putting something big up close. | 2
