# 1. Build a 12-Color Wheel
minutes: 40
materials: crayons, colored pencils or paint in red, yellow and blue (plus white paper), pencil, a round lid or compass to trace
pairs: paint-mixer
ican: I can build a 12-part color wheel and label primary, secondary and tertiary colors.
directions: Fill in the chart first. Then trace a circle in the box, divide it into 12 parts like a clock face, and color each part. Put yellow at 12 o'clock. Label every part with its color name and P (primary), S (secondary) or T (tertiary).
mistakes: Students often put the secondary colors in the wrong gap (green between red and yellow). Check that each secondary sits halfway between the two primaries that make it. Tertiary names always say the primary first: red-orange, not orange-red.
notes: This wheel uses the red, yellow and blue primaries that students mix with paint and crayons. If you paint, mix each secondary from equal parts of its two primaries, then each tertiary from a primary plus the secondary next to it. The Paint Mixer tool shows the same wheel if you want to check colors on screen.

## Warm up: Three kinds of colors
example: **Primary** colors cannot be mixed from other colors: red, yellow, blue. **Secondary** colors mix two primaries: orange, green, violet. **Tertiary** colors mix a primary with the secondary next to it: yellow-orange.
fill:
  - red + yellow = {orange}
  - yellow + blue = {green}
  - blue + red = {violet}

## Main: Plan the wheel
table: Clock position | Color | P, S or T
  - 12 | yellow | {P}
  - 1 | {yellow-orange} | {T}
  - 2 | {orange} | {S}
  - 3 | {red-orange} | {T}
  - 4 | red | {P}
  - 5 | {red-violet} | {T}
  - 6 | {violet} | {S}
  - 7 | {blue-violet} | {T}
  - 8 | blue | {P}
  - 9 | {blue-green} | {T}
  - 10 | {green} | {S}
  - 11 | {yellow-green} | {T}
draw: My 12-color wheel (yellow at the top) | 3.6

## Check your wheel
choice: Which two colors mix to make red-violet?
  - red and blue
  * red and violet
  - violet and blue
  - red and orange
fill:
  - The color straight across the wheel from yellow is {violet}.
  - The color straight across the wheel from orange is {blue}.
  - The color straight across the wheel from red is {green}.

## Stretch
write: Colors straight across from each other are called complementary colors. Look at your three answers above. What kind of color is always across from a primary?
  lines: 2
  key: A secondary color. Each primary sits across from the secondary made of the other two primaries (yellow and violet, red and green, blue and orange).

# 5. Warm Night, Cool Night
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can tell warm colors from cool colors and explain how each one changes the mood of a painting.
directions: Look closely at the two paintings your teacher shows: Vincent van Gogh's Café Terrace at Night and Pablo Picasso's The Old Guitarist. List the colors you see, decide if each painting is mostly warm or mostly cool, and name the mood it gives you. Use evidence from the paintings.
mistakes: Students often say Café Terrace is "all yellow" and miss the deep blue sky and the stars: it is the mix of warm and cool that makes the café glow. Others call blue "sad" as a rule. Cool colors can feel calm or peaceful too; the mood comes from the colors together with the subject, the pose and the light. Ask "What in the painting tells you that?"
notes: Café Terrace at Night was painted by Vincent van Gogh in Arles, France, in September 1888; it is in the Kröller-Müller Museum in the Netherlands. The café glows in yellow and orange under a gaslight, against a dark blue starry sky and blue-violet street. The Old Guitarist was painted by Pablo Picasso in 1903 to 1904 in Barcelona, during his Blue Period, when he painted mostly in blues and blue-greens; it is in the Art Institute of Chicago. It shows a thin, bent, blind old man playing a brown guitar, the only warm color. Both museums show the paintings on their websites; project them large. Warm colors: red, orange, yellow. Cool colors: blue, green, violet.

## Warm up: Warm or cool?
sort: Sort the colors.
  - Warm colors: red, orange, yellow
  - Cool colors: blue, green, violet
choice: Which word best describes how warm colors often feel?
  - cold and quiet
  * sunny, lively and energetic
  - wet and gloomy
  - empty

## Main: Compare the two paintings
table: Question | Café Terrace at Night (Van Gogh, 1888) | The Old Guitarist (Picasso, 1903 to 1904)
  height: 0.6
  - Colors I see | {yellow, orange, deep blue, white stars} | {blues and blue-greens, a brown guitar}
  - Mostly warm, mostly cool or both? | {both: a warm café under a cool sky} | {mostly cool}
  - What is happening? | {people sit at an outdoor café on a starry night} | {a thin old man bends over his guitar}
  - Mood word | {cheerful, inviting, lively} | {sad, lonely, tired}
choice: In Café Terrace at Night, why does the café look so bright?
  - The whole painting is yellow.
  - There are no colors near it.
  * The warm yellow café sits right next to the dark, cool blue sky, so it seems to glow.
  - The painting is very small.
choice: Picasso painted The Old Guitarist during his "Blue Period." What does using almost only blue do to the painting?
  * It makes the scene feel cold, quiet and sad, and the warm guitar stands out.
  - It makes it feel like a sunny summer day.
  - It makes it look funny.
  - It makes the guitar disappear.

## Explain
write: Choose one painting. Explain how its colors create its mood. Use at least two details you can see.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: In The Old Guitarist, almost everything is blue and blue-green, which feels cold and lonely. The man is thin and bent over with his eyes closed, and the only warm color is his brown guitar, as if music is the one warm thing he has left. The cool colors and the pose together make the mood sad.

## Stretch: Switch the colors
write: Imagine The Old Guitarist painted in bright yellows and oranges. How would the mood change? Would the man's pose still tell part of the story?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. The painting would feel warmer and less lonely, maybe like a sunny street concert, but the bent, thin body would still look tired or sad, so the mood would feel mixed. Color changes mood a lot, but the subject and pose matter too.

# 15. Landscape with Depth Plan
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser, colored pencils
ican: I can plan a landscape that shows depth with a horizon line, overlapping and size.
directions: Read about the four depth tricks and answer the questions. Then sketch a landscape plan in the box: draw a horizon line, three overlapping hills, and the same kind of object big in front and small far away. Check off each trick when you have used it.
mistakes: Students put the horizon line at the very bottom, leaving no room for a foreground; place it about one third of the way down. Hills drawn side by side do not overlap: the front hill should cover part of the one behind it. Far-away objects should be smaller and higher on the page, not just smaller in the same row.
notes: Depth tricks: overlapping (the thing in front covers part of the thing behind), size (closer objects look bigger), placement (farther objects sit higher on the page, nearer the horizon), and detail and color (far objects have less detail and look lighter and bluer, called atmospheric perspective). A strong plan: horizon one third down, three hills where the front hill covers the middle one and the middle one covers the back one, three trees shrinking as they move up toward the horizon, and the far hill drawn lighter.

## Warm up: Four depth tricks
text: **Overlapping:** a closer thing covers part of a farther thing. **Size:** closer things look bigger. **Placement:** farther things sit higher on the page, closer to the horizon. **Detail and color:** far things look paler, bluer and less detailed.
fill:
  - The line where the land seems to meet the sky is the {horizon line}.
  - A tree that is far away is drawn {smaller} and {higher} on the page.
  - When one hill covers part of another, the covered hill looks {farther away}.
choice: Which far-away mountain looks most real?
  - Dark and full of tiny details
  - Bright red with sharp edges
  * Pale and a little blue, with few details
  - The same size as a nearby tree

## Main: My landscape plan
draw: Landscape plan: horizon line, 3 overlapping hills, 3 trees (or houses) from big to small | 3.8
table: Depth trick | I used it (check) | Where in my plan?
  height: 0.45
  - Horizon line about one third down | |
  - 3 hills that overlap | |
  - Big object in front, small objects far away | |
  - Far objects placed higher on the page | |
  - Far hills paler with less detail | |
note: Check each row against the sketch: the front hill should cover part of the middle hill, and objects should shrink as they move up toward the horizon.

## Stretch: Explain your trick
write: Which depth trick makes your landscape look deepest? Explain why it works.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: "Overlapping, because when the front hill covers the hill behind it, your eyes know the covered one must be farther away." A strong answer names the trick and tells how it fools the eye.

# 21. Hokusai's Mount Fuji
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; the teacher shows the three prints on a screen or as printouts
ican: I can describe how an artist made the same subject look big or small and explain why.
directions: Your teacher will show three prints from Hokusai's series Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji. Find Mount Fuji in each one. Fill in the chart, then answer the questions.
mistakes: In The Great Wave, many students first think the small peak is another wave. Point out its snowy top: the wave curls around it. Students may say Fuji is "far away" without saying how they can tell; push for clues: it is small, low in the picture, behind other things.
notes: Show these three prints (all public domain, with high-quality images from museum open-access collections such as The Metropolitan Museum of Art): Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave), Fine Wind, Clear Morning (often called Red Fuji), and Fujimigahara in Owari Province (a barrel maker working inside a huge barrel, with Fuji tiny in the distance through it). Katsushika Hokusai made the series as color woodblock prints in about 1830 to 1832. This sheet has no pictures, so students need the images in front of them the whole time.

## Warm up: Meet the artist
fill:
  - Hokusai was an artist from {Japan}.
  - Mount Fuji is a {mountain} (it is also a volcano).
  - These pictures are woodblock {prints}: carved wood blocks were inked and pressed onto paper.

## Main: Find Fuji
table: Print | Where is Fuji in the picture? | Big or small? | How did Hokusai make it look that way?
  - The Great Wave | {low in the picture, framed by the curling wave} | {small} | {the huge wave fills the front, so Fuji looks far away}
  - Fine Wind, Clear Morning | {fills most of the picture} | {big} | {nothing else is in front; the mountain almost touches the edges}
  - Fujimigahara in Owari Province | {inside the round barrel, far away} | {small} | {the giant barrel is close; Fuji peeks through it}

## Think about it
choice: In The Great Wave, what is the biggest thing in the picture?
  - Mount Fuji
  * the wave
  - the boats
  - the sky
choice: Why do faraway things look small in a picture?
  * Things look smaller the farther away they are from us.
  - Faraway things are always small in real life.
  - The artist ran out of room.
write: Fuji is the subject of the whole series. Why do you think Hokusai sometimes made it tiny?
  lines: 3
  key: To surprise us and make us look for it; to show Fuji is always there in everyday life while people work; to make the wave or the barrel feel huge and powerful next to it. Accept any reason tied to the prints.

## Stretch
draw: Draw your own view of a mountain. Make it tiny by putting something big up close. | 2

# 25. Handle with Care Rules
minutes: 35
materials: scissors, glue stick, pencil
ican: I can sort actions that keep artwork safe from actions that harm it and explain why.
directions: Cut out the 10 action cards. Glue each one under "Safe for artwork" or "Harmful to artwork." Then write rules for your class and explain one of them.
mistakes: Students sometimes put "store art in a sunny window" under safe because sunlight seems nice. Light, especially sunlight, slowly fades colors. Watch for "stack wet paintings" in the safe pile: wet paint sticks to the paper on top and both get ruined.
notes: Key: safe, wash and dry hands first; carry it flat with two hands; let paintings dry flat before stacking; put clean paper between chalk pastel drawings; store art in a flat folder out of the sun. Harmful: stack wet paintings; fold a painting to fit in a backpack; touch the front with sticky fingers; eat a snack next to drying art; hang art in a sunny window for months. Museums protect art the same ways: clean hands or gloves, carrying with two hands, flat storage and low light to keep colors from fading.

## Warm up: Why care?
choice: Why do museums keep the lights dim near old paintings and drawings?
  - To save money on light bulbs
  * Strong light, especially sunlight, slowly fades colors.
  - So visitors cannot see well
  - Paintings like the dark

# 35. Same Place, New Mood
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, crayons or colored pencils (include bright and dark colors)
ican: I can change the mood of a place by changing only its colors and weather.
directions: Plan two versions of the playground: one cheerful, one spooky. Keep the same swings, slide and trees in both. Change only the colors and the weather. Fill in the plan, draw both pictures, and explain what you changed.
mistakes: Students often add monsters or ghosts to the spooky picture. The challenge is to change only colors and weather: same slide, same swings, same trees. Others make both pictures with the same colors and only add rain. Push for a full color change: sky, ground, shadows and light.
notes: Cheerful choices: warm and bright colors (yellow, orange, light green, sky blue), a sunny sky, soft clouds, short shadows. Spooky choices: dark and cool colors (dark blue, purple, gray, black-green), fog, a stormy sky, a pale moon, long shadows. Draw the same outline in both boxes first, lightly in pencil, then color. Lightly tracing the first drawing on a window onto the second box helps students keep the scene the same.

## Warm up: Mood makers
choice: Which change would make a picture feel most spooky?
  - Adding a bright yellow sun
  * Making the sky dark purple with fog near the ground
  - Coloring the grass light green
  - Adding puffy white clouds

## Plan
table: Part of the picture | Cheerful | Spooky
  height: 0.45
  - Sky color | |
  - Weather | |
  - Ground and grass colors | |
  - Light and shadows | |
note: Answers vary. Cheerful: sky blue, sunny, bright green grass, short shadows. Spooky: dark purple or gray, fog or a storm, dark green or gray ground, long shadows and a pale moon.

## Reflect
write: Which change made the biggest difference to the mood: the colors or the weather? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks one and gives evidence: "The colors, because when I made the sky dark purple, the same slide looked creepy even before I added fog."
