# 1. Five Shot Sizes
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can choose a shot size to show a place, a person or a detail.
directions: Match each shot size to what fills the frame. Then sketch the same subject in all five shot sizes: a student reading on a park bench under a tree. Answer the questions about when to use each shot.
mistakes: Students often draw a medium shot and a close-up that look the same. Check where the frame cuts the body: a medium shot cuts at the waist, a close-up shows the face and maybe the shoulders. In an extreme wide shot the person should be tiny; many students draw them too big. Keep the subject and setting the same in every frame so only the shot size changes.
notes: Stick figures and simple shapes are fine: storyboard artists sketch fast. A quick demo with your hands as a frame (or a rectangle cut out of paper) helps students see the five sizes before drawing. Shot names vary a little between filmmakers; these five are the common core.

## Warm up: What fills the frame?
match: Write the letter next to each shot size.
  - Extreme wide shot = The whole place; the person is tiny
  - Wide shot = The whole body, head to feet, with some setting around it
  - Medium shot = The person from the waist up
  - Close-up = The face fills most of the frame
  - Extreme close-up = One small detail, like an eye or a hand on a page

## Main: Sketch the five sizes
text: Subject: a student reading on a park bench under a tree. Keep the subject and setting the same in every frame.
draw: 1. Extreme wide shot | 1.3
draw: 2. Wide shot | 1.3
draw: 3. Medium shot | 1.3
draw: 4. Close-up | 1.3
draw: 5. Extreme close-up | 1.3

## Main: Choose the shot
choice: Which shot best shows a character's fear?
  - Extreme wide shot
  - Wide shot
  * Close-up
  - Medium shot
choice: Which shot best tells the audience where the story takes place?
  * Extreme wide shot
  - Close-up
  - Extreme close-up
  - Medium shot
choice: A character reads a secret note. Which shot lets the audience read the words?
  - Wide shot
  - Medium shot
  * Extreme close-up
  - Extreme wide shot
choice: Two characters talk while sitting side by side. Which shot shows both of them and their gestures?
  - Extreme close-up
  * Medium shot
  - Extreme wide shot
  - Close-up of one face

## Stretch
write: Put three of your shots in order to open a short film about the reader. Explain why you chose that order.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A common strong order: extreme wide (show the park), medium (meet the reader), close-up (see the reaction to the book). This moves the audience from place to person to feeling. Starting close and pulling back can also work if the student says it builds mystery.

# 7. Photo Walk Shot List
minutes: 40
materials: pencil; for the walk: a camera or tablet, this sheet on a clipboard
ican: I can plan photos by choosing a subject, shot size, angle and light for each one.
directions: Match the terms in the warm up. Answer the questions about light. Then plan 8 shots for a photo walk around the school. Your list must meet every requirement in the box.
mistakes: Students plan 8 photos of the same thing from the same height. Check the list against the requirements before the walk. Light notes are often left blank or say "good light"; ask where the light comes from (front, side, behind) and what it will do to the subject. Remind students that a shot list is a plan: it can change on the walk when they find something better.
notes: Before the walk, agree on boundaries and a meeting time. Students stay with their group, photograph only places on school grounds, and ask permission before including any person in a photo (or plan shots with no people). If there are no cameras, students can make the walk with a paper "viewfinder" (a card with a rectangle cut out) and sketch their best three frames.

## Warm up: Words for the walk
match: Write the letter of the meaning next to each term.
  - Wide shot = Shows the whole subject and lots of what is around it
  - Close-up = Fills the frame with one small part, like a face or a leaf
  - Low angle = Camera below, looking up; the subject looks tall or strong
  - High angle = Camera above, looking down; the subject looks small
  - Side light = Light from one side; shows texture and shadows
  - Backlight = Light from behind; makes a glowing edge or a silhouette
choice: At noon on a sunny day, what are the shadows like?
  - Long and soft
  * Short, dark and hard-edged
  - There are never any shadows at noon
  - Long and blue
choice: Why do many photographers like the hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset?
  - The sun is directly overhead
  - Cameras work faster in the morning
  * The light is low, warm and soft, with long shadows
  - There is no light at all

## Main: Plan 8 shots
example: **Requirements.** At least 2 close-ups, at least 1 wide shot, 1 low angle, 1 high angle, 1 shot using side light or backlight, 1 pattern (bricks, fence, tiles) and 1 leading line (a path, a railing, a hallway). **Model:** Subject: the bike rack's repeating bars. Size: close-up. Angle: high, looking down. Light: side light from the morning sun makes stripes of shadow. Requirement: pattern.

## Stretch
write: Choose one shot from your list. Describe how it will look if you take it at noon and how it will look late in the afternoon. Which will you choose, and why?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer compares hard, short shadows overhead at noon with low, warmer side light and long shadows late in the day, and chooses based on the subject, such as "late afternoon, because the side light will show the rough bark of the tree."

# 20. Animation Basics Check
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can identify animation principles in a frame strip and calculate frames and seconds.
directions: Read each frame strip and choose the principle it shows. Then solve the frame problems. Show your work in the margin.
mistakes: Students confuse anticipation (the move before the action) with follow-through (the parts that keep moving after the action stops). For "on twos," students forget that each drawing fills two frames, so they need half as many drawings as frames.
notes: The four principles here come from the twelve principles described by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in The Illusion of Life (1981). Frames per second (fps) is how many frames play each second; 24 fps is the film standard, and 12 fps is common in classroom animation. Item 4 shows spacing that starts small and grows, which animators call easing, or slow in and slow out.

## Warm up: Frame strips
table: Strip 1 | Frame 1 | Frame 2 | Frame 3 | Frame 4
  - A bouncing ball | Round, at the top | Stretched long as it falls | Squashed flat on the floor | Stretched tall as it bounces up
choice: Which principle does strip 1 show?
  * Squash and stretch
  - Anticipation
  - Follow-through
  - Slow in and slow out
table: Strip 2 | Frame 1 | Frame 2 | Frame 3 | Frame 4
  - A jumper | Standing | Crouches low and swings arms back | Springs up | In the air
choice: Which principle do frames 1 and 2 of strip 2 show?
  - Squash and stretch
  * Anticipation
  - Follow-through
  - Slow in and slow out
table: Strip 3 | Frame 1 | Frame 2 | Frame 3 | Frame 4
  - A running dog stops | Running | Paws plant to stop | Body has stopped; ears and tail still fly forward | Ears and tail settle
choice: Which principle does strip 3 show?
  - Anticipation
  - Slow in and slow out
  * Follow-through
  - Squash and stretch
table: Strip 4 | Frame 1 | Frame 2 | Frame 3 | Frame 4
  - A car pulls away | Car at 0 cm | Car at 1 cm | Car at 3 cm | Car at 7 cm
choice: Which principle does strip 4 show?
  - Squash and stretch
  - Anticipation
  - Follow-through
  * Slow in and slow out (easing)

## Main: Frame math
example: At 24 frames per second (fps), 2 seconds = 2 x 24 = 48 frames. "On twos" means each drawing is shown for 2 frames, so 48 frames need 48 / 2 = 24 drawings.
fill:
  - A 3-second walk at 24 fps needs {72} frames.
  - Animated on twos at 24 fps, the same 3-second walk needs {36} drawings.
  - 90 frames at 12 fps lasts {7.5} seconds.
  - A blink lasts 6 frames at 24 fps. That is {0.25} of a second.

## Stretch
draw: Draw a 4-frame strip of a new action that uses anticipation, such as throwing a ball or kicking. Label the anticipation frame. | 1.8
note: Look for a clear move in the opposite direction before the action: the arm pulls back before the throw, the leg swings back before the kick. The anticipation frame should come before the main action, not after it.

# 21. One Story, Four Media
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan how one story changes when it is told in different media.
directions: Read the story. Answer the questions about the four media. Then plan the story for each medium: tell how you would show the story, what tools you would use, its biggest strength and its biggest limit. Finish by choosing the best medium for an audience.
mistakes: Students often plan the same thing four times ("show the dog"). Push them to use what each medium does best: a poster has one image and few words; a podcast has only sound; a comic controls time panel by panel; a video has motion and sound together. A poster is not a story told in order: it has to grab attention in one look.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. Accept a range of strengths and limits if students explain them. Partners can each take one medium and then compare plans.

## Read the story
example: **Biscuit Comes Home:** Biscuit, a small brown dog, slips out of the yard during a thunderstorm. Her owner, Leo, makes signs and asks neighbors for help. Two days later, a girl named Ana hears whimpering under the library steps. She reads the tag on Biscuit's collar and calls Leo, who runs over in his slippers.

## Warm up: What can each medium do?
choice: Which medium tells the story with no pictures at all?
  - Comic
  - Poster
  * Podcast
  - Short video
choice: Which medium has to tell the story in a single image?
  - Comic
  * Poster
  - Podcast
  - Short video
choice: Which medium uses moving pictures and sound together?
  - Comic
  - Poster
  - Podcast
  * Short video
choice: In a podcast, how could you show the thunderstorm?
  - Draw lightning
  * Use rain and thunder sound effects and a narrator
  - Write the word STORM in big letters
  - Use a close-up shot

## Main: Planning chart
example: For each medium, write how you would show the story, the tools you need, its biggest strength and its biggest limit. **Model, comic:** 6 panels: storm and escape, Leo's signs, empty yard, Ana hears a sound, close-up of the tag, the reunion. Tools: pencil, pens, paper. Strength: readers control the pace and see every key moment. Limit: no real sound, so the whimper has to be written.
write: **Poster**
  lines: 3
  key: Show: one image of Biscuit and Leo hugging on the library steps, a title like "Biscuit Comes Home." Tools: pencil, markers, paper. Strength: grabs attention in one look. Limit: cannot show events in order, so most of the story is left out.
write: **Podcast**
  lines: 3
  key: Show: narrator plus voices for Leo and Ana, sound effects for rain, thunder and whimpering. Tools: a recorder, script, sound objects. Strength: sound builds mood and lets listeners imagine. Limit: visual details like the tag and the slippers must be described.
write: **Short video**
  lines: 3
  key: Show: shots of the storm, Leo hanging signs, a close-up of the tag, the run in slippers. Tools: a camera, actors, a dog or a stuffed toy, editing. Strength: motion and sound together feel real. Limit: hardest to make; needs a dog, locations and time.
write: Which part of the story is easiest to tell in a podcast, and which is hardest? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Easiest: the storm or the whimper under the steps, because sound tells it well. Hardest: reading the tag or seeing Leo's slippers, because those are visual details a narrator has to describe.

## Stretch
write: A preschool class will hear or see this story. Which medium would you choose for them, and how would you change the story?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks a medium that fits young children (a comic read aloud or a short video), keeps the storm gentle, uses few words and big clear pictures or sounds, and ends happily as the story does.

# 33. Emotion and Sharing
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, scissors, glue
ican: I can sort videos by the emotions they cause and use research to predict which will spread.
directions: Cut out the 8 video cards and sort them by the main emotion each one would make a viewer feel. Glue them down. Rate how fired up each emotion makes people feel, read about the research, then predict which videos will be shared most.
mistakes: Students sort by topic ("animal videos") instead of by feeling. Ask "How would you feel right after watching it?" Some students decide that angry videos are good because they spread; the stretch is the place to talk about why spreading is not the same as being worth sharing.
notes: Every video on the cards is made up. The research is real: Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman studied which New York Times articles readers emailed most (Journal of Marketing Research, 2012). Content that caused high-energy emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) was shared more; content that caused sadness, a low-energy emotion, was shared less. Positive content was shared more than negative content overall. Card sorts can reasonably differ; accept a different column if the student explains it.

## Warm up
choice: Which emotion is "high energy," the kind that makes you want to do something right away?
  - Feeling sleepy
  - Feeling a little sad
  * Feeling amazed
  - Feeling bored
choice: What does it mean when a video "goes viral"?
  - It has a computer virus
  * People share it so fast that it reaches a huge audience
  - It is about a disease
  - Only one person watches it

## Main: Sort the videos
sort: Glue each card under the main emotion it would cause.
  - Awe (amazed): Time-lapse of a thunderstorm rolling over a desert canyon; A diver films a whale shark gliding past
  - Amusement (funny): A goat tries to stand on a wobbly ball and keeps sliding off; A parrot copies a microwave beep perfectly
  - Anger: A video shows a company dumping trash into a river at night; A referee's clearly wrong call ends a youth championship
  - Sadness: A lighthouse keeper's last day before the light shuts down; An old bakery closes after 60 years and the owner says goodbye

## Main: Energy and the research
chart: Rating: 1 (calm) to 5 (very fired up) | Why do you think so?
  labels: Awe; Amusement; Anger; Sadness
  key: Typical ratings: awe 4 or 5, amusement 4, anger 5, sadness 1 or 2. Sadness is a low-energy emotion that makes people quiet and still; the others make people want to react or tell someone.
text: **The research:** Two researchers studied which news articles people emailed to others most. Articles that caused high-energy emotions like awe, anger and worry were shared more. Articles that made people sad were shared less.
choice: Using the research, which pair of videos is most likely to spread the most?
  * The thunderstorm time-lapse and the river dumping video
  - The lighthouse keeper and the old bakery
  - The bakery and the parrot
  - All eight will spread the same amount
write: Which single video do you predict will spread the most? Explain using the research and your ratings.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks an awe or anger video (or a funny one) and explains that it causes a high-energy emotion, which the research links to more sharing, while the sad videos cause low energy and are shared less.

## Stretch
write: Anger makes videos spread. Why could that be a problem? What should you ask yourself before sharing an angry video?
  lines: 3
  key: Creators may make people angry on purpose just to get shares, even with videos that are misleading or missing context. Before sharing, ask: Is it true? Is the whole story shown? Who made it, and why? Will sharing it help or just spread anger?
