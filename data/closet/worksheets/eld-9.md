# 1. Fact, Value or Policy Claim?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell fact, value and policy claims apart and write each kind on a school topic.
directions: Cut out the 12 claim cards. Sort each one: a fact claim (can be checked as true or false), a value claim (judges something as good, bad, better or more important) or a policy claim (says what should be done). Then write one claim of each kind about a school topic.
mistakes: Students often call any claim they agree with a fact. A fact claim can be checked with evidence even if it turns out to be false. Watch for "should" hidden in value claims and "best" hidden in policy claims; the key words are clues, not rules, so ask what the claim is really asking the listener to accept.
notes: Signal words: fact claims use is, are, was, more than, causes; value claims use best, worse, important, fair, moving; policy claims use should, ought to, must, needs to. A fact claim does not have to be true to be a fact claim: "Our school day starts at 7:45 a.m." is checkable either way. Teenagers needing more sleep than most adults matches CDC and AASM guidance (8 to 10 hours for teens, 7 or more for adults). The glue boxes are short for sentence strips: students can glue the cards in overlapping rows, or glue them on the back under the same three headings.

## Warm up
text: **Word bank:** fact claim, value claim, policy claim, evidence, judge, should, ought to, must, best, important, can be checked, I claim that
example: **Fact:** The school library has 9,000 books. (Count them to check.)
  **Value:** Reading for fun is more important than reading for a grade. (A judgment.)
  **Policy:** The library should stay open until 5:00 p.m. (An action.)

## Main: Sort the claims
sort: Glue each claim card under its type.
  - Fact claim: Our school day starts at 7:45 a.m., The new library has more computers than the old one., More students rode bikes to school this year than last year., Teenagers need more sleep each night than most adults.
  - Value claim: Group projects are the best way to learn., The fall play was the most moving performance in years., Honesty matters more than winning a game., Art class is just as important as math class.
  - Policy claim: The school should start the day at 8:30 a.m., Students ought to be allowed to use phones at lunch., The city must add a bike lane on the road to school., Every student should take a public speaking class before graduating.

## Main: Explain a choice
write: Pick one card that was hard to sort. Explain where you put it and why.
  lines: 3
  starter: I sorted "___" as a ___ claim because ___.
  key: Example: I sorted "Teenagers need more sleep each night than most adults" as a fact claim because it can be checked with sleep research, even though it sounds like advice.

## Main: Write your own
write: Write a **fact claim** about your school or a school topic.
  lines: 2
  starter: It is a fact that ...
  key: Example: It is a fact that our cafeteria serves about 600 lunches a day. (Checkable with a number or record.)
write: Write a **value claim** on the same topic.
  lines: 2
  starter: I believe ___ is (better, more important, fairer) than ___ because ...
  key: Example: I believe a longer lunch is more important than a longer class period because students need time to eat and rest.
write: Write a **policy claim** on the same topic.
  lines: 2
  starter: The school should ___ so that ___.
  key: Example: The school should add a second lunch line so that every student has at least 20 minutes to eat.

## Stretch: Claims work together
write: How could your fact claim support your policy claim in a debate?
  lines: 3
  starter: My fact claim supports my policy because ...
  key: Example: My fact claim supports my policy because if the cafeteria serves 600 lunches with one line, the numbers show why the wait is long, which gives a reason to add a second line.

# 7. Academic Argument Check
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can classify claims, write a rebuttal and connect ideas with argument transitions.
directions: Classify each claim as fact, value or policy. Write a rebuttal to a counterclaim. Then complete the argument with the best transitions from the bank.
mistakes: Students often label a claim "fact" because it has a number in it or because they agree with it. Ask, "Could we check it with a record or a measurement?" For rebuttals, watch for "Critics are wrong" with no reason; a rebuttal answers the counterclaim with a reason or evidence. With transitions, students mix up consequently (a result) and furthermore (one more point).
notes: Items 1 to 3 are a quick check of the fact, value and policy sort from sheet 1. The rebuttal can take either a compromise (lockers with a lunch check) or a direct answer (office phones for emergencies); accept any rebuttal that answers the emergency concern with a reason. The graduation rate in item 2 is a made-up school's.

## Warm up: What kind of claim?
text: **Word bank:** fact claim, value claim, policy claim, counterclaim, rebuttal, however, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, in contrast
choice: "The town should build a skate park next to the library."
  - Fact claim
  - Value claim
  * Policy claim
choice: "Our school's graduation rate rose from 84 percent to 90 percent in five years."
  * Fact claim
  - Value claim
  - Policy claim
choice: "Volunteering is the most meaningful way to spend a Saturday."
  - Fact claim
  * Value claim
  - Policy claim

## Main: Write a rebuttal
text: **Claim:** Students should keep their phones in their lockers during class.
text: **Counterclaim:** Critics argue that students need their phones in class in case of an emergency.
write: Write a rebuttal that answers the counterclaim with a reason.
  lines: 4
  starter: Critics argue that ___. However, ___.
  key: Critics argue that students need their phones in class in case of an emergency. However, every classroom has a phone and the front office can reach families quickly, so students can still be contacted in an emergency without having a phone at their desks.

## Main: Add transitions
text: **Bank:** furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, in contrast
fill:
  - Phones distract students during lessons. {Furthermore}, they make it easier to cheat on tests.
  - Teachers at schools with locker rules report fewer interruptions. {Consequently}, more class time goes to learning.
  - Some students say they use phones to look up words. {Nevertheless}, classroom dictionaries and laptops can do the same job.
  - At schools without a phone rule, students check phones many times per class. {In contrast}, students at locker-rule schools focus on the lesson.

## Stretch: Explain a choice
write: Why does "consequently" fit the second sentence better than "furthermore"?
  lines: 3
  starter: "Consequently" shows ___, while "furthermore" shows ___. In the second sentence, ___.
  key: "Consequently" shows a result, while "furthermore" adds another point. In the second sentence, more learning time is the result of fewer interruptions, so "consequently" fits.

# 20. Model Explanation Critique
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can judge an explanation of a model for accuracy, precise words and use of data.
directions: Read about the class water cycle model and its data. Then read two students' explanations. Score each one from 1 to 4 on the rubric, give a reason for each score, and improve the weaker one.
mistakes: Students often reward the longer or friendlier explanation. Score what it says: is the science right, are the terms exact, and are numbers used? Watch for students who accept "boiled" and "steam": the water was warm, not boiling, and water vapor is invisible.
notes: The model and data are sample class results for a common demonstration. Explanation A uses evaporation, water vapor, condensation and precipitation correctly and cites the 10-minute droplets, 14 mL and the clear water. Explanation B has three science errors (the water did not boil; "steam" is not the right word for invisible water vapor; the water in the cup does not flow back on its own in this model), no data and informal grammar ("really good"). Accept scores within one point of the key if the reason is sound.

## The model
example: **Model:** A clear bowl holds 500 mL of warm water (about 40 °C) with a few drops of blue food coloring. An empty cup sits in the middle. Plastic wrap covers the bowl, and a bag of ice sits on the wrap right above the cup. The bowl sits in a sunny window.
  **Data:** After 10 minutes, tiny droplets formed under the plastic wrap. After 45 minutes, 14 mL of water had collected in the cup. The water in the cup was clear, not blue.
text: **Word bank:** evaporation, water vapor, condensation, precipitation, collection, accurate, precise, data, model, represents, invisible

## Read the explanations
text: **Explanation A:** The warm water in the bowl evaporated: it changed from a liquid into invisible water vapor. When the vapor reached the plastic wrap, which was cooled by the ice, it condensed into droplets, which we saw after 10 minutes. The droplets grew heavy and dripped into the cup, like precipitation. After 45 minutes, 14 mL had collected. The cup water was clear even though the bowl water was blue, which shows that only water evaporates and the dye stays behind, the way salt stays in the ocean.
text: **Explanation B:** The water got hot and boiled, so steam went up. The steam turned into rain because of the ice and fell in the cup. Then the water went back into the bowl and did it all again. The model worked really good.

## Main: Score with the rubric
table: Explanation | Accuracy (1 to 4) | Precise words (1 to 4) | Use of data (1 to 4) | Reason for the scores
  height: 0.7
  - A | {4} | {4} | {4} | {All steps are correct; uses evaporation, water vapor, condensation; cites 10 minutes, 14 mL and the clear water.}
  - B | {1} | {1} | {1} | {Water did not boil; "steam" and "went up" are vague; no numbers or observations.}
choice: Which sentence from B has a science error?
  - "The steam turned into rain because of the ice."
  * "The water got hot and boiled, so steam went up."
  - Both are correct.
  - Neither has an error.

## Main: Improve B
write: Rewrite Explanation B so it is accurate, precise and uses data.
  lines: 6
  starter: The warm water ___. When the water vapor ___. The droplets ___. After 45 minutes, ___.
  key: The warm water evaporated into invisible water vapor without boiling. When the water vapor touched the cold plastic wrap, it condensed into droplets, which formed after 10 minutes. The droplets fell into the cup like precipitation. After 45 minutes, 14 mL of clear water had collected, which shows the food coloring stayed behind when the water evaporated.

## Stretch: Limitations
write: Name one way this model is different from the real water cycle.
  lines: 3
  starter: One limitation of the model is that ___, but in the real water cycle ___.
  key: One limitation of the model is that the ice cools the air right above the water, but in the real water cycle water vapor rises high into the atmosphere and cools as it rises, forming clouds before precipitation falls over a huge area.

# 21. Explaining Models and Data Check
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can describe a graph, label the parts of a model and name a limitation of a model.
directions: Plot the data and describe what the graph shows. Then match the parts of a classroom model to what they stand for, and explain what the model cannot show.
mistakes: Students describe a graph by listing every point. Push for the trend and two numbers that show it. For limitations, "it's not real" is too vague; name a specific thing the model leaves out or gets wrong (size, distance, tilt).
notes: The oxygen values are standard figures for fresh water at sea level (about 14.6 mg/L at 0 °C, 11.3 at 10 °C, 9.1 at 20 °C and 7.6 at 30 °C), rounded to one decimal. The drop is steeper at low temperatures. The lamp and globe model is a common day and night demonstration.

## Warm up: Language for data and models
text: **Word bank:** increase, decrease, trend, steady, steep, level off, model, represent, stands for, scale, limitation, does not show, dissolved oxygen
fill:
  - When a line goes down from left to right, the values {decrease}.
  - A model {represents} a real thing in a simpler way.

## Main: Describe a graph
text: Dissolved oxygen is the oxygen gas mixed into water. Fish and other water animals breathe it. The table shows the most oxygen that fresh water can hold at different temperatures.
table: Water temperature (°C) | Dissolved oxygen (mg/L)
  - 0 | 14.6
  - 5 | 12.8
  - 10 | 11.3
  - 15 | 10.1
  - 20 | 9.1
  - 25 | 8.3
  - 30 | 7.6
graph: Water temperature (°C) | 0 30 5 | Dissolved oxygen (mg/L) | 0 16 2
  key: 0,14.6 5,12.8 10,11.3 15,10.1 20,9.1 25,8.3 30,7.6
write: Describe the trend in the graph. Use two numbers.
  lines: 3
  starter: As water temperature increases from ___ to ___, dissolved oxygen ___ from ___ to ___. The line is steeper ___.
  key: As water temperature increases from 0 °C to 30 °C, dissolved oxygen decreases from 14.6 to 7.6 mg/L, almost half. The line is steeper at low temperatures and levels off a little as the water warms.
write: What could this mean for fish in a pond on a hot summer day?
  lines: 2
  starter: The graph suggests that ...
  key: The graph suggests that warm water holds less oxygen, so fish may have less oxygen to breathe on hot days.

## Main: Label a model
text: **Model:** In a dark room, a student shines a lamp at a globe and slowly spins the globe.
match: What does each part of the model represent?
  - The lamp = The Sun
  - The globe = Earth
  - Spinning the globe = Earth's rotation on its axis
  - The side of the globe facing the lamp = Places having daytime
  - The side of the globe away from the lamp = Places having night

## Stretch: Limitation
write: Name one limitation of the lamp and globe model.
  lines: 3
  starter: One limitation of this model is that it does not show ___, because in reality ___.
  key: One limitation is that it does not show true size and distance, because in reality the Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth and about 150 million km away, not a lamp a few feet from a globe.

# 33. Register Ladder
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can rank messages from very casual to very formal and choose the right register for an audience.
directions: Jordan needs to reschedule a meeting and wrote the request eight ways. Read versions A to H. Write each letter on the ladder, from very casual at the bottom to very formal at the top. Then choose the best version for each audience and explain one choice.
mistakes: Students often rank by length. Point to the features that raise register: a greeting with a title, no abbreviations or slang, an apology, polite modal verbs (could, would it be possible), a reason, a specific time and a closing. Watch for students who pick the most formal version for every audience; a very formal message to a best friend sounds cold.
notes: Ladder from bottom to top: C, G, E, A, H, D, B, F. Rungs 3 and 4 (E and A) and rungs 5 and 6 (H and D) are close; accept a swap if the student explains it with a feature. Jordan, Ms. Rivera and Mr. Okafor are made-up people. For audiences, accept C or G for a best friend, G or E for a teammate, D or B for a teacher and only F for a hiring manager.

## Warm up
text: **Word bank:** register, casual, formal, audience, purpose, greeting, closing, abbreviation, polite, reschedule, appropriate
text: **A.** Hi, I'm sorry, but I can't meet today. Could we meet tomorrow instead?
text: **B.** Dear Ms. Rivera, I apologize for the short notice, but I am unable to attend our meeting today because of a schedule conflict. Would it be possible to reschedule for tomorrow at 3:30 p.m.? Thank you, Jordan
text: **C.** cant make it today lol. tmrw?
text: **D.** Hello Ms. Rivera, I apologize, but I have a schedule conflict today. Would it be possible to meet tomorrow after school?
text: **E.** Hi, sorry, I can't make it today. Can we meet tomorrow?
text: **F.** Dear Mr. Okafor, Thank you for the opportunity to interview for the library assistant position. Unfortunately, a scheduling conflict prevents me from attending on Tuesday. I would be grateful if we could reschedule at a time that is convenient for you. Sincerely, Jordan Lee
text: **G.** Hey, something came up. Can we do tomorrow?
text: **H.** Hello, I'm sorry, but I have a schedule conflict today. Would tomorrow work for you?

## Main: Build the ladder
chart: Version letter | One feature that puts it here
  labels: 8 Very formal; 7; 6; 5; 4; 3; 2; 1 Very casual
  height: 0.45
  key: 8 F, 7 B, 6 D, 5 H, 4 A, 3 E, 2 G, 1 C. Sample features: F has a full closing and thanks; B has a specific time; D has a title and "would it be possible"; H gives a reason; A uses "could"; E has a greeting and apology; G is slangy ("do tomorrow"); C has no capitals or punctuation and uses "lol".

## Main: Match the audience
table: Audience | Best version | Why?
  height: 0.5
  - A best friend | {C or G} | {Short and friendly; a friend expects casual words.}
  - A teammate you know a little | {G or E} | {Friendly but clear.}
  - A teacher | {D or B} | {Uses a title, an apology and a polite question.}
  - A hiring manager | {F} | {Most formal: thanks, a full closing and no contractions.}

## Stretch: Climb a rung
write: Rewrite version G so it moves up at least two rungs. Name the features you added.
  lines: 4
  starter: New version: ___. I added ___.
  key: New version: Hello, I'm sorry, but I have a conflict today. Would it be possible to meet tomorrow? I added a greeting, an apology, a reason and a polite modal question, and I removed the slang "do tomorrow."
