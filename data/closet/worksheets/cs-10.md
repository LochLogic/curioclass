# 1. Binary Search by Halves
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can trace binary search by recording low, middle and high at each step.
directions: Use the binary search below on each sorted list. Indexes start at 0, and mid rounds down. Record low, high, mid and the value at mid for every step, and what the search does next.
mistakes: The most common error is setting low = mid or high = mid instead of mid + 1 or mid - 1, which can loop forever. Others round mid up, or compute it from values instead of indexes. On list 3 students often stop when they reach a value bigger than 40; the search stops only when low passes high.
notes: Each step cuts the part still to search about in half, so the worst case for n items is floor(log2 n) + 1 steps: 10 steps for 1,000 items and 20 for 1,000,000, where linear search could need 1,000 or 1,000,000. List 4 (16 items, target last) shows the worst case for 16: 5 steps. Binary search needs the list sorted; on an unsorted list it can miss a target that is there.

## The algorithm
table: binary_search(list, target)
  - low = 0, high = length - 1
  - WHILE low <= high
  - mid = (low + high) / 2, rounded down
  - IF list[mid] = target THEN RETURN mid
  - IF list[mid] < target THEN low = mid + 1 ELSE high = mid - 1
  - END WHILE, then RETURN -1

# 6. Pick the Right Library Function
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can read a library's documentation and choose the function that does exactly what a task needs.
directions: The table is the documentation for a made-up library called kit. For each task, choose the best function, write the exact call, and write what it returns. Read every word of the docs: some functions look alike but behave differently.
mistakes: Students reach for round_half_up whenever they see a decimal. The bus task needs ceil (2.7 buses means 3 buses), and the egg-carton task needs floor (only full cartons count). Others pick truncate for money; it chops digits instead of rounding, so 0.4875 becomes 0.48. In the date task, the order of the arguments matters: days_between counts from the first date to the second, so swapping them gives -18.
notes: Answers: 1 format_money(12.5) returns "$12.50". 2 sort_by(events, "date") returns Chess match, Car wash, Bake sale. 3 ceil(130 / 48) returns 3 (130 / 48 is about 2.71). 4 round_half_up(0.4875, 2) returns 0.49. 5 days_between(2026-11-02, 2026-11-20) returns 18. 6 floor(50 / 12) returns 4 (50 / 12 is about 4.17). Stretch: sort_by(scores, "points", reverse=True). A real language has its own versions of these, often with surprises: in Python, round() rounds halves to the nearest even number and works on binary floats, which is why money code often uses a decimal library. This is a good follow-up: "Never guess from the name; read the docs."

## The documentation
table: Function | What it does | Example
  - round_half_up(x, places) | Rounds x to that many decimal places. A 5 or more in the next place rounds up. | round_half_up(2.345, 2) returns 2.35
  - truncate(x, places) | Cuts x to that many decimal places. Never rounds. | truncate(2.349, 2) returns 2.34
  - ceil(x) | The smallest whole number that is greater than or equal to x. | ceil(4.1) returns 5
  - floor(x) | The largest whole number that is less than or equal to x. | floor(4.9) returns 4
  - format_money(x) | Text with a $ sign, commas and exactly two decimal places (rounds half up). | format_money(1234.5) returns "$1,234.50"
  - sort_by(items, field, reverse=False) | A new list sorted by that field, smallest or earliest first. reverse=True sorts largest or latest first. | sort_by(books, "pages") lists the shortest book first
  - days_between(start, end) | The number of days from start to end. Negative if end comes before start. | days_between(2026-03-01, 2026-03-05) returns 4

# 17. Data and Networks Check
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator if you like
ican: I can compute recall, read a simulation graph, and trace a packet through routing tables.
directions: Three short parts. In Part 1, compute recall from a spam filter's results. In Part 2, read the graph from a rumor simulation. In Part 3, follow the routing tables hop by hop to deliver two packets.
mistakes: Students divide by everything the filter flagged (that is precision) instead of by all the real spam (recall). On the graph, students give the round when the most people know the rumor instead of the round with the biggest jump; have them write the increase for each round. In Part 3, students often stop at the first router that "knows about" the destination; a packet is delivered only when a router's table says "deliver here."
notes: Part 1: 80 real spam emails, 68 caught, 12 missed: recall = 68 / 80 = 0.85 = 85%. Precision (stretch) = 68 / (68 + 10) = 68 / 78, about 0.87 = 87%. Part 2 (made-up run of the class simulation): people who know after each round: 1, 2, 4, 8, 14, 21, 26, 29, 30. Increases: 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 5, 3, 1, so the biggest jump is from round 4 to round 5 (+7). Half the class (15) knows between rounds 4 and 5. The spread slows because fewer people are left who have not heard: most people a teller meets already know. Part 3: to 10.4.0.9: R1, R3, R4, delivered (3 routers). To 10.3.0.7: R1, R2, R4, delivered. If the R1 to R3 link fails, R1 could send 10.4 traffic to R2 instead, which forwards it to R3 then R4 (or update R2 to send it to R4).

## Part 1: Recall
text: A spam filter checked 500 emails. **80** of them were really spam. The filter flagged **78** emails as spam: **68** were real spam and **10** were not.
fill:
  - Real spam the filter missed: {12}
  - Recall = real spam caught / all real spam = {68} / {80} = {0.85}, or {85} percent
write: A school cares most that dangerous spam never reaches students. Is recall or precision the more important number for them? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Recall, because it measures how much of the real spam gets caught. A low recall means spam slips through to students; a few good emails sent to the spam folder (a precision problem) are less harmful here.

## Part 2: Read a simulation graph
text: A class of 30 simulated a rumor. In each round, everyone who knows the rumor tells one person chosen at random. Some of those people already know it.
graph: Round | 0 8 1 | People who know the rumor | 0 30 5
  line: 0,1 1,2 2,4 3,8 4,14 5,21 6,26 7,29 8,30
  minor: off
table: Round | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8
  - New people who heard it | {1} | {2} | {4} | {6} | {7} | {5} | {3} | {1}
fill:
  - The biggest jump came in round {5}.
  - Half the class (15 people) knew the rumor between round {4} and round {5}.
write: Why does the spread slow down near the end, even though more people are telling it?
  lines: 2
  key: Most people a teller picks already know the rumor, so fewer tellings reach someone new. With few people left who have not heard, the number of new hearers drops.

## Part 3: Trace a packet
text: Four routers connect four networks: 10.1.x.x is next to R1, 10.2.x.x next to R2, and 10.3.x.x and 10.4.x.x are both next to R4. Each router reads only its own table.
table: Router | Destination | Next hop
  - R1 | 10.1.x.x | deliver here
  - R1 | 10.4.x.x | R3
  - R1 | anything else | R2
  - R2 | 10.2.x.x | deliver here
  - R2 | 10.3.x.x or 10.4.x.x | R4
  - R2 | anything else | R1
  - R3 | 10.4.x.x | R4
  - R3 | anything else | R1
  - R4 | 10.3.x.x or 10.4.x.x | deliver here
  - R4 | anything else | R2
fill:
  - A packet for 10.4.0.9 starts at R1. Its path: R1, {R3}, {R4}, then {delivered}
  - A packet for 10.3.0.7 starts at R1. Its path: R1, {R2}, {R4}, then {delivered}
write: Stretch: The link between R1 and R3 breaks. How could you change one line of R1's table so packets for 10.4.0.9 still arrive?
  lines: 2
  key: Change R1's 10.4.x.x row to next hop R2. R2 sends 10.4.x.x to R4, which delivers it (path R1, R2, R4).

# 23. Stakeholder Analysis: School Cameras
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can map the stakeholders of a technology decision and weigh their interests, benefits and risks.
directions: Read the proposal. Map six stakeholders: what each one cares about, how the cameras could help them, and how they could be harmed. Then place them by power and interest, and recommend rules for the system.
mistakes: Students often list "the school" as one stakeholder; push them to split it (students, teachers, administrators, security staff). Others list only benefits for the people who proposed the plan and only risks for students. Every stakeholder can have both.
notes: The proposal is made up for Curio Class. Many states and districts limit cameras in places with an expectation of privacy, such as restrooms and locker rooms; students should not need to know the specific law to reason about it. Strong answers notice that students have the most at stake and the least power in the decision, and that rules about who sees the footage, for what, and for how long matter as much as where the cameras go.

## Read the proposal
text: **Proposal.** Northfield High School would install 60 cameras in hallways, entrances, the cafeteria, the parking lot and on school buses. Video would be stored for 30 days on a company's cloud servers. The principal and two security staff could watch live video and look back at recordings. Police could ask to see recordings during an investigation. The company offers an optional add-on that could recognize faces and send an alert when someone on a list enters the building.

## Map the stakeholders
chart: Stakeholder | Interests (what they care about) | Possible benefits | Possible risks
  rows: 6
  key: Students: safety, privacy, fair discipline; fewer fights and thefts; being watched all day, footage used for minor discipline, face data misused. Teachers: safe classrooms and halls, trust with students; evidence in incidents; being monitored at work. Parents and guardians: their children's safety and privacy; proof of what happened; data about their children held by a company. Administrators: safety, liability, budget; investigating incidents; cost, legal risk if footage leaks. The camera company: sales and add-ons; a contract; a data breach on its servers. Local police: solving crimes; leads in investigations; community trust if footage is used for minor issues.

## Power and interest
chart: High power, high interest | High power, low interest | Low power, high interest | Low power, low interest
  rows: 2
  key: High power, high interest: administrators, school board. High power, low interest: local police, the camera company once the contract is signed. Low power, high interest: students, parents and guardians, teachers. Low power, low interest: taxpayers and visitors. Placements can be argued; reasons matter.
write: Which stakeholder has the most at stake but the least power in this decision? What would give them a voice?
  lines: 3
  key: Students: they are recorded every day but do not vote on the plan. A student seat on the planning group, a survey before the decision, and a posted policy they can question would give them a voice.

## Recommend
write: Recommend three rules for the camera system. Explain which risk each rule reduces.
  lines: 6
  key: No cameras where people change or use the restroom (privacy). Delete footage after 30 days unless it is needed for a specific incident (limits misuse and breaches). Keep a log of who views footage and why (prevents snooping). Police get footage only with a written request for a specific incident (limits surveillance). Do not turn on face recognition without a public vote and accuracy testing (false matches, tracking).
write: Should the school buy the face recognition add-on? Take a side and give one reason from your stakeholder map.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer uses the map: for example, no, because students carry the risk of false matches and constant tracking while having the least say; or yes, only with strict limits, because administrators need to keep out people barred by a court order.

# 28. Building the Transatlantic Cable
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain the cost, the labor and the impact of laying the first transatlantic telegraph cables.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight one detail about money, one about workers, and one about what changed. Then answer the questions, citing paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students often think the 1858 cable worked for years; it failed after about three weeks, and lasting service came in 1866. Others credit one person (Cyrus Field) with building it; push them to list the people in paragraphs 2 and 3. On the impact question, students say "it was faster" without a number: the passage gives about ten days by ship versus minutes by cable.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class. Facts to verify before teaching: the Atlantic Telegraph Company, led by the American businessman Cyrus Field, raised about 350,000 pounds in 1856. Two ships, HMS Agamemnon and USS Niagara, completed the cable between Ireland and Newfoundland on August 5, 1858; Queen Victoria's message to President James Buchanan on August 16 took many hours to send (often reported as about 16). The cable failed within about a month, and too high a voltage applied by the company's electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, is usually blamed. After a failed attempt in 1865, the Great Eastern landed a working cable at Heart's Content, Newfoundland, on July 27, 1866, then recovered and completed the 1865 cable. The 1866 cable sent about 8 words a minute. Early prices were very high: commonly reported as about $100 in gold for a message of 10 to 20 words; check a source before giving students an exact figure. The insulation, gutta-percha, was a latex from trees in Southeast Asia, collected by local workers, often by cutting trees down.

## Read
passage: A Wire Across the Ocean | numbered
  In the 1850s, a message from New York to London traveled by steamship and took about ten days. Telegraph wires already crossed many countries, but the Atlantic Ocean was more than 2,000 miles wide and up to two miles deep. An American businessman, Cyrus Field, believed a cable could be laid along the ocean floor. In 1856, his Atlantic Telegraph Company raised about 350,000 British pounds from investors, an enormous sum at the time.
  Building the cable took the work of thousands of people. In factories in England, workers twisted copper wires into a core, wrapped it in layers of gutta-percha, a rubbery material that keeps water out, and covered it with strands of iron wire for strength. The gutta-percha came from trees in Southeast Asia. Local workers collected the sap, often by cutting the trees down, and it was shipped halfway around the world.
  Laying the cable was hard and risky. In 1857, the cable snapped and sank on the first attempt. In 1858, two warships, one British and one American, met in the middle of the ocean, joined their halves of the cable, and steamed in opposite directions toward Ireland and Newfoundland. Sailors worked day and night feeding cable over the side while storms rocked the ships. On August 5, 1858, the cable was complete.
  The celebration was huge. Queen Victoria sent a message to President James Buchanan, although it took many hours to get through. New York held parades and fireworks. But the signals grew weaker, and within about a month the cable stopped working. Many experts blamed the company's electrician, who had pushed very high voltage through the cable and likely damaged it.
  Field and his investors tried again. In 1865, the giant steamship Great Eastern, the only ship big enough to carry the whole cable, laid most of a new line before it broke and was lost. In 1866, the ship succeeded. On July 27, the cable reached Heart's Content, Newfoundland. Weeks later, the crew pulled the lost 1865 cable up from the deep sea floor, spliced it, and finished it too.
  The new cables changed the world. A message that once took ten days now crossed in minutes, at about eight words per minute. Governments, newspapers and traders used it most, because the price was very high: at first, a short message cost more than many workers earned in a month. Over the next decades, more cables were laid, prices fell, and a network of undersea cables grew. Today's internet still travels across oceans on undersea cables, now made of glass fiber.

## Questions
choice: Why did the 1858 cable stop working?
  - A ship cut it with its anchor.
  - Investors ran out of money and turned it off.
  * The signals weakened, likely after very high voltage damaged it.
  - The gutta-percha melted in the warm water.
write: Cost: Who paid for the cable, and why might investors risk so much money after the failures of 1857 and 1858? Cite a paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: Investors in Cyrus Field's Atlantic Telegraph Company paid, starting with about 350,000 pounds (paragraph 1). They risked more because a working cable would be very valuable: messages that took ten days could cross in minutes, and governments, newspapers and traders would pay high prices to use it (paragraph 6).
write: Labor: Name three different groups of workers the cable depended on, and the work each did.
  lines: 3
  key: Factory workers in England who made the core, insulation and iron armor (paragraph 2); workers in Southeast Asia who collected gutta-percha sap (paragraph 2); sailors who fed the cable over the side day and night in storms (paragraphs 3 and 5). write: Impact: How did the 1866 cable change communication? Use one number from the passage and say who could use it at first.
  lines: 3
  key: News that took about ten days by ship now crossed in minutes, at about eight words per minute (paragraphs 1 and 6). At first it was so expensive that mainly governments, newspapers and traders used it; ordinary people benefited later as more cables were laid and prices fell.

  lines: 3
  key: Alike: both carry messages under the ocean on cables laid by ships, and both needed huge investment and many workers. Different: today's cables are glass fiber carrying light, much faster than eight words a minute, and they carry data for billions of ordinary people, not just governments and businesses.
