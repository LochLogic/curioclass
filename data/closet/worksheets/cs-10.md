# 1. Binary Search by Halves
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can trace binary search by recording low, middle and high at each step.
directions: Use the binary search below on each sorted list. Indexes start at 0, and mid rounds down. Record low, high, mid and the value at mid for every step, and what the search does next.
mistakes: The most common error is setting low = mid or high = mid instead of mid + 1 or mid - 1, which can loop forever. Others round mid up, or compute it from values instead of indexes. On list 3 students often stop when they reach a value bigger than 40; the search stops only when low passes high.
notes: Each step cuts the part still to search about in half, so the worst case for n items is floor(log2 n) + 1 steps: 10 steps for 1,000 items and 20 for 1,000,000, where linear search could need 1,000 or 1,000,000. List 4 (16 items, target last) shows the worst case for 16: 5 steps. Binary search needs the list sorted; on an unsorted list it can miss a target that is there.

## The algorithm
table: binary_search(list, target)
  - low = 0, high = length - 1
  - WHILE low <= high
  - mid = (low + high) / 2, rounded down
  - IF list[mid] = target THEN RETURN mid
  - IF list[mid] < target THEN low = mid + 1 ELSE high = mid - 1
  - END WHILE, then RETURN -1

## Stretch
write: Why must the list be sorted for binary search to work? Use list 2 to explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Binary search throws away half the list based on one comparison. In list 2, seeing 31 at index 7 means 9 must be to the left only because every value to the right is bigger. If the list were not sorted, 9 could be on the right side, and the search would throw it away and wrongly return -1.

# 23. Stakeholder Analysis: School Cameras
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can map the stakeholders of a technology decision and weigh their interests, benefits and risks.
directions: Read the proposal. Map six stakeholders: what each one cares about, how the cameras could help them, and how they could be harmed. Then place them by power and interest, and recommend rules for the system.
mistakes: Students often list "the school" as one stakeholder; push them to split it (students, teachers, administrators, security staff). Others list only benefits for the people who proposed the plan and only risks for students. Every stakeholder can have both.
notes: The proposal is made up for Curio Class. Many states and districts limit cameras in places with an expectation of privacy, such as restrooms and locker rooms; students should not need to know the specific law to reason about it. Strong answers notice that students have the most at stake and the least power in the decision, and that rules about who sees the footage, for what, and for how long matter as much as where the cameras go.

## Read the proposal
text: **Proposal.** Northfield High School would install 60 cameras in hallways, entrances, the cafeteria, the parking lot and on school buses. Video would be stored for 30 days on a company's cloud servers. The principal and two security staff could watch live video and look back at recordings. Police could ask to see recordings during an investigation. The company offers an optional add-on that could recognize faces and send an alert when someone on a list enters the building.

## Map the stakeholders
chart: Stakeholder | Interests (what they care about) | Possible benefits | Possible risks
  rows: 6
  key: Students: safety, privacy, fair discipline; fewer fights and thefts; being watched all day, footage used for minor discipline, face data misused. Teachers: safe classrooms and halls, trust with students; evidence in incidents; being monitored at work. Parents and guardians: their children's safety and privacy; proof of what happened; data about their children held by a company. Administrators: safety, liability, budget; investigating incidents; cost, legal risk if footage leaks. The camera company: sales and add-ons; a contract; a data breach on its servers. Local police: solving crimes; leads in investigations; community trust if footage is used for minor issues.

## Power and interest
chart: High power, high interest | High power, low interest | Low power, high interest | Low power, low interest
  rows: 2
  key: High power, high interest: administrators, school board. High power, low interest: local police, the camera company once the contract is signed. Low power, high interest: students, parents and guardians, teachers. Low power, low interest: taxpayers and visitors. Placements can be argued; reasons matter.
write: Which stakeholder has the most at stake but the least power in this decision? What would give them a voice?
  lines: 3
  key: Students: they are recorded every day but do not vote on the plan. A student seat on the planning group, a survey before the decision, and a posted policy they can question would give them a voice.

## Recommend
write: Recommend three rules for the camera system. Explain which risk each rule reduces.
  lines: 6
  key: No cameras where people change or use the restroom (privacy). Delete footage after 30 days unless it is needed for a specific incident (limits misuse and breaches). Keep a log of who views footage and why (prevents snooping). Police get footage only with a written request for a specific incident (limits surveillance). Do not turn on face recognition without a public vote and accuracy testing (false matches, tracking).
write: Should the school buy the face recognition add-on? Take a side and give one reason from your stakeholder map.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer uses the map: for example, no, because students carry the risk of false matches and constant tracking while having the least say; or yes, only with strict limits, because administrators need to keep out people barred by a court order.
