# 1. What the Reader Knows: The Monkey's Paw
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can explain how dramatic irony creates suspense when readers know more than the characters.
directions: After reading "The Monkey's Paw" by W. W. Jacobs (1902), read the seven excerpts. Choose four moments where you, the reader, sense danger that the characters ignore or laugh off. Fill in the chart, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students sometimes call any scary moment dramatic irony. Dramatic irony needs a gap: the reader knows or suspects something the character does not. Herbert's jokes (excerpts 5 and 6) are the clearest cases: he jokes about the money and about coming back, and on a second reading the reader knows how the wish will be granted. Watch for answers that summarize the excerpt without naming what the reader senses.
notes: The excerpts follow Project Gutenberg eBook #12122 (The Lady of the Barge, 1902) word for word, with curly quotation marks made straight. The story includes a death in a factory accident and a grieving mother; it is commonly taught in grade 8, but preview it. Any four well-explained excerpts can fill the chart; the key gives the strongest four.

## Background
text: Sergeant-Major Morris, an old soldier back from India, visits the White family: Mr. and Mrs. White and their grown son, Herbert. He shows them a dried monkey's paw that he says will grant three wishes. Mr. White takes the paw, and at Herbert's urging wishes for two hundred pounds.

## Read: Seven excerpts
source: W. W. Jacobs, "The Monkey's Paw," in The Lady of the Barge (1902)
  [1] Morris, about the man who put a spell on the paw: "He wanted to show that fate ruled people's lives, and that those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow."
  [2] Morris, about the paw's first owner: "I don't know what the first two were, but the third was for death. That's how I got the paw."
  [3] Morris, after Mr. White pulls the paw out of the fire: "If you keep it, don't blame me for what happens. Pitch it on the fire again like a sensible man."
  [4] Herbert, joking: "Why, we're going to be rich, and famous and happy."
  [5] The next morning, when Mrs. White asks how two hundred pounds could hurt anyone: "Might drop on his head from the sky," said the frivolous Herbert.
  [6] "Well, don't break into the money before I come back," said Herbert as he rose from the table.
  [7] "I expect you'll find the cash tied up in a big bag in the middle of your bed," said Herbert, as he bade them good-night, "and something horrible squatting up on top of the wardrobe watching you as you pocket your ill-gotten gains."

## Warm up
choice: Which is the best definition of dramatic irony?
  - A character says the opposite of what he means.
  * The reader knows or senses something that a character does not.
  - An event happens that nobody expected.
  - The story ends sadly.
choice: In excerpt 3, how does Mr. White respond to Morris's warning?
  - He throws the paw back into the fire.
  - He gives the paw back to Morris.
  * He keeps the paw anyway.
  - He makes Morris promise never to use it.

## Main: Mark four moments
chart: Excerpt number | What the characters think or do | What the reader senses
  rows: 4
  key: [1] The family laughs; the reader senses the paw punishes anyone who meddles with fate. [3] Mr. White keeps the paw; the reader senses something terrible will happen and he has been warned. [5] Herbert jokes the money may fall on his head; the reader senses the money will come through harm to Herbert. [6] Herbert jokes "before I come back"; the reader suspects he will not come back. Also accept [2] (wishes lead to death) and [7] (something horrible watching them).
write: Choose one of Herbert's jokes. Explain why it is more frightening to the reader than to Herbert.
  lines: 4
  key: In excerpt 6 Herbert says "don't break into the money before I come back." He means it as a joke, but readers who heard Morris's warnings sense he may not come back; the money arrives as payment for his death, so his own words point to what happens.
write: How does Morris's dialogue build suspense before the first wish? Cite two excerpts.
  lines: 4
  key: Morris says the paw was made to show that people who interfere with fate do so "to their sorrow" [1], and that the last owner's third wish "was for death" [2]. He even begs Mr. White to "Pitch it on the fire" [3]. Each warning makes readers expect harm while the family laughs.

## Stretch
write: After Herbert dies, the company pays the Whites exactly two hundred pounds. How does knowing the ending change the way you read excerpts 4 to 7 a second time?
  lines: 4
  key: On a second reading, Herbert's jokes sound like terrible predictions: the money does come with harm to him, and he does not come back. The reader now hears the gap between his cheerful words and the truth, which is dramatic irony.
# 25. Strongest Evidence from Nonfiction
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can choose and explain the strongest evidence from a nonfiction text to support an analysis.
directions: Read the article. For each analysis question, choose the strongest of three pieces of evidence, then explain why it is the strongest and answer the question.
mistakes: Students often choose the most exciting or the longest quotation instead of the one that proves the point. Strongest evidence is direct (it answers the exact question), specific (names, numbers, events) and needs the least guessing. A piece of evidence can be true and still be weak if it is about a different point.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 340 words). Facts: Otto Lilienthal made about 2,000 glider flights in the 1890s and died after a glider crash in 1896; the Wrights built a wind tunnel in 1901 and tested about 200 wing shapes; Samuel Langley's Aerodrome failed in launches on October 7 and December 8, 1903; on December 17, 1903, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville flew 120 feet in 12 seconds and Wilbur's last flight that day lasted 59 seconds over 852 feet; Bessie Coleman earned her pilot's license in France in 1921.

## Read
passage: How the Wright Brothers Learned to Fly
  **[1]** In the 1890s, many people were trying to fly, and some of them died trying. A German engineer, Otto Lilienthal, made about 2,000 flights in gliders he built himself. In 1896 one of his gliders stalled in a gust of wind, and he died from the fall.
  **[2]** Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. They had no college degrees, but they read everything they could find about flight. They decided that the hardest problem was not power but control: a flying machine had to be steered and balanced in moving air, the way a cyclist balances on a bicycle.
  **[3]** Their gliders did not lift as well as the published numbers said they should. Instead of giving up, the brothers built a small wind tunnel in their shop in 1901 and tested about 200 tiny wing shapes. Their careful measurements showed that some of the old figures were wrong, and they used their own data to design new wings.
  **[4]** To control the plane, they invented "wing warping," twisting the wingtips with cables so the machine could tilt and turn. They tested it on hundreds of glider flights before they added an engine. When no company could sell them an engine that was light enough, their mechanic, Charlie Taylor, helped them build one.
  **[5]** Meanwhile, Samuel Langley, a famous scientist, had received $50,000 from the U.S. Army to build a flying machine. In October and December of 1903, his Aerodrome was launched from a boat and plunged into the Potomac River both times.
  **[6]** Nine days after Langley's second failure, on December 17, 1903, Orville Wright flew 120 feet in 12 seconds near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. By the fourth flight that day, Wilbur stayed in the air for 59 seconds and covered 852 feet.
  **[7]** Others soon followed. In 1909 Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel. In 1921 Bessie Coleman, who had been turned away by American flight schools because she was Black and a woman, earned her pilot's license in France.

## Warm up
choice: Which makes a piece of evidence strongest?
  - It is the longest sentence in the article.
  - It contains the most exciting event.
  * It directly answers the question with specific details.
  - It comes from the first paragraph.

## Main: Choose the strongest evidence
choice: Question 1: Why did the Wrights succeed when Langley failed?
  - "Samuel Langley, a famous scientist, had received $50,000 from the U.S. Army" [5]
  * "Their careful measurements showed that some of the old figures were wrong, and they used their own data to design new wings." [3]
  - "Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio." [2]
write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 1.
  lines: 3
  key: It shows the cause of their success: careful testing and their own data, which Langley's well-funded project lacked. The bicycle shop is background, and Langley's money shows funding was not the key.
choice: Question 2: What did the Wrights believe was the most important problem to solve?
  * "the hardest problem was not power but control" [2]
  - "They had no college degrees" [2]
  - "they added an engine" [4]
write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 2.
  lines: 3
  key: It states their belief directly, in their reasoning: control, not power. The other two are true details but do not say what they believed.
choice: Question 3: How does the author show that early flight was dangerous?
  - "Others soon followed." [7]
  * "In 1896 one of his gliders stalled in a gust of wind, and he died from the fall." [1]
  - "many people were trying to fly" [1]
write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 3.
  lines: 3
  key: It gives a specific, real result of the danger: an experienced pilot died. "Many people were trying to fly" shows interest, not danger.
choice: Question 4: Why does the author include Bessie Coleman?
  - "In 1909 Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel." [7]
  - "Orville Wright flew 120 feet in 12 seconds" [6]
  * "Bessie Coleman, who had been turned away by American flight schools because she was Black and a woman, earned her pilot's license in France." [7]
write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 4.
  lines: 3
  key: It is the only evidence about Coleman herself, and it shows that like the Wrights she succeeded despite obstacles; the author includes her to show that flight's history also includes people who were shut out and found another way.
