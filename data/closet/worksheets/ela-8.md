# 1. What the Reader Knows: The Monkey's Paw
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can explain how dramatic irony creates suspense when readers know more than the characters.
directions: After reading "The Monkey's Paw" by W. W. Jacobs (1902), read the seven excerpts. Choose four moments where you, the reader, sense danger that the characters ignore or laugh off. Fill in the chart, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students sometimes call any scary moment dramatic irony. Dramatic irony needs a gap: the reader knows or suspects something the character does not. Herbert's jokes (excerpts 5 and 6) are the clearest cases: he jokes about the money and about coming back, and on a second reading the reader knows how the wish will be granted. Watch for answers that summarize the excerpt without naming what the reader senses.
notes: The excerpts follow Project Gutenberg eBook #12122 (The Lady of the Barge, 1902) word for word, with curly quotation marks made straight. The story includes a death in a factory accident and a grieving mother; it is commonly taught in grade 8, but preview it. Any four well-explained excerpts can fill the chart; the key gives the strongest four.

## Background
text: Sergeant-Major Morris, an old soldier back from India, visits the White family: Mr. and Mrs. White and their grown son, Herbert. He shows them a dried monkey's paw that he says will grant three wishes. Mr. White takes the paw, and at Herbert's urging wishes for two hundred pounds.

## Read: Seven excerpts
source: W. W. Jacobs, "The Monkey's Paw," in The Lady of the Barge (1902)
  [1] Morris, about the man who put a spell on the paw: "He wanted to show that fate ruled people's lives, and that those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow."
  [2] Morris, about the paw's first owner: "I don't know what the first two were, but the third was for death. That's how I got the paw."
  [3] Morris, after Mr. White pulls the paw out of the fire: "If you keep it, don't blame me for what happens. Pitch it on the fire again like a sensible man."
  [4] Herbert, joking: "Why, we're going to be rich, and famous and happy."
  [5] The next morning, when Mrs. White asks how two hundred pounds could hurt anyone: "Might drop on his head from the sky," said the frivolous Herbert.
  [6] "Well, don't break into the money before I come back," said Herbert as he rose from the table.
  [7] "I expect you'll find the cash tied up in a big bag in the middle of your bed," said Herbert, as he bade them good-night, "and something horrible squatting up on top of the wardrobe watching you as you pocket your ill-gotten gains."

## Warm up
choice: Which is the best definition of dramatic irony?
  - A character says the opposite of what he means.
  * The reader knows or senses something that a character does not.
  - An event happens that nobody expected.
  - The story ends sadly.
choice: In excerpt 3, how does Mr. White respond to Morris's warning?
  - He throws the paw back into the fire.
  - He gives the paw back to Morris.
  * He keeps the paw anyway.
  - He makes Morris promise never to use it.

## Main: Mark four moments
chart: Excerpt number | What the characters think or do | What the reader senses
  rows: 4
  key: [1] The family laughs; the reader senses the paw punishes anyone who meddles with fate. [3] Mr. White keeps the paw; the reader senses something terrible will happen and he has been warned. [5] Herbert jokes the money may fall on his head; the reader senses the money will come through harm to Herbert. [6] Herbert jokes "before I come back"; the reader suspects he will not come back. Also accept [2] (wishes lead to death) and [7] (something horrible watching them).
write: Choose one of Herbert's jokes. Explain why it is more frightening to the reader than to Herbert.
  lines: 4
  key: In excerpt 6 Herbert says "don't break into the money before I come back." He means it as a joke, but readers who heard Morris's warnings sense he may not come back; the money arrives as payment for his death, so his own words point to what happens.
write: How does Morris's dialogue build suspense before the first wish? Cite two excerpts.
  lines: 4
  key: Morris says the paw was made to show that people who interfere with fate do so "to their sorrow" [1], and that the last owner's third wish "was for death" [2]. He even begs Mr. White to "Pitch it on the fire" [3]. Each warning makes readers expect harm while the family laughs.

## Stretch
write: After Herbert dies, the company pays the Whites exactly two hundred pounds. How does knowing the ending change the way you read excerpts 4 to 7 a second time?
  lines: 4
  key: On a second reading, Herbert's jokes sound like terrible predictions: the money does come with harm to him, and he does not come back. The reader now hears the gap between his cheerful words and the truth, which is dramatic irony.
# 6. Key Concept Development Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can trace how an author develops and refines a key idea across the paragraphs of a text.
directions: Read the article. In each paragraph, highlight the sentence that adds something new to the idea of renewable energy. Fill in the map to show how each paragraph develops or refines the concept, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often summarize each paragraph ("this one is about dams") instead of saying how it changes the concept ("it shows a renewable source can still harm wildlife"). Ask: "What do you understand about renewable energy now that you did not before this paragraph?" Watch for students who equate renewable with clean; paragraph 2 exists to separate them.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 340 words). Facts in it are standard: fossil fuels formed over millions of years; burning biomass releases carbon dioxide; pumped storage moves water uphill to store energy; dams can block salmon migration; turbines and panels need mined metals. The concept moves from a simple definition (paragraph 1) through three complications (2 to 4) to a refined, three-question definition (5).

## Read
passage: What Counts as Renewable? | numbered
  Renewable energy is energy that comes from sources nature refills on its own. Sunlight, wind, flowing water and the heat inside Earth will not run out the way coal, oil and natural gas will. Those fossil fuels formed over millions of years, so once we burn them, they are gone for any length of time that matters to people.
  But "renewable" does not always mean "clean." Wood and other plant material, called biomass, can grow back, so it counts as renewable. Burning it, however, still releases carbon dioxide and smoke. Solar panels and wind turbines, by contrast, give off no smoke while they run. Engineers often separate two ideas: whether a source can be refilled, and how much pollution it makes.
  Renewable energy also has a timing problem. The sun sets, and the wind does not blow on command. Power companies must match the electricity they supply to the electricity people use every second, so they need ways to store energy or other sources to fill the gaps. Large batteries, water pumped uphill into reservoirs to be released later, and long power lines that connect sunny and windy regions all help.
  Even the cleanest sources have costs. A hydroelectric dam produces steady power with no smoke, but it floods valleys and can block fish such as salmon from swimming upstream to lay their eggs. Wind turbines and solar panels need metals that must be mined. A source can be renewable and still change the land and the living things around it.
  So the idea of renewable energy is more complex than "power that never runs out." A careful definition asks three questions. Can the source refill itself in a human lifetime? How much pollution does it make? What does it cost the land, water and wildlife? Most experts do not expect any single source to do it all. The future will likely depend on a mix of renewable sources, ways to store energy and smarter use of the energy we have.

## Warm up
choice: How does paragraph 2 refine the definition from paragraph 1?
  - It lists more fossil fuels.
  * It shows that a renewable source is not always a clean one.
  - It explains how solar panels are built.
  - It argues that biomass should be banned.
choice: Which phrase signals that paragraph 3 adds a new problem?
  - "Large batteries"
  - "every second"
  * "also has a timing problem"
  - "sunny and windy regions"

## Main: The development map
chart: How this paragraph develops or refines the idea | Key words or evidence
  labels: ¶1; ¶2; ¶3; ¶4; ¶5
  height: 0.8
  key: ¶1: introduces the basic definition, energy from sources nature refills, in contrast to fossil fuels; "will not run out," "millions of years." ¶2: separates renewable from clean; "does not always mean 'clean,'" biomass "still releases carbon dioxide." ¶3: adds that sun and wind are not always available, so storage is needed; "timing problem," batteries, pumped water. ¶4: shows renewable sources still have environmental costs; dams "block fish such as salmon," metals "must be mined." ¶5: pulls it together into a refined definition with three questions and calls for a mix; "more complex than 'power that never runs out.'"
write: Compare the meaning of "renewable energy" in paragraph 1 with its meaning by paragraph 5. Cite a phrase from each.
  lines: 4
  key: In paragraph 1, renewable energy simply means sources that "will not run out." By paragraph 5, the author says the idea is "more complex than 'power that never runs out'" and judges a source by three questions: whether it refills, how much it pollutes and what it costs the land and wildlife.

## Stretch
write: Why might the author have placed the "costs" paragraph (4) right before the conclusion?
  lines: 3
  key: It is the strongest complication, showing that even the "cleanest sources" have costs, so it sets up the conclusion that no single source is perfect and a mix is needed.
# 18. Verbals and Voice Escape Maze
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify verbals, active and passive voice, and verb moods, and fix shifts in voice and mood.
directions: Each square holds a sentence and a label. A square is true only if the label correctly describes the sentence (or the quoted words). Start at the top left square of each maze. Move right or down, only onto true squares, until you reach the finish. Shade your path.
mistakes: Gerunds and present participles both end in -ing; the test is the job they do. A gerund acts as a noun (subject or object: "Dad loves fishing"); a participle describes a noun ("the smiling coach"). In maze 2, students often call any sentence with "was" passive; passive voice needs a form of be plus a past participle, with the subject receiving the action.
notes: Each maze has one path of 8 true squares, so students make a choice at every turn (14 moves across the two mazes); a wrong choice leads to a dead end. Moods follow Common Core L.8.1c (indicative, imperative, interrogative, conditional, subjunctive). "If I was you" and "I wish it was summer" are common in speech, but formal writing uses the subjunctive "were."

## Maze 1: Verbals
maze: Is the label on the quoted words right?
  * Swimming laps keeps Ana strong. "Swimming": *gerund*
  * The broken vase sat on the shelf. "broken": *participle*
  * We hope to win the debate. "to win": *infinitive*
  * Leo enjoys sketching birds. "sketching": *gerund*
  * Laughing, the twins ran outside. "Laughing": *participle*
  * Her goal is to finish first. "to finish": *infinitive*
  * The howling wind kept us awake. "howling": *participle*
  * Reading before bed helps me relax. "Reading": *gerund*
  - I like to read mysteries. "to read": *gerund*
  - Painting is Rosa's hobby. "Painting": *infinitive*
  - The frozen pond sparkled. "frozen": *gerund*
  - Max went to the gym to exercise. "to exercise": *participle*
  - Cooking dinner takes an hour. "Cooking": *participle*
  - The smiling coach waved. "smiling": *gerund*
  - To win was all she wanted. "To win": *gerund*
  - We heard the crying baby. "crying": *infinitive*
  - Dad loves fishing. "fishing": *participle*
  - The written note was unsigned. "written": *infinitive*

## Maze 2: Voice and mood
maze: Is the label on the sentence right?
  * The cake was baked by Leo. → *passive*
  * Leo baked the cake. → *active*
  * Close the door and turn off the light. → *no mood shift*
  * The window was broken during the storm. → *passive*
  * If I were taller, I would play center. → *subjunctive*
  * Did you finish your essay? → *interrogative*
  * Our team won the game. → *active*
  * Please sit down. → *imperative*
  - The ball was kicked by Ana. → *active*
  - Maya painted the fence. → *passive*
  - Open your books, and you should read page 10. → *no mood shift*
  - The song was sung by the choir. → *active*
  - If I was you, I would ask. → *correct subjunctive*
  - First mix the flour, and then you will add the eggs. → *no mood shift*
  - The dog chased the cat. → *passive*
  - Mistakes were made by the referee. → *active*
  - Turn off your phone. → *indicative*
  - I wish it was summer. → *correct subjunctive*

## Stretch: Fix the shifts
write: Rewrite "Open your books, and you should read page 10." with no shift in mood.
  lines: 1
  key: Open your books and read page 10.
write: Rewrite "Mistakes were made by the referee." in the active voice. Why might a writer choose the passive version?
  lines: 2
  key: The referee made mistakes. A writer might choose the passive to avoid naming or blaming the person who made the mistakes.
# 25. Strongest Evidence from Nonfiction
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can choose and explain the strongest evidence from a nonfiction text to support an analysis.
directions: Read the article. For each analysis question, choose the strongest of three pieces of evidence, then explain why it is the strongest and answer the question.
mistakes: Students often choose the most exciting or the longest quotation instead of the one that proves the point. Strongest evidence is direct (it answers the exact question), specific (names, numbers, events) and needs the least guessing. A piece of evidence can be true and still be weak if it is about a different point.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 340 words). Facts: Otto Lilienthal made about 2,000 glider flights in the 1890s and died after a glider crash in 1896; the Wrights built a wind tunnel in 1901 and tested about 200 wing shapes; Samuel Langley's Aerodrome failed in launches on October 7 and December 8, 1903; on December 17, 1903, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville flew 120 feet in 12 seconds and Wilbur's last flight that day lasted 59 seconds over 852 feet; Bessie Coleman earned her pilot's license in France in 1921.

## Read
passage: How the Wright Brothers Learned to Fly
  **[1]** In the 1890s, many people were trying to fly, and some of them died trying. A German engineer, Otto Lilienthal, made about 2,000 flights in gliders he built himself. In 1896 one of his gliders stalled in a gust of wind, and he died from the fall.
  **[2]** Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. They had no college degrees, but they read everything they could find about flight. They decided that the hardest problem was not power but control: a flying machine had to be steered and balanced in moving air, the way a cyclist balances on a bicycle.
  **[3]** Their gliders did not lift as well as the published numbers said they should. Instead of giving up, the brothers built a small wind tunnel in their shop in 1901 and tested about 200 tiny wing shapes. Their careful measurements showed that some of the old figures were wrong, and they used their own data to design new wings.
  **[4]** To control the plane, they invented "wing warping," twisting the wingtips with cables so the machine could tilt and turn. They tested it on hundreds of glider flights before they added an engine. When no company could sell them an engine that was light enough, their mechanic, Charlie Taylor, helped them build one.
  **[5]** Meanwhile, Samuel Langley, a famous scientist, had received $50,000 from the U.S. Army to build a flying machine. In October and December of 1903, his Aerodrome was launched from a boat and plunged into the Potomac River both times.
  **[6]** Nine days after Langley's second failure, on December 17, 1903, Orville Wright flew 120 feet in 12 seconds near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. By the fourth flight that day, Wilbur stayed in the air for 59 seconds and covered 852 feet.
  **[7]** Others soon followed. In 1909 Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel. In 1921 Bessie Coleman, who had been turned away by American flight schools because she was Black and a woman, earned her pilot's license in France.

## Warm up
choice: Which makes a piece of evidence strongest?
  - It is the longest sentence in the article.
  - It contains the most exciting event.
  * It directly answers the question with specific details.
  - It comes from the first paragraph.

## Main: Choose the strongest evidence
choice: Question 1: Why did the Wrights succeed when Langley failed?
  - "Samuel Langley, a famous scientist, had received $50,000 from the U.S. Army" [5]
  * "Their careful measurements showed that some of the old figures were wrong, and they used their own data to design new wings." [3]
  - "Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio." [2]

  lines: 3
  key: It shows the cause of their success: careful testing and their own data, which Langley's well-funded project lacked. The bicycle shop is background, and Langley's money shows funding was not the key.
choice: Question 2: What did the Wrights believe was the most important problem to solve?
  * "the hardest problem was not power but control" [2]
  - "They had no college degrees" [2]
  - "they added an engine" [4]
write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 2.
  lines: 3
  key: It states their belief directly, in their reasoning: control, not power. The other two are true details but do not say what they believed.
choice: Question 3: How does the author show that early flight was dangerous?
  - "Others soon followed." [7]
  * "In 1896 one of his gliders stalled in a gust of wind, and he died from the fall." [1]
  - "many people were trying to fly" [1]

write: Explain why your choice is strongest and answer question 4.

# 30. Discussion Building Tracker
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, a clipboard for the observer
ican: I can track how speakers in a discussion build on, question and challenge each other's ideas, and how the group reaches a conclusion.
directions: You are the observer for a group discussion. First practice sorting discussion moves. Then, during the discussion, log each turn: who spoke, a few key words of what they said, and the move they made. Afterward, tally the moves and explain how the group reached its conclusion.
mistakes: Observers often log only the loudest speakers or write down every word. Key words are enough; the move is what matters. Watch for confusing "question" with "challenge": a question asks for more information or evidence ("What makes you think that?"), while a challenge disagrees and gives a reason ("I don't think so, because...").
notes: Use with any discussion of 10 to 15 minutes, in a fishbowl (an inner circle talks while observers sit outside) or with one observer per group. Codes: B builds on, Q questions, C challenges, N new idea, S sums up. The log and tally have no single key; the warm up does, and the key shows a model conclusion for a discussion about whether the school should start later.

## Warm up: Name the move
match: Write the letter of the move each speaker makes.
  - "Adding to what Priya said, a later start would also help kids who take the early bus." = Builds on (B)
  - "What evidence do you have that students would actually sleep more?" = Questions (Q)
  - "I see it differently, because sports practice would run later into the evening." = Challenges (C)
  - "Something nobody has mentioned yet is the cost of changing bus routes." = New idea (N)
  - "So far, most of us agree sleep matters, but we disagree about after-school activities." = Sums up (S)

## Main: Discussion log
write: Discussion question:
  lines: 1
  key: Example: Should our school start 30 minutes later?
chart: Speaker | Key words of what they said | Move (B, Q, C, N, S)
  rows: 9
  height: 0.42
  key: Logs will vary. A strong log records most turns, uses key words rather than full sentences, and codes each move.

## Tally and conclusion
table: Builds on (B) | Questions (Q) | Challenges (C) | New idea (N) | Sums up (S)
  - | | | | 
  height: 0.5
write: Which move did the group use most? Which did it use least? What did that do to the discussion?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: The group built on ideas most (6 times) and challenged least (once). Because almost no one disagreed, the discussion stayed friendly but did not test the weaker ideas.
write: How did the group reach its conclusion? Name a turning point in the discussion.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: The group agreed a later start would help sleep, but split over sports. The turning point came when Marco asked what evidence there was, and Priya cited a study the class had read. Then Ana summed up: start later, but move practices to weekends. Everyone agreed to that.

## Stretch
write: Write one sentence you could have said to build on or challenge an idea in the discussion.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: I want to challenge the idea that a later start means less homework time, because we would also get home later only if practices stay the same.
# 42. Dramatic Irony from Point of View
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can use point of view to create dramatic irony and build suspense in a scene.
directions: Dramatic irony happens when readers know something a character does not. Study the example, plan your scene, and write it. Use point of view on purpose: show readers the secret first, then follow the character who does not know it.
mistakes: Students often reveal the secret to the character too soon, which ends the suspense. Keep the character in the dark for most of the scene. Also watch for a secret that the reader never actually learns; dramatic irony needs the reader to know first, clearly.
notes: Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet is the classic example: the audience knows Juliet has taken a potion that only makes her seem dead, but Romeo does not. The model scene is original to Curio Class. One reliable technique: a short opening section from one character's point of view that reveals the secret, then a switch to the character who does not know it.

## Warm up
choice: Which situation creates dramatic irony?
  - A character finds a secret letter and tells the reader what it says at the same time.
  * The reader has seen the class plan a surprise party, but the birthday girl thinks everyone forgot her birthday.
  - A character is surprised by a storm the reader did not expect either.
  - The narrator explains the ending in the first sentence.
choice: How does dramatic irony build suspense?
  - It hides information from the reader.
  * The reader waits, worried or excited, for the character to discover what the reader already knows.
  - It makes the story shorter.
  - It removes all conflict from the scene.

## Plan your scene
chart: My plan
  labels: The secret the reader knows; The character who does not know it; How the reader learns it (which point of view); Moments where the character comes close to finding out; How it ends
  height: 0.55
  key: Example: Secret: the old bridge on the trail was washed out last night. Character: Tess, hiking alone at dawn. How the reader learns: an opening paragraph from the ranger's point of view as he puts up a warning sign that blows away. Close calls: Tess hears rushing water; she sees a broken branch with ranger tape. Ending: Tess stops at the edge just in time and spots the sign in the bushes.

## Write the scene
write: Part 1: Reveal the secret to the reader (from one point of view).
  lines: 4
  key: Model: Ranger Diaz hammered the last nail into the sign: TRAIL CLOSED. BRIDGE OUT. Behind him, the creek roared through the gap where the bridge had stood yesterday. He climbed into his truck as the wind picked up, never looking back as the sign wobbled and tipped into the ferns.
write: Part 2: Switch to the character who does not know. Build suspense as they get close.
  lines: 8
  key: Model: Tess loved the trail at dawn, when nobody else was awake. She jogged past the ferns, humming. The roar of water grew louder, but she smiled; the creek was always loud after rain. A strip of yellow tape fluttered from a branch. "Weird," she said, and kept going. The trail curved. The roar became thunder. Tess slowed, then stopped, the toes of her shoes at the edge of nothing. Below, the creek swirled where the bridge should have been. In the ferns behind her, a sign lay face down.

## Stretch
write: Point to one sentence in your scene where the reader knows more than the character. What do you want the reader to feel there?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: "She smiled; the creek was always loud after rain." The reader knows the bridge is gone, so I want them to feel worried and want to shout a warning.
