# 1. Ball-and-Stick Molecules
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencils (black, red, blue; leave hydrogen white)
ican: I can model molecules with balls and sticks and count the atoms in each one.
directions: Draw a ball-and-stick model of each molecule in its box. Use the color key: carbon black, oxygen red, nitrogen blue, hydrogen white (just an outline). Each stick is a bond; draw a double bond as two sticks. Then count each kind of atom.
mistakes: Students often read the small number in a formula as belonging to the next atom (reading CO2 as two carbons). The subscript counts the atom just before it. Another common slip: drawing O2 as a single oxygen atom; oxygen in air is a molecule of two atoms.
notes: Shapes for reference: water is bent; carbon dioxide is a straight line with double bonds, O=C=O; methane has a carbon in the middle with 4 hydrogens; ammonia has a nitrogen with 3 hydrogens in a low pyramid; oxygen gas is two oxygens joined by a double bond; hydrogen peroxide is H-O-O-H. Exact 3D angles are not expected; correct atoms and connections are. Modeling clay and toothpicks make a good hands-on version.

## Warm up
example: Water, H2O: one red oxygen ball in the middle, a stick to each of 2 white hydrogen balls, in a bent (V) shape.
  Atoms: H = 2, O = 1. Total = 3.

## Main: Draw and count
draw: 1. Water, H2O (draw the example) | 1.0
draw: 2. Oxygen gas, O2 | 1.0
draw: 3. Carbon dioxide, CO2 | 1.0
draw: 4. Methane, CH4 | 1.0
draw: 5. Ammonia, NH3 | 1.0
draw: 6. Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2 | 1.0
table: Molecule | Formula | Carbon | Hydrogen | Oxygen | Nitrogen | Total atoms
  - water | H2O | 0 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 3
  - oxygen gas | O2 | {0} | {0} | {2} | {0} | {2}
  - carbon dioxide | CO2 | {1} | {0} | {2} | {0} | {3}
  - methane | CH4 | {1} | {4} | {0} | {0} | {5}
  - ammonia | NH3 | {0} | {3} | {0} | {1} | {4}
  - hydrogen peroxide | H2O2 | {0} | {2} | {2} | {0} | {4}

## Think
choice: Which molecule is made of only one kind of atom?
  - water
  * oxygen gas
  - methane
  - ammonia
write: Water and hydrogen peroxide are made of the same two elements. Why are they different substances?
  lines: 2
  key: They have different numbers and arrangements of atoms: water has one oxygen (H2O), hydrogen peroxide has two (H2O2) joined to each other. A different molecule means a different substance with different properties.

## Stretch
write: Methane burns with oxygen to make carbon dioxide and water: CH4 + 2O2 makes CO2 + 2H2O. Count the atoms of each element on both sides. What do you notice?
  lines: 3
  key: Left: C 1, H 4, O 4. Right: C 1, H 4 (2 x 2), O 4 (2 + 2). The same atoms are on both sides, only rearranged into new molecules.

# 6. From Oil to Fleece
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter (optional)
ican: I can explain how a synthetic material is made from natural resources through chemical and physical changes.
directions: Read the flowchart passage. Each numbered step leads to the next, like boxes and arrows in a flowchart. Then answer the questions, citing step numbers as evidence.
mistakes: Students often call every step a chemical change because the material "looks totally different." Only step 3 makes a new substance; melting, chopping, stretching and knitting change the shape and form of PET but not what it is made of. Some also think recycled fleece no longer comes from a natural resource; the PET in it was made from oil or natural gas in the first place.
notes: PET (polyethylene terephthalate) is the plastic in most clear drink bottles; its recycling code is 1. The two starting chemicals are made from substances separated out of crude oil or natural gas. Claims about how many bottles go into one jacket vary widely, so the passage does not give a number. Fleece sheds tiny plastic fibers in the wash; researchers are still studying how much reaches rivers and oceans.

## Read
passage: From Oil to Fleece: A Flowchart | numbered
  **Drill.** Deep underground, crude oil and natural gas formed over millions of years from the remains of tiny sea organisms. Wells pump them to the surface. They are natural resources, and they are nonrenewable: once we use them, they will not be replaced in a human lifetime.
  **Refine.** At a refinery, crude oil is heated and separated into its parts. Some of those parts are turned into two chemicals: ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid. Each is made of small molecules.
  **React.** In a factory, the two chemicals are heated together. A chemical reaction links their small molecules into very long chains called polymers. The new substance is PET, a plastic. PET has properties that neither starting chemical had: it is clear, strong, light and does not break when you drop it.
  **Shape.** PET pellets are melted, formed into bottles and filled with drinks. After someone finishes a drink, the bottle can go into a recycling bin.
  **Recycle.** Trucks bring bottles to a recycling center. Machines and workers sort out the PET, remove caps and labels, wash the bottles and chop them into small flakes.
  **Spin.** The flakes are melted and pushed through a spinneret, a metal plate with tiny holes like a shower head. Thin streams of PET cool into long fibers, which are stretched to make them stronger and crimped to make them wavy.
  **Knit.** The fibers are spun into yarn, and the yarn is knitted into fabric. Machines brush the fabric so the loops stand up into a soft, fluffy surface. The result is fleece, ready to be sewn into a jacket.
  Recycling a bottle into fleece means less new oil is used and fewer bottles go to landfills. But the fleece is much harder to recycle again, and it sheds tiny fibers each time it is washed.

## Warm up
choice: What is the natural resource that fleece comes from?
  - cotton plants
  - sheep's wool
  * crude oil or natural gas
  - sand
fill:
  - Long chains of linked molecules are called {polymers}.
  - The plate with tiny holes that turns melted PET into fibers is a {spinneret}.

## Main: Chemical or physical?
table: Step | What happens | New substance? (yes or no) | Chemical or physical change?
  - 3. React | small molecules link into PET | {yes} | {chemical}
  - 4. Shape | PET is melted and formed into bottles | {no} | {physical}
  - 5. Recycle | bottles are chopped into flakes | {no} | {physical}
  - 6. Spin | flakes are melted and drawn into fibers | {no} | {physical}
  - 7. Knit | fibers become yarn and fabric | {no} | {physical}
choice: What evidence in step 3 shows a chemical change?
  - The chemicals are heated.
  * A new substance forms with properties neither starting chemical had.
  - The pellets can be melted.
  - The factory is large.
choice: Why is fleece called a synthetic material?
  - It is found in nature.
  - It comes from an animal.
  * People make it from natural resources through chemical reactions.
  - It cannot be recycled.

## Explain
write: A classmate says, "Fleece is a new substance because it looks and feels nothing like a bottle." Do you agree? Use the steps as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: No. The bottle and the fleece are both PET. Steps 5 to 7 only chop, melt, stretch and knit it, which are physical changes: the shape and texture change, but the substance does not. The only new substance was made in step 3.
write: Name one benefit and one cost of making fleece from recycled bottles. Cite the step or paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: Benefit (paragraph 8): less new oil is used and fewer bottles go to landfills. Cost (paragraph 8): the fleece sheds tiny plastic fibers when washed, and it is hard to recycle again. (Also accept: the process uses energy for trucks and machines, steps 5 and 6.)

## Stretch
write: Oil is nonrenewable. How does that make recycling PET more important? Explain using step 1.
  lines: 2
  key: Step 1 says oil takes millions of years to form, so once it is used it is gone for us. Recycling PET reuses the polymer already made, so less new oil has to be pumped to make new plastic.

# 11. Melting Ice Temperature Log
minutes: 45
materials: crushed ice, a clear cup, a larger bowl of warm tap water, a thermometer (digital or alcohol), a timer, safety goggles, pencil
ican: I can collect temperature data and explain what happens to energy while ice melts.
directions: Work with a partner. Set the cup of crushed ice in the warm water bath. Read the temperature of the ice every minute until it has all melted, then keep going for 3 more minutes. Graph your data and explain the flat part.
mistakes: Students often expect the temperature to rise steadily. The flat stretch at about 0 °C is the point: the energy is going into breaking the ice's structure (melting), not into raising the temperature. Thermometers resting on the cup wall read the warm bath, not the ice: keep the bulb in the middle of the ice and stir gently.
notes: Use warm tap water only, never boiling. A digital probe thermometer works best; no mercury thermometers. Expect a start around -2 to 0 °C, a plateau near 0 °C for 6 to 10 minutes while ice remains, then a rise toward the bath temperature. The key graph shows typical data, not the only right answer.

## Before you start
write: Predict: What will the temperature do while the ice is melting? Sketch or describe it.
  lines: 2
  key: Many students predict a steady rise. Accept any prediction with a reason; the data will test it.

## Explain
write: Find the flat part of your graph. What temperature was it, and how long did it last?
  lines: 2
  key: About 0 °C, for several minutes (typically 6 to 10) while ice was still in the cup.
write: The warm water kept adding energy the whole time. Where did that energy go during the flat part?
  lines: 3
  key: Into melting the ice: breaking the bonds that hold water molecules in the solid structure, so the temperature stayed at 0 °C until all the ice was liquid.
write: What would the graph look like if you started with twice as much ice? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: The flat part would last longer, because more ice needs more energy to melt; the plateau would still be at 0 °C.

# 17. Sealed Bag Mass Test
minutes: 40
materials: for each group: a gallon zip-top bag, a small paper cup, a clear plastic cup, baking soda, vinegar, a measuring spoon (1/2 teaspoon), a graduated cylinder, a digital scale (0.1 g), safety goggles, paper towels, pencil
ican: I can use mass data to show that atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, in a chemical reaction.
directions: Wear goggles the whole time. Test 1: put 1/2 teaspoon of baking soda in the bag. Pour 30 mL of vinegar into the paper cup and stand it upright in the bag. Seal the bag, find the mass, tip the cup over and let it react, then find the mass again. Test 2: do the same in an open plastic cup with the vinegar in the paper cup beside it on the scale.
mistakes: Many students expect the bag to get heavier because it puffs up, or lighter because "gas weighs nothing." Gas has mass: the sealed bag's mass stays the same, and the open cup loses the mass of the gas that escapes. Students also forget to put the empty paper cup on the scale for the "after" mass in Test 2.
notes: Typical results: the sealed bag changes by 0.0 to 0.3 g, and the open cup loses about 1 g. A puffed bag can read a few tenths of a gram light because it pushes aside more air (buoyancy, the same effect that holds up a balloon); this is not lost matter, and it is a good discussion point. 1/2 teaspoon of baking soda with 30 mL of household vinegar makes well under a liter of gas, which a gallon bag holds safely. Do not use more baking soda or a smaller bag, and never seal the reaction in a hard container. Wipe up spills; everything goes down the drain with water.

## Predict
choice: The bag will puff up during the reaction. What will happen to its mass?
  - It will go up, because the bag gets bigger.
  - It will go down, because gas has no mass.
  * It will stay the same, because nothing gets in or out.
  - It will drop to zero.
write: Predict what will happen to the mass of the open cup. Give a reason.
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason. (The mass goes down because the gas escapes into the air.)

## Data
table: Test | Mass before (g) | Mass after (g) | Change (g) | What I observed
  - 1. Sealed bag | | | |
  - 2. Open cup | | | |
  - 1. Sealed bag, trial 2 | | | |
  - 2. Open cup, trial 2 | | | |
note: Typical: sealed bag, change 0.0 to -0.3 g (bubbling, bag puffs up, cup feels cold); open cup, change about -1.0 g (bubbling and fizzing, no puffing).

## Analyze
text: The reaction: baking soda (NaHCO3) + acetic acid in vinegar (CH3COOH) makes sodium acetate (NaCH3COO) + water (H2O) + carbon dioxide gas (CO2).
table: Element | Atoms before (reactants) | Atoms after (products)
  - Na (sodium) | {1} | {1}
  - H (hydrogen) | {5} | {5}
  - C (carbon) | {3} | {3}
  - O (oxygen) | {5} | {5}
write: Use your atom count to explain why the sealed bag's mass stayed (about) the same.
  lines: 3
  key: The same atoms are there before and after; they are only rearranged into new molecules. In the sealed bag, none of the atoms could escape, so the total mass stayed the same.
write: Why did the open cup lose mass? Where did the "missing" atoms go?
  lines: 2
  key: The carbon dioxide gas escaped into the room. Those atoms still exist; they are just in the air, where the scale cannot weigh them.

## Stretch
write: A group's sealed bag read 0.3 g less after the reaction, and no gas leaked. Give a reason other than lost atoms.
  lines: 2
  key: Any one: the puffed-up bag pushes aside more air, so the air holds it up a little (buoyancy); the scale's error is about 0.1 g; liquid on the outside of the bag dried; the bag touched the edge of the scale.

# 28. Radish Crowding Lab
minutes: 30
materials: 3 identical cups with drainage holes, potting soil, radish seeds, a ruler, a digital scale (0.1 g), scissors, a sunny window or grow light, water, labels, pencil
ican: I can design and run an experiment to show how competition for resources affects plant growth.
directions: Fill 3 cups with the same amount of soil. Plant 1 seed in cup A, 5 seeds in cup B and 15 seeds in cup C, spread evenly and 1 cm deep. Give each cup the same water and light. After 14 days, measure the plants, cut them at the soil, and find their mass.
mistakes: Students often compare the total mass in each cup and conclude that crowding helps plants. Compare the mass per plant: each plant in a crowded cup usually gets much smaller. Watch also for unequal watering: crowded cups dry out faster, so measure the same water into each cup.
notes: Radishes sprout in 3 to 5 days and grow their first true leaves in about a week. Typical results at 14 days: the single plant is largest (around 1.5 to 2.5 g); plants in the 5-seed cup are about half that; plants in the 15-seed cup are often under 0.5 g each and may be tall and thin as they stretch for light. The total mass per cup often ends up fairly similar, because the cup's water, light and nutrients set a limit. Thin nothing out: the point is crowding. Time: about 20 minutes to plant, 5 to 10 minutes for each check, and 30 minutes to harvest and analyze.

## Plan
fill:
  - Independent variable (what we change): {the number of seeds in the cup}
  - Dependent variable (what we measure): {plant size: height, leaves and mass per plant}
write: List three things you will keep the same in all cups.
  lines: 1
  key: Any three: cup size, amount and kind of soil, kind of seed, amount of water, light, temperature, planting depth.
write: Predict: How will the plants in cup C compare with the plant in cup A? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason. (Most predict smaller plants in cup C, because they share the same water, light and nutrients.)

## Data
table: Cup | Seeds planted | Seeds sprouted (day 5) | Average height (mm, day 14) | Leaves on the biggest plant | Total mass of plants (g) | Mass per plant (g)
  height: 0.45
  - A | 1 | | | | |
  - B | 5 | | | | |
  - C | 15 | | | | |
note: Typical: A, mass per plant about 2.0 g; B, about 1.0 g; C, about 0.3 to 0.5 g. Mass per plant = total mass ÷ number of plants that grew.
example: Mass per plant = total mass of the plants in the cup ÷ number of plants that grew. Example: 4.8 g ÷ 4 plants = 1.2 g per plant.

## Analyze
choice: Which result is the best evidence of competition?
  - Cup C has the most plants.
  * Each plant in cup C has much less mass than the plant in cup A.
  - All the seeds sprouted.
  - Cup A's soil was dry.
write: Describe the pattern in your data. Use numbers.
  lines: 2
  key: Typical: the more seeds in a cup, the smaller each plant: about 2.0 g in cup A, 1.0 g in cup B and 0.4 g in cup C. Crowded plants were thinner, with fewer leaves.
write: Explain the pattern. Which resources were the plants competing for?
  lines: 3
  key: All the plants in a cup share the same limited water, light, nutrients in the soil and space for roots. With more plants, each one gets less, so each grows less. The plants are competing for these resources.

## Stretch
write: Compare the total mass of plants in each cup. What does it suggest about the resources in one cup?
  lines: 2
  key: Typical: the totals are much closer than the masses per plant (cup C may even match or pass A). The cup has a limited amount of resources that can only grow a certain amount of plant, however many plants share it. This is like a carrying capacity in an ecosystem.

# 39. Cookie Mining Lab
minutes: 40
materials: for each student: one chocolate chip cookie (check for allergies first), a flat toothpick, a round toothpick, a paper clip, a paper plate, grid paper (1 cm squares), a timer, pencil
ican: I can model the costs and the land damage of mining a natural resource and calculate whether it makes a profit.
directions: Trace your cookie on grid paper and count the squares it covers. Buy your tools. You have up to 5 minutes to dig out chocolate chips, without touching the cookie with your fingers. Then put the cookie back inside its outline, count the damage, and find your profit.
mistakes: Students often forget to add every cost: tools, time and reclamation all count. Watch for students who count crumbs as chips; a chip counts only if it is whole or pieces add up to about one chip. In the reasoning, some say the cheapest way is always best; push them to weigh profit against land damage.
notes: Check every student's allergies first, and use allergy-safe cookies or skip the food; a block of modeling clay with dried beans works the same way. Cookies are not eaten in the lab. Prices: cookie (land) $3; flat toothpick $2; round toothpick $4; paper clip $6; tools that break must be bought again; mining time $1 per minute; each chip sells for $5; reclamation costs $1 for each grid square of crumbs outside the original outline. The example gives a profit of $9, but student results vary a lot; check their math, not their numbers.

## The price list
table: Item | Cost or value
  - Land (one cookie) | $3
  - Flat toothpick | $2
  - Round toothpick | $4
  - Paper clip | $6
  - Mining time | $1 per minute (5 minutes at most)
  - Reclamation (cleanup) | $1 per square of crumbs outside the outline
  - Each chocolate chip mined | sells for $5
example: Land $3 + round toothpick $4 + paper clip $6 + 4 minutes $4 = $17 in costs before cleanup.
  Mined 6 chips: 6 x $5 = $30. Cleanup: 4 squares outside the outline = $4. Total costs: $17 + $4 = $21.
  Profit = $30 - $21 = **$9**.

## Warm up
fill:
  - A student mines 3 chips with a flat toothpick in 2 minutes and has 1 square to clean up. Costs: $3 + $2 + $2 + $1 = ${8}. Value: ${15}. Profit: ${7}.
  - The squares of crumbs outside the outline stand for land that was {damaged} by mining.

## Main: My mine
table: Step | My numbers | Cost or value ($)
  - Squares the cookie covered before mining | | (no cost)
  - Land | 1 cookie | 3
  - Tools I bought | |
  - Minutes I mined | |
  - Chips I mined | |
  - Squares of crumbs outside the outline | |
  - Total costs | |
  - Profit (value - costs) | |
note: Numbers vary. Check that total costs = 3 + tools + minutes + cleanup squares, value = chips x 5, and profit = value - total costs (it can be negative, a loss).

## Analyze
write: Did your mine make a profit or a loss? What cost you the most money?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Common answers: the paper clip or the cleanup cost the most; students who dug fast often made more chips but more damage.
write: Which tool would you buy next time, and why? Think about chips, time and damage.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary with a reason. Example: one round toothpick, because it pulled chips out with less crumbling than the paper clip and cost less.
choice: Why did the lab charge you for crumbs outside the outline?
  - Crumbs are worth money.
  - It makes the math harder.
  * Real mining companies must pay to repair the land they damage.
  - Toothpicks make crumbs.

## Stretch
write: Real mines must restore the land when they are done. Explain one way this rule protects the environment, and one way it changes a mining company's choices.
  lines: 3
  key: It makes companies repair damaged land, refill pits and replant, so habitats and soil can recover. Because cleanup costs money, companies plan to dig more carefully, mine only where it pays, or choose methods that cause less damage.
