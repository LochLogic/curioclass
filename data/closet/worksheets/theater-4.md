# 1. I Want, But: Objectives and Obstacles
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can name a character's objective and the obstacle in the way.
directions: Read each scene situation. Speak as the character and fill in "I want to ... but ..." The first part is the objective (what the character wants). The second part is the obstacle (what is in the way).
mistakes: Students often write a feeling as the objective ("I want to be sad"). An objective is something the character can try to get or do. Watch for obstacles that just repeat the want ("but I can't"); the obstacle names what stops them: a person, a thing, a rule, a fear.
notes: Answers in the key are models; accept any wording that names a playable want and a specific obstacle. Follow up by having pairs act one situation: the actor playing the character tries three different ways (tactics) to get past the obstacle.

## Example
example: Mia's dog has run into the neighbor's yard. The gate is locked, and the neighbor is not home.
  I want to **get my dog back** but **the gate is locked and nobody is home**.

## Warm up
fill: Leo is starving after soccer. The last slice of pizza is on his big sister's plate. I want to {eat the last slice} but {my sister already has it}.
fill: Rosa has a solo in the concert tonight. Her voice is hoarse and scratchy. I want to {sing my solo well} but {my voice is hoarse}.

## Main
fill: Ana stands outside the gym holding her trumpet. Band tryouts start in 2 minutes. The door is locked, and nobody answers her knock. I want to {get into the gym for tryouts} but {the door is locked}.
fill: Jamal found a wallet on the bus. The owner's name is inside, but the bus is pulling away from the owner's stop. I want to {return the wallet} but {the bus is leaving the stop}.
fill: Grandpa wants the family photo to look perfect. The baby keeps crying and will not look at the camera. I want to {take a perfect family photo} but {the baby keeps crying}.
fill: Priya is the new student. She wants to sit with the art club at lunch, but every seat at their table is full. I want to {join the art club at lunch} but {there are no empty seats}.

## Stretch
write: Write your own scene situation, then fill in "I want to ... but ..." for your character.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a want the character can act on and a clear obstacle, for example: Sam wants to fly his kite, but it is stuck in a tall tree. I want to get my kite down but it is too high to reach.
write: Pick one situation above. Name one thing the character could try to get past the obstacle.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: Ana could run around to another door, or bang louder and shout for the band teacher; Jamal could ask the driver to stop or turn the wallet in at the bus office.

# 7. Conflict Match-Up
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can name the type of conflict in a scene and explain my thinking.
directions: Read about the 4 types of conflict. Match each scene summary to its conflict type by writing the type in the box. Then explain one of your matches in a sentence, using evidence from the summary.
mistakes: Students call every scene with two people "character vs. character." Ask what the main obstacle really is: another person, the character's own fear or choice, a force of nature, or a rule or group in society. In scene 4 the coach is only enforcing the league's rule, so the conflict is with society.
notes: Answers: 1 character vs. character, 2 character vs. nature, 3 character vs. self, 4 character vs. society, 5 character vs. self, 6 character vs. nature. Some scenes hold more than one conflict (the hiker in scene 2 may also fight her fear); accept a second type in the justification if the student names the main one first. Pair this with sheet 1 (objectives and obstacles): the obstacle is where the conflict lives.

## Warm up: The 4 types
table: Conflict type | The character struggles against...
  - Character vs. character | another person
  - Character vs. self | their own fear, doubt or choice
  - Character vs. nature | weather, animals, illness or the land
  - Character vs. society | a rule, a law or what a group expects
example: Scene: A boy's dog runs off during a thunderstorm, and he searches the flooded park. **Character vs. nature**: the storm and the flood are what stand in his way.

## Main: Match-up
table: Scene summary | Conflict type
  height: 0.55
  - 1. Two cousins both want the last ticket to the school play, and neither will give it up. | {character vs. character}
  - 2. A hiker is caught on a mountain trail as a blizzard rolls in and the path disappears. | {character vs. nature}
  - 3. Dev has practiced his solo for weeks, but backstage his hands shake and he wonders whether to walk onstage. | {character vs. self}
  - 4. Rosa wants to join the town's baseball league, but the league's rule says only boys may play, and the coach says rules are rules. | {character vs. society}
  - 5. Kai finds a wallet full of money. He needs money for a new bike, but he knows the right thing is to return it. | {character vs. self}
  - 6. A family's farm is drying up. Day after day, no rain falls, and the crops wither. | {character vs. nature}
write: Choose one match. Explain it in a sentence with evidence from the summary.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: Scene 4 is character vs. society because Rosa's obstacle is the league's rule that only boys may play; the coach is not her enemy, he is only following the rule.

## Stretch
write: Rewrite scene 1 so it becomes character vs. self. Change only what the character struggles against.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: Maya has the last ticket to the school play, and she knows her cousin wanted it more. She has to decide whether to keep it or give it away.

# 20. Toy Theater Build Sheet
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils; for building (optional): a cereal box or shoebox, cardstock, glue, craft sticks, scissors
ican: I can design a toy theater with a backdrop, set pieces and character cutouts, and choose materials for each part.
directions: Design a paper toy theater for one scene of our crosswalk play. Draw the backdrop, 3 set pieces and 2 paper character cutouts, and label the material for each. Then plan how the characters move onstage.
mistakes: Students draw set pieces and characters at mismatched sizes (a car smaller than a person). Ask them to check scale against the stage: if the stage is 10 inches wide, a person is about 3 inches tall. Watch for materials that will not stand up (thin paper set pieces without a fold or a stand) and characters with no stick or slider to move them.
notes: Paper toy theaters were popular in Britain and Europe in the 1800s: printed sheets of scenery and characters were colored, cut out and glued to card, and characters were pushed on from the wings on sliders. A cereal box with a window cut in the front makes a quick proscenium; slots in the top let backdrops drop in. Accept any design with a backdrop, 3 set pieces, 2 characters, a material for each and a way to move characters. Model answers in the key describe one example scene: the busy corner by the school.

## Warm up: Materials that work
match: Match each job to a material that does it well.
  - A stiff backdrop that will not curl = Cardstock or a cereal box panel
  - A set piece that stands by itself = Folded card with a tab glued to the floor
  - A character that slides on from the wings = A paper figure glued to a craft stick
  - A see-through window or a pond = Clear plastic from packaging
choice: Your stage is 10 inches wide. About how tall should a paper person be?
  - Half an inch
  * About 3 inches
  - 10 inches

## Main: Design plates
draw: Backdrop (the scene at the back of the stage) | 1.9
fill: Backdrop material: {cardstock or a cereal box panel}
table: Set piece | Sketch it in this box | Material | How it stands up
  height: 1.0
  - 1 | | {cardstock} | {folded tab glued to the stage floor}
  - 2 | | {cereal box panel} | {glued to a small block}
  - 3 | | {clear plastic and card} | {lies flat on the floor}
table: Cutout | Sketch it in this box | Material | How it moves
  height: 1.1
  - 1 | | {paper glued to cardstock} | {on a craft stick from the stage left wing}
  - 2 | | {paper glued to cardstock} | {on a slider from the stage right wing}
note: Model scene: the busy corner by the school. Backdrop: storefronts and the school on cardstock. Set pieces: a stop sign, a bench, a painted crosswalk strip. Characters: the crossing guard and a student, each on a craft stick.

## Stretch
write: Which part of your toy theater will be hardest to build, and how will you solve it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: The stop sign will flop over because the pole is thin, so I will glue it to a folded cardstock triangle at the base so it stands up straight.

# 21. Costume Design Plate: Show Who They Are
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils or crayons
ican: I can design costumes whose colors and fabrics tell the audience who a character is.
directions: Our class play, Night Market on Elm Street, takes place at a neighborhood market. Draw a costume for each of 3 characters. Label the colors and fabrics, then explain what each choice tells the audience.
mistakes: Students often label "red" without saying why. Push every label to a "because": red because she is bold and loud. Watch for fabrics that do not fit the job (a satin gown on someone who lifts crates all night).
notes: The play premise is original. Colors and fabrics carry meaning by convention, not by rule: dark, plain colors often read as serious or tired; warm, bright colors as friendly or bold; shiny fabrics as fancy; rough, sturdy ones as working clothes. Accept any choice the student can explain. Hang finished plates as a costume shop wall.

## Warm up: Fabrics talk
match: Match each fabric to what it usually suggests to an audience.
  - Denim = Sturdy clothes for hard work
  - Satin = A fancy party or a rich character
  - Flannel = Cozy, outdoors or at home
  - Lace = Delicate or old-fashioned
choice: A designer wants the audience to feel that a character is gloomy and tired. Which colors work best?
  - Bright yellow and orange
  * Grays and dark blues
  - Hot pink and lime green

## Main: Three plates
draw: Plate 1: Ms. Okafor, the fruit seller. Cheerful, loud, has run her stall for 30 years. | 2.3
write: Colors and fabrics I chose, and what they tell the audience:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: a bright orange and yellow wrap skirt in cotton (cheerful, warm, like her fruit); a sturdy canvas apron with worn pockets (hard work and many years at the stall).
draw: Plate 2: Mr. Reyes, the night-shift nurse. Kind, but tired after a long shift. | 2.3
write: Colors and fabrics I chose, and what they tell the audience:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: soft blue scrubs in plain cotton (his job, calm and kind) under a faded gray knit sweater (tired, ready to go home).
draw: Plate 3: Councilor Vance, who wants to close the market. Strict and proud. | 2.3
write: Colors and fabrics I chose, and what they tell the audience:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: a stiff black or dark gray wool suit with sharp edges (strict, serious), a shiny silver tie or pin (proud, wants to look important).

## Stretch
write: At the end of the play, Councilor Vance decides to save the market. Change one costume piece for the last scene. What do you change, and what does it tell the audience?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: she takes off the stiff jacket and wears an orange scarf from Ms. Okafor's stall, showing she has softened and joined the neighborhood.

# 33. Script vs. Stage
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter (optional)
ican: I can compare a script with how it was staged and explain why a director made changes.
directions: Read the script excerpt from our crosswalk play. Then read how one class staged it. Answer the questions about what changed and why.
mistakes: Students list differences without the "why." Every change answers a problem: hearing, seeing, safety, time or meaning. Watch for students who think staging must copy the script exactly; directors and actors make choices the script leaves open, and sometimes change what it says.
notes: Both texts are original. The changes: the car horn became a drum and a voice; Jaylen whispers instead of shouting; the real traffic light became an actor holding a red and green paddle; the line "I'll go ask the mayor" was cut and replaced with a look. Possible reasons appear in the description and in the key. Accept any reason the student can tie to the audience, the actors or the story.

## Read
passage: Script Excerpt: Corner of Elm and Fifth
  (A busy street. A traffic light hangs above the corner. Cars rush past. JAYLEN, 10, stands at the curb with MS. ORTIZ, the crossing guard.)
  JAYLEN: (shouting over the traffic) Ms. Ortiz! How long do we have to wait?
  MS. ORTIZ: Until the light turns green. Stay behind the line.
  (A car horn blasts. JAYLEN jumps back.)
  JAYLEN: That car didn't even slow down!
  MS. ORTIZ: (pulling him gently back to the curb) Some drivers are in a hurry. They forget that kids walk here every morning.
  JAYLEN: Why doesn't somebody fix it?
  MS. ORTIZ: That's why this corner needs a real crosswalk. Painted lines. A light that stays red long enough.
  JAYLEN: Then I'll go ask the mayor myself.
  (The light turns green. They cross together, looking both ways.)
passage: How One Class Staged It
  The class had no traffic light, so a student stood on a box at the back holding a paddle painted red on one side and green on the other. When the light changed, she slowly turned the paddle, and the audience could see it from every seat.
  For the car horn, a student hit a drum once and shouted "HONK!" The director wanted the sound loud and sudden, and a real horn was not allowed in the school.
  The actor playing Jaylen tried shouting his first line, but it sounded angry. The director asked him to whisper it instead, as if he were nervous. The audience leaned in to listen.
  The director cut "Then I'll go ask the mayor myself." Instead, Jaylen looked straight out at the audience for three seconds before crossing. The director said the look made the audience wonder what Jaylen would do next.

## Questions
choice: Why did the class use a student with a paddle instead of a traffic light?
  - The script said to use a paddle.
  * They did not have a traffic light, and the paddle could be seen from every seat.
  - The director wanted the scene to be longer.
choice: What did whispering Jaylen's first line change for the audience?
  - It made Jaylen seem angry.
  - It made the line harder to understand, so they stopped listening.
  * It made Jaylen seem nervous, and the audience leaned in.
write: The director cut Jaylen's line about the mayor and replaced it with a look. What does the look do that the line did not?
  lines: 3
  key: Model: The line told the audience exactly what Jaylen would do. The look lets the audience wonder and guess, so they stay curious about what happens next and feel Jaylen thinking.
write: Name one other change the class made from the script. Was it a good choice? Explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: The car horn became a drum hit and a shout of "HONK!" It was a good choice because the sound was still loud and sudden, so the audience jumped with Jaylen, and it followed the school's rule against real horns.
