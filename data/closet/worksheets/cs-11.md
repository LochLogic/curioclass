# 1. Growth Rates Table
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, a calculator if you like
ican: I can compare how the work of an algorithm grows as its input grows.
directions: Use log2 10 ≈ 3.32 and the rule log2 (10 to the k) = k x log2 10. Fill in the number of steps for each growth rate, match each rate to an algorithm, graph two of the rates, and explain what the numbers mean.
mistakes: Students often treat log2 100 as 100 / 2 or as 10 x 3.32. Since 100 = 10 x 10, log2 100 = 2 x 3.32. Another slip: reading n log2 n as (n log2) n. On the graph, n looks flat next to n log2 n, and n squared shoots off the top; that is the lesson, not a mistake in scale.
notes: With log2 10 ≈ 3.32: log2 100 ≈ 6.64 and log2 1,000 ≈ 9.96 (a calculator gives 9.97, and n log2 n for n = 1,000 gives about 9,966; accept either). From n = 100 to 1,000 (10 times bigger), n grows 10 times, n squared 100 times, n log2 n about 15 times and log2 n only 1.5 times. At one microsecond a step and n = 1,000,000: n takes 1 second, n log2 n about 20 seconds, and n squared 1,000,000 seconds, about 11.6 days.

## Warm up
example: log2 8 = 3 because 2 x 2 x 2 = 8. log2 10 ≈ 3.32, so log2 100 = 2 x 3.32 = 6.64.
fill:
  - log2 16 = {4}
  - log2 1,024 = {10}

## Main: Fill the table
table: Growth rate | n = 10 | n = 100 | n = 1,000
  - 1 (constant) | 1 | 1 | 1
  - log2 n | 3.32 | {6.64} | {9.96}
  - n | {10} | {100} | {1,000}
  - n log2 n | {33.2} | {664} | {9,960}
  - n squared | {100} | {10,000} | {1,000,000}
match: Match each growth rate to an algorithm that grows that way.
  - 1 (constant) = Look up the item at a given index in an array
  - log2 n = Binary search on a sorted list
  - n = Linear search through an unsorted list
  - n log2 n = Merge sort
  - n squared = Selection sort, comparing each item with every item after it

## Graph
text: Plot n and n log2 n at n = 10, 100 and 1,000. Connect each set of points and label the lines.
graph: n (input size) | 0 1000 100 | Steps | 0 10000 1000
  key: 10,33.2 100,664 1000,9960
fill:
  - At n = 1,000, n squared would be plotted {100} times higher than the top of this graph.
  - From n = 100 to n = 1,000, n squared grows {100} times, but log2 n grows only {1.5} times.

## Apply
fill:
  - A computer does 1 step per microsecond. For n = 1,000,000, an n algorithm takes {1} second.
  - For n = 1,000,000, an n squared algorithm takes {1,000,000} seconds, about {11.6} days.
write: A team's sorting algorithm is n squared. A teammate says, "It finishes 1,000 items in a blink, so it is fine." When could that be a problem?
  lines: 3
  key: When the data grows. At 1,000 items n squared is 1,000,000 steps, about 1 second at a microsecond per step, but at 1,000,000 items it is about 11.6 days. An n log2 n sort like merge sort would take about 20 seconds.

## Stretch
write: Two algorithms take 100n steps and n squared steps. For which n is the n squared algorithm faster? What does that teach about growth rates?
  lines: 3
  key: n squared is smaller when n < 100 (they tie at n = 100). For small inputs the "worse" growth rate can win, but past n = 100 the 100n algorithm wins by more and more; growth rates describe what happens as n gets large.

# 23. Algorithmic Accountability
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use sources to argue who should be accountable when an algorithm causes harm.
directions: Read the two sources. Sort out who could be held responsible, then write a three-paragraph response: your claim, your evidence from both sources, and a counterargument with your answer to it.
mistakes: Students often blame "the algorithm," which cannot be held accountable; push them to name the people and organizations who built it, bought it, used it and checked it. Others summarize the sources without using them as evidence for a claim. A strong response cites both sources and explains how each supports the claim.
notes: Both sources are summaries adapted for Curio Class, not quotations. Source A summarizes widely reported events and official findings in the Netherlands: the parliamentary inquiry report was published in December 2020 and the government resigned on January 15, 2021; the Dutch Data Protection Authority found the tax agency's use of nationality in its processing unlawful and discriminatory. Source B paraphrases the five principles of the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, a non-binding framework from the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (October 2022); it was guidance, not law. Check current details before teaching if you add more sources.

## Read the sources
source: Source A. Adapted summary of the Dutch childcare benefits scandal (about 2013 to 2021)
  [1] In the Netherlands, families can get government help paying for childcare. For several years, the tax agency treated thousands of these families as fraudsters, often over small paperwork errors, and demanded that they pay back large amounts, sometimes tens of thousands of euros. Many families fell deep into debt.
  [2] The agency used a risk-scoring system to pick which applications to check. Having a nationality other than Dutch was one of the factors it used. Families with immigrant backgrounds were flagged far more often.
  [3] Families who protested found it hard to learn why they had been flagged or to get a person to review their case. A parliamentary inquiry in 2020 called what happened an unprecedented injustice, and in January 2021 the Dutch government resigned over the scandal. The country's data protection authority later ruled that using nationality this way was unlawful and discriminatory.
source: Source B. Adapted from the principles of the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, October 2022)
  [4] Automated systems should be tested before and after they are used, to show they are safe and work as intended. [5] People should not face discrimination from algorithms; designers and users should check for unfair differences between groups and fix them. [6] People should be protected from abusive data practices and have a say in how their data is used.
  [7] People should know when an automated system is being used on them and get a clear explanation of how it affects decisions about them. [8] Where appropriate, people should be able to opt out and reach a human who can quickly consider and fix problems.

## Sort it out
chart: Who could be held accountable? | Argument for | Argument against
  rows: 3
  key: The agency that used the system (it chose to rely on it and to punish families; it could say it trusted the tool). The people who designed the risk model (they chose nationality as a factor; they may say they followed the agency's request). The officials and leaders who oversaw the agency (they are answerable to the public, and the government did resign; they may not have known the details). Lawmakers or regulators (they set the rules and could require testing and explanation; they cannot inspect every system). The algorithm itself is not on the list: it cannot be accountable, because only people and organizations can answer for choices.

## Write your response
write: Paragraph 1: Your claim. Who should be accountable when an algorithm harms someone, and for what?
  lines: 4
  starter: When an algorithm harms people, ... should be held accountable because ...
  key: Model: When an algorithm harms people, the organization that chose to use it should be held accountable first, along with the people who designed it, because people decide what data a system uses, how much to trust its output, and whether anyone can appeal. "The computer decided" is never an acceptable answer.
write: Paragraph 2: Your evidence. Use at least one detail from each source, with its number.
  lines: 6
  starter: In the Netherlands, ... [ ]. This shows ... The Blueprint says ... [ ], which means ...
  key: Model: In the Netherlands, the tax agency's risk system used nationality as a factor [2], and families could not find out why they were flagged or reach a person to review their cases [3]. The agency, not the software, chose those rules and that process, and it was the government that resigned [3]. The Blueprint says people should get an explanation and a human who can fix problems [7] [8] and that systems should be tested for unfair differences [5]. By those standards, the agency failed duties that belonged to it, so accountability belongs with the people who built, bought and ran the system.
write: Paragraph 3: A counterargument and your answer to it.
  lines: 5
  starter: Some people argue that ... However, ...
  key: Model: Some people argue that no one person can be blamed because the harm came from a complex system with many hands. However, that is exactly why rules must name who is responsible before a system is used: the organization that deploys it should test it [4] [5], explain it [7], and give people a way to appeal [8], and it should answer for harm when it skips those steps.
