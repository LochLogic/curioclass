# 1. Ledger Lines and Middle C
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can read notes from middle C up to high G on the treble staff.
directions: Learn how ledger lines stretch the staff. Draw middle C and D below the staff. Then name 12 notes and decode a word written in notes.
pairs: note-ninja
mistakes: Students often give D its own ledger line. D sits just under the bottom line, touching it, with no ledger line; only middle C needs one. Watch for ledger lines drawn too long or too far below the staff: a ledger line is short, just wider than the note, and spaced like a staff line. High G sits in the space just above the top line.
notes: Notes from middle C up: C4 (ledger line), D4 (below line 1), E4 line 1, F4 space 1, G4 line 2, A4 space 2, B4 line 3, C5 space 3, D5 line 4, E5 space 4, F5 line 5, G5 above line 5. Middle C is the C nearest the middle of the piano keyboard, and it sits on one ledger line below the treble staff or one ledger line above the bass staff. In Part B, the notes in order are C E G D B F A C G E F D.

## Warm up: What is a ledger line?
fill:
  - A short line added above or below the staff is a {ledger} line.
  - Middle C sits {on} one ledger line below the treble staff.
  - D sits in the space just {below} the bottom line.
  - The note in the space just above the top line is {G}.
text: Use a ruler to draw a five-line staff in the box. Then draw middle C (with its ledger line) and D just below the staff.
draw: Middle C and D | 1.5

## Main: Name the notes
text: Write the letter name under each note.
staff: treble
  - C4
  - E4
  - G4
  - D4
  - B4
  - F5
staff: treble
  - A4
  - C5

  - F4

write: Which of the 12 notes is the lowest, and which is the highest? How can you tell from the staff?
  lines: 2
  key: Middle C (the first note) is lowest, because it sits on a ledger line below the staff. High G (the third note on the second staff) is highest, because it sits above the top line.

# 7. Recorder Fingerings: Low D to High D
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; a soprano recorder
ican: I can finger and name notes from low D to high D on the recorder.
directions: Use the G example to see how the chart works. Shade the holes you cover for each new note. Then name the notes in 4 short songs and play them.
mistakes: Students often forget the thumb hole on the back, or leave a small gap at holes 5 and 6 so low D and E squeak: have them press until they feel a ring on each finger pad. Watch for F sharp fingered with hole 4 covered: with 5 and 6 also down, that is the low D fingering. For high C and high D the thumb moves: thumb on for C, thumb off for D.
notes: The chart uses Baroque (English) fingering, the most common on school soprano recorders; on a German-fingered recorder, F sharp and F are fingered differently, so check the chart that came with it. Holes: 0 is the thumb hole on the back; 1 to 3 are the left hand (top), 4 to 7 the right hand. Fingerings: G 0 1 2 3; low D 0 1 2 3 4 5 6; E 0 1 2 3 4 5; F sharp 0 1 2 3 5 6 (4 open); high C 0 2; high D 2 only. Songs: 1 Ode to Joy (Beethoven) in D: F-sharp F-sharp G A A G F-sharp E. 2 Twinkle Twinkle in D: D D A A B B A. 3 Old MacDonald in C: C C C G A A G. 4 When the Saints Go Marching In in G: G B C D G B C D. A soprano recorder sounds an octave higher than written, so low D is written below the staff and high D on the fourth line.

## Warm up: How to read the chart
text: ● means cover the hole. ○ means leave it open. Hole 0 is the thumb hole on the back. Holes 1 to 3 are your left hand; 4 to 7 are your right hand.
table: Note | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7
  - G (example) | ● | ● | ● | ● | ○ | ○ | ○ | ○

# 20. Major Scale Maze
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can name the notes of the G major and F major scales in order.
directions: A major scale climbs in the pattern whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. In each maze, start on the top left square and shade the next note of the scale at each step. Wrong notes are dead ends.
pairs: melody-maker
mistakes: The trap in G major is F: the seventh note is F sharp, a half step below G. The trap in F major is B: the fourth note is B flat, a half step above A. Students who only say the letter names (F G A B C D E F) walk straight into the trap; have them check each step against the pattern on a keyboard picture.
notes: G major: G A B C D E F♯ G (one sharp, F♯). F major: F G A B♭ C D E F (one flat, B♭). Every square that is not on the path holds a note that is not in that scale, so a shaded square that is not on the path is always a mistake. Half steps fall between notes 3 and 4 and between 7 and 8: in G major B to C and F♯ to G; in F major A to B♭ and E to F.

## Warm up: The pattern
fill:
  - The major scale pattern is W W {H} W W W {H}.
  - A half step is the closest note: from E up a half step is {F}.
  - G major has one sharp: {F♯}. F major has one flat: {B♭}.
choice: In C major (no sharps or flats), which note comes after B? It is a half step up.
  - C♯
  - B♭
  * C

## Stretch: Explain the trap
write: Both mazes have a trap. Name the trap note in each maze and explain, using whole and half steps, why it is wrong.
  lines: 3
  key: In G major, F is wrong: from E the scale needs a whole step to F♯ so that F♯ to G is the last half step. In F major, B is wrong: from A the scale needs a half step to B♭; A to B would be a whole step and break the W W H pattern.

# 21. Melody in G Composer
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, staff paper, a keyboard, recorder or barred instrument
ican: I can compose a melody in G major that moves by steps and ends on G.
directions: Learn the G major scale. Plan your 8 measures in the chart with letter names. Then copy your melody onto staff paper and play it.
pairs: melody-maker
mistakes: The most common error is F natural instead of F sharp: remind students the key signature's sharp on the top line changes every F. Watch for melodies with many leaps (count them: the task asks for one) and for endings on D, which sound unfinished.
notes: G major: G A B C D E F# G, one sharp (F#). A step moves to the next letter (G to A); a leap skips at least one letter (G to B or G to D). Suggest a question and answer plan: measures 1 to 4 end on D or A (the question), measures 5 to 8 end on G (the answer). Students who finish early can add rhythm (quarter, eighth pairs, half notes) under the letters. The sheet uses a measure chart instead of printed staff paper; give each student one sheet of staff paper for the final copy.

## Warm up: The G major scale
fill:
  - G major has one sharp: {F#}.
  - G A B {C} D {E} {F#} G
  - G to A is a {step}.
  - G to D is a {leap}.
  - E to F# is a {step}.
  - B to G is a {leap}.
example: A model first phrase: | G A B G | A B C B | A G A B | A - - - |
  It moves by steps, and its leap is B down to G in measure 1. It ends on A, so it sounds like a question.

## Main: Plan your melody
chart: Measure | Notes (letter names) | Steps or a leap? | Ending note of the phrase
  rows: 8
  key: Answers vary. Check that every note is from G A B C D E F# G, that F is always F#, that there is exactly one leap, and that measure 8 ends on G. Measure 4 should end on a note other than G (D or A works well).
write: Copy your melody onto staff paper with the key signature (one sharp on the F line). Play it. Where is your one leap?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. The student names the measure and the two notes, for example: "Measure 6, from D up to G."

## Stretch: Check your ending
write: Play your last measure two ways: once ending on G, once ending on D. Which sounds finished? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Ending on G sounds finished because G is the home note (do) of G major; ending on D sounds like the melody is waiting or asking a question.

# 33. Balance Problem Solvers
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can solve balance problems so the melody is heard in an ensemble.
directions: Read each rehearsal problem. Choose what the section should change so the most important part is heard. Then plan the balance for your own ensemble.
mistakes: Students often fix every problem by asking the melody to play louder. That usually makes everyone too loud and the tone harsh. First ask the parts that cover the melody to play softer. Watch for answers that stop a section completely: playing softer keeps the full sound.
notes: Balance means every part can be heard at the right level, with the most important part on top. Dynamic words: p (piano) soft, mp medium soft, mf medium loud, f (forte) loud. A good rule of thumb for school ensembles: the melody plays one level louder than the accompaniment (melody mf, accompaniment p or mp). Answers: 1 B, 2 D, 3 A, 4 C, 5 B, 6 C. Act a few of these out with your own ensemble.

## Warm up: Balance words
fill:
  - p stands for piano, which means {soft}.
  - f stands for forte, which means {loud}.
  - Balance means every part is heard, and the {melody} is on top.

## Main: Fix the balance
choice: The recorders play the melody, but the xylophones are so loud the tune disappears. What should change?
  - The recorders play as loud as they can.
  * The xylophones play softer, like p.
  - The xylophones stop playing.
  - Everyone plays faster.
choice: The song ends very quietly, but the drums keep playing loud. What should change?
  - The singers sing louder to match the drums.
  - Everyone gets louder at the end.
  - The drums play faster.
  * The drums play softer, with softer mallets or fewer players.
choice: The audience cannot understand the singers' words over the instruments. What should change?
  * The instruments play softer, and the singers say their consonants clearly.
  - The singers shout the words.
  - The instruments play louder so it sounds fuller.
  - Everyone stops and claps.
choice: The low bass xylophone bordun is so soft that the song feels like it is floating with no base. What should change?
  - The melody plays softer.
  - The bordun stops playing.
  * The bass xylophone plays a little louder, or a second player joins it.
  - The drums play louder to fill the space.
choice: One alto xylophone player plays much louder than the rest of the section, so you hear one person instead of a section. What should change?
  - The rest of the section plays louder to match.
  * That player listens to the neighbors and blends in.
  - That player stops playing for the concert.
  - The whole ensemble plays slower.
choice: During a short recorder solo, the class clapping along gets louder and louder. What should change?
  - The solo player plays as loud as possible.
  - The solo is cut from the concert.
  * The clappers clap softly or stop until the solo ends.
  - The class claps faster.

## Stretch: Plan your balance
write: Think of a song your class performs with a melody, a bordun and a drum. Which part is loudest, and what dynamic would you give each part? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. For example: "The melody is loudest at mf. The bordun plays mp so it supports without covering, and the drum plays p because it carries easily."
write: Why is it usually better to ask the accompaniment to play softer than to ask the melody to play louder?
  lines: 2
  key: When the melody pushes harder, its tone gets harsh and out of tune and the whole group gets louder. When the accompaniment plays softer, the melody is heard with a good tone and the music keeps room to grow.
