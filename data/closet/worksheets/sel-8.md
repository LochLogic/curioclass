# 1. The Conflict Cycle
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can describe how a conflict moves through stages and where it can be stopped.
directions: Read the five stages of the conflict cycle and label the diagram. Then read the story about the Hawks volleyball team and match each event to its stage. Finish by finding where the cycle could have been broken sooner.
mistakes: Students mix up Response and Escalation. The response is the first thing a person does about their feelings; escalation is when others react and the conflict grows bigger than the two people who started it. Also watch for students who call the resolution "the coach fixed it": the players did the talking.
notes: This sheet uses a simple five-stage teaching model (Trigger, Feelings, Response, Escalation, Resolution). Other models name the stages differently; the idea that an unresolved conflict feeds the next one is common to most. The story is original to Curio Class. The diagram is drawn by students in the box as a circle of five arrows.

## Warm up: The five stages
text: **Trigger:** an event sets things off. **Feelings:** emotions rise inside each person. **Response:** someone acts on those feelings. **Escalation:** others react and the conflict grows. **Resolution:** people talk it through and agree on a fix. If there is no resolution, leftover feelings become the trigger for the next round, so the cycle repeats.
draw: Draw the cycle as a circle of five arrows. Label each arrow with a stage, in order, starting with Trigger at the top. | 2.6
fill:
  - The stage where emotions rise inside each person: {Feelings}
  - The stage where others react and the conflict grows: {Escalation}
  - What happens if a conflict never reaches resolution? {Leftover feelings trigger the next round, so it repeats.}

## Read
passage: The Captain Problem
  For two summers, Maria ran early morning workouts for the Hawks volleyball team. She texted reminders, brought the ball bag and stayed late to help new players with their serves. So when Coach Brooks announced that Jaylen would be team captain, Maria's stomach dropped.
  Maria felt overlooked and embarrassed. Everyone knew she had wanted the job. Jaylen noticed the quiet that fell over the gym and felt worried that the team did not want him. He had been picked for his calm voice in close games, but nobody had told the team that.
  At the next practice, Maria stopped calling out plays and skipped the warm-up circle. When Jaylen asked her to join, she shrugged and said, "You're the captain. You do it."
  Things got worse. Teammates started taking sides in the team group chat. During Saturday's match, a missed pass turned into shouting between the two sides of the court, and the Hawks lost in straight sets.
  On Monday, Coach Brooks asked Maria and Jaylen to stay after practice. Maria said she felt like her summer work had not counted. Jaylen said he felt she did not respect him. They listened to each other, then agreed on a plan: Maria would lead warm-ups and serving drills, and Jaylen would talk with referees and run team meetings. At the next practice, they explained the plan to the team together.

## Main: Match each event to its stage
match: Write the letter of the event next to its stage.
  - Trigger = Coach Brooks announces that Jaylen will be captain.
  - Feelings = Maria feels overlooked and embarrassed; Jaylen feels worried.
  - Response = Maria skips the warm-up circle and says, "You do it."
  - Escalation = Teammates take sides and the match ends in shouting.
  - Resolution = Maria and Jaylen agree to share leadership jobs.

## Stretch: Break the cycle
write: Pick one stage before Escalation. What could Maria, Jaylen or a teammate have done at that stage to stop the conflict from growing?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: At the Response stage, Maria could have told Jaylen, "I'm disappointed I wasn't picked, but I want the team to do well," instead of skipping the circle. Or at Feelings, Jaylen could have asked Maria to help lead warm-ups right away, showing respect for her summer work.

# 7. Conflict and Power
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can recognize when one side of a conflict has more power and suggest a resolution that is fair to both.
directions: Read the four kinds of power. For each conflict, name who has more power and where it comes from, then explain what the person with less power needs and write a fair resolution.
mistakes: Students assume "fair" means the person with more power simply gives in. A fair resolution meets both sides' real interests and does not depend on who is stronger. Another miss: telling the person with less power to "just stand up to them" when the safest step is to bring in a neutral third person. Conflict 4 is repeated and public, so it may be bullying; say so and talk about reporting.
notes: The four sources of power here (position, age or size, social status and knowledge) are a simplified classroom list. Teach the key idea: when power is unequal, the person with less power often stays quiet because speaking up feels risky, so the person with more power carries extra responsibility to listen. Bullying is usually defined by three features: it is intentional, repeated and involves a power imbalance. Share your school's reporting process when you discuss Conflict 4. All characters are fictional.

## Warm up: Where power comes from
text: **Position:** a role that lets someone make decisions (captain, club president, group leader). **Age or size:** being older, bigger or in a higher grade. **Social status:** popularity, many friends or followers. **Knowledge:** knowing something others need, or knowing the rules better.
choice: Which is the best sign that someone is using their power **fairly**?
  - They make every decision quickly so there is no argument.
  - They remind others who is in charge.
  - They avoid the problem and hope it goes away.
  * They listen to the other side and explain the reasons for their decisions.

## Main: Four conflicts
text: **Conflict 1: Captain and player.** Malik, the soccer captain, helps the coach plan the starting lineup. Ana joined the team this year and has played in only one game. When she asks why, Malik says, "Captains decide. Deal with it."
text: **Conflict 2: Older and younger students.** Every afternoon on the bus, a group of eighth graders tells sixth grader Jonah to move out of the back seats. One day they toss his backpack into the aisle.
text: **Conflict 3: Group leader and member.** Ms. Reed chose Priya to lead a history project because she has the highest grade. Priya rewrote Ben's whole section the night before it was due without asking him, and now Ben's name is on work he didn't write.
text: **Conflict 4: Social power.** Kira has hundreds of followers. She posted a photo of Sam's art project with a joke caption, and dozens of people commented laughing faces. It is the third time she has posted about Sam this month.
table: Conflict | Who has more power? | Main source of power
  - 1 | {Malik} | {position (captain who helps plan the lineup)}
  - 2 | {the eighth graders} | {age, size and numbers}
  - 3 | {Priya} | {position (leader chosen by the teacher) and knowledge (top grade)}
  - 4 | {Kira} | {social status (many followers)}
write: Conflict 1: What does Ana need? Write a fair resolution.
  lines: 3
  key: Ana needs to know why she is not playing and what she can do to earn more time. Fair resolution: Ana asks the coach and Malik for a short meeting; Malik explains the lineup reasons instead of "Captains decide," and the coach gives Ana two specific skills to work on and a chance to show them in practice.
write: Conflict 2: What does Jonah need? Write a fair resolution.
  lines: 3
  key: Jonah needs to be safe and to have a seat without being pushed around. Throwing his backpack goes beyond a disagreement, so Jonah or a friend tells the bus driver or an adult at school. A fair fix could be assigned seats or a rule that seats are first come, first served, enforced by the driver, not by the older students.
write: Conflict 3: What does Ben need? Write a fair resolution.
  lines: 3
  key: Ben needs a say in his own work and credit that is honest. Priya and Ben talk with Ms. Reed: they explain what happened, Ben fixes his section with Priya's suggestions, and next time the leader gives feedback before a deadline instead of rewriting someone's work.
write: Conflict 4: What does Sam need? Write a fair resolution.
  lines: 3
  key: Sam needs the posts to stop and to feel safe at school. Because the posts are repeated, public and one-sided, this may be bullying, so Sam or a bystander reports it to a trusted adult and to the app. Kira takes the posts down and apologizes directly to Sam, and friends can support Sam by not adding comments.

## Stretch
write: Choose one conflict. Write two sentences the person with **more** power could say to make the situation fairer.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example (Malik): "You're right, you deserve a real answer. The coach and I picked players who've been at every practice, but let's talk about what you can work on so you get more minutes."

# 20. Conflict Resolution Review
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can analyze a conflict with several sides and plan a negotiation and a restorative follow-up.
directions: Read the case; its paragraphs are numbered. Map each party's position and interests, plan a negotiation that could meet all three groups' needs, and then design a restorative follow-up for the harm that was done.
mistakes: Students try to pick a winner ("the garden was there first"). In a multi-party negotiation the goal is an option that meets as many interests as possible. Watch restorative plans that are really punishments ("ban them from the courtyard"). A restorative plan asks who was harmed and how the person responsible can make it right.
notes: The case is original to Curio Class and the people are fictional. The restorative questions follow the common school model also used in this unit's Restorative Questions sheet: What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who has been affected, and how? What do you need to do to make things right? Accept any negotiation plan that includes all three parties, ground rules, at least two options that meet more than one interest, and a clear way to decide.

## Read
passage: Trouble in the Courtyard | numbered
  For three years, the school courtyard was mostly empty. Then, last fall, the Eco Club got permission to build six raised garden beds along its east side. By spring the beds held tomatoes, beans and sunflowers, and the club had promised half the harvest to the community food pantry.
  The courtyard is also where many eighth graders spend lunch. A group of about twelve students plays a pickup soccer game there every day. With the garden beds in place, their playing space shrank, and the ball started landing in the beds.
  Mr. Hale, the head custodian, has his own concern. He has to keep the courtyard paths clear and clean, and he has seen dirt tracked onto the paths and water left running from the club's hose.
  Last Tuesday, a hard kick sent the soccer ball into the tomato bed and snapped four plants. That afternoon, Eco Club member Rosa taped a sign to the courtyard door: "SOCCER PLAYERS: You ruin everything. Stay out." On Wednesday, the club's watering cans disappeared. On Thursday, both groups showed up at the assistant principal's office, each demanding that the other be banned from the courtyard.
  The assistant principal, Ms. Boateng, did not ban anyone. Instead, she asked a teacher and two student mediators to bring all three parties together the following week.
  Before the meeting, the mediators talked to each group alone. The Eco Club said they need the plants protected and enough sun and water to grow food for the pantry. The soccer players said lunch is their only time to run around and blow off steam, and they felt insulted by the sign. Mr. Hale said he needs clear paths and no running water left behind, because he is the one who has to clean it up.
  The watering cans turned up on Friday behind the gym. No one has said who moved them.

## Map the conflict
table: Party | Position (what they demand) | Interests (what they really need)
  - Eco Club | {ban soccer from the courtyard} | {safe plants, sun and water, food for the pantry}
  - Soccer players | {ban the garden, or at least keep it out of their way} | {a place to run and play at lunch; respect after the insulting sign}
  - Mr. Hale | {keep the courtyard clean} | {clear paths, no running water, less cleanup work}
choice: Which move **escalated** the conflict from a mistake into a feud?
  - The Eco Club building the garden beds
  * Rosa's sign telling all soccer players to stay out
  - Ms. Boateng refusing to ban either group
  - The mediators meeting each group alone

## Plan the negotiation
chart: Your plan
  labels: Who sits at the table; Two ground rules; Three options that meet more than one party's interests; How the group will decide; Fallback if they cannot agree
  height: 0.6
  key: Model. Table: two Eco Club members, two soccer players, Mr. Hale, the student mediators and a teacher. Ground rules: one person talks at a time; no put-downs or blaming the whole group. Options: a low fence or netting on the side of the beds that faces the field; moving the soccer game to the open west half with a marked line; a weekly courtyard schedule; the club adds a hose timer and sweeps the paths after watering; soccer players help guard the beds by setting up the net. Decide: by consensus on a package of options, with the teacher's approval. Fallback: Ms. Boateng sets a temporary schedule for two weeks, then they meet again.

## Restorative follow-up
write: Rosa's sign and the missing watering cans both caused harm. Choose one. Write two restorative questions you would ask the person responsible.
  lines: 3
  key: For Rosa: "What were you thinking when you wrote the sign?" and "Who was affected by it, and how?" (Accept any two restorative questions about what happened, who was affected and how to make it right.)
write: Plan how the harm could be repaired. Include the snapped plants, the sign and the watering cans.
  lines: 4
  key: Model: The player who kicked the ball helps the club replant four tomato plants. Rosa takes down the sign and apologizes to the soccer players in person, saying what she should have said: "We need help keeping the ball out of the beds." Whoever moved the watering cans returns them and offers to water for a week, or the group agrees to a no-blame return if they reappear. The groups check in after two weeks to see if the plan is working.

## Stretch
write: Why might Ms. Boateng's choice not to ban anyone lead to a better result than a ban?
  lines: 3
  key: A ban would leave one group feeling like the loser and could keep the feud going. Bringing everyone together lets the groups find options that meet all their interests, repair the harm and build trust, so the problem is less likely to return.

# 21. Stakeholder Decision Grid
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can weigh how a decision affects different groups of people before choosing.
directions: Ridgeview Middle School is choosing a new phone policy. Read the three options and the facts. In the grid, write how each option would affect each group: a plus (+) for a good effect and a minus (-) for a problem, with a few words. Then recommend one option.
mistakes: Students fill the grid only from a student's point of view. Have them read each row header aloud and ask "What does this group care about?" Watch for empty cells: every option has at least one effect on every group, even if small.
notes: Ridgeview Middle School and its facts are fictional. Real schools and states have a range of phone policies; this sheet is about the decision process, not about which policy is right. Students with medical needs, for example those who use phone-linked glucose monitors, usually need an exception under any ban; credit students who notice this. The grid key is a model: accept any effect a student can tie to the facts. Accept any recommendation backed by the grid.

## Read
text: **Option A, all-day lockup:** phones stay in locked pouches from first bell to last bell. **Option B, lunch only:** phones stay off and away in class but are allowed at lunch. **Option C, teacher's choice:** each teacher decides when phones can be used in class.
text: **Facts:** Teachers report that phones distract students in about half of classes. Families say they want to reach their children quickly if plans change. Pouches would cost the school about 9,000 dollars. Office staff spend time each day handling taken-away phones. A few students use phones to run medical devices.

## Warm up
choice: What is a stakeholder?
  - The person who makes the final decision
  * Anyone who is affected by a decision or cares about its outcome
  - A person who owns part of the school
choice: Which fact belongs most clearly in the Families row?
  - Pouches would cost about 9,000 dollars.
  * Families want to reach their children quickly if plans change.
  - Phones distract students in about half of classes.

## Main: The grid
table: Stakeholder group | A: all-day lockup | B: lunch only | C: teacher's choice
  - Students | {+ fewer distractions; - no phone at lunch} | {+ social time at lunch; - no phones in class} | {+ phones for class tools; - rules change room to room}
  - Teachers | {+ no phone fights in class} | {+ calmer classes} | {- must make and enforce own rules}
  - Families | {- harder to reach child in the day} | {+ can reach child at lunch} | {+ some contact; - unclear rules}
  - Principal and office staff | {- 9,000 dollar cost; + fewer phones to collect} | {+ low cost; - some phones collected} | {- most rule questions and complaints}
  - Students with medical needs | {- need an exception to use devices} | {- still need an exception in class} | {+ easier to arrange with each teacher}

## Recommend
write: Which option do you recommend? Use at least two cells from your grid as evidence, and name one group the option is hardest on and how to help them.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary. Example: Option B, because it calms classes (teachers +) and lets families reach students at lunch (families +), at low cost. It is hardest on students with medical needs, so the policy should include a written exception for medical devices.

# 33. Decision Terms Sort
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue
ican: I can sort decision words by how they work in a choice and use them in my own writing.
directions: Cut out the 12 term cards. Sort them into thinking tools (ways to think a choice through), influences (things that push a choice) and outcomes (what happens because of a choice). Glue them down, then use four terms in a paragraph about a decision.
mistakes: "Opportunity cost" gets sorted as a thinking tool because it sounds like math. It is an outcome: the next best thing you give up when you choose. "Values" is sometimes sorted as a thinking tool; values are an influence, because they push you toward some choices. Some students also put "decision matrix" under outcomes.
notes: Definitions for the key. **Thinking tools:** pros and cons list (two columns of good and bad points); decision matrix (a table that scores each option on chosen criteria); decision tree (a branching drawing of options and possible outcomes); cost-benefit analysis (comparing what an option costs with what it gains). **Influences:** peer pressure, values, advertising, emotions. **Outcomes:** consequence (a result of a choice), benefit (a good result), opportunity cost (the next best option given up), unintended consequence (a result nobody planned). The paragraph scenario is fictional.

## Warm up
fill:
  - The next best option you give up when you choose is the {opportunity cost}.
  - A table that scores each option on several criteria is a {decision matrix}.
  - A result that nobody planned or expected is an {unintended consequence}.

## Main: Sort the terms
sort: Thinking tools, influences and outcomes
  - Thinking tools: pros and cons list, decision matrix, decision tree, cost-benefit analysis
  - Influences: peer pressure, values, advertising, emotions
  - Outcomes: consequence, benefit, opportunity cost, unintended consequence

## Use the terms
text: **The scenario:** Andre has 60 dollars saved. He can buy a video game his friends keep talking about, or he can pay for a weekend robotics workshop he has wanted to try.
write: Write a paragraph (4 to 6 sentences) about Andre's decision. Use at least four terms from the sort, and underline each one.
  lines: 7
  key: Model: Andre's friends are a strong influence, and the peer pressure to buy the game is real. But his values include learning new skills, so he makes a pros and cons list. The benefit of the workshop is that he learns to build robots, while the opportunity cost is not playing the game with his friends this month. One unintended consequence might be that he meets new friends at the workshop. (Any four terms used correctly.)

## Stretch
write: Which thinking tool would help Andre most? Explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: A decision matrix, because he could score both options on cost, fun, learning and time with friends, and see which one scores higher overall instead of just going with his feelings.
