# 1. Chekhov's The Proposal: What's So Funny?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify each character's objective and obstacle and explain what makes a scene funny.
directions: Read the opening of The Proposal. Highlight one line for each character that shows what they want. Then answer the questions about objectives, obstacles and comedy.
mistakes: Students often say the comedy is "they yell." Push for the source: the gap between what Lomov came to do and what he ends up doing, and the audience knowing things the characters do not (Chubukov thinks he wants money; Natalya does not know he came to propose). Watch for Lomov's obstacle answered only as "Natalya argues"; his own nerves and pride are obstacles too.
notes: The scene is adapted and shortened by Curio Class from Anton Chekhov's one-act comedy The Proposal (late 1880s), which is in the public domain; lines are paraphrased, not quoted from a translation. In the full play, the fight over the Oxen Meadows is followed by a second fight over whose hunting dog is better, and Lomov's proposal barely survives both. About 275 words. Read it aloud in three voices before students write.

## Read
passage: The Proposal (opening, adapted from Anton Chekhov)
  (A drawing room in CHUBUKOV's country house in Russia. LOMOV enters in his best evening suit and white gloves.)
  CHUBUKOV: My dear neighbor! What a surprise! But why are you dressed so formally? Are you going to a party?
  LOMOV: No, I came only to see you, my honored Stepan Stepanovitch. I have a request to make. (He sips water.) Excuse me, I am trembling all over.
  CHUBUKOV: (aside) He has come to borrow money. I will not lend him a single coin! (aloud) Out with it, my dear fellow.
  LOMOV: I have come to ask for the hand of your daughter, Natalya Stepanovna.
  CHUBUKOV: (overjoyed, hugging him) My dear boy! I have wanted this for years! I will call her at once. (He hurries out.)
  LOMOV: (alone) It is cold in here. I am shaking as if before an exam. I must decide. If I wait for perfect love, I will never marry. I am thirty-five, and I need a quiet, regular life. She keeps a good house and she is educated. But my heart is pounding, my lip twitches, and I cannot sleep at night.
  (NATALYA enters in a kitchen apron.)
  NATALYA: Oh, it is only you! Papa said a merchant had come for his goods. Forgive my apron; we are shelling peas.
  LOMOV: (nervously) Natalya Stepanovna, I will be brief. Our families have been friends for years. As you know, my land, the Oxen Meadows, borders your birch woods...
  NATALYA: Excuse me. Your land? The Oxen Meadows are ours.
  LOMOV: No, they are mine.
  NATALYA: Ours! They have always been ours!
  LOMOV: (clutching his chest) Mine! I can show you the papers!

## Warm up
choice: Why does Chubukov first think Lomov has come to visit?
  - To sell him a horse
  * To borrow money
  - To argue about land
  - To invite him to a party
choice: What does Natalya think when she first sees Lomov?
  - That he has come to propose
  * That he is only a visiting neighbor; she was told a merchant had come
  - That he is angry with her
  - That he is sick

## Main: Objectives and obstacles
table: Character | Objective (what they want in this scene) | Obstacle (what is in the way)
  - Lomov | {to propose to Natalya} | {his nerves, and his pride about the meadows}
  - Chubukov | {to get his daughter married to Lomov} | {he is not in the room when it goes wrong}
  - Natalya | {to win the argument: prove the meadows are hers} | {Lomov insists they are his}
write: Find the moment where the proposal goes off track. What exactly sends it there?
  lines: 3
  key: When Lomov, trying to show the families are close, says "my land, the Oxen Meadows." Natalya cannot let that pass, and the proposal turns into a property fight.
write: Name two different sources of comedy in this scene and quote a line for each.
  lines: 4
  key: Any two: dramatic irony, because the audience knows Lomov came to propose but Natalya does not ("Oh, it is only you!"); misunderstanding, as Chubukov expects a loan ("I will not lend him a single coin!"); Lomov's exaggerated nerves and body complaints ("my lip twitches, and I cannot sleep at night"); a tiny issue blown up into a fight, as two people who should be getting engaged argue over a meadow ("Ours! They have always been ours!").
choice: Which statement best explains why the meadow fight is funny?
  - The meadows are very valuable, so the fight is serious.
  * The characters care more about winning a small argument than about the proposal they both want.
  - Natalya does not like Lomov at all.
  - Chubukov planned the fight.

## Stretch
write: You are directing this scene. Give one acting or staging choice that would make the audience laugh at the start of the meadow fight, and explain why it works.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: Lomov drops to one knee to propose just as he says "my land," then has to get up to argue, so the physical picture flips from romance to fight; or the two lean closer and closer as they shout "Mine!" and "Ours!", ending nose to nose, which shows their stubbornness.

# 7. 1890s Costume Rendering
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can use period research to design and label costumes that fit a play's time, place and characters.
directions: Read the research cards, which describe photographs of clothing from the 1890s. Then draw a front-view costume rendering for Lomov and for Natalya from The Proposal. Label each garment with its fabric and color, and note what it shows about the character.
mistakes: Students often dress all three characters as peasants because the play is set in the countryside. Lomov, Natalya and Chubukov are landowners, so they dress like the gentry on cards A to C; card D is for contrast. Watch for modern details (zippers, sneakers, short skirts) and for labels that name only a color.
notes: The research cards are written descriptions, so no images are needed; if you can, show public-domain photographs of 1890s Russian dress from a library or museum collection. Chekhov wrote The Proposal in 1888, and it was first staged in 1889. Lomov arrives in formal evening clothes and white gloves to propose; Natalya comes in wearing a kitchen apron because she has been shelling peas. Fashion facts: big puffed sleeves at the shoulder peaked about 1894 to 1896; Russian landowners dressed in European styles, while peasants wore traditional clothing such as the man's belted shirt fastened at the side (the kosovorotka). The key's labels are models.

## Read: Research cards
text: **A. Country gentleman, formal (1890s).** Black tailcoat, short in front with long tails behind; white shirt with a stiff high collar; white bow tie or dark necktie; white waistcoat or dark vest; dark trousers; polished black shoes or boots; white gloves for a formal call. Hair short, often a mustache or beard.
text: **B. Country gentleman, everyday.** A loose jacket or a long frock coat in brown or gray wool, a vest with a watch chain, a soft collar, sturdy leather boots for walking the fields.
text: **C. Young woman of a country estate, at home.** A long skirt reaching the ankle; a fitted blouse with a high collar and sleeves puffed wide at the shoulder; hair pinned up on the head; a cotton work apron tied over the dress for kitchen or garden jobs.
text: **D. Peasant farm workers (for contrast).** A man's long linen shirt fastened at the side and tied with a belt, loose trousers tucked into boots; a woman's headscarf, long full skirt and simple blouse.

## Warm up
choice: Which card best fits Natalya when she first enters?
  - Card A
  - Card B
  * Card C
  - Card D
choice: Lomov is a landowner, so why does card A fit him better than card B on this visit?
  - He works in the fields that day.
  * He has come to propose, so he wears his most formal clothes.
  - He is going to a funeral.
  - He is a farm worker.

## Main: Costume renderings
draw: Lomov, front view, head to toe | 2.8
table: Garment | Fabric and color | What it shows
  - {tailcoat} | {black wool} | {he dressed up to propose}
  - {gloves} | {white cotton} | {formal, a little too fancy for a neighbor's visit}
  - {high collar} | {stiff white linen} | {he is stiff, nervous and uncomfortable}
draw: Natalya, front view, head to toe | 2.8
table: Garment | Fabric and color | What it shows
  - {skirt} | {plain brown wool} | {everyday clothes: she was not expecting a proposal}
  - {work apron} | {plain cotton, a little stained} | {busy and practical, shelling peas}
  - {blouse with puffed sleeves} | {light cotton} | {a young woman of the 1890s gentry}

## Stretch
write: Put your two renderings side by side. How does the contrast between the costumes help the comedy of the first scene?
  lines: 3
  key: Lomov is dressed for a grand formal occasion while Natalya is in an apron from the kitchen, so the audience sees at once that he is taking the visit very seriously and she has no idea why he came. The mismatch sets up the comic misunderstanding.

# 20. Linear, Flashback or Episodic?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare ways to structure a devised scene and choose one that suits the audience.
directions: Read the story your group devised. Imagine telling it three ways. Fill in the chart, then choose a structure and give two reasons tied to your audience.
mistakes: Students often describe a structure in general terms without applying it to this story. Each box should say what this audience would see first and when they would learn the key facts. Watch for reasons that are about the actors ("it is easier for us") instead of the audience.
notes: Structure definitions: linear tells events in time order; flashback opens at a later moment and jumps back to show how it happened; episodic tells the story in separate scenes or snapshots that may move between times, places or people, linked by a theme. The story and audience are invented. The chart's key is a model; accept any reasoned choice. Strong reasons connect to what the audience knows, feels or wonders and when.

## Read: Our story
text: **The issue:** the town plans to close the only public pool to save money. **The events:** (1) Maya learns to swim at the pool when she is 8. (2) Six years later, the town council announces the pool will close. (3) Maya and her friends collect stories from pool users: a lifeguard, an older swimmer, a toddler's parent. (4) Maya speaks at the council meeting, and the vote is delayed. **Our audience:** families and students at an evening show, many of whom use the pool.

## Warm up
match: Structures
  - Linear = events shown in the order they happen
  - Flashback = opens later, then jumps back to show how it happened
  - Episodic = separate snapshot scenes linked by a theme

## Main: Three ways to tell it
chart: How it would open | What the audience knows, and when | Strength for our audience | Weakness for our audience
  labels: Linear; Flashback; Episodic
  height: 1.1
  key: Model. Linear: opens with 8-year-old Maya's first swim; the audience learns each fact as it happens; easy to follow and builds Maya's love of the pool first; the issue does not arrive until late. Flashback: opens with Maya at the microphone at the council meeting, then jumps back; the audience knows the stakes from the first minute and wonders how she got there; grabs attention right away; jumps in time can confuse younger viewers. Episodic: opens with the lifeguard's story, then the older swimmer, the parent and Maya; the audience sees the pool through many people; shows that the issue affects the whole community, which fits an audience of pool users; less suspense about one main character.
write: Which structure will your group use? Give two reasons tied to the audience.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Flashback. First, opening at the council meeting tells the audience the stakes right away, so families pay attention from the start. Second, since many audience members use the pool, jumping back to Maya's first swim will remind them of their own memories just before the vote.

## Stretch
write: How could you mix two structures? Describe your opening and closing moments.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Open in flashback at the council meeting, then tell the middle episodically through the three interviews, and close back at the meeting with the vote delayed, so the snapshots become Maya's evidence.

# 21. Devising Room Log
minutes: 60
materials: pencil, a folder to keep the log in
ican: I can record the work of a devising process: what we tried, what we found, what we decided and what we still need to solve.
directions: Fill in one section at the end of each devising session. Write in short notes, not full sentences. A discovery is something you learned by trying it; a decision locked in is something the group agreed to keep.
mistakes: Students often list exercises with no discoveries ("did tableaux"). Ask "What did that show you?" Decisions and discoveries get mixed up: a discovery is what happened; a decision is the group choosing to keep it. Open questions should be real questions the group will try to answer next time.
notes: Plan about 15 minutes at the end of each of 4 sessions (60 minutes in all). Use this log alongside any devised piece. Collect it after session 2 to check that groups are locking in decisions, not restarting every day. The key shows what a strong entry looks like; logs will differ by group.

## Model entry
example: **Exercises:** 3 frozen tableaux of "waiting"; hot-seating the bus driver. **Discovery:** the scene is funnier when nobody talks for the first 20 seconds. **Locked in:** open in silence at the bus stop; Jo plays the driver. **Still open:** How does the scene end? Who gets on the bus last?

## Session 1
fill: Date: {any date}   Our goal today: {a goal, for example find our opening image}
chart: Exercises we ran | What we discovered | Decisions locked in | Questions still open
  rows: 3
  key: A strong entry names exercises specifically (tableaux, hot-seating, an improvisation on a theme), records at least one discovery made by trying something, locks in at least one choice, and ends with a real question to test next session.

## Session 2
fill: Date: {any date}   Our goal today: {a goal, for example build the middle of the scene}
chart: Exercises we ran | What we discovered | Decisions locked in | Questions still open
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary. Check that the session tried to answer at least one question left open in Session 1.

## Session 3
fill: Date: {any date}   Our goal today: {a goal, for example find the ending}
chart: Exercises we ran | What we discovered | Decisions locked in | Questions still open
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary. By now more decisions should be locked in than questions left open.

## Session 4
fill: Date: {any date}   Our goal today: {a goal, for example run the whole piece and polish transitions}
chart: Exercises we ran | What we discovered | Decisions locked in | Questions still open
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary. The piece should run start to finish; remaining questions are about polish, not structure.

## Stretch
write: Look back over all four sessions. Which discovery changed your piece the most, and how?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one specific discovery, the session it came from, and what the group changed because of it.

# 33. Classmate Perspectives Map
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can gather and compare classmates' views of a production choice and explain how experience shapes a viewpoint.
directions: Choose one production choice from a show your class watched. Ask 5 classmates what they thought of it and what in their own experience shaped their view. Write only what they choose to share. Then look for patterns.
mistakes: Students often record reactions ("liked it") without the experience behind them. Model a follow-up question: "What made you see it that way?" Watch for students pushing classmates to share private or family information; experiences such as other shows seen, music they know, a sport they play or a book they read are plenty.
notes: Use first names or initials only. Experiences to suggest as examples: other plays or films seen, being in a show, knowing the story already, a hobby, a place they have been. Nobody has to share anything personal; "I just liked the look of it" is a fine answer to record. The key shows a model map for a sample choice: a production of The Proposal played the meadow fight with the two actors circling each other like boxers while a bell rang.

## Warm up
write: The production choice I am asking about:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: In our class's production of The Proposal, the director staged the meadow fight like a boxing match, with a bell ringing between rounds.
write: My own reaction, and the experience that shaped it:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: I loved it, because I take karate and I know how fighters circle each other; it made the argument look like a real match.

## Main: The map
chart: Their reaction | Experience that shaped it | Agree with the choice? (yes, no, mixed)
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5
  height: 0.85
  key: Model: 1 thought it was hilarious; watches boxing with a cousin; yes. 2 found it too loud; prefers quiet comedy from old films; no. 3 liked the bell but not the shouting; plays in the band and noticed the sound timing; mixed. 4 did not get the joke at first; had never seen a boxing match; mixed. 5 said it showed how stubborn both characters are; has read the play; yes.
write: What patterns do you see? Which experiences led to similar views, and which led to different ones?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Classmates who knew boxing or sports laughed the most, because they recognized the joke. People who prefer quieter comedy, or who did not know boxing, were less sure. Knowing the play made one person see the choice as character, not just a gag.

## Stretch
write: If you were the director, would you keep, change or cut the choice after hearing these views? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer uses the map: for example, keep the boxing picture but lower the bell's volume, since most people liked the idea but some found the sound too loud.
