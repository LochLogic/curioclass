# 1. Parentheses Change Everything
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can evaluate expressions with parentheses, brackets and braces, and use parentheses to change the value of an expression.
directions: Solve each expression, working from the innermost grouping symbol out. Find each answer in the decoder and write its letter in the box with the same number to read the message. Then add parentheses to make each equation true.
mistakes: Students who work left to right and ignore the grouping get the decoy answers in the decoder (18 - 4 + 6 = 20 looks right but is not item 1). With brackets inside braces, some students do the outer symbol first; have them underline the innermost parentheses before starting. In Part C, students may add parentheses that do not change anything, like (4) + 6 × 2.
notes: The message is GROUP FIRST. Braces are printed as ｛ ｝ on this sheet. The decoys 32, 18, 42 and 6 come from common errors. For the last equation in Part C there is only one placement that works; let students test placements on scrap paper. Stretch: (2 + 3) × 4 × 5 = 100 is the greatest, because adding the two smallest numbers and multiplying by the two largest gives the most; (2 + 4) × 3 × 5 = 90 and 2 × 3 × 4 + 5 = 29 are smaller.

## Part C: Add the parentheses
text: Copy each equation and add one pair of parentheses to make it true.
table: Equation | With parentheses
  - 4 + 6 × 2 = 20 | {(4 + 6) × 2 = 20}
  - 24 ÷ 4 + 2 = 4 | {24 ÷ (4 + 2) = 4}
  - 30 - 10 - 5 = 25 | {30 - (10 - 5) = 25}
  - 3 × 5 + 2 × 4 = 84 | {3 × (5 + 2) × 4 = 84}

# 22. Would You Rather: Scaling Deals
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can predict whether multiplying by a fraction makes a number bigger or smaller, then check by multiplying.
directions: For each pair of offers, predict which one gives more money by asking if each fraction is more or less than 1. Then compute both and write the better deal: A, B, or = if they are the same.
mistakes: Students often assume the offer with the fraction greater than 1 always wins. In pairs 1 to 4 the offer with the fraction less than 1 wins, because the starting amount matters too: a fraction less than 1 of a big amount can beat a fraction greater than 1 of a small one. Watch for students who multiply the amount by the denominator instead of dividing.
notes: Two pairs are ties (the example and pair 5), which surprises students and makes a good discussion. Fast method: divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator (2/3 of $30: $30 ÷ 3 = $10, × 2 = $20). The prediction column is the student's own reasoning and is not marked right or wrong.

## Warm up: More or less?
example: Would you rather have 3/4 of $20 or 5/4 of $12? 3/4 is less than 1, so 3/4 of $20 is less than $20: it is $15. 5/4 is more than 1, so 5/4 of $12 is more than $12: it is $15. They are the same!
fill:
  - 3/4 × 20 is {less} than 20.
  - 4/3 × 12 is {more} than 12.
  - 5/5 × 15 is {equal to} 15.

## Main: Pick a deal

  - 2 | 5/6 of $36 | 7/6 of $24 |  | {$30} | {$28} | {A}
  - 3 | 3/2 of $10 | 3/4 of $24 |  | {$15} | {$18} | {B}
  - 4 | 1/2 of $50 | 9/8 of $16 |  | {$25} | {$18} | {A}
  - 5 | 7/5 of $20 | 4/5 of $35 |  | {$28} | {$28} | {=}
  - 6 | 3/8 of $64 | 5/3 of $15 |  | {$24} | {$25} | {B}
write: Which pair surprised you most? Explain why your prediction worked or did not work.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a pair such as 6 (3/8 of $64 is less than $64 but still $24, close to 5/3 of $15 = $25) and explains that a scale factor only tells whether the result is bigger or smaller than its own starting amount, so you must compare the starting amounts too.

## Stretch: Make a tricky pair
write: Make your own pair: Offer A uses a fraction less than 1, Offer B uses a fraction greater than 1, and Offer A is the better deal. Show both answers.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: A = 1/2 of $100 = $50, B = 5/4 of $20 = $25. A wins because its starting amount is much larger.
