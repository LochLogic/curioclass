# 1. Parentheses Change Everything
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can evaluate expressions with parentheses, brackets and braces, and use parentheses to change the value of an expression.
directions: Solve each expression, working from the innermost grouping symbol out. Find each answer in the decoder and write its letter in the box with the same number to read the message. Then add parentheses to make each equation true.
mistakes: Students who work left to right and ignore the grouping get the decoy answers in the decoder (18 - 4 + 6 = 20 looks right but is not item 1). With brackets inside braces, some students do the outer symbol first; have them underline the innermost parentheses before starting. In Part C, students may add parentheses that do not change anything, like (4) + 6 × 2.
notes: The message is GROUP FIRST. Braces are printed as ｛ ｝ on this sheet. The decoys 32, 18, 42 and 6 come from common errors. For the last equation in Part C there is only one placement that works; let students test placements on scrap paper. Stretch: (2 + 3) × 4 × 5 = 100 is the greatest, because adding the two smallest numbers and multiplying by the two largest gives the most; (2 + 4) × 3 × 5 = 90 and 2 × 3 × 4 + 5 = 29 are smaller.

example: 2 × [3 + (8 - 5)]: first (8 - 5) = 3, then [3 + 3] = 6, then 2 × 6 = 12.

## Part C: Add the parentheses
text: Copy each equation and add one pair of parentheses to make it true.

# 5. Graph the Pattern Pairs
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, three colored pencils
ican: I can make ordered pairs from two patterns, graph them and describe how the patterns are related.
directions: Finish each table by following the two rules. Write the ordered pairs (A, B). Graph each table's points on the grid in its own color, and connect them with a ruler. Then compare the three sets of points.
mistakes: Students often swap the coordinates and plot (4, 2) for (2, 4); Pattern A is always the x-coordinate (across) and Pattern B the y-coordinate (up). Some keep adding to the wrong column. On the grid, points with odd numbers (3, 9) sit on the thin lines between the numbered lines.
notes: Each table makes a straight line through (0, 0). Table 1: B is 2 times A. Table 2: B is half of A. Table 3: B is one third of A. The steeper the line, the faster B grows compared with A. The key draws table 1 solid, table 2 dashed and table 3 dotted.

## Warm up
example: Pattern A: start at 0, add 5. Pattern B: start at 0, add 10. The terms are 0, 5, 10 and 0, 10, 20, so the pairs are (0, 0), (5, 10), (10, 20).

# 16. Decimal Operations Review
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can evaluate expressions, use powers of ten, round decimals, and add, subtract, multiply and divide whole numbers and decimals.
directions: Solve each part. Show your work in the boxes for Part D.
mistakes: In expressions, watch for students who work left to right and skip the parentheses. With powers of ten, the most common slip is moving the decimal point the wrong way when dividing (52.7 ÷ 10 written as 527). When rounding 12.951 to the nearest tenth, students may write 12.10 or 13; the answer is 13.0. In 6.75 + 3.8, students who line up the last digits instead of the decimal points get 7.13.
notes: Part D: 3,486 × 27 = 94,122 (3,486 × 20 = 69,720 and 3,486 × 7 = 24,402). 2,592 ÷ 24 = 108 (check: 24 × 108 = 2,592). For 2.4 × 1.5, students can multiply 24 × 15 = 360 and place the decimal: two decimal places in all, so 3.60. Accept 2 × (8 + 7) or (8 + 7) × 2 in Part A.

# 22. Would You Rather: Scaling Deals
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can predict whether multiplying by a fraction makes a number bigger or smaller, then check by multiplying.
directions: For each pair of offers, predict which one gives more money by asking if each fraction is more or less than 1. Then compute both and write the better deal: A, B, or = if they are the same.
mistakes: Students often assume the offer with the fraction greater than 1 always wins. In pairs 1 to 4 the offer with the fraction less than 1 wins, because the starting amount matters too: a fraction less than 1 of a big amount can beat a fraction greater than 1 of a small one. Watch for students who multiply the amount by the denominator instead of dividing.
notes: Two pairs are ties (the example and pair 5), which surprises students and makes a good discussion. Fast method: divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator (2/3 of $30: $30 ÷ 3 = $10, × 2 = $20). The prediction column is the student's own reasoning and is not marked right or wrong.

## Warm up: More or less?
example: Would you rather have 3/4 of $20 or 5/4 of $12? 3/4 is less than 1, so 3/4 of $20 is less than $20: it is $15. 5/4 is more than 1, so 5/4 of $12 is more than $12: it is $15. They are the same!
fill:
  - 3/4 × 20 is {less} than 20.
  - 4/3 × 12 is {more} than 12.
  - 5/5 × 15 is {equal to} 15.

## Main: Pick a deal
table: Pair | Offer A | Offer B | My prediction | A is | B is | Better deal
  - 1 | 2/3 of $30 | 4/3 of $12 |  | {$20} | {$16} | {A}
  - 2 | 5/6 of $36 | 7/6 of $24 |  | {$30} | {$28} | {A}
  - 3 | 3/2 of $10 | 3/4 of $24 |  | {$15} | {$18} | {B}
  - 4 | 1/2 of $50 | 9/8 of $16 |  | {$25} | {$18} | {A}
  - 5 | 7/5 of $20 | 4/5 of $35 |  | {$28} | {$28} | {=}
  - 6 | 3/8 of $64 | 5/3 of $15 |  | {$24} | {$25} | {B}
write: Which pair surprised you most? Explain why your prediction worked or did not work.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a pair such as 6 (3/8 of $64 is less than $64 but still $24, close to 5/3 of $15 = $25) and explains that a scale factor only tells whether the result is bigger or smaller than its own starting amount, so you must compare the starting amounts too.

## Stretch: Make a tricky pair
write: Make your own pair: Offer A uses a fraction less than 1, Offer B uses a fraction greater than 1, and Offer A is the better deal. Show both answers.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: A = 1/2 of $100 = $50, B = 5/4 of $20 = $25. A wins because its starting amount is much larger.

# 26. Unit Fractions and Whole Numbers
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can divide a unit fraction by a whole number and a whole number by a unit fraction, and check with multiplication.
directions: For each problem, draw a model (a bar or a number line), find the quotient, and check it by multiplying. Then solve the two stories.
mistakes: Students often get the two kinds mixed up: 1/3 ÷ 4 is smaller than 1/3 (a small piece shared 4 ways), but 5 ÷ 1/2 is bigger than 5 (how many halves fit in 5). Ask "Should the answer be bigger or smaller?" before computing. Watch for 1/3 ÷ 4 = 4/3, which comes from multiplying instead.
notes: Every check uses the fact that division undoes multiplication: if 1/3 ÷ 4 = 1/12, then 1/12 × 4 = 1/3. Models: for a unit fraction ÷ whole number, shade the unit fraction of a bar and split it into equal parts; for a whole number ÷ unit fraction, draw the wholes and count the unit pieces. Story 1 is 1/2 ÷ 3 = 1/6; story 2 is 4 ÷ 1/3 = 12.

## Warm up
example: 1/3 ÷ 2: shade 1/3 of a bar and split it into 2 equal parts. Each part is 1/6 of the bar. Check: 1/6 × 2 = 2/6 = 1/3.
fill:
  - Is 1/4 ÷ 3 bigger or smaller than 1/4? {smaller}
  - Is 3 ÷ 1/4 bigger or smaller than 3? {bigger}

## Main: Draw, divide, check
table: Problem | Model | Quotient | Check

  - 1/3 ÷ 4 |  | {1/12} | {1/12 × 4 = 1/3}
  - 5 ÷ 1/2 |  | {10} | {10 × 1/2 = 5}
  - 1/2 ÷ 3 |  | {1/6} | {1/6 × 3 = 1/2}
  - 4 ÷ 1/3 |  | {12} | {12 × 1/3 = 4}
  - 1/4 ÷ 2 |  | {1/8} | {1/8 × 2 = 1/4}
  - 3 ÷ 1/5 |  | {15} | {15 × 1/5 = 3}
  - 1/5 ÷ 2 |  | {1/10} | {1/10 × 2 = 1/5}
  - 2 ÷ 1/6 |  | {12} | {12 × 1/6 = 2}
  - 1/6 ÷ 3 |  | {1/18} | {1/18 × 3 = 1/6}
  - 6 ÷ 1/4 |  | {24} | {24 × 1/4 = 6}

## Stretch: Stories
write: Three friends share 1/2 of a pan of cornbread equally. What fraction of the whole pan does each friend get?
  lines: 2
  key: 1/2 ÷ 3 = 1/6. Each friend gets 1/6 of the pan. Check: 1/6 × 3 = 1/2.
write: A scoop holds 1/3 cup of rice. How many scoops are in 4 cups of rice?
  lines: 2
  key: 4 ÷ 1/3 = 12 scoops. Check: 12 × 1/3 = 4.

# 37. Treasure Map Coordinates
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, colored pencil, ruler
ican: I can plot points on the coordinate plane and use coordinates to find a location.
directions: The dots on the map are the landmarks in the list. Follow the clues in order. Plot and label each point (A to H) and write its coordinates. Connect the points in order to see your path. The last point is the treasure: name the landmark where it is buried.
mistakes: The most common error is switching the coordinates: (5, 3) plotted 5 up and 3 across. Remind students: x first, across the bottom; y second, up the side ("walk to the ladder, then climb"). Clues G and D change one coordinate using the other point, so students must read them carefully.
notes: The path is A (1, 1), B (5, 3), C (5, 6), D (7, 6), E (7, 2), F (9, 2), G (9, 8), H (6, 8). The treasure is at Lookout Hill. Point A is the dock, the only other point on a landmark. The stretch clue back to the dock is 5 left and 7 down.
