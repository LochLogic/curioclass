# 1. Describe Before You Judge
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pen for checking; the teacher shows Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring
ican: I can describe an artwork objectively before I interpret or judge it.
directions: Sort the sample statements first. Then look closely at the painting your teacher shows and write 10 statements that describe only what anyone could see. When you finish, reread your list with a colored pen and cross out any statement that slipped into opinion or interpretation. Rewrite one.
mistakes: Feelings and guesses hide in single words: "beautiful," "sad," "mysterious," "expensive," "she is waiting." Ask: could two people argue about it? If so, it is not a description. Measurable or checkable details (colors, positions, light direction, what is in the background) are descriptions.
notes: Show Johannes Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring (about 1665, Mauritshuis, The Hague), public domain; the Mauritshuis website has a large image. It is a tronie, a Dutch study of a face or character type, not a portrait of a known person, which is a good discussion point after the description stage. Description is the first step of the familiar critique sequence (describe, analyze, interpret, judge). Do not let students look up the painting's story first.

## Warm up: Description or judgment?
table: Statement | Description or judgment?
  - The background is very dark, almost black. | {description}
  - The girl looks lonely. | {judgment}
  - She wears a blue and yellow head wrap. | {description}
  - This is the most beautiful painting Vermeer made. | {judgment}
  - Light falls on her face from one side. | {description}
  - She is about to tell a secret. | {judgment}

## Main: Ten describing statements
example: Description: "Her lips are slightly apart." Not description: "She looks surprised."
write: Write 10 statements that describe what you see. Number them.
  lines: 12
  key: Sample statements: 1. A young woman is shown from the shoulders up. 2. She turns her head to look toward the viewer over her shoulder. 3. The background is a plain, very dark area. 4. She wears a blue cloth wrapped around her head. 5. A yellow cloth hangs down from the head wrap. 6. A large, pale, teardrop-shaped pearl hangs from her ear. 7. Light comes from one side and lights her face. 8. Her lips are slightly open. 9. A white collar shows at her neck. 10. Small white highlights appear in her eyes, on her lip and on the pearl.

## Check your work
text: Number of statements I crossed out: ______
write: Rewrite one crossed-out statement (or one from the warm up) so it describes instead of judges.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: "The girl looks lonely" becomes "She is alone in the picture against an empty dark background."

## Stretch
write: Now that you have described carefully, what is one interpretation you could make? Point to two of your statements as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: The painting feels like a quick, private moment, because she turns over her shoulder (statement 2) and her lips are slightly open as if she is about to speak (statement 8).

# 5. Critique Words in Use
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Edward Hopper's Nighthawks
ican: I can use critique vocabulary accurately to describe and analyze an artwork.
directions: Look closely at Nighthawks. Fill in each blank with the best term from the word bank; use each term once. Then write sentences of your own about a second artwork, using critique terms correctly.
mistakes: Students mix up focal point and emphasis (emphasis is the principle; the focal point is the place it creates), and value and color (value is only light and dark). Palette is the set of colors an artist chooses, not the board they mix on, in a critique. Negative space is the empty area around and between subjects, not "the dark parts."
notes: Show Edward Hopper's Nighthawks (1942, Art Institute of Chicago); the museum's website has a large image. It shows an all-night diner on a city corner, seen through a long curved window: three customers and a server under harsh light, an empty street and dark shops across the way, and no visible door into the diner. Each blank has one best answer; accept "emphasis" for the focal point blank only if the sentence still reads correctly. For the second artwork, use any image the class has studied.

## Warm up: The word bank
text: **Word bank:** composition, focal point, palette, contrast, value, texture, negative space, medium, movement, balance
choice: Which term means how light or dark a color is?
  - palette
  - texture
  * value
  - medium
choice: Which term means the material an artwork is made with, such as oil paint or clay?
  - composition
  * medium
  - balance
  - contrast

## Main: Nighthawks in ten terms
fill:
  - Hopper arranged the diner, the street and the shops across the way so the diner's long glass window cuts across the canvas. This overall arrangement is the painting's {composition}.
  - The brightly lit counter with the four figures is the {focal point}: it is where the eye lands first.
  - Hopper's {palette} is mostly greens, yellows and deep reds and browns.
  - The strong {contrast} between the glowing diner and the dark street makes the light feel harsh.
  - In {value}, the painting moves from the pale yellow wall inside to the near-black shop windows outside.
  - Hopper's smooth, flat brushwork gives the painting very little visible {texture}.
  - The empty sidewalk and street around the diner are {negative space} that makes the people seem alone.
  - Nighthawks is oil on canvas; this {medium} let Hopper build smooth, even areas of color.
  - The long counter and the curved window lead the eye along the diner, creating {movement}.
  - The large, bright diner on the right is set against the darker shops on the left, giving the painting asymmetrical {balance}.

## Your turn
text: Second artwork (title and artist): ________________________________________
write: Write three sentences about the second artwork. Use a different critique term in each and underline it.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Each sentence should use a term accurately with evidence, for example: "The red umbrella is the focal point because it is the only warm color." "The thick, swirled paint gives the sky a rough texture." "The tiny figure surrounded by negative space looks lonely."

## Stretch
write: Use at least three of the terms to explain the mood of Nighthawks in two or three sentences.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: The harsh contrast between the bright diner and the dark street, plus the wide negative space of the empty sidewalk, make the people seem isolated. The cool green and yellow palette feels artificial, like late-night light, so the mood is lonely and quiet.

# 15. Thirds Thumbnails
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler, eraser
ican: I can compose a two-point perspective scene on a rule of thirds grid.
directions: Read the example. In each box, rule a thirds grid lightly (two lines each way, dividing the box into 9 equal parts). Then draw four different thumbnails of a street corner in two-point perspective, each placing the horizon and the focal point in a different spot on the grid. Label each choice.
mistakes: Students often put the nearest building corner and the horizon dead center in every thumbnail. Each thumbnail should try a different arrangement: horizon on the top third line, then the bottom; corner on the left line, then the right. Watch the vanishing points too: in two-point perspective both sit on the horizon, usually far apart (often off the thumbnail), and verticals stay vertical.
notes: The rule of thirds is a guideline, not a law; strong compositions break it on purpose. A low horizon (bottom third) makes buildings tower and feels dramatic; a high horizon (top third) shows more street and ground and can feel like looking down. Thumbnails are small and quick: two or three minutes each. Students can tape a strip of scrap paper on each side to place vanishing points beyond the box.

## Warm up
example: The **rule of thirds**: divide the frame into a 3 by 3 grid. Placing the horizon on one of the lines, and the focal point where two lines cross, usually makes a livelier composition than placing them in the exact center. In **two-point perspective**, a street corner is seen corner first: its two sides recede to two vanishing points on the horizon.
choice: Where does the rule of thirds suggest placing the focal point?
  - in the exact center of the frame
  * where two of the grid lines cross
  - in a corner touching the edge
  - anywhere outside the grid
choice: In two-point perspective, where do both vanishing points sit?
  * on the horizon line
  - one at the top and one at the bottom
  - at the nearest corner of the building
  - in the center of the frame
choice: You place the horizon on the bottom third line. How will the buildings likely feel?
  - flat and far away
  - exactly the same as with a centered horizon
  - like we are looking down from a plane
  * tall and towering, since we look up at them

## Choose
write: Which thumbnail is strongest? Explain using the grid, the horizon and the focal point.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: Thumbnail 1, because the low horizon on the bottom line makes the corner building tower, and the focal point (a lit cafe window) sits on the lower right crossing, so the eye goes there and then up the building.

## Stretch
write: Describe a street scene where breaking the rule of thirds (a dead-center horizon or focal point) would be the better choice. Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A centered, symmetrical view down a street, such as a long avenue with a monument at the end, can feel formal, still or powerful; centering suits calm, order or confrontation.

# 21. What Is a Portfolio For?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain the purposes of working, showcase and process portfolios and choose the right one for a task.
directions: Read the passage and highlight the purpose of each kind of portfolio. Then decide which kind of portfolio fits each of the 8 examples and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think a portfolio is only a collection of finished best work. Point out that a working portfolio and a process portfolio are just as real and are where most growth happens. Process and working portfolios overlap: the key difference is that a process portfolio is curated to tell the story of how one idea developed.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 440 words). If students keep portfolios in your class, have them label each item W, S or P at the end of the sheet.

## Read
passage: Three Portfolios, Three Jobs
  Ask an artist to see their portfolio and you might get three very different things. A portfolio is a collection of an artist's work, but the collection changes depending on its job. Most art teachers and working artists use three kinds: the working portfolio, the showcase portfolio and the process portfolio.
  A working portfolio is the big, messy folder or box where everything goes. It holds finished pieces, abandoned starts, sketches, color tests, scraps of reference and notes. Nothing is thrown away just because it failed. The working portfolio is the artist's storage and memory. Its audience is mostly the artist and maybe a teacher, and its purpose is to keep everything in one place so that the artist can look back, reuse ideas and notice patterns. When an artist flips through a working portfolio and sees that the same subject keeps returning, that discovery can become the start of a new series.
  A showcase portfolio is the opposite: small, selective and polished. It contains only the strongest finished works, chosen for a particular audience such as a gallery, a scholarship committee, a school or a client. Every piece earns its place, and weaker pieces stay out even if they took a long time. Presentation matters: clean photographs, consistent labels with title, medium, size and year, and a sensible order. A showcase portfolio answers the question, "What can this artist do at their best?"
  A process portfolio tells the story of how work gets made. It follows one project or idea from first spark to final piece: the brainstorm, the thumbnail sketches, the experiments that did not work, the feedback received and the changes that followed. Written reflections connect the steps. Teachers often ask for process portfolios because they show thinking, risk taking and growth, which a single finished piece can hide. Many art programs grade the process as seriously as the product.
  The three kinds feed each other. The working portfolio is the raw material. From it, an artist pulls the pages that explain a project's development into a process portfolio, and pulls the best results into a showcase portfolio. A strong artist keeps all three in mind: saving everything, documenting how ideas grow and selecting with care.
  Today many portfolios are digital, but the same three jobs apply. A folder of phone photos of every sketch is a working portfolio. A slide sequence of eight finished paintings with clean labels is a showcase. A blog or journal tracing one sculpture from idea to installation is a process portfolio. Before you collect anything, ask what job the portfolio has to do and who will see it.

## Sort: Which portfolio?
table: Example | Working, showcase or process?
  - A box of every sketch, test and unfinished drawing from this semester | {working}
  - Ten polished pieces with labels, sent with a scholarship application | {showcase}
  - Thumbnails, two failed versions, peer feedback and the final mural design, with reflections | {process}
  - A phone album of every page from your sketchbook | {working}
  - Six framed paintings chosen for a gallery show | {showcase}
  - A journal tracing one sculpture from idea to installation | {process}
  - A website of your best illustrations, made for clients | {showcase}
  - Color tests and torn magazine reference images in a folder | {working}

## Questions
choice: Which portfolio is mainly for the artist's own storage and memory?
  * working
  - showcase
  - process
write: Why might a teacher grade a process portfolio as seriously as the final product?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows thinking, experiments, risk taking, use of feedback and growth, which a single finished piece can hide.
write: Explain how a working portfolio can become the source for the other two.
  lines: 3
  key: The working portfolio holds all the raw material; the artist pulls the pages that show how a project developed into a process portfolio, and pulls the strongest finished results into a showcase portfolio.

## Stretch
write: You are applying to a summer art program that asks for "8 works that show who you are as an artist." Which portfolio do you pull from, and how do you choose?
  lines: 3
  key: Pull from the working portfolio (and any process work) to build a showcase: choose the strongest finished pieces that also show range and a personal voice, cut weaker or repetitive pieces, label them consistently and order them with care.

# 25. Portfolio Self-Assessment
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, your portfolio (finished work, sketches and process pages)
ican: I can assess my portfolio against clear criteria and support each score with evidence.
directions: Sort the warm up statements. Then spread out your portfolio. Score it from 1 to 4 on each of the 5 criteria, using the descriptions, and write specific evidence for every score: name the piece and point to what in it proves the score. Finish with goals.
mistakes: Students often give scores with evidence like "I worked hard" or "I like it." Evidence names a piece and something visible in it. Watch for all-4 or all-1 scores; most honest portfolios have a strongest and a weakest area, and the goal should come from the weakest.
notes: The five criteria match common high school art rubrics: craft, range, ideas, growth and presentation. If you use your own rubric, swap the criteria before copying. Conference with a few students about the gap between their score and yours; a large gap is the most useful conversation. The sheet asks only about artwork, nothing personal.

## Warm up: Evidence or not?
table: Statement | Evidence or not evidence?
  - "In Still Life with Bottles, the reflections on the glass show five values." | {evidence}
  - "I spent a lot of time on my portfolio." | {not evidence}
  - "My sketchbook shows three thumbnails before the final linocut." | {evidence}
  - "I think my art is really good." | {not evidence}
  - "My first and last self-portraits show stronger proportions in the eyes and nose." | {evidence}
  - "Everyone liked my mural." | {not evidence}

## Main: Score my portfolio
table: Criterion | What a 4 looks like
  - Craft | Skilled, careful use of each medium; clean edges, controlled values, finished surfaces.
  - Range | Several media, subjects and approaches, chosen with purpose.
  - Ideas | Original ideas and personal meaning; takes creative risks.
  - Growth | Clear improvement over time, shown in process work and revisions.
  - Presentation | Work is labeled, in a sensible order, clean and well chosen.
chart: Score (1-4) | Evidence: which piece, and what in it?
  labels: Craft; Range; Ideas; Growth; Presentation
  height: 0.85
  key: Model row: Growth, 3. My September and December portraits show the eyes moving down to the middle of the head and much smoother shading, but my sketchbook has few revision pages.

## Goals
write: My strongest area and why:
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary; it should match the highest score and cite a piece.
write: My weakest area, and one specific step I will take in the next project to raise it:
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong goal is specific and doable, for example: "Presentation: I will photograph every finished piece in daylight and add title, medium, size and year labels."

## Stretch
write: Ask a classmate to score one criterion for your portfolio. Where did your scores differ, and what might explain it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name the criterion, both scores and a reason, such as knowing the effort behind a piece that the viewer cannot see.

# 35. Issue Meets Tradition
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, notes or images about the tradition you are studying
ican: I can investigate what an artistic tradition offers an issue I care about: its materials, meanings and audiences.
directions: Read the example about El Anatsui. Then choose an issue you care about and an artistic tradition you have studied (it does not need to be your own family's). Fill in the chart to show what the tradition offers your issue, and plan how you will honor the tradition respectfully.
mistakes: Students often borrow only the look of a tradition (a pattern, a color) without its meanings or its people. The chart asks for meanings and audiences as well as materials. Watch also for traditions treated as costumes or jokes; students should learn from sources inside the tradition, credit them and avoid sacred objects or symbols that are not theirs to use.
notes: El Anatsui (born 1944 in Ghana, working in Nigeria for decades) makes huge, flexible wall hangings from thousands of flattened aluminum bottle caps and seals joined with copper wire. Their look recalls kente, a woven cloth of Ghana; the caps from discarded liquor bottles point to consumption, waste and the long history of trade between Africa and Europe. It is a strong model of an issue meeting a tradition. Students may choose any tradition they have studied: quilting, weaving, mural painting, calligraphy, pottery, papel picado, beadwork and so on. Nothing on the sheet should require personal or family information.

## Warm up: A model
text: The Ghanaian artist **El Anatsui** joins thousands of flattened metal bottle caps with copper wire to make shimmering hangings as large as a wall. They look like **kente**, a famous woven cloth from Ghana. The caps are trash from liquor bottles, so the works also speak about waste, consumption and the long history of trade between Africa and Europe.
choice: What does the kente tradition give El Anatsui's work?
  - a way to make art faster
  * a familiar look and the meaning of cloth as a sign of identity and pride
  - a ban on using metal
  - a single fixed audience
choice: Why does it matter that the caps are used, discarded material?
  - It makes the work cheaper to ship.
  - Used caps are easier to paint.
  * The material itself carries the issue of waste and trade.
  - Viewers cannot tell what they are.

## Main: My chart
text: My issue: ____________________________ My tradition: ____________________________
chart: What the tradition offers | How it could speak to my issue
  labels: Materials; Techniques; Meanings and symbols; Audiences (who makes it, who sees it); Respect check (who I will learn from, how I will credit)
  height: 0.85
  key: Model (issue: food waste; tradition: quilting): Materials: scraps of fabric saved from old clothes, so reuse is built in. Techniques: piecing many small parts into one whole. Meanings: comfort, memory, family and community stories. Audiences: quilting groups, families, community exhibitions. How it speaks: a quilt pieced from scraps printed with images of wasted food could show that small saved pieces add up. Respect check: learn from a local quilting guild and published quilters, credit the patterns used.

## Reflect
write: Which part of the chart surprised you most? How might it change your artwork?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: I expected materials to matter most, but the audience surprised me: quilts are shared at community shows, so my piece about food waste could be shown where neighbors gather instead of in a gallery.

## Stretch
write: What is the difference between learning from a tradition and copying it? Give one action that keeps your project on the learning side.
  lines: 3
  key: Learning means understanding the materials, meanings and people behind a tradition and making something new that credits them; copying takes its look without understanding or permission. Actions: research sources from inside the tradition, talk with a practitioner, credit them, and avoid sacred symbols.
