# 1. Follow a Protein Out
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can describe how organelles work together to make and secrete a protein.
directions: Draw a large animal cell and label the 12 organelles in the word bank, using the functions to help you. Then trace a protein (such as insulin, made in pancreas cells) from the ribosome to outside the cell with numbered arrows.
mistakes: Students often send the protein from the ribosome straight to the cell membrane, skipping the ER and Golgi, or put ribosomes inside the nucleus as the place proteins are made. Free ribosomes make proteins that stay in the cytoplasm; secreted proteins are made by ribosomes on the rough ER. Watch for cell walls and chloroplasts drawn in an animal cell.
notes: The drawing is the diagram: there is no printed cell, so students build it from the word bank. A strong drawing has the nucleus with a nucleolus and a double nuclear envelope with pores; rough ER next to the nucleus, studded with ribosomes; the Golgi as a stack of flattened sacs; vesicles moving between them and to the membrane. Insulin is a real secreted protein: it is made on the rough ER, processed through the Golgi, stored in vesicles and released by exocytosis.

## Warm up: Word bank
example: nucleus, nucleolus, nuclear envelope, ribosome, rough ER, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, vesicle, cell membrane, mitochondrion, lysosome, centriole
match: Match each organelle to its job.
  - nucleus = holds the DNA that codes for the protein
  - nucleolus = makes the parts of ribosomes
  - nuclear envelope = double membrane with pores that let mRNA out
  - ribosome = reads mRNA and links amino acids into a protein
  - rough ER = membranes studded with ribosomes, where secreted proteins are made and folded
  - smooth ER = makes lipids and breaks down some toxins
  - Golgi apparatus = modifies, sorts and packages proteins
  - vesicle = small membrane bubble that carries materials
  - cell membrane = controls what enters and leaves; fuses with vesicles
  - mitochondrion = releases energy from food as ATP
  - lysosome = breaks down worn-out parts and wastes with enzymes
  - centriole = helps organize the spindle when the cell divides

## Main: Draw and trace
draw: Draw an animal cell. Label all 12 organelles. Then draw numbered arrows (1, 2, 3...) to show the path of the protein from ribosome to outside the cell. | 3.2
fill:
  - Step 1: The gene's code is copied into {mRNA}, which leaves the nucleus through a pore in the {nuclear envelope}.
  - Step 2: A {ribosome} on the rough ER reads the mRNA and builds the protein.
  - Step 3: The new protein moves into the {rough ER}, where it folds.
  - Step 4: A transport {vesicle} carries it to the {Golgi apparatus}.
  - Step 5: There it is modified, sorted and packed into a new vesicle.
  - Step 6: The vesicle fuses with the {cell membrane} and releases the protein outside the cell. This is called {exocytosis}.

## Explain
choice: Which organelle supplies most of the ATP energy for building and moving the protein?
  - lysosome
  - Golgi apparatus
  * mitochondrion
  - centriole
write: Pancreas cells that make digestive enzymes have huge amounts of rough ER and Golgi. Explain why, using structure and function.
  lines: 2
  key: These cells make and export large amounts of protein. Rough ER is where secreted proteins are made and folded, and the Golgi packages them for export, so a cell whose job is secretion needs a lot of both.

## Stretch
write: A mutation stops vesicles from fusing with the cell membrane. Predict what happens to the protein and to the cell.
  lines: 2
  key: The protein is made and packaged but cannot be released. Vesicles full of protein pile up inside the cell, and the body does not get the protein (for insulin, blood sugar would not be controlled).

# 24. Secret Protein Message
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can transcribe DNA into mRNA and translate mRNA into a protein using a codon chart.
directions: Each DNA strand below is a template strand, written from its 3' end to its 5' end. Transcribe it into mRNA, then translate the mRNA codon by codon with the chart. Each protein starts with AUG (methionine, M): do not use that M in the message. Stop when you reach a stop codon. The one-letter codes of the other amino acids spell a hidden message.
mistakes: Students often write T in mRNA; RNA uses U in place of T, so template A pairs with mRNA U. Another slip is translating the DNA instead of the mRNA, or reading the stop codon as a letter. Watch for students who lose the reading frame: count off three bases at a time from the start codon.
notes: The message reads LIFE IS WRITTEN IN DNA. The one-letter codes are the standard ones (L leucine, I isoleucine, F phenylalanine, E glutamate, S serine, W tryptophan, R arginine, T threonine, N asparagine, D aspartate, A alanine). Real genes are much longer, and in real cells methionine is often trimmed off after translation, which fits the rule of skipping it here.

## Warm up
example: Template DNA 3' TAC GAA ACT 5' is transcribed to mRNA 5' AUG CUU UGA 3'. Translate: AUG = Met (start), CUU = Leu (L), UGA = Stop. The protein is Met-Leu, and its message letter is L.
fill:
  - DNA template base A pairs with mRNA base {U}.
  - DNA template base T pairs with mRNA base {A}.
  - DNA template base C pairs with mRNA base {G}.

## Stretch
write: In gene 1, the codon CUU changes to CUC by a mutation. What happens to the protein? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Nothing changes: CUC also codes for leucine (L). The code is redundant, so this silent mutation does not change the protein.
write: In gene 4, the codon AAC changes to UAA. What happens to the protein?
  lines: 2
  key: UAA is a stop codon, so translation stops early: the protein is just Met-Ile and loses its last amino acid (the N). This is a nonsense mutation.
