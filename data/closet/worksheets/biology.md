# 1. Follow a Protein Out
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can describe how organelles work together to make and secrete a protein.
directions: Draw a large animal cell and label the 12 organelles in the word bank, using the functions to help you. Then trace a protein (such as insulin, made in pancreas cells) from the ribosome to outside the cell with numbered arrows.
mistakes: Students often send the protein from the ribosome straight to the cell membrane, skipping the ER and Golgi, or put ribosomes inside the nucleus as the place proteins are made. Free ribosomes make proteins that stay in the cytoplasm; secreted proteins are made by ribosomes on the rough ER. Watch for cell walls and chloroplasts drawn in an animal cell.
notes: The drawing is the diagram: there is no printed cell, so students build it from the word bank. A strong drawing has the nucleus with a nucleolus and a double nuclear envelope with pores; rough ER next to the nucleus, studded with ribosomes; the Golgi as a stack of flattened sacs; vesicles moving between them and to the membrane. Insulin is a real secreted protein: it is made on the rough ER, processed through the Golgi, stored in vesicles and released by exocytosis.

## Warm up: Word bank
example: nucleus, nucleolus, nuclear envelope, ribosome, rough ER, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, vesicle, cell membrane, mitochondrion, lysosome, centriole
match: Match each organelle to its job.
  - nucleus = holds the DNA that codes for the protein
  - nucleolus = makes the parts of ribosomes
  - nuclear envelope = double membrane with pores that let mRNA out
  - ribosome = reads mRNA and links amino acids into a protein
  - rough ER = membranes studded with ribosomes, where secreted proteins are made and folded
  - smooth ER = makes lipids and breaks down some toxins
  - Golgi apparatus = modifies, sorts and packages proteins
  - vesicle = small membrane bubble that carries materials
  - cell membrane = controls what enters and leaves; fuses with vesicles
  - mitochondrion = releases energy from food as ATP
  - lysosome = breaks down worn-out parts and wastes with enzymes
  - centriole = helps organize the spindle when the cell divides

## Main: Draw and trace
draw: Draw an animal cell. Label all 12 organelles. Then draw numbered arrows (1, 2, 3...) to show the path of the protein from ribosome to outside the cell. | 3.2
fill:
  - Step 1: The gene's code is copied into {mRNA}, which leaves the nucleus through a pore in the {nuclear envelope}.
  - Step 2: A {ribosome} on the rough ER reads the mRNA and builds the protein.
  - Step 3: The new protein moves into the {rough ER}, where it folds.
  - Step 4: A transport {vesicle} carries it to the {Golgi apparatus}.
  - Step 5: There it is modified, sorted and packed into a new vesicle.
  - Step 6: The vesicle fuses with the {cell membrane} and releases the protein outside the cell. This is called {exocytosis}.

## Explain
choice: Which organelle supplies most of the ATP energy for building and moving the protein?
  - lysosome
  - Golgi apparatus
  * mitochondrion
  - centriole
write: Pancreas cells that make digestive enzymes have huge amounts of rough ER and Golgi. Explain why, using structure and function.
  lines: 2
  key: These cells make and export large amounts of protein. Rough ER is where secreted proteins are made and folded, and the Golgi packages them for export, so a cell whose job is secretion needs a lot of both.

## Stretch
write: A mutation stops vesicles from fusing with the cell membrane. Predict what happens to the protein and to the cell.
  lines: 2
  key: The protein is made and packaged but cannot be released. Vesicles full of protein pile up inside the cell, and the body does not get the protein (for insulin, blood sugar would not be controlled).

# 6. Feedback Loop Diagrams
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can diagram negative feedback loops that keep body temperature and blood glucose steady, labeling the stimulus, sensor, control center, effector and response.
directions: Study the example loop. Then complete the table for four situations and draw two of the loops as circles of arrows. Use the character Sam, a runner, for the temperature loops.
mistakes: Students often name the hypothalamus as the effector; it is the control center that decides, and the sweat glands, muscles or blood vessels carry out the response. In the glucose loop, the pancreas islet cells act as both sensor and control center; accept "pancreas" for both. Watch for students who write that insulin "turns into" glycogen: insulin is the signal, and liver and muscle cells do the storing. Some students call any loop "positive" because the result is good; positive feedback means the response makes the change bigger.
notes: Normal core body temperature is about 37 °C; normal fasting blood glucose is roughly 70 to 100 mg/dL. In type 1 diabetes the beta cells no longer make insulin, so the high-glucose loop is broken and insulin must be supplied; this is a good extension, kept in the third person. Positive feedback examples for the stretch: during childbirth, pressure on the cervix triggers oxytocin release, which strengthens contractions, which raise the pressure further until the baby is born; blood clotting, where platelets at a cut release chemicals that attract more platelets.

## Example: a house thermostat
example: Stimulus: the room cools below 20 °C. Sensor: the thermometer in the thermostat. Control center: the thermostat compares 18 °C with its set point and switches on the furnace. Effector: the furnace. Response: the room warms back to 20 °C, so the thermostat switches the furnace off. The response undoes the change: **negative feedback**.

## Main: Four loops
table: Part of the loop | Sam is too hot | Sam is too cold | Blood glucose too high | Blood glucose too low
  - Stimulus | {core temperature rises above 37 °C while running} | {core temperature falls below 37 °C} | {glucose rises after a meal} | {glucose falls between meals or during exercise}
  - Sensor | {temperature receptors in the skin and brain} | {temperature receptors in the skin and brain} | {beta cells in the pancreas} | {alpha cells in the pancreas}
  - Control center | {hypothalamus} | {hypothalamus} | {pancreas (beta cells) release insulin} | {pancreas (alpha cells) release glucagon}
  - Effector | {sweat glands; skin blood vessels widen} | {skeletal muscles shiver; skin blood vessels narrow} | {liver, muscle and fat cells} | {liver}
  - Response | {sweat evaporates and skin loses heat; temperature falls} | {heat is made and kept in; temperature rises} | {cells take in glucose and the liver stores it as glycogen; glucose falls} | {liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose; glucose rises}
  height: 0.7
draw: Draw the "Sam is too hot" loop as a circle of 5 labeled boxes and arrows | 2.4
draw: Draw the "blood glucose too high" loop the same way | 2.4

## Think
choice: After Sam drinks a sugary sports drink, insulin levels rise. What happens to insulin levels once blood glucose returns to normal?
  - They keep rising.
  * They fall, because the stimulus is gone.
  - They stay high forever.
  - They turn into glucagon.
write: Why are these called **negative** feedback loops?
  lines: 2
  key: The response works against (reverses) the change that started it: high temperature leads to cooling, low glucose leads to more glucose. This brings the body back toward its set point.

## Stretch
write: In a positive feedback loop, the response makes the change bigger. Describe one example in the body, and explain why it must have an end point.
  lines: 3
  key: Childbirth: stretching of the cervix triggers oxytocin, which makes contractions stronger, which stretches the cervix more, until the baby is born and the loop stops. (Or blood clotting: platelets attract more platelets until the cut is sealed.) Without an end point, the change would grow out of control.

# 17. Chloroplast to Mitochondrion
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional)
ican: I can connect photosynthesis and cellular respiration, showing the inputs, outputs and where ATP is made in each organelle.
directions: Draw the chloroplast and the mitochondrion side by side and link them with arrows. Then fill in the table of stages and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often say plants do photosynthesis "instead of" respiration; plant cells have both organelles and respire all the time. Watch for "the mitochondrion makes energy": it transfers energy from glucose into ATP. Students also think the chloroplast's ATP powers the rest of the cell; nearly all of it is spent in the Calvin cycle to build sugar.
notes: The equations are opposites in their overall inputs and outputs, but not in their steps, so avoid saying respiration is "photosynthesis backwards." Modern estimates give about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose from aerobic respiration (older books say 36 to 38): 2 from glycolysis, 2 from the Krebs cycle and the rest from the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. Accept any total in that range.

## Warm up
fill:
  - Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6{H2O} + light energy makes C6H12O6 + 6{O2}
  - Cellular respiration: C6H12O6 + 6{O2} makes 6{CO2} + 6H2O + energy stored in {ATP}

## Main: The diagram
text: In the box, draw a chloroplast on the left (outer membranes, stacks of thylakoids called grana, the fluid stroma) and a mitochondrion on the right (outer membrane, folded inner membrane called cristae, the fluid matrix). Draw one arrow from the chloroplast to the mitochondrion and one arrow back. Label each arrow with the substances it carries. Draw a lightning bolt where light enters and a star at each place ATP is made.
draw: Chloroplast and mitochondrion | 3.0
fill:
  - Arrow from chloroplast to mitochondrion carries {glucose} and {oxygen}.
  - Arrow from mitochondrion back to chloroplast carries {carbon dioxide} and {water}.
table: Stage | Organelle and exact place | Inputs | Outputs
  - Light reactions | {chloroplast: thylakoid membranes} | {light, water, ADP, NADP+} | {oxygen, ATP, NADPH}
  - Calvin cycle | {chloroplast: stroma} | {carbon dioxide, ATP, NADPH} | {sugar (G3P, then glucose), ADP, NADP+}
  - Glycolysis | {cytoplasm (not in an organelle)} | {glucose, 2 ATP} | {2 pyruvate, 2 net ATP, NADH}
  - Krebs cycle | {mitochondrion: matrix} | {pyruvate (as acetyl-CoA)} | {carbon dioxide, 2 ATP, NADH, FADH2}
  - Electron transport chain | {mitochondrion: inner membrane (cristae)} | {NADH, FADH2, oxygen} | {water, about 26 to 28 ATP}
  height: 0.5

## Think
choice: Where is most of a cell's ATP made during cellular respiration?
  - the cytoplasm, in glycolysis
  - the matrix, in the Krebs cycle
  - the stroma, in the Calvin cycle
  * the inner membrane of the mitochondrion, by the electron transport chain and ATP synthase
write: A leaf is kept in the dark for two days. Which organelle keeps working, and where does its glucose come from?
  lines: 2
  key: The mitochondria keep working (respiration never stops). With no light the chloroplasts cannot make new sugar, so the cells use glucose from stored starch made earlier.

## Stretch
write: Both organelles have folded inner membranes (thylakoids and cristae) and both make ATP with ATP synthase. Why does folding a membrane help?
  lines: 2
  key: Folding packs much more membrane surface into a small space, so there is room for many more electron transport chains and ATP synthase enzymes, and more ATP can be made.

# 24. Secret Protein Message
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can transcribe DNA into mRNA and translate mRNA into a protein using a codon chart.
directions: Each DNA strand below is a template strand, written from its 3' end to its 5' end. Transcribe it into mRNA, then translate the mRNA codon by codon with the chart. Each protein starts with AUG (methionine, M): do not use that M in the message. Stop when you reach a stop codon. The one-letter codes of the other amino acids spell a hidden message.
mistakes: Students often write T in mRNA; RNA uses U in place of T, so template A pairs with mRNA U. Another slip is translating the DNA instead of the mRNA, or reading the stop codon as a letter. Watch for students who lose the reading frame: count off three bases at a time from the start codon.
notes: The message reads LIFE IS WRITTEN IN DNA. The one-letter codes are the standard ones (L leucine, I isoleucine, F phenylalanine, E glutamate, S serine, W tryptophan, R arginine, T threonine, N asparagine, D aspartate, A alanine). Real genes are much longer, and in real cells methionine is often trimmed off after translation, which fits the rule of skipping it here.

## Warm up
example: Template DNA 3' TAC GAA ACT 5' is transcribed to mRNA 5' AUG CUU UGA 3'. Translate: AUG = Met (start), CUU = Leu (L), UGA = Stop. The protein is Met-Leu, and its message letter is L.
fill:
  - DNA template base A pairs with mRNA base {U}.
  - DNA template base T pairs with mRNA base {A}.
  - DNA template base C pairs with mRNA base {G}.

## Stretch
write: In gene 1, the codon CUU changes to CUC by a mutation. What happens to the protein? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Nothing changes: CUC also codes for leucine (L). The code is redundant, so this silent mutation does not change the protein.
write: In gene 4, the codon AAC changes to UAA. What happens to the protein?
  lines: 2

# 29. Pedigree Puzzles
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can analyze a pedigree to decide whether a trait is dominant or recessive, and autosomal or sex-linked.
directions: Each pedigree shows a made-up family. Squares [ ] are males and circles ( ) are females. A filled shape, [#] or (#), has the trait. A horizontal line joins parents; their children hang below. Use the tests in the box to decide each pattern.
mistakes: Students often decide "recessive" just because few people have the trait; count the parents of affected children instead. The strongest clues are two unaffected parents with an affected child (recessive) and two affected parents with an unaffected child (dominant). Watch for students who call a trait X-linked because more males happen to be affected; check the rules in the box. Students also forget that people who marry into the family (like 6 in pedigree D) can be carriers.
notes: All families and traits are made up. A: autosomal recessive (unaffected parents 1 and 2 have an affected daughter, 4; an X-linked recessive daughter would need an affected father). B: autosomal dominant (affected parents 5 and 6 have an unaffected son, 7; affected father 1 passes it to son 3, which rules out X-linked). C: X-linked recessive is the best fit (only males affected; it passes from affected grandfather 1 through his unaffected daughter 4 to grandson 6). Autosomal recessive is possible only if 5 also happens to be a carrier, so accept that with that reasoning. D: autosomal recessive (unaffected 5 and 6 have affected daughter 7; affected mother 2 has unaffected sons, and affected daughter 7 has an unaffected father, both impossible for X-linked recessive).

## The tests
example: Two parents **without** the trait have a child **with** it: the trait is **recessive**.
  Two parents **with** the trait have a child **without** it: the trait is **dominant**.
  X-linked recessive: mostly males; an affected mother has only affected sons; an affected daughter has an affected father.
  A father passes his X only to daughters, so father-to-son passing rules out X-linked.

text: Use A for a dominant allele and a for a recessive one. For X-linked traits, use X^H and X^h.

  - Pedigree C: person 4 is {X^H X^h}, a carrier. Chance her next son has the trait: {1/2}
  - Pedigree D: person 5 is {Aa} because his mother 2 is {aa}.

## Stretch
write: In pedigree A, person 3 does not have the trait. What is the chance he is a carrier? Explain why the answer is not 1/2.
  lines: 3
  key: 2/3. A cross of Aa x Aa gives AA, Aa, Aa and aa in equal chances. Person 3 does not have the trait, so he cannot be aa. Of the three remaining equal outcomes, two are Aa, so the chance is 2/3.

# 40. Energy and Biomass Pyramids
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator, ruler
ican: I can calculate how much energy and biomass passes from one trophic level to the next and build pyramids from real ecosystem data.
directions: Use the measurements from two real freshwater ecosystems. Calculate what percent of each level's energy or biomass reaches the next level, then draw the pyramids and explain them.
mistakes: Students often divide the wrong way (lower level by higher level) and get percents over 100. Percent passed on = (higher level / lower level) x 100. Watch for students who expect exactly 10 percent every time; the 10 percent rule is a rough average, and real values here run from about 5 to 21 percent. Students also confuse energy (a rate, per year) with biomass (the amount of living material present at one time); only energy pyramids can never be upside down.
notes: Cedar Bog Lake, Minnesota: Raymond Lindeman's 1942 study, the first measured energy pyramid; gross production in calories per square centimeter per year, with sunlight about 118,872. Silver Springs, Florida: a spring-fed river studied by Howard Odum (1957); energy in kilocalories per square meter per year and standing biomass in grams of dry mass per square meter. Values are as usually quoted from those studies. The stretch uses the classic English Channel plankton figures (about 4 g/m² of phytoplankton supporting about 21 g/m² of animals), as reported in ecology texts.

## Warm up
fill:
  - Energy that reaches the next level = (higher level / lower level) x 100. If grass has 1,000 units and rabbits get 100, {10} percent passed on.
  - Most of the energy that is not passed on is lost as {heat} from cell respiration.

## Stretch
write: In the English Channel, about 4 g/m² of tiny algae (phytoplankton) support about 21 g/m² of animal plankton, so the biomass pyramid is upside down. How is this possible when the energy pyramid can never be?
  lines: 3
  key: The algae reproduce very fast and are eaten almost as soon as they grow, so at any moment there is little algae biomass, but over a year they produce far more energy than the animals use. Biomass is a snapshot; energy is a flow over time.
