## Read
passage: Expressionism: The Inside Made Visible
  Realism tried to show the world as a camera would. In the years around the First World War, a group of artists, mostly in Germany, decided that a camera missed what mattered most. They wanted to show the world as a person feels it: twisted by fear, longing or rage. This movement, which spread across painting, poetry, film and theater, was called expressionism.
  The Swedish playwright August Strindberg had pointed the way. In A Dream Play (1901), he imitated the shape of a dream, in which places melt into each other and time does not behave. In Germany from about 1910 to the mid-1920s, young playwrights such as Georg Kaiser and Ernst Toller took up the idea. Their plays were often written as a series of short scenes, sometimes called "stations," following one central figure on a journey through a hostile world. In Kaiser's From Morning to Midnight, a bank cashier steals money and spends a single frantic day looking for something that will make life feel real.
  The central character was usually not a full, detailed person but a type with a label instead of a name: the Cashier, the Son, the Woman, the Man. Other characters often appeared only as the hero saw them, flattened into masks or puppets. The audience was meant to see from inside one mind rather than look at many characters from outside.
  Set designers turned feelings into architecture. Walls leaned, doorways tilted, and windows were jagged slashes. Streets stretched in steep, false perspective, so that a city seemed to swallow the people walking in it. Furniture might be too tall or too small. The German director Leopold Jessner staged plays on great flights of steps, so that a character's rise to power or fall to ruin became a climb or a descent that the audience could see. Expressionist designers did not want the audience to believe they were in a real room; they wanted them to feel what the character felt in that room.
  Light did the same work. Instead of soft, even light that imitated daylight, expressionist productions used harsh beams and deep shadow. A single spotlight could cut a frightened figure out of the darkness, isolating him. Shadows were thrown huge on walls, and color could change suddenly with a character's emotion. The famous 1920 German film The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari painted its shadows and streaks of light directly onto the scenery, and its crooked town became a picture of a disturbed mind.
  Speech broke apart too. Actors might deliver lines in short, clipped bursts, sometimes a single word, a style later critics called telegraphic. At moments of crisis characters cried out rather than spoke, and expressionist drama was sometimes nicknamed "scream drama." Movement was often stylized: groups moved like machines, and workers marched in rhythm, showing how a factory or a crowd could crush the individual.
  The movement spread beyond Germany. In the United States, Eugene O'Neill's The Hairy Ape (1922) and Elmer Rice's The Adding Machine (1923) used expressionist scenes to show workers trapped by machines and cities. In Rice's play, an accountant named Mr. Zero loses his job to an adding machine.
  Expressionism as a movement faded by the late 1920s, but its tools never left the theater. Whenever a designer tilts a wall to show a character's panic, or a spotlight isolates someone alone on a dark stage, the inside is once again being made visible.

# 21. Light From Three Sides
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencils (2 colors)
ican: I can adapt a lighting plan from a proscenium stage to a thrust stage so that every audience section sees the actors' faces.
directions: Read the proscenium plan. Draw a thrust stage from above, divide it into areas, and mark the audience on three sides. Draw arrows for the lights that hit one area, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often keep only the two front lights and add a third from straight overhead. Overhead light puts faces in shadow. Each audience section needs light hitting the actor from its own side. Watch for lights aimed straight across the stage at a low angle: they shine into the eyes of the audience on the opposite side.
notes: The proscenium plan follows the common McCandless method: each acting area lit by two front lights about 90 degrees apart (45 degrees to each side), each at about 45 degrees above the actor. On a thrust, a usual approach is three or four lights per area spaced around the actor (about 120 degrees apart for three), each a little steeper, so that the light that is a back light for one section is a front light for another. The key gives one model; accept any plan in which each section has a light on the faces and no beam shines into an audience's eyes.

## The proscenium plan
text: Each area of the proscenium stage is lit by two front lights from out over the audience, one from the left and one from the right, each about 45 degrees to the side and 45 degrees above the actor. A back light from above and behind separates the actor from the scenery. All the audience sits in front, so every face is lit.
