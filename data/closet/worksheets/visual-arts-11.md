# 1. Critical Lenses
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter; the teacher shows Artemisia Gentileschi's Self-Portrait as the Allegory of Painting
ican: I can interpret one artwork through formalist, contextual, psychological and feminist lenses and compare what each reveals.
directions: Read the passage and highlight the key question each lens asks. Look closely at the painting your teacher shows. Read the background facts, then write a short reading of the painting through each lens and compare them.
mistakes: Students often blend the lenses into one general response. Each reading should stay inside its lens: a formalist reading talks about line, color and composition, not the artist's life. Students also treat the feminist lens as only "there is a woman in it"; push toward questions of who gets to make images, of whom, and with what power.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 580 words). Show Artemisia Gentileschi, Self-Portrait as the Allegory of Painting (La Pittura), about 1638-1639, Royal Collection, public domain. The allegory of Painting was traditionally imagined as a woman, so a woman painter could paint herself as Painting itself, which a male painter could not. Avoid using her biography as the only lens: the point of the sheet is that each lens is partial.

## Read
passage: Four Ways of Looking
  Two people can stand in front of the same painting and come away with completely different interpretations. Often the difference is not that one of them is wrong but that they asked different questions. Art critics and historians call these sets of questions critical lenses. Each lens brings some things into sharp focus and leaves others blurry, so careful viewers learn to switch among them.
  The formalist lens looks at the work itself and nothing else. It asks how the parts are organized: line, shape, color, value, texture, space and composition. A formalist might trace how a strong diagonal pulls the eye across a canvas, or how a patch of warm color pops forward against a cool background. The artist's biography and the history around the work are set aside. Formalism was especially influential in the twentieth century, when critics such as Clement Greenberg argued that the most important thing about modern painting was what painting alone could do. Its strength is close looking. Its weakness is that it can treat art as if it existed outside of time and place.
  The contextual lens does the opposite. It asks when, where, by whom and for whom a work was made. Who paid for it? What did its first viewers believe? What materials and ideas were available? A contextual reading of a Renaissance altarpiece might explain the role of the patron, the cost of the blue pigment and the religious rules for showing saints. This lens can reveal meanings that are invisible to a modern eye, but it can also bury the work under facts until no one is really looking at it.
  The psychological lens asks about the mind: the artist's, the subject's or the viewer's. Some critics, influenced by Sigmund Freud, read artworks as expressions of hidden wishes and memories; Freud himself wrote a famous and much-disputed study of Leonardo da Vinci in 1910. Other psychological readings are less about the unconscious and more about emotion and perception: Why does this face seem anxious? Why does this composition make viewers feel uneasy? The lens can open powerful insights, but it risks guessing at an artist's inner life with little evidence.
  The feminist lens asks how gender shapes art: who is allowed to make it, who is shown, how they are shown, and who is imagined as the viewer. In 1971 the art historian Linda Nochlin published an essay titled "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" She argued that the answer lay not in talent but in institutions: for centuries women were often kept out of academies, apprenticeships and the study of the human figure. Feminist critics have since recovered forgotten women artists and questioned the many paintings in which women appear only as objects to be looked at. The lens reveals patterns of power, though it is one lens among several, not the whole story.
  No lens is complete on its own. A formalist reading might praise a painting's daring composition; a contextual reading might explain why that composition was unusual for its time; a psychological reading might ask what the pose suggests about the sitter's mind; and a feminist reading might ask why it mattered that a woman painted it. Put together, the lenses work like a set of lights aimed at a sculpture from different sides. Each one shows a surface the others leave in shadow. When you write about art, name the lens you are using, gather evidence that fits it, and remember what it cannot see.

## Background on the painting
text: Artemisia Gentileschi (1593 to about 1656) was an Italian painter. In 1616 she became the first woman admitted to the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno, the artists' academy in Florence. In this self-portrait she shows herself as the allegory of Painting, a figure artists traditionally imagined as a woman: she leans in toward an unseen canvas, brush raised, sleeves pushed up, her body making a strong diagonal across the picture.

## Check the lenses
choice: Which question belongs to the formalist lens?
  - Who paid for this work?
  * How does the diagonal of the body lead the eye?
  - What was the artist feeling?
  - Why were women kept out of academies?
choice: Linda Nochlin argued that the lack of famous women artists was mainly caused by:
  - a lack of talent
  * institutions that kept women out of training
  - a lack of interest in painting
  - the high cost of paint

## Apply each lens to the painting
write: Formalist reading:
  lines: 2
  key: The leaning figure and raised arm form a strong diagonal, giving the painting energy. Light falls on her face, arm and sleeve against a dark background, focusing attention on the act of painting. The pushed-up sleeves and twisting pose create movement.
write: Contextual reading:
  lines: 2
  key: Painted in the 1630s, when few women could train as painters. Gentileschi had been the first woman in the Florence academy; the allegory of Painting was a standard figure in Italian art, often a woman with a chain and mask, so viewers would recognize it.
write: Psychological reading:
  lines: 2
  key: She appears absorbed and concentrated, looking away from us toward her work, not posing for the viewer. The pose suggests confidence and total focus on the act of making.
write: Feminist reading:
  lines: 2
  key: Only a woman could paint herself as the female figure of Painting, so she claims the role of the art itself and of the artist at once. She is shown working, not posing to be looked at, which reverses the usual way women appear in paintings.

## Stretch
write: Which lens revealed the most about this painting, and what did it miss that another lens caught?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name one lens, give a specific insight it produced, and identify a limit, for example the feminist lens explains the significance of the pose but the formalist lens explains why the composition feels so energetic.

# 21. Inquiry Question
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, sketchbook
ican: I can write and refine an inquiry question that can guide a sustained investigation of 15 works.
directions: Evaluate the sample questions first. Then move your own question through four drafts, testing each one against the checklist. End with a question that could guide 15 different works.
mistakes: Students often write questions with a yes or no answer, or a single answer ("What is the best color for sadness?"). A strong inquiry question is open, can be explored through both ideas and materials, and keeps producing new work. Others write a question so broad it gives no direction ("What is life?").
notes: A sustained investigation is a body of about 15 works guided by one question and shaped by practice, experimentation and revision. The question can change as the work develops; that change is a sign of real inquiry, so encourage students to note why they revised it. Keep subjects school-appropriate; nobody needs to share private family details to have a strong question.

## Warm up: Judge the question
table: Question | Too narrow, too broad or workable?
  - What color is my cat? | {too narrow}
  - What is art? | {too broad}
  - How can everyday packaging be transformed into portraits of the people who use it? | {workable}
  - Is watercolor better than acrylic? | {too narrow}
  - How do changing light and weather alter the way a single street corner feels? | {workable}
  - What is the meaning of everything? | {too broad}
example: A **workable** inquiry question is open (many possible answers), personal (it matters to you), and generative: it can be explored through both ideas and materials, again and again.

## Main: Four drafts
table: Draft | My question | Open? | Could it lead to 15 different works? | Ideas and materials?
  - 1 (my interest) | | | |
  - 2 (make it open) | | | |
  - 3 (add a material or process) | | | |
  - 4 (final) | | | |
write: Explain the biggest change between Draft 1 and Draft 4 and why you made it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Draft 1 "Drawing my neighborhood" became "How can layered printmaking show the history hidden in my neighborhood's buildings?" because the first was only a subject; the final adds a process and an open question.

## Test it
write: List five quite different works your final question could lead to (vary subject, scale, material or approach).
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. A strong list shows real range: for example a large charcoal drawing, a series of small prints, a collage from found materials, a sculpture and a photograph, each answering the question in a different way.

## Stretch
write: Predict how your question might change after the first five works. What would make you revise it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers expect discovery: a material may suggest a new direction, feedback may narrow the focus, or one piece may raise a better question.
