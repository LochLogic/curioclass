# 1. Melodrama to Realism
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how realism changed acting, design and subject matter in the late 1800s theater.
directions: Read the passage. Fill in the chart of changes from melodrama to realism, then answer the questions, citing paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students often think realism means "plays about real events." It means showing ordinary life as it is lived: believable people, rooms and speech, with no neat moral ending. Watch for students who say melodrama ended in 1879; it stayed popular for decades and moved into film.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 585 words). Dates to know: A Doll's House 1879; Antoine's Theatre Libre 1887; Moscow Art Theatre 1898. Zola's call for naturalism in the theater came in the early 1880s. Pair with a short reading of the last pages of A Doll's House (public domain) to hear realist dialogue.

## Read
passage: Melodrama to Realism
  In the middle of the 1800s, the most popular plays in Europe and America were melodramas. The word means "music drama," and music was everywhere in them: an orchestra played under the action, warning the audience when the villain crept in and swelling when the hero arrived. The characters were types an audience could recognize at once: the pure heroine, the brave hero, the sneering villain, the comic servant. Good and evil were never in doubt, and by the final curtain virtue was rewarded and wickedness punished.
  Melodrama was thrilling. Audiences came to see sensation scenes: a train racing toward someone tied to the tracks, a ship sinking, a house on fire. Scenery was painted on flat wings and backdrops that slid in grooves, and a forest or a palace was a picture behind the actors rather than a place they lived in. Actors played to the audience in large, clear poses, often turning out to deliver big speeches, and the stars of the company sometimes stepped forward to the footlights for their best moments.
  In the second half of the century, a group of writers began to ask whether the stage could show life as it really was. Science and new ideas about society made people curious about how heredity, money and surroundings shape a person. The Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen wrote plays about middle-class homes where the problems were ordinary and serious: debt, illness, lies inside a marriage. In A Doll's House (1879), a wife named Nora comes to see that her marriage has treated her like a doll, and at the end she walks out the door, a choice that shocked audiences. No villain is punished, and nothing is neatly solved.
  Other writers pushed further. The French novelist Emile Zola called for a naturalism that studied characters the way a scientist studies a specimen, shaped by heredity and environment. The Swedish writer August Strindberg and, later, the Russian writer Anton Chekhov wrote plays in which people talk past each other, hide their feelings and live through quiet disappointments, so that the most important things are often left unsaid.
  Design changed to match. The box set, three walls of a real-looking room with a ceiling, real doors and real furniture, replaced the painted wings. Real objects appeared on stage: dishes, food, working lamps. In Paris, the director Andre Antoine founded the Theatre Libre in 1887 and became famous for bringing real objects onto the stage. Gas and then electric light let theaters darken the auditorium and light the stage more subtly, so the audience sat quietly in the dark, looking through an invisible "fourth wall" into a room.
  Acting changed most of all. If the room was real, the people in it had to behave like real people. Actors stopped turning to the audience and began to speak to each other, sit with their backs to the house, and leave pauses. In Moscow, Konstantin Stanislavski and Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko founded the Moscow Art Theatre in 1898. Staging Chekhov's quiet plays, Stanislavski searched for a way to help actors live truthfully in imaginary circumstances. He asked actors to know what their characters wanted, what had happened before each scene, and what lay under the words. His ideas became the root of much of the acting taught today.
  Melodrama did not disappear. Its clear heroes, villains and thrilling rescues moved into early films, and they live on in adventure movies. But realism changed what the stage could be: a mirror held up to ordinary lives.

## Main: Four changes
table: Area | Melodrama | Realism
  - Acting | {big poses, played out to the audience, speeches at the footlights} | {actors talk to each other, turn their backs, use pauses, behave like real people}
  - Scenery | {painted wings and backdrops, a picture behind the actors} | {the box set: a real-looking room with walls, doors, furniture and real objects}
  - Lighting and audience | {audience and stage both lit} | {gas and electric light: a dark auditorium, a subtly lit stage seen through a fourth wall}
  - Subject matter | {good versus evil, stock types, thrilling rescues, virtue rewarded} | {ordinary middle-class problems: money, illness, marriage, with no neat ending}

  lines: 3
  key: Melodrama ended with virtue rewarded and villains punished; Ibsen's play ends with a wife walking out on her marriage and nothing neatly solved, which challenged the audience instead of reassuring it.

  lines: 3
  key: The fourth wall is the invisible wall between stage and audience: the audience watches a real-seeming room as if one wall were removed. The box set and darkened auditoriums with subtle gas and electric stage light made it possible.

# 7. Modern Drama Timeline, 1879 to 1920
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can place key events of early modern drama in order and explain the innovation each one brought.
directions: Write each card's letter at its year on the timeline. Then fill in the chart with the innovation each milestone brought to the theater.
mistakes: Students often see these events as one movement. Some pushed realism further (Theatre Libre, the Moscow Art Theatre), while others broke away from it (Ubu Roi). Watch for 1887, 1888 and 1889 crowded together: the letters can sit above and below the line. Innovation answers should say what was new, not just summarize the plot.
notes: Dates: A Doll's House premiered in Copenhagen in December 1879. Andre Antoine founded the Theatre Libre in Paris in 1887. Strindberg wrote Miss Julie and its preface on naturalism in 1888. Otto Brahm's Freie Buhne (Free Stage) opened in Berlin in 1889. Jarry's Ubu Roi opened in Paris in December 1896, and its first word caused an uproar. The Moscow Art Theatre was founded in 1898 and staged Chekhov's The Seagull that December, after its failed premiere elsewhere in 1896. The Abbey Theatre opened in Dublin in December 1904. Synge's The Playboy of the Western World caused protests at the Abbey in January 1907. The Provincetown Players formed in 1915 and staged Glaspell's Trifles in 1916.

## Stretch
write: Three of these theaters (Theatre Libre, Freie Buhne, Provincetown Players) were small and independent. Why might new kinds of drama start in small theaters instead of big commercial ones?
  lines: 3
  key: Small theaters with subscribers did not need to sell huge numbers of tickets or please everyone, and private subscription could sometimes avoid censorship, so they could take risks on new, shocking or uncommercial plays; once those plays proved themselves, larger theaters followed.

# 20. Beam Angle and Throw
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can use beam angle and throw distance to calculate the size of a pool of light and choose a lens.
directions: Pool diameter = 2 x throw x tan(half the beam angle). Use the tangent table. Round to the nearest tenth of a foot. Then choose the right lens for each job.
mistakes: Students often use the whole beam angle instead of half. Another common error is measuring throw along the floor; throw is the straight-line distance from the instrument to the actor's head height, which is longer than the floor distance. Watch for answers that confuse a bigger angle with a longer throw: a wide lens makes a big pool from close, a narrow lens a small pool from far.
notes: Calculations use the tangent values printed on the sheet. The multiplying factor some designers use is 2 x tan(half the angle): about 0.17 for 10 degrees, 0.34 for 19, 0.46 for 26, 0.65 for 36 and 0.93 for 50; throw x factor = pool diameter. Manufacturers list a field angle (where the light falls to 10 percent of the center); the pool edge you see is close to it. Answers: 7.0, 10.0, 11.5, 13.0 and 14.0 feet. Lens choices: a 12-foot pool at 30 feet needs about 22.6 degrees, so the 26 (13.9 feet) is the smallest that covers it; for the 8-foot special at 24 feet, 19 degrees gives 8.0 feet; a 36 at 30 feet gives 19.5 feet.

## Example
example: A 26-degree lens at a 30-foot throw: half angle 13 degrees, tan 13 = 0.2309. Pool = 2 x 30 x 0.2309 = 13.854, about **13.9 feet** across.

## Stretch
write: The director wants instrument 1's special to be a tight 4-foot pool, but you only own the 10-degree lens. What could you change, and what are the trade-offs?
  lines: 3
  key: Move the instrument closer: at 10 degrees, a 4-foot pool needs a throw of about 4 / (2 x 0.0875) = 22.9 feet. Or shutter or iris the beam down to 4 feet from the same position. Moving closer changes the angle on the actor; shuttering wastes light but keeps the angle.

# 21. Final Scene Cue List
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a cue list in which every light change has a story reason.
directions: Read the beats of the final scene of A Doll's House. Write a 10-cue list: where each cue happens (placement), the intensity, the timing (fade time) and the story reason for the change.
mistakes: Students often write cues for every line or cue changes that have no reason ("make it brighter"). Each cue needs a placement the stage manager can call, such as a line, a sound or an action. Watch for the last cue placed after the door slam; the slam is the button, and the light change must land with it.
notes: The beats summarize Act Three of Henrik Ibsen's A Doll's House (1879, public domain) in Curio Class's words. Intensities are percentages of the main stage look; timing is the fade in seconds. The key is a model; accept any list whose placements are callable and whose changes follow the turns of the story. A room lit by lamps at night is the motivated source throughout.

## The beats of the final scene
text: **A.** Late night, after a costume party. Nora and Torvald return to their lamplit living room. **B.** Torvald reads a letter revealing that Nora secretly borrowed money years ago. He is furious. **C.** A maid brings a second letter: the threat is withdrawn. Torvald says "I am saved!" and forgives her. **D.** Nora goes to take off her party costume. **E.** She returns in everyday clothes, not going to bed. "Sit down. We have a lot to talk about." **F.** Nora says she has been treated like a doll, first by her father and then by her husband. **G.** She says she must leave to understand herself and the world. **H.** She returns her wedding ring and leaves the room. **I.** Torvald, alone, hopes for "the most wonderful thing." **J.** From below, the sound of a door slamming shut.

## Main: Cue list
chart: Cue | Placement (line, action or sound) | Intensity | Time | Story reason
  rows: 10
  key: Model: Q1 lights up as they enter (A), 70%, 3 sec, late night lamplight. Q2 Torvald opens the letter (B), area narrows to Torvald 75% and room 50%, 10 sec, the walls close in. Q3 "I am saved!" (C), room warms back to 70%, 5 sec, false relief. Q4 Nora exits to change (D), 60%, 8 sec, a pause in the room. Q5 Nora re-enters in everyday clothes (E), cooler and plainer, 65%, 15 sec, she has changed. Q6 "Sit down" (E), table area 75%, rest 45%, 10 sec, focus on the talk. Q7 "like a doll" (F), slow build on Nora 80%, 30 sec, she grows stronger. Q8 she says she must leave (G), Torvald's side dims to 35%, 20 sec, he is losing her. Q9 Nora exits (H), 40%, 10 sec, the room empties. Q10 on the door slam (J), snap to a single pool on Torvald at 30%, then fade to black over 8 sec, the end of the marriage.
write: Which cue is the most important in your list, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Many choose the door slam cue: the light change landing exactly on the sound makes the audience feel the finality of Nora's choice.

## Stretch
write: Some directors leave the light unchanged when Nora leaves, so the room looks the same but is suddenly empty. Argue for or against this choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. For: stillness makes the emptiness and the sound do the work, and it avoids telling the audience how to feel. Against: a final change helps mark the turning point and gives the ending a clear visual button.

# 33. Physical Score
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, your monologue
ican: I can build a physical score that sets gestures, stillness and movement beat by beat with a reason for each.
directions: Divide your monologue into 6 beats. For each beat, chart the gesture, the moments of stillness and any movement across the stage, and the reason behind them. Rehearse until the score is set, then test it.
mistakes: Students often plan a gesture for every line, which makes the body busy and the words hard to hear. Stillness is a choice; the strongest moments are often still. Watch for movement with no reason ("I cross because I have been standing too long"); each move should come from a change in thought or tactic.
notes: A physical score sets the actor's physical life the way a musical score sets notes, so it can be repeated in every performance and adjusted on purpose. Beats are units of thought, often marked by a change in tactic. The key shows a model score for an invented monologue: a teenager asking a coach for one more chance to join a team. Students' scores will differ.

## Example
example: **Beat 1** ("Coach, wait. Please."): Gesture: one hand reaches out, then drops. Stillness: freeze after the hand drops. Movement: two quick steps toward the coach. Why: she has to stop the coach from leaving before she loses her nerve.

## Warm up
choice: Why is stillness part of a physical score?
  - Because the actor has forgotten the lines
  * Because a still moment focuses attention on a key thought or line
  - Because movement is not allowed in monologues
  - To give the lighting team time to change cues

## Main: The score
chart: Line it starts on | Gesture | Stillness | Movement (from where to where) | Why
  labels: Beat 1; Beat 2; Beat 3; Beat 4; Beat 5; Beat 6
  height: 0.85
  key: Model (continuing the example): Beat 2, "I know I missed tryouts": hands open, palms up; still while admitting the mistake; none, stays center; she is being honest. Beat 3, "My brother was sick": no gesture; still, looking down; none; it is hard to say. Beat 4, "But watch this": mimes a free throw; none; crosses downstage right toward an imagined court; she switches tactic from explaining to proving. Beat 5, "I practiced every day": counts off on her fingers; brief pause after each count; small steps back to center; building her case. Beat 6, "Just one chance.": arms at her sides; long stillness, eyes on the coach; none; she has said everything and waits.
write: Which beat uses stillness most powerfully? Explain why it works better than a gesture there.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Beat 6, where she says "Just one chance" and stands completely still; any gesture would look like more begging, while stillness shows she has said it all and makes the audience wait for the answer with her.

## Stretch
write: Perform your score for a partner. Record one moment they noticed and one moment that felt unclear. What will you change?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific beat for each and a concrete change, such as cutting a repeated gesture in beat 2 or moving the cross to start on a new line.
