# 1. Theme Hunt: What Is the Story Really About?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter if you have one
ican: I can state the theme of a story and use details to show it.
directions: Read the story. Answer the questions. Use details from the story to prove your answers.
mistakes: Students often give the topic ("a spelling bee") or a summary instead of a theme. A theme is a lesson that could fit many stories: ask "What did Marcus learn that anyone could learn?"
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). A strong theme statement: being too sure of yourself can keep you from preparing, or practice matters more than talent alone.

## Read
passage: The Word Marcus Knew
  Marcus could spell anything. At least, that is what he told everyone the week before the spelling bee.
  "I don't need to study," he said at lunch, waving a carrot stick. "I've never missed a word in my life." His friend Priya shrugged and went back to her flash cards. She had been practicing every night, even the long words with silent letters.
  On the day of the bee, Marcus strolled onto the stage. He spelled "garden" and "planet" without even thinking. He winked at the audience. Priya spelled her words slowly, tapping each letter on her leg.
  Then the judge said, "Marcus, your word is rhythm."
  Marcus opened his mouth. He knew this word. He had heard it a hundred times in music class. "R-H-Y-T-H-U-M," he said.
  The bell rang. Marcus felt his face turn hot as he walked back to his seat. Priya spelled "rhythm" correctly two rounds later and went on to win.
  After the bee, Marcus found Priya by the water fountain. "How did you know that one?" he asked.
  "I didn't, at first," she said. "I missed it at home three times."
  The next week, Marcus borrowed a stack of her flash cards.

## Questions
choice: What is the story mostly about?
  - A spelling bee at school
  * Learning that you have to prepare, even when you feel sure of yourself
  - Why Priya is good at music
  - How to spell the word rhythm
write: State the theme of the story in one sentence.
  lines: 2
  key: Being too sure of yourself can keep you from preparing; practice matters more than bragging.
write: Find two details that show the theme. Write them, then explain how each one shows it.
  lines: 5
  starter: One detail is ... This shows ...
  key: "I don't need to study," and he misspells rhythm (bragging leads to a mistake); Priya practiced every night and missed it at home three times (practice pays off); Marcus borrows flash cards (he learned the lesson).
choice: Why does the author tell us Priya "missed it at home three times"?
  - To show that Priya is not a good speller
  * To show that her success came from practice, not from already knowing
  - To make the reader feel sorry for Marcus
  - To explain why the bee lasted so long
write: What does the last sentence tell you about how Marcus changed?
  lines: 2
  key: He now understands he needs to practice; borrowing the cards shows he will study instead of brag.

# 6. Herculean, Midas Touch and Other Allusions
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain what an allusion to a Greek myth means and use it in a sentence.
directions: An allusion is a short mention of a famous story. Match each phrase to the story it comes from. Then pick the best phrase for each modern sentence, and write three sentences of your own.
mistakes: Students may use the allusion literally ("The horse was a Trojan horse because it was wooden"). The modern meaning is the idea behind the story: a hidden danger, a weak spot, a huge job. Watch for "Achilles' heel" used for any injury; it means one weak spot in someone otherwise strong.
notes: Story details follow the usual retellings: the twelve labors of Heracles (Hercules to the Romans), including cleaning the Augean stables in one day; King Midas and his golden touch (in Ovid his food turns to gold; the daughter turned to gold comes from Nathaniel Hawthorne's 1852 retelling); Achilles dipped in the river Styx; Pandora's jar (called a box in later tellings); the wooden horse at Troy; and Mentor, whose form the goddess Athena takes to guide Odysseus's son Telemachus in Homer's Odyssey.

## Warm up: What is an allusion?
example: "Cleaning my room after the party was a **Herculean** task." Hercules had to finish twelve very hard jobs, so a Herculean task is a job that takes huge strength or effort.

## Main: Match the phrase to its story
match: Write the letter of the story next to each phrase.
  - Herculean task = A hero had to finish twelve very hard jobs, like cleaning giant stables in a single day.
  - the Midas touch = A king wished that everything he touched would turn to gold, even his food.
  - Achilles' heel = A great warrior could be hurt in only one spot, where his mother held him when she dipped him in a magic river.
  - Pandora's box = A woman opened a container she was told to keep shut, and troubles flew out into the world.
  - Trojan horse = Soldiers hid inside a giant wooden horse that was left outside a city as a "gift."
  - a mentor = A goddess took the form of an old family friend to guide a young man searching for his father.

## Use it: Pick the phrase
text: Word bank: Herculean task, the Midas touch, Achilles' heel, Pandora's box, Trojan horse, mentor
fill:
  - Jada is great at every sport, but swimming is her {Achilles' heel}.
  - The email looked like a free game, but it was a {Trojan horse} that carried a computer virus.
  - Moving all the library books to the new building was a {Herculean task}.
  - Every lemonade stand Omar runs makes money. He has {the Midas touch}.
  - When the coach changed one rule, she opened {Pandora's box}, and soon everyone was arguing about all the rules.
  - My older cousin is my {mentor}; she helps me with my writing and gives me good advice.

## Write: Your own modern sentences
write: Use one of the phrases in a sentence about school, sports or home.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Spelling is my Achilles' heel, even though I am good at math.
write: Use a second phrase in a sentence.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Painting the whole fence by Saturday felt like a Herculean task.
write: Use a third phrase in a sentence.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: My grandpa has the Midas touch with plants; everything he grows does well.

## Stretch
write: Why might a writer say "Herculean task" instead of "a very hard job"? What does the allusion add?
  lines: 3
  key: The allusion brings the whole story to mind in two words. Readers picture Hercules and his twelve impossible labors, so the job sounds bigger and more heroic than "very hard."
# 18. Progressive Tense and Modal Verbs
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use progressive verb tenses and the modal verbs can, may and must.
directions: Progressive tenses tell about an action that keeps going for a while. Rewrite each subject and verb in the past, present and future progressive. Then choose can, may or must to finish each sentence.
mistakes: Watch for "they was" and "we is": the helping verb must match the subject (I was, they were; I am, he is, they are). Students also forget spelling changes before -ing: drop the silent e (bake, baking; ride, riding; write, writing) and double the last consonant in swim, run and shop. Future progressive always uses "will be," whatever the subject.
notes: Modal meanings on this sheet: can shows ability, may shows possibility, must shows something required. "May" can also ask permission ("May I go?"); the stretch invites students to notice that. The cheetah fact is accurate: it is the fastest land animal.

## Warm up
example: she / jump: past **She was jumping.** present **She is jumping.** future **She will be jumping.**
choice: Which sentence is in the past progressive?
  - We are walking to the park.
  * We were walking to the park.
  - We will be walking to the park.
choice: Which sentence is in the future progressive?
  - The band is playing.
  - The band was playing.
  * The band will be playing.

## Main: Rewrite in three progressive tenses
table: Subject / verb | Past progressive | Present progressive | Future progressive
  - I / read | {I was reading} | {I am reading} | {I will be reading}
  - she / swim | {she was swimming} | {she is swimming} | {she will be swimming}
  - they / play | {they were playing} | {they are playing} | {they will be playing}
  - we / bake | {we were baking} | {we are baking} | {we will be baking}
  - the dog / run | {the dog was running} | {the dog is running} | {the dog will be running}
  - you / write | {you were writing} | {you are writing} | {you will be writing}
  - he / ride | {he was riding} | {he is riding} | {he will be riding}
  - my friends / sing | {my friends were singing} | {my friends are singing} | {my friends will be singing}
  - the baby / sleep | {the baby was sleeping} | {the baby is sleeping} | {the baby will be sleeping}
  - Ana / shop | {Ana was shopping} | {Ana is shopping} | {Ana will be shopping}

## Can, may or must?
text: can = is able to · may = might happen · must = has to
fill:
  - A cheetah {can} run faster than any other land animal.
  - Look at those dark clouds. It {may} rain this afternoon.
  - Everyone {must} wear safety goggles in the science lab. It is the rule.
  - We are not sure yet, but we {may} visit the museum on Saturday.
  - Drivers {must} stop when the traffic light is red.

## Stretch
write: Write one sentence that uses "may" to ask for permission, and one that uses "can" to show what you are able to do.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: May I borrow your ruler? I can tie my shoes with my eyes closed.
# 25. Character Evidence Web
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter if you have one
ican: I can describe a character in depth using her thoughts, words and actions and what others say.
directions: Read the story. Sort four clues in the warm up. Then fill the web with evidence about Lena, name her trait in the center, and explain it.
mistakes: Students often list what happens (she gives him a seat) without saying what it shows about her. Push for the "so what": what kind of person does that? Watch for traits that are feelings (happy) instead of character traits (kind, observant, brave). "What others say" means what other characters say about Lena, not what Lena says to others.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 195 words). Strong center traits: kind, caring, observant, brave (it takes courage to call out across the lunchroom). Accept any trait supported by at least three kinds of evidence.

## Read
passage: The Empty Chair
  Lena noticed the new boy, Omar, standing alone with his lunch tray. Every table looked full. He must feel like everyone is staring at him, Lena thought. She remembered her own first day last year, when her hands had shaken so hard that she spilled her milk.
  Lena's friends were busy laughing about a video game. She took a deep breath and pulled her backpack off the empty chair beside her. "Omar! There's a seat here," she called, waving her arm.
  Omar walked over slowly. "Thanks," he said quietly. "I didn't know where to go."
  "Everybody gets lost the first week," Lena told him. "I sat by the windows by myself for three days." Omar laughed for the first time all morning.
  After lunch, Lena walked Omar to the art room using the shortcut past the library. She drew him a map of the school on the back of her spelling list.
  Later, Jada caught up with her at the lockers. "That was really nice, Lena. I didn't even notice he was alone."
  Mr. Ruiz, the lunch helper, smiled at her as he passed. "You always see the kid nobody else sees," he said.

## Warm up: What kind of clue?
match: Match each clue about Lena to the kind of clue it is.
  - "He must feel like everyone is staring at him." = A thought
  - "Everybody gets lost the first week." = Something Lena says
  - She drew him a map of the school. = Something Lena does
  - "You always see the kid nobody else sees." = What another character says

## Main: Evidence web
text: Write two pieces of evidence in each part of the web. Then write Lena's trait in the center box.
chart: Lena's thoughts | Lena's words | Lena's actions | What others say about her
  rows: 2
  key: Thoughts: he must feel like everyone is staring; she remembers spilling her milk on her own first day. Words: "Omar! There's a seat here"; "Everybody gets lost the first week"; "I sat by the windows by myself for three days." Actions: notices Omar alone; takes a deep breath and moves her backpack; walks him to art; draws him a map. Others: Jada, "That was really nice"; Mr. Ruiz, "You always see the kid nobody else sees."
draw: Center of the web: Lena is ... | 0.8
write: Name Lena's trait in one word for the center of the web.
  lines: 1
  key: Kind (also observant, caring, brave, thoughtful).

## Explain
write: Use evidence from at least three parts of your web to explain why your trait fits Lena.
  lines: 6
  starter: Lena is ... One clue is ... This shows ... Another clue is ...
  key: Lena is kind and observant. She thinks Omar "must feel like everyone is staring at him," so she notices how others feel. She calls "Omar! There's a seat here" and later draws him a map, which shows she helps without being asked. Mr. Ruiz says she "always see[s] the kid nobody else sees," so others notice her kindness too.

## Stretch
write: Why does the author tell us about Lena's own first day? What does it add to her character?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows why she understands how Omar feels: she has been new and nervous herself. Her kindness comes from remembering her own experience, which makes her a deeper character.
# 31. From Page to Panels
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional)
ican: I can turn a written story into a comic, deciding what to show in pictures and what to tell in words.
directions: Read the story. Then plan and draw it as a 6-panel comic. For each panel, sketch the picture, write a caption (the narrator's words in a box) if you need one, and write a speech bubble (what a character says) if you need one. Not every panel needs both.
mistakes: Students often copy whole sentences into captions. A comic shows actions and feelings in the picture ("Leo's shoulders drooped" becomes a drawing of a sad Leo), so captions should be short and used for time, place or things a picture cannot show. Watch for panels that skip the turning point (the string snapping) or the solution (the rake).
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 190 words). The key gives one strong plan; accept any 6 panels that keep the beginning, problem, solution and ending in order and move feelings into the pictures. Sound effects such as SNAP! may go in a panel as big letters.

## Read
passage: The Runaway Kite
  Saturday morning was windy, perfect for the new red kite. Leo and his grandmother walked to the top of Hill Park. Leo held the spool while Grandma tossed the kite into the air. It shot up, higher and higher, until it was a tiny red dot.
  "Look how high it is!" Leo shouted.
  Then a strong gust hit. Snap! The string broke, and the kite tumbled through the sky. It landed in the branches of a tall oak tree.
  Leo's shoulders drooped. "It's stuck forever," he said.
  A girl with a long rake was cleaning up leaves nearby. "Maybe this can help," she said. Her name was Priya.
  Leo stood on a sturdy bench under the tree. Grandma held his arm steady while he stretched the rake up as far as it could go. On the third try, he hooked the kite's tail. The kite slid down into his arms with only one small tear.
  That afternoon, Leo, Priya and Grandma sat at the kitchen table, patching the kite with tape. "Next Saturday," said Leo, "you're flying it with us, Priya."

## Warm up: Picture or words?
choice: Which part of the story works best as a **caption**?
  - "Look how high it is!"
  * That afternoon, at the kitchen table
  - Leo's shoulders drooped.
  - "Maybe this can help."
choice: Which part is best **shown in the picture** instead of written?
  * Leo's shoulders drooped.
  - "It's stuck forever."
  - That afternoon
  - "Next Saturday, you're flying it with us."
choice: What is the turning point that must get its own panel?
  - Leo and Grandma walk to the park.
  - Priya is raking leaves.
  * The string snaps and the kite lands in the tree.
  - They patch the kite with tape.

## Main: Plan and draw your 6 panels
chart: Picture (sketch it) | Caption (narrator) | Speech bubble (who says what)
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6
  height: 1.05
  key: 1: Leo and Grandma on the hill, kite rising; caption "Saturday at Hill Park"; Leo: "Look how high it is!" 2: the string snapping, kite tumbling; SNAP! in big letters; no bubble. 3: kite in the oak tree, Leo looking sad; no caption; Leo: "It's stuck forever." 4: Priya holding up her rake; no caption; Priya: "Maybe this can help." 5: Leo on the bench reaching up, Grandma holding his arm, rake hooking the tail; caption "On the third try..."; no bubble or "Got it!" 6: all three at the kitchen table taping the kite; caption "That afternoon"; Leo: "Next Saturday, you're flying it with us, Priya."

## Stretch
write: Name one sentence from the story that you did not put in words in your comic. How did your picture show it instead?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: I did not write "The kite tumbled through the sky." In panel 2 I drew the kite upside down with wavy lines behind it and the broken string flying loose.
# 43. Oral Report Organizer
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a timer for practice
ican: I can plan a short oral report with a clear opening, organized facts, a visual and a closing.
directions: Plan a two-minute report on a topic you have researched. Write notes (key words, not whole sentences) for each part. Plan one visual to show. Mark // where you will pause. Then time yourself practicing.
mistakes: Students often write the whole report word for word and then read it aloud with their eyes down. Insist on key words only. Watch for three "facts" that are really one fact said three ways, and for closings that just say "The end"; a closing sums up or leaves the audience with a thought.
notes: Two minutes of speaking is roughly 250 words, so three facts with one or two details each fills it well. Facts in the model plan: a honeybee colony holds tens of thousands of bees; worker bees do a "waggle dance" that tells other bees the direction and distance of flowers; a single worker bee makes only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her life. Good pause spots: after the hook, between facts, and before the closing line.

## Warm up
choice: Which is the strongest opening for a report about honeybees?
  - "My report is about bees."
  * "Imagine working your whole life to make one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey."
  - "Bees are insects. That is my topic."
  - "I did not know much about bees."
choice: Where is the best place to pause in a report?
  - In the middle of a word
  - Never; keep talking so you do not forget
  * Between one fact and the next
  - After every single word

  lines: 1
  key: Example: Honeybees

Visual: poster with a hive drawing and a waggle dance diagram, shown during Fact 2. Closing: "Next time you taste honey, thank thousands of tiny workers." // then "Questions?"
