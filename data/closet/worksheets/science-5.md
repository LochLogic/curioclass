# 1. Where Did the Salt Go?
minutes: 40
materials: table salt, warm tap water, a clear plastic cup, a spoon, a dropper or straw, black or dark blue construction paper, a hand lens, safety goggles, a kitchen scale (optional), pencil
ican: I can use a particle model to explain what happens when salt dissolves and the water evaporates.
directions: Look at salt grains with a hand lens. Stir one spoonful of salt into half a cup of warm water until you cannot see it. Put 10 drops of the salt water on dark paper and let them dry. Record what you see, then explain it with particles.
mistakes: Students often say the salt "melted" or "disappeared." Melting needs heat and turns a solid into a liquid of the same substance; here the salt breaks into particles too small to see that spread out between the water particles. Watch for drawings that show the salt particles vanishing or shrinking: they are all still there.
notes: Allow 1 to 2 days for the drops to dry before the last observation. Never taste lab materials, even salt. Wear goggles while stirring and dropping. Typical results: after 1 to 2 days the drops leave white rings or crusts; with a hand lens, many grains look like tiny cubes. With a scale (optional): typical class data are cup and water 210 g, salt 15 g, and the mixture 225 g, so no mass is lost. Small differences of 1 g can come from spills or the scale.

## Before
write: Look at dry salt with the hand lens. Draw or describe the grains.
  lines: 2
  key: Small white, clear grains; many have flat sides and square corners, like tiny cubes.
write: Predict: Where will the salt go when you stir it into water?
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Observe
table: Step | What I see | Mass (g, optional)
  - Salt grains under the hand lens | |
  - Salt stirred into water, after 2 minutes | |
  - Drops on dark paper, right away | |
  - Drops on dark paper, after 1 to 2 days | |
fill:
  - After stirring, can you see the salt? {no}
  - After the drops dry, what is left on the paper? {white salt crystals}
  - Typical mass before mixing (cup and water 210 g + salt 15 g): {225} g. Typical mass after mixing: about {225} g.

## Explain with particles
draw: Draw a model of the salt water. Use dots for salt particles and circles for water particles. | 2
choice: Why can you not see the salt after it dissolves?
  - It turned into water.
  - It melted.
  * It broke into particles too small to see, spread out in the water.
  - It floated away into the air.
write: Where did the salt crystals on the paper come from? Use the word particles.
  lines: 3
  key: The water particles evaporated into the air, but the salt particles could not. They were left behind on the paper and joined together into crystals we can see.
write: What does the mass tell you about the salt in the water?
  lines: 2
  key: The mass stayed the same, so the salt was still there, even though we could not see it. Matter is not lost when it dissolves.

## Stretch
write: How could you get salt back from a whole cup of salt water? What would you expect to find?
  lines: 2
  key: Leave the cup in a warm, sunny place until all the water evaporates. Salt crystals would be left at the bottom, about the same amount as you put in.

# 5. Conservation Data Check
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can use data to show that the total mass of matter stays the same when it changes, as long as nothing gets in or out.
directions: A class weighed 5 setups before and after a change. Find the difference for each one. Then use the data and the idea of particles to explain what happened.
mistakes: Many students think dissolved salt or melted ice "weighs less" because it looks like less stuff. The data show the mass stays the same: the particles are still there. In the open setups, students may say matter was destroyed; the particles went into the air as a gas, where the scale cannot weigh them.
notes: The data are typical classroom numbers. With real scales, sealed setups often differ by 0.1 g, which is measurement error. A puffed-up sealed bag can also read a few tenths of a gram light, because the bigger bag pushes aside more air (the same effect that holds a balloon up); this is not lost matter. In setup 4, the gas that escapes is carbon dioxide. In setup 5, water evaporated into the air.

## Warm up
choice: A closed jar of ice cubes has a mass of 215.0 g. The ice melts. What will the scale read?
  - less than 215.0 g, because water is lighter than ice
  * 215.0 g, because no particles got in or out
  - more than 215.0 g, because water is heavier than ice
  - 0 g, because the ice is gone

## Main: Check the data
table: Setup | Closed or open? | Mass before (g) | Mass after (g) | Difference (g)
  - 1. Ice melts in a jar with a lid | closed | 215.0 | 215.0 | {0}
  - 2. 10 g of salt dissolves in a cup of water with a lid | closed | 200.0 | 200.0 | {0}
  - 3. Baking soda and vinegar fizz in a sealed zip bag | closed | 286.4 | 286.4 | {0}
  - 4. Baking soda and vinegar fizz in an open cup | open | 286.4 | 284.1 | {2.3 less}
  - 5. Water sits in an open dish in the sun for 2 days | open | 120.0 | 95.5 | {24.5 less}
choice: Which setups kept the same mass?
  - 1 and 4 only
  - 4 and 5 only
  * 1, 2 and 3
  - all 5
choice: In setup 2, the salt seems to disappear. What really happened to the salt particles?
  * They spread out among the water particles, too small to see.
  - They were destroyed.
  - They turned into water.
  - They floated out of the cup.

## Explain
write: Setups 3 and 4 had the same reaction. Why did the open cup lose mass but the sealed bag did not?
  lines: 3
  key: The fizzing makes a gas. In the sealed bag the gas is trapped, so all the particles are still weighed. In the open cup the gas escaped into the air, so the scale no longer weighed those particles.
write: Where did the 24.5 g of water in setup 5 go? Was it destroyed?
  lines: 2
  key: It evaporated: the water particles moved into the air as water vapor. They were not destroyed; they just left the dish.
write: Write a rule that fits all 5 setups.
  lines: 2
  starter: When matter changes, the total mass ...
  key: When matter changes, the total mass stays the same as long as nothing gets in or out. If a gas escapes, the mass we measure goes down.

## Stretch
write: How could you change setup 5 so the mass stays the same? Explain using particles.
  lines: 2
  key: Put a lid on the dish (seal it). The water particles that evaporate stay inside, so they are still weighed and the mass stays the same.

# 15. Gravity Points Down Argument
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a globe (optional)
ican: I can argue from evidence that gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth.
directions: Read the evidence. Draw the globe diagram. Then write an argument with a claim, evidence and reasoning.
mistakes: Many students picture "down" as one direction in space, the way it looks on a flat page, so they worry that people in Australia could fall off. In the diagram, every arrow should point to Earth's center, not to the bottom of the page. Watch for evidence that is only an opinion ("everyone knows").
notes: The drop test results are what anyone, anywhere on Earth, will find: a dropped object falls straight toward the ground beneath it. A plumb line (a weight on a string) hangs the same way everywhere, pointing toward Earth's center. If you have a globe, stick 4 small paper people on it with tape at different places so their feet touch the globe; their heads point out in every direction.

## Warm up: The evidence
table: Place | Where it is on the globe | What a dropped ball did
  - Canada | far north, top of the globe | fell straight down to the ground
  - Kenya | on the equator, side of the globe | fell straight down to the ground
  - Brazil | on the other side, near the equator | fell straight down to the ground
  - Australia | far south, bottom of the globe | fell straight down to the ground
fill:
  - In every place, the ball fell toward the {ground}.
  - No ball fell up or {sideways} into the sky.

## Main: The globe diagram
draw: Draw Earth as a big circle. Add 4 people standing on the ground: top, bottom, left and right. Draw an arrow from each person's hand to show the way a dropped ball falls. | 2.6
choice: Where do all 4 arrows point?
  - to the bottom of the page
  * toward the center of Earth
  - toward the Sun
  - straight out into space

## Write your argument
write: Claim: What does gravity do to objects near Earth?
  lines: 2
  starter: Earth's gravity pulls objects ...
  key: Earth's gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth.
write: Evidence: What did the drop tests show? Use at least two places.
  lines: 3
  starter: In Canada and in Australia, ...
  key: In Canada, at the top of the globe, and in Australia, at the bottom, a dropped ball fell straight down to the ground. Balls in Kenya and Brazil did the same. Everywhere, objects fall toward the ground under them.
write: Reasoning: Use your diagram to explain why "down" means toward Earth's center.
  lines: 3
  key: On the globe, the people stand in different directions, but every ball falls toward the ground under them. When I draw those arrows, they all point to the same place, the center of Earth. So "down" is always toward the center, and gravity pulls that way.

## Stretch: Answer a question
write: A first grader asks, "Why don't people in Australia fall off the bottom of Earth?" Answer using your argument.
  lines: 2
  key: There is no bottom of Earth for them to fall off. Gravity pulls everyone toward Earth's center, so for people in Australia, down is the ground under their feet, just like for us.

# 21. Matter Goes Around
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can trace how matter moves among plants, animals, decomposers and the environment.
directions: Read the cycle clues. Fill in the organizer to trace matter from air and soil into a plant, a rabbit, a fox, and back to the soil and air. Then draw it as a cycle.
mistakes: The most common idea is that a plant's mass comes from the soil. Most of a plant's mass is built from carbon dioxide from the air and water; soil gives only small amounts of minerals. Students also leave out decomposers, so the cycle never closes.
notes: Energy flows in from the Sun and does not cycle; matter cycles. Accept "germs" or "microbes" for bacteria. Decomposers also release carbon dioxide back to the air as they break down dead matter, as do plants and animals when they use food for energy.

## Warm up: Word bank
example: Word bank: carbon dioxide, water, minerals, sugar, eats, decomposers, soil, air
fill:
  - A plant takes in {carbon dioxide} from the air and {water} from the soil.
  - The plant uses energy from sunlight to make {sugar}, which it uses to grow.
  - Bacteria, fungi and worms are {decomposers}. They break down dead plants and animals.

## Main: Trace the matter
table: Step | Where matter is | How it moves to the next step
  - 1 | Air and soil | {a plant takes in carbon dioxide, water and minerals}
  - 2 | A plant | {a rabbit eats the plant}
  - 3 | A rabbit | {a fox eats the rabbit}
  - 4 | A fox | {the fox's waste, and its body when it dies, fall to the ground}
  - 5 | Dead matter and waste | {decomposers break it down}
  - 6 | Decomposers | {they return minerals to the soil and carbon dioxide to the air}
draw: Draw the six steps as a circle with arrows going around. | 2.4

## Check
choice: Where does most of the matter in a plant's body come from?
  - the soil
  * carbon dioxide from the air, and water
  - sunlight
  - fertilizer
choice: What would happen to the soil if there were no decomposers?
  * Dead matter would pile up and minerals would not return to the soil.
  - Plants would grow faster.
  - Nothing would change.
write: The fox does not eat plants. Explain how matter from a plant still ends up in the fox.
  lines: 2
  key: The rabbit ate the plant and used its matter to build its body. When the fox ate the rabbit, that matter moved into the fox.

## Stretch
write: The rabbit also breathes out carbon dioxide. Add this to your cycle: where does that matter go next?
  lines: 2
  key: Into the air, where a plant can take it in again to make sugar. It is a shortcut in the cycle.

# 26. Winter Sky, Summer Sky
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can use star charts to show that we see different stars in different seasons.
directions: The two charts list the constellations you can see in the evening from the middle of the United States, in winter and in summer. Compare them, find the constellations seen in only one season, then explain why.
mistakes: Students often think the stars themselves move away in summer. The stars stay put; Earth moves around the Sun, so the night side of Earth faces a different part of space. Some also think the stars of the other season are gone; they are up during the day, hidden by the bright sunlight.
notes: The charts are for about 9 p.m. in mid-January and about 10 p.m. in mid-July, seen from about 40 degrees north (the middle of the United States). The Little Dipper and Cassiopeia are close enough to the North Star that they never set from there. The Big Dipper is low in the northeast in winter evenings and in the northwest in summer evenings. Orion is behind the Sun, in the daytime sky, in June and July. A free planetarium app or a star wheel lets students check.

## Star charts
table: Where to look | Winter evening (January, 9 p.m.) | Summer evening (July, 10 p.m.)
  - Facing south | Orion, Canis Major (with Sirius, the brightest star at night), Taurus | Scorpius (with red Antares), Sagittarius
  - High in the sky | Gemini, Auriga | Lyra (with Vega), Cygnus, Aquila
  - Facing north | Little Dipper (with the North Star), Cassiopeia, Big Dipper | Little Dipper (with the North Star), Cassiopeia, Big Dipper

## Warm up
choice: A constellation is ...
  - one very bright star
  * a group of stars that makes a pattern in the sky
  - a planet seen at night
  - a cloud of gas near Earth
fill:
  - Two constellations seen only in winter: {Orion} and {Taurus}
  - Two constellations seen only in summer: {Scorpius} and {Lyra}
note: Accept any two from each list: winter only, Orion, Canis Major, Taurus, Gemini, Auriga; summer only, Scorpius, Sagittarius, Lyra, Cygnus, Aquila.

## Main: Compare the skies
venn: Winter evening | Summer evening
  both: Both seasons
  key: Winter only: Orion, Canis Major, Taurus, Gemini, Auriga. Summer only: Scorpius, Sagittarius, Lyra, Cygnus, Aquila. Both: Little Dipper, Cassiopeia, Big Dipper.
choice: Where are all the constellations you see in both seasons?
  - facing south
  - high in the sky
  * facing north, near the North Star
write: Why can you see the constellations near the North Star all year?
  lines: 2
  key: They are close to the North Star, so as Earth turns they circle around it and never go below the horizon. They are up every night of the year.
draw: Draw the Sun in the middle. Draw Earth on one side of it in January and on the other side in July. Shade the night side of Earth each time and draw an arrow out into space from the night side. | 2.2
write: Use your drawing to explain why Orion is in the winter sky but not the summer sky.
  lines: 3
  key: Earth orbits the Sun. In January the night side of Earth faces toward Orion, so we see it at night. In July Earth is on the other side of the Sun, so Orion is in the same direction as the Sun and is up during the day, hidden by sunlight.

## Stretch
write: Where will Scorpius be in the evening sky in January? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: It will not be in the evening sky. In January Scorpius is close to the direction of the Sun, so it is up mostly in the daytime, hidden by sunlight; it only rises shortly before sunrise.

# 36. Eruption Effects Chain
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how one event in the geosphere affects the air, water and living things.
directions: Read about the eruption of Mount Pinatubo. Then build a cause-and-effect chain that shows how it changed Earth's other spheres.
mistakes: Students often list effects without linking them ("ash, cold, mud"). Each link in the chain should say what caused it. Some also think the cooling came from ash blocking the Sun; ash falls out of the air in days to weeks, and it was the haze of tiny droplets from sulfur dioxide gas that cooled the planet for about two years.
notes: Mount Pinatubo erupted on June 15, 1991, in the Philippines. Scientists warned people in time, and tens of thousands left before the largest blast, which saved many lives. A typhoon hit the same day, and its rain made the ash wet and heavy. Lahars kept flowing in the rainy seasons for years afterward. The geosphere is the rock and land, the atmosphere is the air, the hydrosphere is the water, and the biosphere is all the living things.

## Read
passage: The Day the Mountain Woke Up | numbered
  In June 1991, Mount Pinatubo, a volcano in the Philippines, erupted. It was one of the biggest eruptions of the 1900s. Scientists had watched the mountain for weeks and warned people in time, so tens of thousands of people left before the biggest blast.
  The volcano blasted ash and gas more than 30 kilometers into the sky. Ash fell like gray snow over towns and farms. Heavy, wet ash piled up on roofs, and some roofs caved in.
  A gas from the volcano, sulfur dioxide, rose high into the air. It formed a thin haze of tiny droplets that spread around the whole world. The haze reflected some sunlight back into space. For about two years, Earth's average temperature was about half a degree Celsius cooler.
  On the ground, ash buried forests and fields. Animals lost the plants they ate and the places they lived. When heavy rain fell on the loose ash, it turned into thick rivers of mud called lahars. Lahars rushed down the river valleys for years, burying villages and filling rivers with mud.
  Life came back slowly. Grasses and small plants sprouted in the ash, and later trees grew again. Rain even filled the new crater at the top of the volcano, making a lake.

## Warm up
match: Match each sphere to what it includes.
  - geosphere = rock, land and volcanoes
  - atmosphere = the air around Earth
  - hydrosphere = rivers, lakes, oceans and rain
  - biosphere = plants, animals and people

## Main: Build the chain
text: Each effect becomes the cause in the next link.
table: Link | Cause | Effect | Sphere that changed
  height: 0.65
  - 1 | The volcano erupts. | |
  - 2 | | |
  - 3 | | |
  - 4 | | |
  - 5 | | |
note: A model chain: 1. The volcano erupts, so ash and gas blast into the sky (geosphere to atmosphere). 2. Sulfur dioxide makes a haze that reflects sunlight, so Earth cools for about two years (atmosphere). 3. Ash falls and buries forests and fields, so animals lose food and homes (biosphere). 4. Rain mixes with the loose ash, so lahars of mud fill river valleys and rivers (hydrosphere and geosphere). 5. Rain fills the crater, so a new lake forms, and plants slowly grow back in the ash (hydrosphere and biosphere). Accept any chain where each effect becomes the next cause and each link is supported by the passage.

## Explain
write: Pick one link in your chain where a change in one sphere caused a change in another sphere. Explain it, and name the paragraph that gives the evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: In paragraph 4, rain from the hydrosphere fell on ash from the geosphere and made lahars, which buried villages and filled rivers with mud. A change in the geosphere (ash) and the hydrosphere (rain) together changed the land and rivers.

## Stretch
write: Paragraph 5 says life came back slowly. How did the hydrosphere and geosphere help the biosphere recover?
  lines: 2
  key: Rain gave seeds water to sprout, and the ash on the ground became a place for new plants to grow (volcanic ash can make rich soil over time). The new crater lake gave water too.
