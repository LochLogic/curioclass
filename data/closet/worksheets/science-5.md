# 1. Where Did the Salt Go?
minutes: 40
materials: table salt, warm tap water, a clear plastic cup, a spoon, a dropper or straw, black or dark blue construction paper, a hand lens, safety goggles, a kitchen scale (optional), pencil
ican: I can use a particle model to explain what happens when salt dissolves and the water evaporates.
directions: Look at salt grains with a hand lens. Stir one spoonful of salt into half a cup of warm water until you cannot see it. Put 10 drops of the salt water on dark paper and let them dry. Record what you see, then explain it with particles.
mistakes: Students often say the salt "melted" or "disappeared." Melting needs heat and turns a solid into a liquid of the same substance; here the salt breaks into particles too small to see that spread out between the water particles. Watch for drawings that show the salt particles vanishing or shrinking: they are all still there.
notes: Allow 1 to 2 days for the drops to dry before the last observation. Never taste lab materials, even salt. Wear goggles while stirring and dropping. Typical results: after 1 to 2 days the drops leave white rings or crusts; with a hand lens, many grains look like tiny cubes. With a scale (optional): typical class data are cup and water 210 g, salt 15 g, and the mixture 225 g, so no mass is lost. Small differences of 1 g can come from spills or the scale.

## Before
write: Look at dry salt with the hand lens. Draw or describe the grains.
  lines: 2
  key: Small white, clear grains; many have flat sides and square corners, like tiny cubes.
write: Predict: Where will the salt go when you stir it into water?
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Observe
table: Step | What I see | Mass (g, optional)
  - Salt grains under the hand lens | |
  - Salt stirred into water, after 2 minutes | |
  - Drops on dark paper, right away | |
  - Drops on dark paper, after 1 to 2 days | |
fill:
  - After stirring, can you see the salt? {no}
  - After the drops dry, what is left on the paper? {white salt crystals}
  - Typical mass before mixing (cup and water 210 g + salt 15 g): {225} g. Typical mass after mixing: about {225} g.

## Explain with particles
draw: Draw a model of the salt water. Use dots for salt particles and circles for water particles. | 2
choice: Why can you not see the salt after it dissolves?
  - It turned into water.
  - It melted.
  * It broke into particles too small to see, spread out in the water.
  - It floated away into the air.
write: Where did the salt crystals on the paper come from? Use the word particles.
  lines: 3
  key: The water particles evaporated into the air, but the salt particles could not. They were left behind on the paper and joined together into crystals we can see.
write: What does the mass tell you about the salt in the water?
  lines: 2
  key: The mass stayed the same, so the salt was still there, even though we could not see it. Matter is not lost when it dissolves.

## Stretch
write: How could you get salt back from a whole cup of salt water? What would you expect to find?
  lines: 2
  key: Leave the cup in a warm, sunny place until all the water evaporates. Salt crystals would be left at the bottom, about the same amount as you put in.

# 21. Matter Goes Around
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can trace how matter moves among plants, animals, decomposers and the environment.
directions: Read the cycle clues. Fill in the organizer to trace matter from air and soil into a plant, a rabbit, a fox, and back to the soil and air. Then draw it as a cycle.
mistakes: The most common idea is that a plant's mass comes from the soil. Most of a plant's mass is built from carbon dioxide from the air and water; soil gives only small amounts of minerals. Students also leave out decomposers, so the cycle never closes.
notes: Energy flows in from the Sun and does not cycle; matter cycles. Accept "germs" or "microbes" for bacteria. Decomposers also release carbon dioxide back to the air as they break down dead matter, as do plants and animals when they use food for energy.

## Warm up: Word bank
example: Word bank: carbon dioxide, water, minerals, sugar, eats, decomposers, soil, air
fill:
  - A plant takes in {carbon dioxide} from the air and {water} from the soil.
  - The plant uses energy from sunlight to make {sugar}, which it uses to grow.
  - Bacteria, fungi and worms are {decomposers}. They break down dead plants and animals.

## Main: Trace the matter
table: Step | Where matter is | How it moves to the next step
  - 1 | Air and soil | {a plant takes in carbon dioxide, water and minerals}
  - 2 | A plant | {a rabbit eats the plant}
  - 3 | A rabbit | {a fox eats the rabbit}
  - 4 | A fox | {the fox's waste, and its body when it dies, fall to the ground}
  - 5 | Dead matter and waste | {decomposers break it down}
  - 6 | Decomposers | {they return minerals to the soil and carbon dioxide to the air}
draw: Draw the six steps as a circle with arrows going around. | 2.4

## Check
choice: Where does most of the matter in a plant's body come from?
  - the soil
  * carbon dioxide from the air, and water
  - sunlight
  - fertilizer
choice: What would happen to the soil if there were no decomposers?
  * Dead matter would pile up and minerals would not return to the soil.
  - Plants would grow faster.
  - Nothing would change.
write: The fox does not eat plants. Explain how matter from a plant still ends up in the fox.
  lines: 2
  key: The rabbit ate the plant and used its matter to build its body. When the fox ate the rabbit, that matter moved into the fox.

## Stretch
write: The rabbit also breathes out carbon dioxide. Add this to your cycle: where does that matter go next?
  lines: 2
  key: Into the air, where a plant can take it in again to make sugar. It is a shortcut in the cycle.
