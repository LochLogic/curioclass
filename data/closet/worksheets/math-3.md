# 1. Equal Groups Everywhere
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a multiplication equation for equal groups.
directions: Each box is one group. Count how many groups there are and how many are in each group. Then write the multiplication equation. Last, explain one picture in words.
mistakes: Students often write the factors in the wrong roles (5 groups of 2 for 2 groups of 5). In this unit, the first factor is the number of groups and the second is the number in each group, though both give the same product. Some students add the groups (5 + 5 = 10): that is correct, so connect it: "two fives, 2 x 5".
notes: Write "groups" and "in each group" on the board with arrows to the two factors. Before the sheet, show 4 bags of 6 apples with real objects or a drawing: 4 groups of 6, 4 x 6 = 24 apples. For picture 8, accept 6 x 3 = 18 with any clear explanation; students who write 3 x 6 should say which number is the groups.

# 6. Break Apart Hard Facts
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can break apart a hard multiplication fact into two easier facts.
directions: Each hard fact is broken into a 5s fact and a small fact. Draw the array, draw a line after the fifth row, and fill in the two easier facts. Then add the parts. In Part B, solve the facts in your head.
mistakes: The most common slip is splitting both factors (7 × 8 as 5 × 5 + 2 × 3), which loses most of the array. Only one factor is broken apart; the other stays the same in both parts. Watch also for children who multiply the parts correctly but forget to add them.
notes: The rows of each array are the first factor, so 7 × 8 is 7 rows of 8, split into 5 rows of 8 and 2 rows of 8. Children do not need to draw every dot: a rectangle with a line across it and the side lengths labeled is a fine array sketch. In Part B, any break-apart is fine; 8 × 8 can be 5 × 8 + 3 × 8 or 8 × 4 + 8 × 4.

## Warm up
example: 7 × 8: 7 rows of 8. Break the 7 into 5 + 2. 5 × 8 = 40 and 2 × 8 = 16. 40 + 16 = 56, so 7 × 8 = 56.

## Stretch: Another way

# 11. Odd and Even Products Color by Code
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, a yellow and a light blue crayon
ican: I can multiply and tell if a product is odd or even.
directions: Find each product. Then color each square of the picture: odd products yellow, even products light blue. Last, write the rule you notice.
mistakes: Students who color by the factors instead of the product (7 x 4 colored yellow because 7 is odd) need to look at the answer, not the problem. A wrong product shows up as a stray square in the picture, which makes a good self-check.
notes: The picture is a star. Squares are lettered; each letter's product decides the color, so a student can check their facts by whether the star looks right.

## Warm up: Odd or even?
fill:
  - 6 is {even}
  - 9 is {odd}
  - 14 is {even}
  - 21 is {odd}

## Main: Solve and color
colorcode: Find each product, then color the squares with that letter.
  code: y = yellow: odd | b = light blue: even
  problems: A 3 x 5, B 4 x 6, C 7 x 9, D 6 x 7, E 5 x 5, F 2 x 9, G 9 x 3, H 5 x 8, I 7 x 7, J 6 x 6, K 1 x 9, L 4 x 9, M 5 x 7, N 8 x 7, O 3 x 3, P 2 x 5, Q 9 x 9, R 6 x 5, S 7 x 3, T 8 x 3

## Stretch: Write the rule
write: Look at the yellow problems. When is a product odd? When is it even?
  lines: 3
  starter: A product is odd when ...
  key: A product is odd only when both factors are odd (3 x 5). If either factor is even, the product is even (4 x 9, 6 x 7).

# 17. Times Tens
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can multiply a one-digit number by a multiple of 10 using place value.
directions: Think of each multiple of 10 as a number of tens. Multiply the tens, then write the product. Last, explain one problem in words.
mistakes: Students who learn "just add a zero" get stuck on 5 × 40 and 5 × 60, where the basic fact already ends in zero (5 × 4 = 20, so 5 × 40 = 200 has two zeros). Keep the language of tens: 5 × 4 tens = 20 tens = 200. Watch for 6 × 40 written as 2,400 or 64.
notes: The "Think" column is the place-value step the standard asks for (3.NBT.3). Problems 4 and 12 have basic facts that end in zero. For the explanation, look for "tens": 20 tens is 200 because 10 tens make one hundred.

## Warm up
example: 6 × 40. 40 is 4 tens. 6 × 4 tens = 24 tens. 24 tens = 240. So 6 × 40 = 240.
fill:
  - 3 × 2 tens = {6} tens = {60}
  - 7 × 3 tens = {21} tens = {210}

## Main: Multiply by tens
table: # | Problem | Think | Product
  - 1 | 3 × 70 | 3 × 7 tens = {21} tens | {210}
  - 2 | 8 × 20 | 8 × 2 tens = {16} tens | {160}
  - 3 | 9 × 30 | 9 × 3 tens = {27} tens | {270}
  - 4 | 5 × 60 | 5 × 6 tens = {30} tens | {300}
  - 5 | 4 × 80 | 4 × 8 tens = {32} tens | {320}
  - 6 | 7 × 50 | 7 × 5 tens = {35} tens | {350}
  - 7 | 2 × 90 | 2 × 9 tens = {18} tens | {180}
  - 8 | 8 × 60 | 8 × 6 tens = {48} tens | {480}
  - 9 | 6 × 70 | 6 × 7 tens = {42} tens | {420}

  - 11 | 6 × 40 | 6 × 4 tens = {24} tens | {240}
  - 12 | 5 × 40 | 5 × 4 tens = {20} tens | {200}
write: Pick one problem. Explain how you found the product using tens.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample for 5 × 40: 40 is 4 tens. 5 groups of 4 tens is 20 tens. 10 tens make 100, so 20 tens make 200. 5 × 40 = 200.

## Stretch: Zeros puzzle
write: Why does 5 × 40 have two zeros at the end, but 6 × 40 has only one?
  lines: 2
  key: Because 5 × 4 = 20 already ends in a zero, so 20 tens = 200. 6 × 4 = 24 does not end in zero, so 24 tens = 240 has only the one zero from the tens.

# 28. Kitchen Measure Problems
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can solve word problems about liquid volume and mass.
directions: Read each kitchen problem. Write an equation with a symbol for the unknown, then solve it. Write the unit (liters, grams or kilograms) with your answer.
mistakes: Students often grab the operation from a key word. Problem 6 shares equally, so it is division, and problem 8 asks how much was used, so it is subtraction. Watch for missing units, and for students who mix grams and kilograms in the same problem.
notes: All six are one-step problems, one or more with each operation: problem 4 subtracts, 5 adds, 6 divides, 7 multiplies, 8 subtracts and 9 divides. Accept any true equation for the same model (for problem 6, 6 × ? = 24 is fine). A liter is about a large water bottle; a kilogram is about a bag of sugar; a gram is about a paper clip.

## Warm up: Which unit?
choice: Which is the better estimate for the mass of a bag of flour?
  - 2 grams
  * 2 kilograms
choice: Which is the better estimate for the water in a full kitchen sink?
  * 30 liters
  - 30 grams
choice: Which is the better estimate for the mass of a grape?
  - 5 kilograms
  - 5 liters
  * 5 grams

## Main: Kitchen problems
example: A baker uses 4 bags of flour. Each bag has a mass of 3 kilograms. 4 × 3 = ?, ? = 12. The baker uses 12 kilograms of flour.
fill: A pot holds 8 liters of soup. The cook pours out 3 liters. How many liters are left? Equation: {8 - 3 = ?} Answer: {5 liters}
fill: A bowl of rice has a mass of 350 grams. The cook adds 250 grams of beans. What is the mass of the rice and beans? Equation: {350 + 250 = ?} Answer: {600 grams}
fill: A cook pours 24 liters of water equally into 6 jugs. How many liters are in each jug? Equation: {24 ÷ 6 = ?} Answer: {4 liters}
fill: A recipe uses 3 liters of milk for each batch of pudding. How many liters of milk are needed for 7 batches? Equation: {7 × 3 = ?} Answer: {21 liters}
fill: A crate of apples has a mass of 15 kilograms. After some apples are used, the crate has a mass of 9 kilograms. What mass of apples was used? Equation: {15 - 9 = ?} Answer: {6 kilograms}
fill: A chef shares 36 kilograms of potatoes equally into 9 bags. What is the mass of each bag? Equation: {36 ÷ 9 = ?} Answer: {4 kilograms}

## Stretch: Write your own
write: Write a kitchen problem about liters that you would solve with division. Then solve it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: 18 liters of lemonade are poured equally into 3 coolers. How many liters are in each cooler? 18 ÷ 3 = 6, so 6 liters.

# 39. Same Perimeter, Different Area
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencils
ican: I can find rectangles with the same perimeter and different areas.
directions: Draw every rectangle with a perimeter of 16 units on the grid, using whole-number side lengths. Record each one in the table. Then circle the rectangle with the largest area.
mistakes: Students often count the squares along the edge instead of the edges themselves, especially at corners. Have them count unit lengths, not squares. Some students list 2 by 6 and 6 by 2 as different rectangles; they are the same rectangle turned, so there are four. Others forget that a square is a rectangle and miss 4 by 4.
notes: The four rectangles are 1 by 7 (area 7), 2 by 6 (area 12), 3 by 5 (area 15) and 4 by 4 (area 16). The key shows them on the grid; students can place them anywhere. The pattern: the closer the sides are to each other, the larger the area, and the square has the most. Rectangles in any order in the table are fine.

## Warm up
example: A rectangle 3 units long and 2 units wide has perimeter 3 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 10 units and area 3 × 2 = 6 square units.
fill:
  - A 5 by 2 rectangle has perimeter {14} units and area {10} square units.
  - A 4 by 3 rectangle has perimeter {14} units and area {12} square units.

## Stretch: Predict
write: A rectangle has a perimeter of 20 units. Which whole-number rectangle has the largest area? Explain how you know.
  lines: 3
  key: 5 by 5, with area 25 square units. Length and width add to 10, and the area is largest when the sides are equal: 1 × 9 = 9, 2 × 8 = 16, 3 × 7 = 21, 4 × 6 = 24, 5 × 5 = 25.
