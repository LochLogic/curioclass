# 1. Trifles: Reading the Room
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can find the stage directions and objects that set a play's place and mood.
directions: Read the opening of Trifles. Highlight every object in the kitchen. Then answer the questions about how the stage directions and objects build the place and the mood.
mistakes: Students often list objects without saying what they suggest. The half-done jobs suggest work suddenly stopped; the cold suggests the house has been empty and unloved. Watch for students who miss the staging of the women: where they stand is itself a stage direction about mood and power.
notes: The excerpt is adapted and shortened by Curio Class from Susan Glaspell's one-act play Trifles (1916, public domain); it paraphrases the opening stage directions and dialogue rather than quoting them, and one stretch of dialogue is summarized in brackets. The play centers on the investigation of a farmer's death; the excerpt stays with the opening and does not describe the death. About 300 words. The title comes from Mr. Hale's dismissive line: the women's "trifles" turn out to matter most.

## Read
passage: Trifles (opening, adapted from Susan Glaspell)
  (The kitchen of John Wright's farmhouse, now empty of its family. It is a gloomy room, left exactly as it was: dirty pans under the sink, a loaf of bread sitting outside the bread box, a dish towel lying on the table, and other jobs left half done. It is a bitter winter morning. The back door opens. The SHERIFF comes in, followed by the COUNTY ATTORNEY and MR. HALE, a farmer from down the road. The men are bundled up against the cold and go straight to the stove to warm their hands. Two women follow them: MRS. PETERS, the sheriff's wife, a small, thin, nervous woman, and MRS. HALE, larger and usually more at ease, who looks around the room with worry. The women come in slowly and stand close together near the door.)
  COUNTY ATTORNEY: (rubbing his hands) This feels good. Come up to the fire, ladies.
  MRS. PETERS: (one step forward) I'm not cold.
  SHERIFF: Nothing has been moved since yesterday morning. I sent my deputy over to light the stove, that's all.
  COUNTY ATTORNEY: Mr. Hale, tell us what you saw when you came here yesterday.
  (MR. HALE explains that he stopped at the house and found Mrs. Wright sitting in her rocking chair, twisting her apron, acting strangely. Her husband was dead upstairs. She is now held in the county jail.)
  MRS. PETERS: (looking at the cupboard) Oh, her fruit! It froze in the cold, and the jars have cracked. She worried the fire would go out and her jars would break.
  SHERIFF: Well, can you beat the women! A serious matter like this, and she is worrying about her preserves.
  MR. HALE: Well, women are used to worrying over trifles.
  (The two women move a little closer together.)

## Warm up
fill: The play takes place in the {kitchen} of a farmhouse.
fill: The season is {winter}, and the room is very {cold}.

## Main: Reading the room
table: Object or stage direction | What it suggests about the place or the people
  - Dirty pans under the sink | {work stopped suddenly; the house was left in a hurry}
  - Bread outside the bread box | {a chore left half done; something interrupted daily life}
  - The cold room and the men going straight to the stove | {the house has been empty; it feels harsh and unwelcoming}
  - The women standing close together near the door | {they feel uneasy, like outsiders; they stick together}
  - The cracked jars of frozen fruit | {Mrs. Wright's hard work is ruined; her care for her home}
write: Glaspell calls the room "gloomy." Name two other details that build a gloomy mood.
  lines: 2
  key: Any two: the bitter cold; the half-finished chores; the empty house; the nervous women standing by the door; the frozen, cracked jars.
choice: Where do the men go first, and what does that show?
  * Straight to the stove; they take over the room and think about their own comfort.
  - Upstairs; they are eager to see the scene.
  - To the cupboard; they care about the preserves.
  - Back outside; they are afraid of the house.
write: Mr. Hale says women "worry over trifles." What does he mean, and why do you think Glaspell made this word the title?
  lines: 3
  key: He means small, unimportant things like preserves. Glaspell uses the word as the title to make the audience question him; the "trifles" in the kitchen turn out to be the important clues, and the women who notice them see more than the men.

## Stretch
write: You are the set designer. Choose one object to add to the kitchen that would deepen the mood, and explain what it tells the audience.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: a rocking chair still facing the window (someone sat alone here for hours); a calendar not turned to the new month (time stopped); a single cup on the table (loneliness).

# 7. Costume Plot With Research
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can build a costume plot from period research that shows each character's look and condition.
directions: Read the research notes on rural clothing in 1916 and the clues from Trifles. Fill in the costume plot for each character. Note the condition of the clothes (new, worn, mended) and the research note that supports your choice.
mistakes: Students often give every character the same "old-fashioned" outfit. The men's jobs, the women's ages and moods, and the cold all change what each one wears. Watch for condition left blank; whether clothes are new, worn or mended tells the audience about money and care, which matters in this play. Check for modern details (zippers, short skirts, jeans).
notes: Trifles by Susan Glaspell (1916) is in the public domain. Clues in the script, paraphrased: the men come in bundled up against the cold and go straight to the stove; the County Attorney is young, the Sheriff and Mr. Hale are middle-aged; Mrs. Peters is slight and nervous, Mrs. Hale larger and more comfortable looking. Minnie Wright is never seen, but the women gather an apron and a shawl to take to her, and Mrs. Hale remembers that Minnie wore pretty clothes and sang in the choir before she married, and says a person does not enjoy things when she feels shabby. The research notes are a Curio Class summary of rural Midwestern dress of the mid-1910s. The key is a model.

## Read: Research notes, rural Midwest, 1916
text: **1. Farmers in winter.** Wool trousers or bib overalls, flannel shirts, heavy wool or sheepskin-lined coats, wool caps or felt hats, scarves, thick gloves, boots. Work clothes were patched and mended until they wore out.
text: **2. Town officials.** A three-piece wool suit with a starched collar and tie, a long dark overcoat and a felt hat. A sheriff usually wore an ordinary suit with a badge, not a uniform.
text: **3. Farm and town women.** Wool skirts reaching to about the ankle, a long-sleeved blouse called a shirtwaist, sturdy laced boots, a long wool coat, a hat, a shawl or a knitted scarf in cold weather. Clothes were made over and mended; a new coat or hat was a big event.
text: **4. Clues from the script.** It is bitter cold. The men are bundled up and hurry to the stove. The County Attorney is young; the Sheriff and Mr. Hale are middle-aged. Mrs. Peters is slight and nervous; Mrs. Hale is sturdier and more comfortable looking. Minnie has had no new clothes in years.

## Warm up
choice: Why would the Sheriff wear a suit and badge instead of a uniform?
  - Uniforms had not been invented.
  * The research says a 1916 sheriff usually wore an ordinary suit with a badge.
  - He forgot his uniform at home.
  - He wants to hide that he is the sheriff.
choice: What does a costume plot show?
  - The lighting cues for each scene
  * Every character's costume pieces, scene by scene
  - Where each actor stands
  - The music for the show

## Main: Costume plot (one scene, a winter morning)

  labels: County Attorney (young); Sheriff Peters; Mr. Hale (farmer); Mrs. Peters; Mrs. Hale; Minnie's apron and shawl (props)
  height: 0.8
  key: Model. County Attorney: dark three-piece suit, starched collar, long overcoat and felt hat; new and well pressed, he is young and ambitious; note 2. Sheriff: brown wool suit with a badge on the vest, overcoat, scarf; worn but neat; note 2. Mr. Hale: overalls, flannel shirt, sheepskin-lined coat, wool cap; patched and muddy at the knees; notes 1 and 4. Mrs. Peters: dark wool skirt, plain shirtwaist, thin coat, small hat; neat but not warm enough, she is nervous and careful; notes 3 and 4. Mrs. Hale: gray wool skirt, shawl over her coat, knitted scarf; warm and comfortable, an apron edge showing because she left her work in a hurry; notes 3 and 4. write: Choose one character. How will the condition of their clothes help the audience understand them?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Minnie's faded, often-mended apron and thin shawl show years without anything new, which helps the audience understand Mrs. Hale's line about feeling shabby and how lonely Minnie's life has been.

## Stretch
write: The men and the women are on stage together at the start. How could color and fabric set the two groups apart before anyone speaks?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Dress the men in heavy, dark, bulky coats that fill space by the stove, and the women in softer grays and browns with shawls, close together by the door, so the audience sees two groups before the play shows how differently they see the kitchen.

# 20. Composition Exercise Log
minutes: 15
materials: pencil
ican: I can record composition exercises and choose moments to keep for a devised piece.
directions: A composition exercise gives a group a short time and a list of rules, called constraints, to make a piece. After each session, log the constraint, what your group made and the moments worth keeping.
mistakes: Students often write the topic instead of the constraint. A constraint is a rule the group had to follow ("include 15 seconds of silence, one repeated gesture and a surprise entrance"). Watch for "keep" notes that are too vague to rebuild later; a good note says who did what, where, and when.
notes: Composition work is common in devising: the group gets a theme or source, a list of required ingredients and a time limit (often 10 to 20 minutes), then shows the piece. Plan about 5 minutes after each of 4 sessions. The key shows model entries from a sample group working on an issue piece about a closing library; logs will differ.

## Example
example: **Constraint:** 15 minutes; must include a moment of total stillness, one repeated line and an object used in two ways. **What we made:** a 2-minute piece where a library cart became a car, then a hospital bed. **Keep:** the stillness when the lights "go out"; the line "Is anyone still here?" repeated three times.

## Warm up
choice: Which of these is a constraint?
  - Our piece was about friendship.
  * Use only three spoken words and one chair.
  - We liked the ending.
  - Sam was the main character.
write: Why might limits make a group more creative instead of less?
  lines: 2
  key: Limits force the group to find new solutions quickly instead of falling back on the first idea; with fewer tools, every choice has to count.

## Main: Session log
chart: Constraint | What the group made | Moments to keep (who, what, when)
  labels: Session 1; Session 2; Session 3; Session 4
  height: 1.1
  key: Model. 1: 10 minutes, a piece with no words and a sound made only with bodies; a "storm" of stamping that turns into a crowd leaving the library; keep the stamping fading to one person. 2: must use a list of 5 objects and begin at the end; a piece that opens with an empty shelf and goes back to the day the books arrived; keep the opening image. 3: include a song, a fall and a reveal; the librarian sings while stacking books; keep the reveal that the books are being donated. 4: everyone speaks one line from a real interview; a chorus of voices about the library; keep Lee's line about learning to read there, spoken in silence.

## Stretch
write: Which kept moment would you use as the opening of your final piece? Explain why it would hook the audience.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one moment and gives a reason: it raises a question, it creates a strong image, or it sets the mood of the whole piece.

# 21. Talk to the Audience?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare ways a devised piece can involve its audience and choose one with reasons.
directions: Read how each technique works. Fill in the pros and cons chart. Then choose the technique that best fits your group's devised scene and explain why.
mistakes: Students often list "fun" as the only pro. Push for what each technique does for the story or the audience. Watch for confusing a talkback (a conversation after the show) with direct address (characters speaking to the audience during the show).
notes: Direct address: a character speaks straight to the audience, breaking the fourth wall. Audience vote: the audience chooses what happens next, by a show of hands, cards or applause. Talkback: after the performance, the actors (and sometimes the director) answer questions and discuss the piece with the audience. The key's pros and cons are models; accept other accurate points.

## Warm up: Match
match: Match each technique to how it works.
  - Direct address = A character speaks straight to the audience during the scene
  - Audience vote = The audience chooses what happens next
  - Talkback = The audience and the cast discuss the piece after it ends

## Main: Pros and cons
table: Technique | Pros | Cons
  - Direct address | {draws the audience in, shares a character's private thoughts} | {breaks the illusion; can feel like a lecture if overused}
  - Audience vote | {audience feels powerful, every show is different} | {cast must rehearse every possible path; votes can be slow or silly}
  - Talkback | {audience can ask questions and share reactions; cast learns how the piece landed} | {happens after the story, so it does not change the experience during the scene; some people leave}
write: Our devised scene is about:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example a student deciding whether to report a friend who cheated.
write: The technique I choose, and two reasons it fits our scene:
  lines: 4
  key: Model: An audience vote, because the scene is about a hard choice, so letting the audience decide makes them feel the weight of it; and the two endings show the different results of each choice. A talkback after would also work for a serious topic.

## Stretch
write: Choose a technique you did not pick. How could you change your scene so that technique would work well?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: add a moment where the main character turns to the audience and admits what she is afraid of (direct address), or plan five questions to open a talkback about what the audience would have done.

# 33. Who Played Juliet?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how casting traditions shape what an audience sees and believes on stage.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight each casting tradition and the year or place it belongs to. Then answer the questions, citing paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students often think boy actors played women because "women were not allowed outside." The rule was about the public stage, not daily life. Watch for answers that treat one tradition as correct and the others as mistakes; the question is what each tradition makes the audience notice.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 370 words). Romeo and Juliet dates from about 1595. The first professional actress on the English stage is usually said to have played Desdemona in December 1660; Margaret Hughes is the name most often given, though historians debate it. Royal licenses for the new theaters in the early 1660s allowed, and encouraged, women to play women's parts. Charlotte Cushman played Romeo in London in 1845, with her sister Susan as Juliet. Women were barred from the kabuki stage in Japan in 1629, and male actors who specialize in women's roles, called onnagata, still perform today.

## Read
passage: Who Played Juliet? | numbered
  When Romeo and Juliet was first performed, around 1595, the audience saw Juliet played by a boy. In England at that time, women did not perform on the public stage. Acting companies trained boys, usually in their early or middle teens, to play women's roles: Juliet, her mother and her Nurse, queens and heroines. A good boy actor was prized, and a company might plan its plays around the talents of its best ones. Since Juliet is thirteen in the play, the boy may have been close to her age.
  Audiences of the time accepted this as normal. They did not watch to forget that an actor was playing a part. Shakespeare even jokes about it: in some plays, a female character disguises herself as a young man, so the audience watched a boy playing a girl playing a boy.
  After the English theaters were closed for almost twenty years, they reopened in 1660 with a new rule. Women could now act, and in December 1660 an actress played Desdemona in Othello. Within a few years, audiences expected to see women in women's roles, and actresses became stars.
  Casting kept changing. In 1845, the American actress Charlotte Cushman played Romeo in London, with her sister Susan as Juliet. Critics praised the passion of her Romeo, and audiences came to see a famous woman in a man's role.
  Other theater traditions made different choices. In Japan, women were barred from the kabuki stage in 1629. Male actors called onnagata trained for years to play women through movement, voice and costume, and the tradition continues today. For kabuki audiences, the onnagata's art is a skill to admire, not a disguise.
  In recent decades, some theaters have staged all-male Shakespeare to explore how the plays first looked. Others have staged all-female productions or cast actors of any gender in any role. Each choice changes what the audience notices. An all-male cast can make an audience think about how Shakespeare's first actors worked. An all-female cast can make familiar lines about power or love sound new.
  Casting is never just about who is available. It tells the audience what to pay attention to, what to accept as natural, and what to question.

## Stretch
write: You are casting Romeo and Juliet for your school. Choose one casting approach from the passage or your own, and explain what it would make your audience notice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the approach and connects it to the audience: for example, casting any gender in any role so the audience focuses on the young lovers' feelings and the families' feud rather than on who is playing whom.
