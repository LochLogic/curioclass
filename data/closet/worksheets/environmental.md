# 1. Biome Climatographs
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler, two colored pencils
ican: I can build climatographs from monthly data and use them to identify biomes.
directions: Use the data to find each city's yearly precipitation and temperature range and match it to its biome. Then build climatographs for cities B and D on the grids. Draw precipitation as a bar for each month (read the scale as centimeters) and temperature as a line of points (read the scale as °C).
mistakes: Students often plot temperature and precipitation against the wrong scale, or join the precipitation values into a line. Bars for rain, a line for temperature. Watch the desert: its precipitation bars are tiny, under 3 cm, and that is the point. Students also call any cold place tundra; Fairbanks gets warm summers above 15 °C, which supports forest.
notes: The cities, in order: A Washington, D.C. (temperate deciduous forest), B Phoenix, Arizona (hot desert), C Manaus, Brazil (tropical rainforest), D Fairbanks, Alaska (taiga, or boreal forest). Values are rounded monthly averages, about 1991 to 2020 normals; check against the national weather services if exact numbers matter. To keep the sheet to two pages, students build full climatographs for the two cities that are easiest to confuse (B and D, both dry); A and C are read from the table. Extra grid paper lets students build all four. The key graphs show the temperature line only; precipitation bars should match the table. Both use the same scale numbers: °C for temperature, cm for precipitation.

## Stretch
write: City C has a "drier season" from June to October. Why is it still a rainforest and not a grassland?
  lines: 2
  key: Even its driest months get about 6 to 12 cm, and the yearly total is about 237 cm, with warm temperatures all year. That is enough water year-round for dense forest; savanna grasslands have a long dry season with much less rain.

# 5. Schoolyard Species Survey
minutes: 50
materials: 3 square quadrats (1 m by 1 m, string and 4 stakes or a hoop of known area), hand lens, pencil, clipboard, field guide or plant and insect ID app on a teacher device (optional)
ican: I can survey plants and insects with quadrats and compare species richness between habitats.
directions: Your teacher will mark three zones. In each zone, toss or place the quadrat 3 times without choosing a "good" spot. In each quadrat, count how many **different kinds** of plants and insects you see in 3 minutes (you do not need their names: "small white flower" is fine). Look, do not collect. Then work the sample data.
mistakes: Students often count individuals (12 ants) instead of kinds (1 kind of ant); species richness counts kinds. Watch for groups that place the quadrat on the most interesting spot; random placement keeps the comparison fair. Students also compare one quadrat from each zone and call it proven; three quadrats and an average are better, and more would be better still.
notes: Zones that work well: a mowed lawn, an unmowed edge or garden bed, and a shady spot under trees. Species richness is the number of species in an area. Sample data are made up but typical: mowed lawns hold few plant species, so they support few insects; an unmowed edge with flowers has the most. Safety: check the zones first for poison ivy, broken glass and stinging insect nests; know which students have bee sting allergies; students do not pick up insects or touch unknown plants, and wash hands afterward. Release anything moved for a closer look exactly where it was found.

## Warm up
fill:
  - **Species richness** is the number of different {species (kinds of organisms)} in an area.
  - We use the same size quadrat in every zone so that the comparison is {fair}.
text: Prediction: Which zone will have the most kinds of plants and insects? Why?
lines: 2

  - A. Mowed lawn | 3 | 4 | 3 | {3.3}

choice: Which zone in the sample data had the greatest species richness?
  - the mowed lawn
  * the unmowed edge with wildflowers
  - the shady ground under trees
  - All three were the same.
write: Explain why zone B had about three times as many species as zone A.
  lines: 3
  key: Mowing keeps only a few kinds of grass that can survive being cut and removes flowers. The unmowed edge has many kinds of plants, including flowers, which give insects food (nectar, pollen, leaves) and shelter, so more kinds of insects live there too. More plant variety supports more animal variety.
write: Give one reason your class's results might not show the true richness of each zone.
  lines: 2
  key: Only 3 small quadrats per zone; 3 minutes is short, so small or hidden insects are missed; flying insects leave; different groups may call the same species two kinds (or two species one kind); time of day and weather change what is active.

## Stretch
write: The school wants more pollinators. Using your data, propose one change to the schoolyard and predict its effect on species richness.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: leave a strip of lawn unmowed and plant native wildflowers. Based on zone B, richness there should rise from about 3 to about 10 kinds per quadrat, including more bees and other pollinators. Survey again next year to test it.

# 15. Fix the Population Graph
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, red pen or pencil
ican: I can check a graph and claims about human population growth against real data and correct the mistakes.
directions: A student made this graph of world population and wrote six claims about it. Compare the student's data with the real data. Correct the graph, then mark each claim right or wrong and fix the wrong ones using the real data.
mistakes: Students often mix up the growth rate (percent per year) with the number of people added. The rate peaked in the 1960s, yet about 70 million people are still added each year, because 1 percent of 8 billion is large. Watch for students who say the population "will" reach a number; projections are estimates that depend on future birth rates. Students also treat carrying capacity as a fixed number for Earth; for people it changes with technology, diet and how resources are used.
notes: Real data are rounded from United Nations estimates (World Population Prospects): about 1.0 billion around 1800 to 1804, 1.3 in 1850, 1.6 in 1900, 2.5 in 1950, 4.1 in 1975, 6.1 in 2000 and 7.8 in 2020. Milestones: 2 billion in 1927, 4 billion in 1974, 8 billion in November 2022. The world growth rate peaked at a little over 2 percent per year in the 1960s and has fallen below 1 percent. The UN's 2024 projections show population peaking at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s. Update these numbers when the UN publishes a new revision.

## Main: Check the claims
text: **More real data (United Nations):** World population reached 1 billion around 1804, 2 billion in 1927, 4 billion in 1974 and 8 billion in 2022. The yearly growth rate was highest in the 1960s, a little over 2 percent, and is now under 1 percent. The UN projects a peak of about 10.3 billion in the 2080s.
table: # | Student's claim | Right or wrong? | Correction using the real data
  - 1 | "The graph shows population fell after 2000." | {wrong} | {it rose from 6.1 to 7.8 billion; the dip came from a bad data point}
  - 2 | "Population grew at about the same rate the whole time." | {wrong} | {it took about 123 years to go from 1 to 2 billion (1804 to 1927), then only 47 more to double to 4 (1974)}
  - 3 | "It took about 48 years to double from 4 to 8 billion." | {right} | {1974 to 2022 is 48 years}
  - 4 | "The growth rate is higher now than it has ever been." | {wrong} | {the rate peaked a little over 2 percent a year in the 1960s and is now under 1 percent}
  - 5 | "Carrying capacity is the most people Earth has ever held." | {wrong} | {it is the largest population an environment can support for the long term}
  - 6 | "The line will keep doubling every 48 years forever." | {wrong} | {UN projections show growth slowing, peaking near 10.3 billion in the 2080s}

## Stretch
write: Why is Earth's carrying capacity for humans hard to state as one number? Give two factors that could raise or lower it.
  lines: 3
  key: It depends on how people live, not only on how many there are. Better farming, water recycling and clean energy can raise it; high consumption, soil loss, water shortages, pollution and climate change can lower it. Diet matters too: plant-rich diets need less land per person.

# 21. Energy Source Life Cycle
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare the environmental costs of energy sources across their whole life cycle.
directions: For each energy source, fill in the life cycle chart: one box per source, from extraction (getting the resource) through processing, use and waste. Then use the emissions data to compare the sources.
mistakes: Students often rate solar and wind as having no impact because they burn nothing. Their impacts come before and after use: mining, manufacturing and disposal. Students also forget that natural gas leaks methane, a strong greenhouse gas, during extraction and transport.
notes: The emissions values are median life cycle estimates from the IPCC (2014), in grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour; newer studies give similar rankings. Accept any well-supported entries; the key lists typical ones. The chart is laid out as one writing box per source, with the four stages as a sentence starter, so there is room to write.

## Main: Life cycle chart
write: **Coal**
  starter: Extraction: ... Processing: ... Use: ... Waste: ...
  lines: 3
  key: Extraction by surface or underground mining (land disturbance, dust, acid mine drainage, methane); processing by crushing, washing and shipping by rail; use by burning in power plants to make steam (CO2, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, mercury, particles); waste is ash in ponds or landfills, and CO2.
write: **Natural gas**
  starter: Extraction: ... Processing: ... Use: ... Waste: ...
  lines: 3
  key: Extraction by drilling wells, often with hydraulic fracturing (water use, methane leaks); processing removes water and other gases, then pipelines and compressors move it; use by burning in turbines or furnaces (CO2, about half of coal per kWh, and nitrogen oxides); waste is CO2, leaked methane and salty wastewater.
write: **Solar**
  starter: Extraction: ... Processing: ... Use: ... Waste: ...
  lines: 3
  key: Extraction by mining quartz for silicon, plus silver, copper and aluminum; processing purifies silicon (energy-intensive) and builds cells and panels; use turns sunlight into electricity with no emissions, only in daylight; waste is old panels after about 25 to 30 years, partly recyclable.
write: **Wind**
  starter: Extraction: ... Processing: ... Use: ... Waste: ...
  lines: 3
  key: Extraction by mining iron for steel, copper, and rare earth metals for some magnets, plus making fiberglass and concrete; processing makes steel towers and blades, then transport and installation; use turns wind into electricity with no emissions, when the wind blows (some bird and bat deaths); waste is blades that are hard to recycle after about 20 to 25 years, while steel towers are recyclable.

  - Solar (utility scale) | 48

fill:

  - Switching 1,000 kWh from coal to natural gas would avoid about {330,000} g, or {330} kg, of CO2-eq.
choice: For solar panels, in which stage do most of the emissions happen?
  - use
  * extraction and processing (mining and manufacturing)
  - waste
choice: For coal, in which stage do most of the emissions happen?
  - extraction
  * use (burning)
  - waste

## Explain
write: A town must choose between a new natural gas plant and a wind farm with batteries. Using the chart and data, write a recommendation with two pieces of evidence and one trade-off.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: Choose wind with batteries. Its life cycle emissions (about 11 g per kWh) are far below natural gas (about 490 g), and it has no fuel to extract or burn, so no methane leaks or air pollution. Trade-off: wind is variable, so the town needs batteries or backup, and the blades are hard to recycle. A recommendation for gas is acceptable if it is supported, such as steady power on demand.

## Stretch
write: Which stage of a solar panel's life cycle would you improve first to lower its impact? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: processing, since purifying silicon uses a lot of energy; powering factories with clean electricity would cut emissions. Or waste: designing panels for recycling would recover glass, aluminum, silicon and silver.

# 26. Water Quality Testing
minutes: 45
materials: 3 water samples collected by the teacher (for example, tap water, a pond or stream, a rain barrel), pH and nitrate test strips, turbidity tube (or clear jars and a printed black X), gloves, goggles, timer
ican: I can test water samples for pH, nitrate and turbidity and compare the results with safe limits.
directions: Wear gloves and goggles. Dip each strip as its package says, wait the stated time, and match the color to the chart. For turbidity, pour the sample into the tube until you can no longer see the mark at the bottom, or rate the jar from clear to very cloudy. Record your data, then compare all results with the limits.
mistakes: Students often read the strip too early or too late; the color keeps changing, so time it. Check whether your nitrate strips report nitrate as nitrogen (nitrate-N) or as nitrate ion: the drinking water limit is 10 mg/L as nitrogen, which equals about 44 mg/L as nitrate ion. Students also call rainwater "polluted" for having a pH below 7; clean rain is naturally about 5.6 because carbon dioxide dissolves in it.
notes: Limits: EPA's legal limit for nitrate in drinking water is 10 mg/L as nitrogen; EPA's recommended pH range for drinking water is 6.5 to 8.5, and many streams' aquatic life criteria use 6.5 to 9. Treated drinking water must stay at or below 1 NTU of turbidity. There is no single turbidity limit for streams; the stream guide below is a rough classroom rule. Sample data are made up but typical: B is a stream below a fertilized field after rain. High nitrate in drinking water is especially dangerous for babies under 6 months. Safety: students never collect samples from deep or fast water; the teacher collects. Do not drink any sample; wash hands after.

## Warm up
table: Test | Safe or healthy range | Why it matters
  - pH | 6.5 to 8.5 for drinking water; 6.5 to 9 for streams | {too acidic or basic water harms fish and can dissolve metals from pipes}
  - Nitrate (as nitrogen) | 10 mg/L or less for drinking water | {it is dangerous for babies, and in streams it feeds algae blooms}
  - Turbidity (cloudiness) | 1 NTU or less for treated drinking water; streams: under 10 NTU is clear | {cloudy water blocks sunlight, clogs gills and can hide germs from disinfection}

table: Sample | pH | Nitrate-N (mg/L) | Turbidity (NTU) | Problems
  - A. Tap water | 7.6 | 0.5 | 0.2 | {none: meets all limits}
  - B. Stream below a farm field, after rain | 7.9 | 14 | 60 | {nitrate above 10; very cloudy}

  - A
  * B

write: Sample B was collected after a rainstorm. Explain where its nitrate and its cloudiness probably came from.
  lines: 3
  key: Rain washed fertilizer (and possibly manure) from the field into the stream, carrying dissolved nitrate. The same runoff carried loose soil, making the water muddy and raising the turbidity.
write: Should anyone drink sample C? Explain using the data.
  lines: 2

## Stretch
write: Propose one way a farm could lower the nitrate and turbidity in the stream, and explain how it works.
  lines: 3
  key: Plant a buffer strip of grass, shrubs and trees between the field and the stream. Plant roots take up nitrate and hold the soil, and the plants slow runoff so soil settles out before reaching the water. Applying less fertilizer, at the right time, also helps.

# 36. Dam Stakeholder Claim Cards
minutes: 40
materials: scissors, glue or tape, pencil
ican: I can sort stakeholder claims about a project into criteria, constraints and tradeoffs, and identify a conflict between stakeholders.
directions: A river valley is deciding whether to build a dam. Read each stakeholder's claim card. Cut out the cards and glue each one under the right heading. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often call any requirement a criterion. A criterion says what the dam should do to succeed (a goal you can measure); a constraint is a limit the design must stay within, whether it wants to or not (money, laws, geology). Tradeoffs give up some of one good thing to get more of another. Some cards could be argued two ways; accept a different column if the student's reason uses these definitions.
notes: The valley, stakeholders and numbers are made up. Key: criteria are the city water supply, the power for 50,000 homes and flood protection for farms; constraints are the budget, the fish passage law and the bedrock site; tradeoffs are flooding homes for water storage and a higher dam for power versus whitewater rafting. Real dam decisions weigh the same kinds of claims, and many older dams have added fish ladders or been removed to restore salmon runs.

## Definitions
example: **Criteria:** what the design must do to succeed. **Constraints:** limits the design has to work within. **Tradeoffs:** giving up some of one benefit to get more of another.

## Main: Sort the claims
sort: Glue each claim card under its heading.
  - Criteria: City water manager: "The reservoir must supply drinking water for 200,000 people through a dry summer."; Power company: "The dam should make enough electricity for 50,000 homes."; Downstream farmer: "It should hold back the spring floods that ruin our fields."
  - Constraints: State budget office: "The whole project cannot cost more than $400 million."; Wildlife agency: "By law, salmon must still be able to swim upstream to lay their eggs."; Geologist: "The only solid bedrock for the dam's base is at the narrow canyon, so it must go there."
  - Tradeoffs: Valley family: "Filling the reservoir floods 40 homes and an old town site so the city can store water."; River guide company: "A taller dam makes more power but ends the whitewater rafting below it, and our jobs with it."

## Think
write: Name one conflict between two stakeholders. Who are they, and what does each want?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: the power company wants a taller dam for more electricity, while the river guides want the river below to keep flowing for rafting. (Or: the city needs a full reservoir, which floods the valley families' homes; or the budget limit clashes with adding an expensive fish passage.)
write: Suggest one design change that could reduce the conflict you named. What tradeoff does your change create?
  lines: 3
  key: Example: build a shorter dam with timed water releases for rafting on summer weekends. It keeps some rafting jobs but makes less power and stores less water for the city.

## Stretch
write: The wildlife agency's claim is a constraint, not a criterion. Explain why, and say what would happen to a design that ignores it.
  lines: 2
  key: It is a legal limit the design must meet no matter what, not a goal to do well on. A design without fish passage would break the law and could not be built, no matter how well it met the other criteria.
