# 1. Ramp Height Cup Crash
minutes: 40
materials: for each group: a ramp (a grooved ruler, a cardboard tube cut in half, or a stiff folder), books to stack, a marble or small rubber ball, a paper cup with a doorway cut in its rim, a meter stick or ruler, tape, pencil
ican: I can use evidence to explain how the speed of an object relates to its energy.
directions: Set the cup upside down at the bottom of the ramp with its doorway facing the ramp. Release the ball from the top without pushing. Measure how far the cup slides. Do 3 trials at each of 3 ramp heights, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often push the ball to "help" it, which changes the energy. They also measure from different places each time: always measure from the bottom of the ramp to the front edge of the cup. Watch for changing two things at once, like the height and the ball.
notes: Heights of 5, 10 and 15 cm work well. Tape a start line on the ramp and a mark for the cup. Typical results: the cup slides farther each time the ramp goes up, roughly 2 times as far for twice the height. Typical class data (middle values on the key graph): 5 cm: 8, 9, 7 cm (middle 8); 10 cm: 16, 15, 17 (middle 16); 15 cm: 23, 25, 24 (middle 24). Real numbers depend on the cup, ball and floor. Marbles roll: sweep the floor area clear and collect marbles at the end.

## Plan
fill:
  - We change: {the ramp height}  We measure: {how far the cup slides}
write: List two things you must keep the same so the test is fair.
  lines: 1
  key: The same ball, the same cup, the same starting spot on the ramp, the same floor, and letting go without pushing.
write: Predict: What will happen to the cup's slide as the ramp gets higher? Why?
  lines: 1
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason. Many predict the cup slides farther because the ball goes faster.

## Data
table: Ramp height (cm) | Trial 1 (cm) | Trial 2 (cm) | Trial 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
  - 5 | | | |
  - 10 | | | |
  - 15 | | | |
graph: Ramp height (cm) | 0 20 5 | Cup slide (cm) | 0 40 5
  key: 5,8 10,16 15,24

## Explain
write: What pattern do you see in your data?
  lines: 2
  key: Typical: the higher the ramp, the farther the cup slides; doubling the height about doubles the slide.
write: Use the words speed and energy to explain your results.
  lines: 3
  key: A ball from a higher ramp is going faster at the bottom, so it has more energy. When it hits the cup, it passes more energy to the cup, so the cup slides farther.
choice: Why do we do 3 trials at each height?
  - To make the ball roll faster
  * To check that our results are not a fluke
  - So everyone gets a turn to measure
write: When the ball hits the cup, where does the ball's energy go?
  lines: 2
  key: Into the cup (making it move), and into sound and heat as the cup rubs on the floor until it stops.

## Stretch
write: Predict how far the cup would slide from a 20 cm ramp. Use your data to explain.
  lines: 2
  key: Typical: about 32 cm, since the slide grew about 8 cm for each 5 cm of height. Accept a prediction that follows the student's own pattern.

# 5. Fix the Energy Mistakes
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how energy moves through sound, light, heat, electric current and motion.
directions: Each box has a short scene and a claim about energy. Every claim is wrong. Cross out the wrong part and rewrite the claim so it is true.
mistakes: Students often fix a claim by just adding "not," which can still leave it fuzzy ("Energy does not disappear" is true, but say where it goes). Push for the full fix: where the energy goes or what it becomes. The heat item trips many up: cold does not flow; energy flows from the warmer thing to the cooler thing.
notes: The key gives one good fix for each claim; accept any correct rewrite that explains the energy. Items 1, 2 and 8 are motion and collisions, 3 and 9 sound, 4 and 10 light, 5 and 6 heat, 7 electric current. Good partner follow up: one student reads a wrong claim aloud, the other fixes it without looking.

## Warm up
example: A soccer ball is kicked hard. Claim: A ball kicked hard moves slower than one kicked softly.
  Fixed: A ball kicked hard moves **faster** than one kicked softly, because the kick gives it more energy.
choice: Which is true about energy?
  - Energy gets used up and disappears.
  * Energy moves from place to place and changes form.
  - Only moving things have energy.
  - Energy can only travel through wires.

## Main: Fix the claims
write: A bowling ball rolls slowly, then the same ball rolls fast. Claim: The slow ball has more energy than the fast ball.
  lines: 1
  key: The fast ball has more energy than the slow ball; the faster an object moves, the more energy it has.
write: Two toy cars crash. Claim: When they crash, all their energy disappears.
  lines: 1
  key: The energy does not disappear; it moves into the other car and changes into sound and heat.
write: A drummer hits a drum. Claim: The drum makes sound without anything moving.
  lines: 1
  key: The drum skin vibrates (moves back and forth), and the vibrations carry energy through the air to our ears.
write: A flashlight shines on a dark wall. Claim: Light from a flashlight carries no energy.
  lines: 1
  key: Light carries energy from the flashlight to the wall; light energy can even warm what it hits.
write: A metal spoon sits in a bowl of hot soup, and its handle gets warm. Claim: Cold flows out of the spoon into the soup.
  lines: 1
  key: Heat (energy) flows from the hot soup into the cooler spoon, so the spoon warms up.
write: You rub your hands together fast. Claim: Rubbing your hands together makes them colder.
  lines: 1
  key: Rubbing makes your hands warmer; the energy of motion changes into heat.
write: A battery is connected to a bulb, and the bulb glows. Claim: No energy moves through the wires.
  lines: 1
  key: Electric current carries energy through the wires from the battery to the bulb, where it becomes light and heat.
write: A ball rolls into a wall. Claim: The ball speeds up after it hits the wall.
  lines: 1
  key: The ball slows down or bounces back slower, because some of its energy goes into the wall, sound and heat.
write: You walk away from a ringing bell. Claim: The bell gets louder the farther away you walk.
  lines: 1
  key: The bell sounds softer the farther you walk, because the sound energy spreads out as it travels.
write: A solar panel runs a small fan in the sunshine. Claim: The solar panel makes energy out of nothing.
  lines: 1
  key: The solar panel changes light energy from the Sun into electrical energy, which runs the fan.

## Stretch: Write your own
write: Write a wrong energy claim about something in your classroom. Trade with a partner and fix each other's claims. Write your partner's fixed claim here.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A good fixed claim names where the energy comes from and where it goes (for example, "The lamp changes electrical energy into light and heat").

# 15. Flashlight Code Messages
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; a flashlight for each pair (optional)
ican: I can use a pattern code to send and receive information.
directions: In this blink code, • is a short blink and – is a long blink. A slash ( / ) is a short pause between letters, and a double slash ( // ) is a longer pause between words. Use the code key to decode and encode the messages.
mistakes: Students often skip the pauses when encoding, which makes the message impossible to read. Others misread • – (A) as – • (N): the order of the blinks matters. Have students say "short, long" out loud as they read each letter.
notes: This is International Morse Code, used with light, sound and radio since the 1800s. It grew out of the code Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail made for the electric telegraph, and it gives the shortest codes to letters used most often in English, like E and T. If you have flashlights, let pairs blink the encoded messages across the room; a long blink is about three times as long as a short one.

## Code key
table: Letter | Blinks | Letter | Blinks | Letter | Blinks | Letter | Blinks
  - A | • – | H | • • • • | O | – – – | V | • • • –
  - B | – • • • | I | • • | P | • – – • | W | • – –
  - C | – • – • | J | • – – – | Q | – – • – | X | – • • –
  - D | – • • | K | – • – | R | • – • | Y | – • – –
  - E | • | L | • – • • | S | • • • | Z | – – • •
  - F | • • – • | M | – – | T | – | |
  - G | – – • | N | – • | U | • • – | |

## Warm up
fill:
  - The letter M is {– –}.
  - The letter with one short blink is {E}.
  - • • – is the letter {U}.

## Main: Decode the messages
fill:
  - • • • / – / • – / • – • = {STAR}
  - • – • • / – – – / – – – / – • – // • • – / • – – • = {LOOK UP}
  - – • – • / • – / • – • • / • – • • // – – / • = {CALL ME}
  - – – • / – – – // • • • • / – – – / – – / • = {GO HOME}

## Main: Encode the messages
fill:
  - YES = {– • – – / • / • • •}
  - NO = {– • / – – –}
  - HELP = {• • • • / • / • – • • / • – – •}

## Explain
write: Your friend blinks • • • with no pauses at all. Name two different messages it could be. Why do the pauses matter?
  lines: 2
  key: It could be S, or EEE, or IE, or EI. The pauses show where one letter ends and the next begins, so the pattern has only one meaning.
write: How can a pattern of light blinks carry a message across a dark field?
  lines: 2
  key: Each letter is a different pattern of short and long blinks. The light travels to the other person, who sees the pattern and uses the same code key to turn it back into words.

## Stretch: Short codes
write: E is the most common letter in English, and its code is just one short blink. Why is it smart to give the most common letters the shortest codes?
  lines: 2
  key: Messages use those letters the most, so short codes for them make every message faster to send.

# 21. Waves and Senses Check-In
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can describe waves, use a pattern to send information, and explain how we see objects.
directions: Plot and label a wave, decode and encode a signal, and explain how light helps you see.
mistakes: The most common mistake on the wave is measuring amplitude from the bottom of a trough to the top of a crest; amplitude is from the rest line to a crest. On the light question, many students draw light going out of the eye to the object; light travels from the object into the eye.
notes: Morse code is a real pattern code: short and long pulses of sound, light or electricity. The decoder shows only the letters used here. The key graph joins the points with straight lines; students should draw a smooth wave. A light path answer should include a light source, reflection off the object, and the light entering the eye.

## Warm up: Plot a wave
text: Plot these points and connect them with a smooth curve: (0, 0) (1, 3) (2, 0) (3, -3) (4, 0) (5, 3) (6, 0) (7, -3) (8, 0). The line y = 0 is the rest position.
graph: Distance (m) | 0 8 1 | Height (m) | -4 4 1
  key: 0,0 1,3 2,0 3,-3 4,0 5,3 6,0 7,-3 8,0
fill:
  - Label a **crest** and a **trough** on your wave.
  - The amplitude of this wave is {3} m.
  - The wavelength (crest to crest) is {4} m.
  - If the amplitude grew, the wave would carry {more} energy.

## Main: Decode the signal
table: Letter | A | E | G | H | I | L | O | S | T | V | W
  - Code | • – | • | – – • | • • • • | • • | • – • • | – – – | • • • | – | • • • – | • – –
text: In Morse code, a dot is a short beep or flash and a dash is a long one. A slash (/) separates the letters.
fill:
  - • • • • / • • = {HI}
  - • – – / • – / • • • – / • / • • • = {WAVES}
  - • – • • / • • / – – • / • • • • / – = {LIGHT}
  - Write SOS in Morse code: {• • • / – – – / • • •}
write: Why is a code like this useful for sending a message far away?
  lines: 2
  key: A simple on and off pattern of sound, light or electricity can travel a long way, and anyone with the code can turn it back into words.

## Explain: How do you see?
draw: Draw a lamp, an apple and an eye. Use arrows to show the path of light. | 1.8
write: Explain how light lets you see the apple, step by step.
  lines: 3
  key: Light from the lamp travels to the apple. It reflects (bounces) off the apple. The reflected light travels in a straight line into my eye, and my eye sends a signal to my brain, which makes sense of it.
choice: In a room with no light at all, can you see the apple?
  - Yes, if you look hard
  * No, because no light reflects off it into your eyes
  - Yes, because eyes give off light

## Stretch
write: An owl has very large eyes for its head. How might this help it hunt at night?
  lines: 2
  key: Large eyes let in more light, so the owl can see better in dim light at night to find prey.

# 26. A Place Through Time
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can use rock layers and fossils to tell how a place changed over a long time.
directions: The walls of a canyon show 5 rock layers, oldest at the bottom. Read the clues in each layer and name the place it shows. Then put the 6 stages in order on the timeline and sketch each one.
mistakes: Many students read the layers top to bottom, like a page. In undisturbed layers, the bottom layer formed first, so it is the oldest. Some also think the canyon is older than the rocks; the river can only cut through layers that are already there, so the canyon is the newest stage.
notes: The canyon is a made-up example, but each clue is a real one geologists use: limestone with shells and coral forms in warm, shallow seas; coal forms from swamp plants; sandstone with sweeping, slanted lines and footprints forms from desert sand dunes; shale with fish fossils forms from mud at the bottom of a lake; rounded pebbles cemented together show a fast river. The Grand Canyon has a real sea-to-desert story like this one. Ages are not given on purpose: this sheet is about order.

## Warm up
choice: In flat rock layers that have not been tipped or flipped, which layer is the oldest?
  - the top layer
  - the middle layer
  * the bottom layer
fill:
  - Fossils of shells and coral show the place was once under {water} (a sea).

## Main: Read the layers
table: Layer | Rock | Clues inside | What was this place like?
  - 5 (top) | conglomerate | rounded pebbles stuck together, like a riverbed | {a fast river}
  - 4 | shale | thin layers of mud with fish fossils | {a lake}
  - 3 | sandstone | big slanted lines of sand and lizard-like footprints | {a sandy desert}
  - 2 | coal | fossil ferns, leaves and tree trunks | {a swamp}
  - 1 (bottom) | limestone | seashells and coral fossils | {a warm, shallow sea}

## Main: The timeline
text: Write each card's letter at its place on the timeline. Stage 1 is the oldest; stage 6 is today.
timeline: 1 6 1
  - 1 | A warm, shallow sea covers the land.
  - 2 | A steamy swamp with giant ferns grows here.
  - 3 | Sand dunes blow across a dry desert.
  - 4 | A lake forms, and fish swim in it.
  - 5 | A fast river rolls pebbles across the land.
  - 6 | The land rises, and a river cuts a canyon through all the layers.
table: Stage 1, oldest | Stage 2, next | Stage 3, next
  height: 1.3
  - | |
table: Stage 4, next | Stage 5, next | Stage 6, today
  height: 1.3
  - | |
note: Sketches should match each stage: sea with shells and coral, swamp with ferns, desert dunes, lake with fish, river with pebbles, and a canyon with layered walls and a river at the bottom.

## Explain
write: How do you know the canyon was cut after all 5 layers formed?
  lines: 2
  key: The river cut through all 5 layers, so the layers had to be there first. You cannot cut through something that does not exist yet.
write: The place changed from a sea to a desert. Give one piece of evidence from the layers.
  lines: 2
  key: The bottom layer (limestone) has seashells and coral, so it was a sea; layer 3 has sand with slanted lines and footprints, so later it was a desert.

## Stretch
write: A geologist finds a seashell fossil at the top of a mountain. What could that tell her about the place?
  lines: 2
  key: The rock there formed under the sea long ago, and then the land was pushed up to become a mountain.

# 36. Where Should the Town Go?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional)
ican: I can use maps of natural hazards to choose a safer place to build.
directions: A new town needs a home. The map grid shows hazards near 3 possible sites. Read the map key, fill in the table, then choose the safest site and explain your choice with evidence from the map.
mistakes: Students often pick the site closest to the river because people need water, without noticing the flood zone. Others think any land away from the fault is safe and miss the steep slopes around site 1. Have them check every hazard in the table for each site.
notes: To find the distance to the fault, count squares straight across the row to column G. Real towns use hazard maps like this: flood maps show land that is likely to flood, fault maps show where earthquakes are more likely, and slope maps show land where landslides can happen. No site is perfectly safe; site 3 has the fewest hazards. Accept a well-argued answer for another site only if it names a way to lower the risk.

## Map key
text: **slope** steep slope (landslides can happen) · **river** the river · **flood** flood zone (low land that floods) · **fault** fault line (earthquakes are more likely here) · **1 2 3** town sites · empty squares are flat, safe land
table: | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | H | I
  - 1 | slope | slope | slope | | | | fault | |
  - 2 | slope | **1** | slope | | | | fault | **2** |
  - 3 | slope | slope | | **3** | | | fault | |
  - 4 | flood | flood | flood | flood | flood | flood | fault | flood | flood
  - 5 | river | river | river | river | river | river | fault | river | river
  - 6 | flood | flood | flood | flood | flood | flood | fault | flood | flood
  - 7 | | | | | | | fault | |

## Warm up
fill:
  - Site 1 is in square {B2}. Site 3 is in square {D3}.
  - The fault line runs down column {G}.
choice: Why is the land marked "flood" dangerous for houses?
  - It has steep slopes.
  - It is on the fault line.
  * It floods when the river rises.

## Main: Check each site
table: Site | Steep slopes around it? | In the flood zone? | Squares to the fault line
  - 1 | {yes} | {no} | {5}
  - 2 | {no} | {no} | {1}
  - 3 | {no} | {no} | {3}
choice: Which site has the biggest danger from earthquakes?
  - site 1
  * site 2
  - site 3
choice: Which hazard is the biggest danger at site 1?
  * landslides
  - floods
  - earthquakes

## Choose and justify
write: Which site is the safest for the town? Use evidence from the map to explain.
  lines: 3
  starter: The safest site is ... because ...
  key: Site 3. It is on flat land, so landslides are unlikely; it is outside the flood zone; and it is 3 squares from the fault, farther than site 2. It is also close to the river for water.
write: Site 3 is still near the fault and the river. Name one thing the town could do to make it even safer.
  lines: 2
  key: Any one: build earthquake-safe buildings, keep homes out of the flood zone, build a wall or levee along the river, plan escape routes, keep an emergency kit.

## Stretch
write: The town wants a park, not houses, in squares D4 to F4. Why is a park a good use of that land?
  lines: 2
  key: Those squares are in the flood zone. A park can flood without hurting anyone or wrecking homes, and the town can still use the land.
