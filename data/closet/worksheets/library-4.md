# 1. Subject Headings
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use subject headings in a catalog record to find related books.
directions: Read the five catalog records. Highlight the subject headings. Use them to find the books that share each topic, and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often search titles only, so they miss Hunters of the Deep, a shark book with no "shark" in its title. Others add Hunters of the Deep to Marine animals because sharks live in the sea. That makes sense, but the catalog only finds what is in the Subjects column, and this record lists Sharks alone.
notes: The five books and authors are made up for practice. The subject headings use real Library of Congress subject heading forms (Sharks; Marine animals; Coral reef ecology; Coral reef conservation). Real records for children's books often add "Juvenile literature" after a heading, such as "Sharks--Juvenile literature." In most online catalogs, clicking a subject heading lists every book with that heading. Accept the record numbers in any order.

## Warm up: Read a record
example: Record 1: Sharks Up Close, by Dana Reyes. Call number 597.3 REY.
  Subjects: **Sharks. Marine animals.**
  The subject headings tell what the book is about, in the same words for every book on that topic.
choice: What do the subject headings in a catalog record tell you?
  - Who wrote the book
  - Where the book was printed
  * What the book is about
  - How many pages the book has

# 7. Website Check With Five Questions
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can check a website with five questions (who, what, when, where, why) and decide which sites are best for research.
directions: Your research question is "How do sea otters stay warm in cold water?" Read the four printed websites. Answer the five questions for each one in the chart. Then rank the sites from 1 (best for research) to 4 (worst) and explain your ranking.
mistakes: Students often rank the most fun or colorful site first. Steer them back to the five questions: a site made to sell or to entertain can still have facts, but it is not the best place to start. Others think an older site is always bad; Site D is a few years old, but sea otter facts like these do not change quickly, so it is still a good source.
notes: All four websites and their makers are made up for practice; the example.org addresses do not lead anywhere. Sea otter facts are checked: sea otters have the thickest fur of any animal (up to about a million hairs per square inch); unlike seals and whales, they have no thick layer of blubber; they keep warm by trapping air in their fur and by eating a lot of food each day; they usually hold their breath for only a few minutes when they dive, so Site B's claim about an hour is false. Ranking in the key: A, D, C, B. Accept D first or C last if the reason uses the five questions.

## Warm up: The five questions
example: **Who** made it? **What** is it about, and does it give facts? **When** was it made or updated? **Where** does the information come from (sources, experts)? **Why** was it made: to teach, to sell, or for fun?
choice: Which question asks about the purpose of a site?
  - Who made it?
  - When was it made?
  * Why was it made?
  - Where does the information come from?

## The four websites
source: Site A: seaotters.aquarium.example.org, from the education team at a public aquarium. Updated March 2025.
  "Sea otters have the thickest fur of any animal: up to about a million hairs on a patch of skin the size of a postage stamp. The fur traps a layer of air that keeps the water away from their skin. They have no blubber, so they also eat about a quarter of their body weight in food each day to stay warm." Sources: two marine scientists who study sea otters are named at the bottom.
source: Site B: otterlyawesome.example.org, a fan page by OtterFan22. No date.
  "OTTERS ARE THE CUTEST!!! They can hold their breath for a whole hour!!! They stay warm because they are so fluffy. Vote for your favorite otter picture below!" No sources.
source: Site C: otterplushshop.example.org, a store that sells otter toys. Updated 2025.
  "Did you know sea otters wrap themselves in seaweed so they do not drift away while they sleep? Snuggle up with our Super Soft Otter, only 19.99! Buy two and save!" No sources.
source: Site D: kidsencyclopedia.example.org, "Sea Otter," written by a science writer and checked by a sea otter expert. Updated 2021.
  "A sea otter's fur is the densest of any animal. Sea otters spend a lot of time grooming and blowing air into their fur, which keeps them warm and helps them float." Sources: lists three science books.

table: Question | Site A | Site B | Site C | Site D

  - What: does it answer our question with facts? | {yes, fur traps air, eats a lot} | {not really, "fluffy"} | {a little, not about warmth} | {yes, fur and grooming}
  - When was it updated? | {2025} | {no date} | {2025} | {2021}
  - Where does the information come from? | {two named scientists} | {no sources} | {no sources} | {three science books}
  - Why was it made? | {to teach} | {for fun} | {to sell toys} | {to teach}
  height: 0.55

## Rank the sites
table: Rank | Site | My best reason
  - 1 (best) | {A} | {made by experts, up to date, answers the question, names scientists}
  - 2 | {D} | {checked by an expert, lists books; a little older}
  - 3 | {C} | {made to sell toys, no sources}
  - 4 (worst) | {B} | {no author name, no date, no sources, false fact}
write: Site B says otters hold their breath for a whole hour. How could you check that fact?
  lines: 2
  key: Look it up on a site I trust, like Site A or a library book about sea otters, and see if it agrees. (Sea otters usually stay under for only a few minutes, so Site B is wrong.)

## Stretch
write: Site C has a true fact about seaweed. Why is it still not the best site for your research?
  lines: 3
  key: It was made to sell toys, it gives no sources, and its fact is about sleeping, not about staying warm; a site made to teach, with named experts, is a better place to get facts.

# 20. Fix This Bibliography
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, red pencil or pen
ican: I can find and fix mistakes in a bibliography so every source is listed the right way.
directions: Read the rules. Then read Jada's bibliography for her report on wolves. It has 6 mistakes. Use the source facts to find each mistake, write what is wrong, and then rewrite the bibliography the right way.
mistakes: Students often find the "wrong" entries but miss the order mistake, because it is about the whole list, not one entry. Others fix the first-name mistake but forget to keep the comma between last and first name. Point to the rule card for each fix.
notes: The format is a simple MLA-style bibliography for grade 4: Book: Last name, First name. *Title*. Publisher, Year. Web page: Last name, First name. "Title of Page." *Website*, Year. A page with no author starts with its title. Students underline book and website titles when writing by hand. The six mistakes: Lopez has no year; Carter's name is first name first; the zoo page is only an address; Patel has no publisher; Reed's page title has no quotation marks; and the list is not in ABC order. All books, websites and authors are made up.

# 21. The Story of Copyright
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain why copyright exists and what it means for creators and users.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions. Use details from the passage to prove your answers.
mistakes: Students often think anything online is free to use because it is easy to copy. Being able to copy something is not the same as having permission. Others think giving credit makes any use fine: credit is the right thing to do, but it is not the same as permission.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). Facts are checked: the US Constitution (Article I, Section 8) gives Congress the power to protect writings "for limited Times"; the first US copyright law passed in 1790; in the United States today copyright usually lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years; copyright begins as soon as an original work is written down or recorded; fair use allows some limited uses, such as quoting a small part for a school report or review. Fair use is decided case by case, so the passage says "some" uses.

## Read
passage: Who Owns a Story?
  Imagine you spend a whole month writing a comic book. Then someone copies it, puts their own name on it, and sells it. That would not feel fair. Copyright is a set of laws that protects people who make things.
  Copyright covers original work such as stories, songs, drawings, photos and movies. It gives the creator the right to decide who can copy, share or sell the work. A creator does not need to fill out a form. As soon as you write your story down or draw your picture, it is protected.
  The idea is old. When the United States wrote its Constitution, it gave Congress the power to protect writers and inventors "for limited Times." The first American copyright law was passed in 1790.
  Why only for a limited time? The writers of the Constitution wanted creators to earn money from their work, so they would keep making more. They also wanted everyone to be able to use old works someday. Today, copyright in the United States usually lasts for the creator's whole life plus 70 more years. After that, the work enters the public domain. Anyone may then copy it, perform it or turn it into something new.
  The law also allows some fair uses. For example, a student may quote a few lines of a book in a school report. Giving credit to the creator is always the right thing to do.

## Questions
choice: What is the main purpose of copyright?
  * To protect people who make things and let them decide how their work is used
  - To stop people from reading books
  - To make sure every book costs money
  - To let anyone copy anything
choice: When does a new story get copyright protection?
  - When the writer fills out a form
  - When the story is sold in a store
  * As soon as it is written down
  - After 70 years
fill:
  - The first American copyright law was passed in the year {1790}.
  - When copyright ends, a work enters the {public domain}.
write: Why did the writers of the Constitution protect creators only "for limited Times"? Give two reasons from the passage.
  lines: 3
  key: So creators could earn money from their work and keep making more, and so that someday everyone could use old works freely (the public domain).
write: Find the sentence that shows what anyone may do with a work in the public domain. Copy it.
  lines: 2
  key: "Anyone may then copy it, perform it or turn it into something new."

## Stretch
write: Your class wants to put on a play of a fairy tale written more than 200 years ago. Do you need permission? Explain using the passage.
  lines: 3
  key: No. A work that old is in the public domain because its copyright (the creator's life plus 70 years) has ended, so anyone may perform it or turn it into something new. Giving credit to the author is still good practice. (Teacher: a new retelling or translation of an old tale can have its own copyright; the old original text is free.)

# 33. Windows I Opened
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, two books you have read this year (or their titles)
ican: I can reflect on books that showed me a life different from my own and explain what each one taught me.
directions: A window book lets you look into a life, place or time that is new to you. Think of two books that were windows for you. Answer the questions about each book. Then write what you want to read next.
mistakes: Students often name a book they liked but cannot say what was new in it; ask, "What did the characters do, eat, celebrate, believe or worry about that you had never thought about?" Others write a lesson that is just the plot ("she won the race"). Push for what the reader learned about people or places. A book can be a window for one reader and a mirror for another.
notes: Students answer about the book and what they learned, not about their own family or private life. The model answers use a made-up book. If a student cannot think of two books, offer class read-alouds or let them use one book and one book you read aloud. Rubric: a strong reflection names a specific detail from the book, says what was new, and explains what the reader now understands or wonders.

## Warm up: Window or mirror?
example: A **window** shows you a life, place or time that is different from yours. A **mirror** shows you a life that feels like your own.
choice: Kai lives in a busy city. He reads a book about a girl who herds reindeer in the far north. For Kai, this book is mostly a:
  * window
  - mirror
choice: What makes a book a good window?
  - It has a lot of pictures.
  - It is very short.
  * It helps you understand a life that is new to you.

## Main: Two windows
example: Model: *Lanterns Over the River* (made up), by L. Tran. It is about a boy in a fishing village in Vietnam who helps his aunt get ready for the Mid-Autumn Festival. I learned how families make lanterns and share mooncakes for the festival, and that kids there wait all year for the lantern parade.
chart: Book 1 | Book 2
  labels: Title and author; Whose life, what place or what time did it show?; One detail that was new to me; What it taught me about people or the world; A question I still wonder about
  height: 0.75
  key: Sample for the model book: Lanterns Over the River by L. Tran; a boy in a fishing village in Vietnam getting ready for the Mid-Autumn Festival; families make paper lanterns and share mooncakes; people everywhere have special days that bring families and neighbors together; how is the festival celebrated in other countries?

## Stretch: My next window
write: What kind of life, place or time would you like to look into next? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Sample: I would like to read about a kid who lives on a boat or a small island, because I have never been on the ocean and I wonder how they go to school.
