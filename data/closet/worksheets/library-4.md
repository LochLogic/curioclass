# 1. Subject Headings
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use subject headings in a catalog record to find related books.
directions: Read the five catalog records. Highlight the subject headings. Use them to find the books that share each topic, and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often search titles only, so they miss Hunters of the Deep, a shark book with no "shark" in its title. Others add Hunters of the Deep to Marine animals because sharks live in the sea. That makes sense, but the catalog only finds what is in the Subjects column, and this record lists Sharks alone.
notes: The five books and authors are made up for practice. The subject headings use real Library of Congress subject heading forms (Sharks; Marine animals; Coral reef ecology; Coral reef conservation). Real records for children's books often add "Juvenile literature" after a heading, such as "Sharks--Juvenile literature." In most online catalogs, clicking a subject heading lists every book with that heading. Accept the record numbers in any order.

## Warm up: Read a record
example: Record 1: Sharks Up Close, by Dana Reyes. Call number 597.3 REY.
  Subjects: **Sharks. Marine animals.**
  The subject headings tell what the book is about, in the same words for every book on that topic.

# 21. The Story of Copyright
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain why copyright exists and what it means for creators and users.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions. Use details from the passage to prove your answers.
mistakes: Students often think anything online is free to use because it is easy to copy. Being able to copy something is not the same as having permission. Others think giving credit makes any use fine: credit is the right thing to do, but it is not the same as permission.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). Facts are checked: the US Constitution (Article I, Section 8) gives Congress the power to protect writings "for limited Times"; the first US copyright law passed in 1790; in the United States today copyright usually lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years; copyright begins as soon as an original work is written down or recorded; fair use allows some limited uses, such as quoting a small part for a school report or review. Fair use is decided case by case, so the passage says "some" uses.

## Read
passage: Who Owns a Story?
  Imagine you spend a whole month writing a comic book. Then someone copies it, puts their own name on it, and sells it. That would not feel fair. Copyright is a set of laws that protects people who make things.
  Copyright covers original work such as stories, songs, drawings, photos and movies. It gives the creator the right to decide who can copy, share or sell the work. A creator does not need to fill out a form. As soon as you write your story down or draw your picture, it is protected.
  The idea is old. When the United States wrote its Constitution, it gave Congress the power to protect writers and inventors "for limited Times." The first American copyright law was passed in 1790.
  Why only for a limited time? The writers of the Constitution wanted creators to earn money from their work, so they would keep making more. They also wanted everyone to be able to use old works someday. Today, copyright in the United States usually lasts for the creator's whole life plus 70 more years. After that, the work enters the public domain. Anyone may then copy it, perform it or turn it into something new.
  The law also allows some fair uses. For example, a student may quote a few lines of a book in a school report. Giving credit to the creator is always the right thing to do.

## Stretch
write: Your class wants to put on a play of a fairy tale written more than 200 years ago. Do you need permission? Explain using the passage.
  lines: 3
  key: No. A work that old is in the public domain because its copyright (the creator's life plus 70 years) has ended, so anyone may perform it or turn it into something new. Giving credit to the author is still good practice. (Teacher: a new retelling or translation of an old tale can have its own copyright; the old original text is free.)
