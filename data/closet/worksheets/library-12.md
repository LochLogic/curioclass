# 1. Capstone Question Design
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can design a focused, researchable capstone question that grows from an enduring issue.
directions: Learn the five criteria. Test five sample questions and name the one criterion each misses. Then choose an enduring issue, draft your capstone question, test it against all five criteria, and revise it.
mistakes: Students often start too broad ("How has fairness changed throughout history?") and fix it by making it a fact question ("When was the Equal Pay Act passed?"). The goal is in between: narrow in place, time or group, but still open. Others write questions that hide their answer ("Why is the dress code unfair?"); a neutral question lets the research decide.
notes: The five criteria are a working checklist for this sheet, built from common capstone and senior project guides; schools may use their own. Each sample question is written to miss exactly one criterion. Sample 1 (voter turnout) has one checkable answer. Sample 2 spans all of history. Sample 3 is about the future, so no sources can show what will happen. Sample 4 assumes the answer. Sample 5 is narrow and researchable but matters to few people beyond the student. Facts in the key: the Equal Pay Act became law in 1963.

## Warm up: The five criteria
example: **1. Open:** cannot be answered with yes or no or a single fact.
  **2. Focused:** names a place, time or group small enough to study in the time you have.
  **3. Researchable:** sources exist that you can actually reach (studies, data, documents, interviews).
  **4. Significant:** connects to an enduring issue (fairness, freedom, power, duty, belonging) and matters beyond you.
  **5. Neutral:** does not assume its own answer.

# 21. Authority and Funding Audit
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can audit sources for the author's authority, who paid for the work, and conflicts of interest.
directions: Read the five source summaries on one question: should high schools start later in the morning? For each, record the author's authority, who paid for the work, and any conflict of interest, then give an overall trust rating (high, medium or low). Answer the questions that follow.
mistakes: Students often treat any funding as corruption. Public research grants and member donations are normal; the question is whether the funder gains from a particular answer. Others rate a source low because it disagrees with the majority; the bus company op-ed has a conflict, but its point about real transportation costs is still worth checking elsewhere.
notes: Every source, person, company and group on this sheet is made up. The real debate is ongoing; this sheet is about judging sources, not settling the policy. Ratings in the key: 1 high, 2 low to medium (real expertise in operations, clear business interest), 3 medium (advocacy group, partly funded by a company that could profit), 4 low (sells the product it recommends, no peer review), 5 high to medium (public agency summarizing research, with citations). A conflict of interest does not prove a source wrong; it means its claims need independent support.

## Read: Five sources
source: Source 1: Journal article, "Start Times and Teen Sleep in 12 Districts" (2025)
  [1] Written by three sleep researchers at a university medical school. Reviewed by other scientists before publication. Funded by a federal health research grant. The authors state they have no financial conflicts. Finding: students in districts that moved start times to 8:30 or later slept about 40 minutes more on school nights.
source: Source 2: Newspaper opinion piece, "Later Starts Would Wreck Our Bus System" (2025)
  [2] Written by the chief executive of Bus Company B, a company that holds bus contracts with several districts and would have to redraw its routes and contracts if start times change. Argues that later starts would raise costs and push sports practices into the evening.
source: Source 3: Report, "Let Teens Sleep" (2026)
  [3] Published by Parent Group F, a parent advocacy group that campaigns for later start times. Paid for by member donations and a grant from Mattress Company M. Summarizes six studies, with citations.
source: Source 4: Web article, "The Truth About Teen Tiredness" (2026)
  [4] Posted by Supplement Company S, a company that sells a sleep supplement for teens. No author named. Says later start times "will not help" and recommends its supplement instead. Cites one study, which it paid for itself.
source: Source 5: State Department of Education fact sheet, "School Start Times: What the Research Says" (2026)
  [5] Written by agency staff and paid for by the state. Summarizes research for and against later starts, with citations, and lists costs that districts have reported.

## Questions
choice: Which source has the strongest combination of authority and independence?
  * Source 1
  - Source 3
  - Source 4
  - Source 2
write: Source 4 and Source 2 both oppose later start times. Compare their conflicts of interest. Is one more serious?
  lines: 4
  key: Source 4's is more serious: it profits directly if readers reject later starts and buy its supplement, names no author and cites only a study it paid for. Source 2 has a business interest too, but its author has real expertise in bus operations and its cost claims can be checked against district budgets.
write: Source 3 supports later starts and cites six studies. How should the mattress company grant affect how you use it?
  lines: 3
  key: The funder could profit from more attention to teen sleep, and the group campaigns for one side, so use the report to find the six studies, then read and cite those studies directly rather than the report's summary.
write: Explain why a conflict of interest does not by itself make a source wrong. Use Source 2 as your example.
  lines: 3
  key: A conflict gives a reason to doubt and to check, not proof of error. Source 2's claim that later starts raise busing costs may be true; it should be confirmed with independent evidence, such as the cost data in Source 5.

## Stretch
write: You may cite only three of these sources in a balanced paper. Which three, and why?
  lines: 4
  key: Sample: Source 1 (strongest original research), Source 5 (independent summary of both sides and real costs), and the studies behind Source 3 or Source 2's cost argument once confirmed elsewhere; leave out Source 4, whose conflict and lack of an author make it the least trustworthy.
