# 1. Lamp and Ball Moon Phases
minutes: 40
materials: a lamp with the shade removed (an LED bulb is best), a foam or polystyrene ball about 8 cm across pushed onto a pencil, a darkened room, pencil
ican: I can use a model to explain why the Moon shows phases.
pairs: moon-phase-model
directions: The lamp is the Sun, your head is Earth, and the ball is the Moon. Stand facing the lamp and hold the ball at arm's length, slightly above your head. Turn slowly to your left in eighths of a circle (45 degrees each). At each stop, look at the lit part of the ball, sketch it, and name the phase.
mistakes: The biggest misconception is that Earth's shadow causes the phases. In the model the ball is only in your head's shadow at full moon if you hold it too low; that is an eclipse, not a phase. Also watch for students sketching the lit side on the wrong side: turning left (counterclockwise), the right side lights up first.
notes: Do not touch the bulb; even LED bulbs warm up. One student can model while a partner records, then switch. Turning counterclockwise matches the Moon's real orbit seen from above the North Pole, so the lit side matches what we see from the Northern Hemisphere. A full cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days. The real Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees, which is why eclipses do not happen every month.

## Warm up
choice: How much of the Moon is lit by the Sun at any time (not counting eclipses)?
  - none of it during new moon
  * half of it, always
  - all of it during full moon only
  - it changes each night
fill:
  - The Moon makes no light of its own. We see it because it {reflects} sunlight.

## Main: Model the phases
table: Stop | Turned left | Phase name
  - 1 | 0° (ball toward lamp) | {new moon}
  - 2 | 45° | {waxing crescent}
  - 3 | 90° | {first quarter}
  - 4 | 135° | {waxing gibbous}
  - 5 | 180° (back to lamp) | {full moon}
  - 6 | 225° | {waning gibbous}
  - 7 | 270° | {third (last) quarter}
  - 8 | 315° | {waning crescent}
draw: Sketch what the ball looks like at each stop, 1 to 8 from left to right. Shade the dark part. | 1.5
draw: Top view: draw the lamp, your head, and the ball at all 8 stops. Shade the half of the ball facing away from the lamp each time. | 2.6

## Explain
write: Use your model to explain why we see phases.
  lines: 3
  key: The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half from Earth: none at new moon (lit side faces away), all of it at full moon, and part of it in between.
choice: At full moon, where is the Moon?
  - between Earth and the Sun
  * on the far side of Earth from the Sun
  - at a right angle to the Sun
write: At new moon, the Moon is between Earth and the Sun. Why can we not see it?
  lines: 2
  key: The lit half faces the Sun, away from Earth, so we face its dark side; it is also in the bright daytime sky near the Sun.

## Stretch
write: Waxing means growing and waning means shrinking. Tonight the Moon is a first quarter. What will it look like in about one week? In about two weeks?
  lines: 2
  key: In about one week, full moon (about 7 days); in about two weeks, third quarter, with the left half lit.

# 6. Planet Data Patterns
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can analyze data about the planets to find patterns in their size, distance, gravity and orbits.
directions: Study the planet data table. Plot each planet's distance and orbit period, answer the questions, then describe 3 patterns you see, with numbers as evidence.
mistakes: Students often assume bigger planets always have much stronger surface gravity. Saturn is more than 9 times as wide as Earth but its surface gravity is a little less than Earth's, because surface gravity depends on mass and on how far the surface is from the center. Watch also for students who read the AU column as millions of kilometers: 1 AU is Earth's average distance from the Sun, about 150 million km.
notes: Data from NASA's planetary fact sheets, rounded. Surface gravity for the giant planets is measured where the air pressure equals Earth's at sea level, since they have no solid surface. The stretch uses Kepler's third law (period in years squared equals distance in AU cubed); students can test it with their calculators. Pluto, a dwarf planet, averages about 39.5 AU from the Sun and takes about 248 years to orbit.

## Read the data
fill:
  - The planet with the strongest surface gravity is {Jupiter}.
  - Jupiter's diameter is about {11} times Earth's (round to the nearest whole number).
  - Two planets with the same surface gravity: {Mercury} and {Mars}

choice: What does the graph show?
  - Planets farther out orbit faster.
  * The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its orbit takes, and the curve bends upward.
  - Orbit period does not depend on distance.
  - Orbit period goes up by the same amount for each AU.

## Describe 3 patterns
write: Pattern 1 (size): Compare the 4 inner planets with the 4 outer planets.
  lines: 2
  key: The 4 inner planets (Mercury to Mars) are small, under 13,000 km across; the 4 outer planets are much bigger, from about 49,500 km (Neptune) to about 143,000 km (Jupiter).
write: Pattern 2 (distance and period): Use numbers from the table.
  lines: 2
  key: The farther from the Sun, the longer the orbit period, every time: Mercury (0.39 AU) takes 0.24 years, Earth (1 AU) 1 year, Neptune (30.1 AU) 164.8 years. The period grows faster than the distance.
write: Pattern 3 (gravity): Does a bigger planet always have stronger surface gravity? Use evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: No. Jupiter is the biggest and has the strongest gravity (2.36), but Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are about 4 to 9 times as wide as Earth and have about Earth's surface gravity (0.89 to 1.12). Mercury and Mars have the same gravity (0.38) though Mars is wider. Surface gravity depends on mass and on the planet's size, not size alone.

## Stretch: Test a rule
write: Kepler found that (orbit period in years) x (orbit period in years) = (distance in AU) x (distance in AU) x (distance in AU). Test it for Jupiter. Then use it to predict the orbit period of a dwarf planet 29.5 AU from the Sun.
  lines: 2
  key: Jupiter: 5.2 x 5.2 x 5.2 = 140.6, and 11.9 x 11.9 = 141.6, about the same, so the rule works. For 39.5 AU: 39.5 x 39.5 x 39.5 = 61,630; the period is the number that times itself makes 61,630, about 248 years (this is Pluto).

# 17. Plot the Earthquakes
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencil, a world map or atlas (optional)
ican: I can plot earthquake locations to find where plate boundaries are.
directions: The table lists 20 strong earthquakes along the edge of the Pacific Ocean, rounded to the nearest degree. Plot each one as a dot on the grid. The solid line is a simplified coastline of North and South America. Then trace the plate boundary the dots reveal.
mistakes: Students often swap latitude and longitude. Here longitude goes across (x) and latitude goes up and down (y). West longitudes and south latitudes are negative numbers, so -73 is to the left of -60 and -38 is below the equator. Some trace the coastline instead of the line of dots; the boundary runs just offshore, where the dots are.
notes: Locations are the epicenters of well-known earthquakes as reported by the US Geological Survey, rounded; the coastline is drawn from a few dozen points and leaves out islands, so treat it as a sketch. Most of these quakes happened where an ocean plate dives under a continent (Aleutians and Alaska, Cascadia, Mexico and Central America, Peru and Chile). The California quakes are on the San Andreas fault system, where the Pacific and North American plates slide past each other. The Andes and the Cascades are volcanic mountains made by these same boundaries.

## Read your map
choice: What pattern do the earthquakes make?
  - They are spread evenly across the whole grid.
  * They line up in a narrow band along the edge of the continents.
  - They cluster in the middle of the continents.
  - They are all south of the equator.

  - The earthquake farthest south is number {20}, in {Chile}.
  - The earthquake farthest west is number {1}, at longitude {-176}.
choice: Why do earthquakes happen in a line like this?
  - The coastline makes the ground shake.
  - The ocean water pushes on the land.
  * They mark the edge where two plates meet and move against each other.
  - Big cities cause earthquakes.

## Explain
write: Along South America, the Nazca Plate (under the ocean) and the South American Plate push together. What other landform would you expect to find near this boundary? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Mountains and volcanoes, like the Andes. When the ocean plate sinks under the continent, the crust is squeezed up into mountains and melted rock rises to form volcanoes.
write: A student says, "The middle of North America must be on a different plate, because there are no earthquakes there." What does the map really show?
  lines: 2
  key: The middle of the continent has few strong earthquakes because it is inside one plate, far from a boundary. Plate boundaries are where most strong earthquakes happen.

## Stretch
write: Earthquakes 6 to 9 in California happen where the Pacific Plate and North American Plate slide past each other. How is that different from the boundary near Chile?
  lines: 2
  key: In California the plates slide sideways past each other (a transform boundary, the San Andreas fault). Near Chile they push together and one plate sinks under the other (a convergent boundary).

# 23. Water Cycle in a Bag
minutes: 30
materials: a zip-top sandwich bag, water, blue food coloring, a permanent marker, clear packing tape, a sunny window, a measuring spoon, pencil
ican: I can model how the Sun's energy drives evaporation, condensation and precipitation in the water cycle.
directions: Draw a sun, clouds and a sea on the bag with marker. Add 4 tablespoons of water and 2 drops of food coloring. Seal the bag, then tape it flat to a sunny window. Observe it once a day for 3 days and record what you see.
mistakes: Students often say the water "turned into" clouds right away; evaporation is invisible, and the drops only appear when water vapor cools and condenses. Some think the colored water stays in the drops: the drops are clear, because the dye does not evaporate with the water.
notes: Set up takes about 20 minutes; then students need about 2 minutes a day for 3 days. The key shows typical observations for a sunny window; on cloudy days changes are slower. Tape the bag with the opening at the top so it cannot leak. Typical: by the end of day 1, fog and tiny drops form on the upper inside of the bag; by day 2 drops grow and some run down the sides; by day 3 drops keep collecting and running down, and the water level looks about the same, since the bag is sealed. Recycle or reuse the bags.

## Predict
write: Predict what you will see on the inside of the bag after one day in the sun.
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Observe
table: Day | Weather (sunny, cloudy) | Drops on the bag (none, few, many) | Are the drops colored? | Drops running down?
  - 1 | | | |
  - 2 | | | |
  - 3 | | | |
draw: Sketch the bag on day 3. Label where you see evaporation, condensation and "rain." | 2.2

## Explain
fill:
  - Sunlight warms the water, and some water {evaporates} into water vapor, an invisible gas.
  - Water vapor cools near the top of the bag and {condenses} into tiny drops.
  - The drops grow and run down the sides, like {precipitation} falling as rain.
  - Typical result: the drops on the bag are {clear} (not blue).
write: Explain why the drops are not blue, even though the water at the bottom is.
  lines: 3
  key: Only water evaporates; the dye particles stay behind in the liquid. When the water vapor condenses, it forms clean, clear drops. Real rain is fresh water for the same reason, even when the water came from a salty ocean.
write: What is the energy source that keeps this cycle going? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: The Sun. Its energy warms the water so it evaporates; without it, evaporation would slow and the cycle would slow down.
choice: The water level at the bottom stays about the same over 3 days. Why?
  - No water evaporates.
  * The bag is sealed, so water that evaporates comes back down.
  - The dye stops evaporation.

## Stretch
write: How is the bag model different from Earth's real water cycle? Give two differences.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: the real cycle is huge and open; water moves into the ground, rivers and plants (transpiration); clouds form high in the cooler atmosphere; wind moves water vapor far away; the bag has no land or living things.

# 28. When Did the Front Pass?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, two colored pencils, ruler
ican: I can use weather data to find when a cold front passed and explain how it changed the weather.
directions: A weather station recorded the weather every 12 hours for 5 days. Graph the temperature and humidity on the first grid and the air pressure on the second. Then use all the data to find when the cold front passed.
mistakes: Students often pick the coldest reading (reading 9) as the front. The front is the sudden change, not the coldest time: look for the big jump between two readings, when the wind shifted and the pressure started to rise. Notice also that reading 6 is an evening reading, which should be warmer than the morning, and was much colder instead.
notes: The data are a realistic made-up example of a cold front in spring in the central United States. Before a cold front: warm, humid air from the south, falling pressure, clouds and storms. As it passes: the wind shifts to the northwest, the temperature drops, the pressure is lowest and then rises fast. After it: cool, dry, clear air. Daily ups and downs (warmer at 6 p.m. than at 6 a.m.) continue on top of the front's change, which is why the temperature line zigzags. 1 millibar (mb) is a unit of air pressure. Extension question: why were there thunderstorms at reading 5? The cold, dense air pushed under the warm, humid air and forced it to rise quickly; rising air cools, its water vapor condenses into tall clouds, and storms form along the front.

  * readings 5 and 6

  lines: 3

write: Describe the air mass before the front and the air mass after it. Where did each come from?
  lines: 2
  key: Before: warm, humid (moist) air from the south, likely from over the Gulf of Mexico (a maritime tropical air mass). After: cool, dry air from the northwest and north, from over land in Canada (a continental polar air mass).

# 39. Ice Cube Keeper Results
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator; your team's ice keeper results (optional)
ican: I can use data to evaluate designs that slow the transfer of thermal energy.
directions: Five teams built ice cube keepers. Each started with 100 g of ice, and the teams weighed the ice left after 60 minutes. Find the grams of ice kept per dollar for each design, rank them, and decide which design is best.
mistakes: Students often divide the wrong way (cost ÷ grams). Grams kept per dollar is grams ÷ cost. Others rank only by grams kept and forget the cost criterion. Watch for students who think insulation "keeps cold in": it slows thermal energy moving from the warm room into the ice.
notes: The results are made-up but realistic for a classroom near 22 °C. To weigh the ice left, pour off the meltwater and weigh the ice in a cup you have already weighed. Prices are a classroom "materials store" price list. Your own class's data can replace the table: add a row for each team. The stretch subtracts the control (ice with no insulation), which measures only what the insulation added.

## Warm up
choice: Why does the ice in a keeper melt more slowly than ice with no insulation?
  - Insulation adds cold to the ice.
  * Insulation slows the transfer of thermal energy from the warm air to the ice.
  - Insulation stops all energy from moving.
  - The ice is heavier in the keeper.
fill:
  - Thermal energy moves from the warmer room {into} the ice. (into or out of)

## Main: Compare the designs
table: Team | Design | Cost ($) | Ice left after 60 min (g) | Grams kept per dollar
  - A | cup in a bigger cup, cotton balls between, foil lid | 1.20 | 60 | {50}
  - B | cup wrapped in newspaper, rubber bands | 0.50 | 45 | {90}
  - C | small foam box lined with bubble wrap | 2.00 | 78 | {39}
  - D | cup wrapped in felt, with a lid | 0.80 | 52 | {65}
  - E | two nested foam cups wrapped in foil | 1.50 | 81 | {54}
  - Control | bare cup, no insulation | 0.00 | 18 | (none)
  - Our team | | | |
fill:
  - Rank by grams kept (most to least): {E, C, A, D, B}
  - Rank by grams kept per dollar (best to worst): {B, D, E, A, C}

## Decide
choice: Which design kept the most ice?
  - Team A
  - Team B
  - Team D
  * Team E
write: Team C kept the second most ice but ranked last for grams per dollar. Explain why.
  lines: 2
  key: Team C's design cost the most ($2.00), so each dollar kept only 78 ÷ 2.00 = 39 g. It worked well but was expensive.
write: You must choose one design for the whole school to build. Which would you choose? Use both criteria (ice kept and cost) in your answer.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary with a reason. Example: Team D. It keeps 65 g per dollar, second best, and 52 g of ice, almost 3 times the control's 18 g, for only $0.80. Team B is the cheapest per gram but kept the least ice; Team E kept the most but costs nearly twice as much as D.

## Stretch: Subtract the control
write: The bare cup kept 18 g with no insulation. For each team, find (ice left - 18) ÷ cost. Does the best design change?
  lines: 3
  key: A 42 ÷ 1.20 = 35; B 27 ÷ 0.50 = 54; C 60 ÷ 2.00 = 30; D 34 ÷ 0.80 = 42.5; E 63 ÷ 1.50 = 42. B is still best per dollar; D and E are almost tied for second. The order stays about the same, but the gap between B and the others shrinks.
