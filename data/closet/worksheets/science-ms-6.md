# 1. Lamp and Ball Moon Phases
minutes: 40
materials: a lamp with the shade removed (an LED bulb is best), a foam or polystyrene ball about 8 cm across pushed onto a pencil, a darkened room, pencil
ican: I can use a model to explain why the Moon shows phases.
pairs: moon-phase-model
directions: The lamp is the Sun, your head is Earth, and the ball is the Moon. Stand facing the lamp and hold the ball at arm's length, slightly above your head. Turn slowly to your left in eighths of a circle (45 degrees each). At each stop, look at the lit part of the ball, sketch it, and name the phase.
mistakes: The biggest misconception is that Earth's shadow causes the phases. In the model the ball is only in your head's shadow at full moon if you hold it too low; that is an eclipse, not a phase. Also watch for students sketching the lit side on the wrong side: turning left (counterclockwise), the right side lights up first.
notes: Do not touch the bulb; even LED bulbs warm up. One student can model while a partner records, then switch. Turning counterclockwise matches the Moon's real orbit seen from above the North Pole, so the lit side matches what we see from the Northern Hemisphere. A full cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days. The real Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees, which is why eclipses do not happen every month.

## Warm up
choice: How much of the Moon is lit by the Sun at any time (not counting eclipses)?
  - none of it during new moon
  * half of it, always
  - all of it during full moon only
  - it changes each night
fill:
  - The Moon makes no light of its own. We see it because it {reflects} sunlight.

## Main: Model the phases
table: Stop | Turned left | Phase name
  - 1 | 0° (ball toward lamp) | {new moon}
  - 2 | 45° | {waxing crescent}
  - 3 | 90° | {first quarter}
  - 4 | 135° | {waxing gibbous}
  - 5 | 180° (back to lamp) | {full moon}
  - 6 | 225° | {waning gibbous}
  - 7 | 270° | {third (last) quarter}
  - 8 | 315° | {waning crescent}
draw: Sketch what the ball looks like at each stop, 1 to 8 from left to right. Shade the dark part. | 1.5
draw: Top view: draw the lamp, your head, and the ball at all 8 stops. Shade the half of the ball facing away from the lamp each time. | 2.6

## Explain
write: Use your model to explain why we see phases.
  lines: 3
  key: The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half from Earth: none at new moon (lit side faces away), all of it at full moon, and part of it in between.
choice: At full moon, where is the Moon?
  - between Earth and the Sun
  * on the far side of Earth from the Sun
  - at a right angle to the Sun
write: At new moon, the Moon is between Earth and the Sun. Why can we not see it?
  lines: 2
  key: The lit half faces the Sun, away from Earth, so we face its dark side; it is also in the bright daytime sky near the Sun.

## Stretch
write: Waxing means growing and waning means shrinking. Tonight the Moon is a first quarter. What will it look like in about one week? In about two weeks?
  lines: 2
  key: In about one week, full moon (about 7 days); in about two weeks, third quarter, with the left half lit.

# 23. Water Cycle in a Bag
minutes: 30
materials: a zip-top sandwich bag, water, blue food coloring, a permanent marker, clear packing tape, a sunny window, a measuring spoon, pencil
ican: I can model how the Sun's energy drives evaporation, condensation and precipitation in the water cycle.
directions: Draw a sun, clouds and a sea on the bag with marker. Add 4 tablespoons of water and 2 drops of food coloring. Seal the bag, then tape it flat to a sunny window. Observe it once a day for 3 days and record what you see.
mistakes: Students often say the water "turned into" clouds right away; evaporation is invisible, and the drops only appear when water vapor cools and condenses. Some think the colored water stays in the drops: the drops are clear, because the dye does not evaporate with the water.
notes: Set up takes about 20 minutes; then students need about 2 minutes a day for 3 days. The key shows typical observations for a sunny window; on cloudy days changes are slower. Tape the bag with the opening at the top so it cannot leak. Typical: by the end of day 1, fog and tiny drops form on the upper inside of the bag; by day 2 drops grow and some run down the sides; by day 3 drops keep collecting and running down, and the water level looks about the same, since the bag is sealed. Recycle or reuse the bags.

## Predict
write: Predict what you will see on the inside of the bag after one day in the sun.
  lines: 2
  key: Accept any prediction with a reason.

## Observe
table: Day | Weather (sunny, cloudy) | Drops on the bag (none, few, many) | Are the drops colored? | Drops running down?
  - 1 | | | |
  - 2 | | | |
  - 3 | | | |
draw: Sketch the bag on day 3. Label where you see evaporation, condensation and "rain." | 2.2

## Explain
fill:
  - Sunlight warms the water, and some water {evaporates} into water vapor, an invisible gas.
  - Water vapor cools near the top of the bag and {condenses} into tiny drops.
  - The drops grow and run down the sides, like {precipitation} falling as rain.
  - Typical result: the drops on the bag are {clear} (not blue).
write: Explain why the drops are not blue, even though the water at the bottom is.
  lines: 3
  key: Only water evaporates; the dye particles stay behind in the liquid. When the water vapor condenses, it forms clean, clear drops. Real rain is fresh water for the same reason, even when the water came from a salty ocean.
write: What is the energy source that keeps this cycle going? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: The Sun. Its energy warms the water so it evaporates; without it, evaporation would slow and the cycle would slow down.
choice: The water level at the bottom stays about the same over 3 days. Why?
  - No water evaporates.
  * The bag is sealed, so water that evaporates comes back down.
  - The dye stops evaporation.

## Stretch
write: How is the bag model different from Earth's real water cycle? Give two differences.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: the real cycle is huge and open; water moves into the ground, rivers and plants (transpiration); clouds form high in the cooler atmosphere; wind moves water vapor far away; the bag has no land or living things.
