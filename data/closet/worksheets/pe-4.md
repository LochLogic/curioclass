# 1. Give and Go
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can use a give-and-go to get past a defender and receive the ball in open space.
directions: Put the steps of a give-and-go in order. Then, on each diagram, draw a solid arrow for the pass, a dashed arrow for the cut, and a solid arrow for the return pass. Label the passer P and the receiver R, and write the number of the space the passer should cut to.
mistakes: Students often draw the cut backward or sideways, away from the goal. The cut goes past the defender toward the goal, into a space no other defender is touching. Watch for passers who stand still after passing: in a give-and-go the passer moves the moment the ball leaves their hands.
notes: The goal is on the right in every diagram. The player with the ball (marked with a dot) is the passer; the teammate is the receiver. Key: Diagram 1, A passes to B, A cuts to space 1, B passes back to A. Diagram 2, B passes to A, B cuts to space 3, A passes back to B. Diagram 3, C passes to A, C cuts to space 3, A passes back to C. The wrong spaces are either behind the passer (away from the goal) or touched by another defender. Walk it in the gym with three players and a cone as the defender.

## Warm up: Put it in order
text: Number the steps 1, 2 and 3.

  - {3} The receiver passes the ball back to the cutter.
  - {1} The passer passes the ball to a teammate.

## Stretch
write: In diagram 3, why is space 1 a poor place to cut to, even though it is toward the goal?
  lines: 2
  key: A defender is right next to space 1 (the X just above it), so the return pass could be stolen.
write: Why does a give-and-go work so well against a defender who watches the ball?
  lines: 3
  key: When the ball is passed, the defender turns to watch it and loses sight of the passer, who cuts behind or past them into open space for the return pass.

# 7. Team Play Planner
minutes: 35 (20 to plan, 15 to test)
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can design an offensive play and a defensive setup for a 4-on-4 game, with roles and a signal word.
directions: Match the strategy words first. Then pick a 4-on-4 invasion game your class plays (for example soccer, basketball, ultimate or flag football). Design one play for offense and one setup for defense. Draw each one, give every player a role, and choose a signal word for each.
mistakes: Offensive plays often have one player doing everything while the other three stand still. Every player needs a job, even if the job is "stay wide to pull a defender away." Defensive setups often leave the goal open; check that someone is goal-side. Signal words should be short and different from each other so teammates do not mix them up.
notes: Match key: 1 width = spreading out toward the sidelines; 2 support = a teammate close enough for a safe pass; 3 give and go = pass, then cut for a return pass; 4 person-to-person = each defender guards one attacker; 5 zone = each defender guards an area; 6 goal-side = between your attacker and your goal. Plans vary. A strong offensive play gives all 4 players a role (ball carrier, support, wide player, deep runner or cutter) and uses width or a give and go. A strong defensive setup says person-to-person or zone, keeps defenders goal-side and names who pressures the ball. Test each play in short rounds, then write one fix.

## Warm up: Strategy words
match: Match each word to what it means.
  - width = spreading out toward the sidelines to stretch the defense
  - support = a teammate moves close enough for a safe pass
  - give and go = pass, then cut to open space for a return pass
  - person-to-person defense = each defender guards one attacker
  - zone defense = each defender guards an area of the field
  - goal-side = standing between your attacker and the goal you defend

## Main: Offensive play
draw: Draw the play. Show the 4 players (O1 to O4), the ball, passes as dashed arrows and runs as solid arrows. | 2.1
chart: Role | What this player does
  labels: O1; O2; O3; O4
  height: 0.45
  key: Example (give and go with width): O1 ball carrier passes to O2 and cuts toward the goal; O2 support player returns the pass to O1; O3 stays wide on the left to pull a defender away; O4 stays deep on the right as an outlet pass.
write: Signal word for this play, and when you call it:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Example: "Bounce" called by O1 when O2 is open close by.

## Main: Defensive setup
draw: Draw the setup. Show the 4 defenders (D1 to D4), the goal you defend, and the space or player each one guards. | 2.1
chart: Role | What this player does
  labels: D1; D2; D3; D4
  height: 0.45
  key: Example (person-to-person): D1 pressures the ball carrier and stays goal-side; D2 and D3 each guard one attacker, goal-side and between their attacker and the ball; D4 guards the deepest attacker and helps near the goal.
write: Signal word for this setup, and what it tells the team:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Example: "Lock" means everyone picks up the nearest attacker and gets goal-side.

## Stretch
write: After you test it, what is one change that would make either play work better?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: Our defense left the middle open, so D4 will stay in the middle instead of chasing the ball.

# 20. Two-Week Activity Log
minutes: 15 to start, then 5 a day for 2 weeks
materials: pencil
ican: I can log my active minutes and the fitness component each activity builds.
directions: Practice with Jordan's sample day first. Then log every day for 2 weeks: what you did, the minutes, and the component each activity built most. Circle the day number on each day that reached 60 minutes.
mistakes: Students add up only one activity per day and miss the rest (recess, walking, chores). Others put C on everything. Ask: "Were you breathing hard for a long time (C), doing a few hard pushes or lifts (S), doing many easier repeats or a long hold (E), or reaching and holding a stretch (F)?"
notes: Jordan's key: bike ride C, curl-ups E, rope climb S, stretching F; total 25 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 55 minutes; 5 more minutes to reach 60, so the day does not reach 60. The 60-minute target matches the US Physical Activity Guidelines for children and teens (at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity a day). Body composition is the fifth component; it is not logged because no single activity builds it. This log is each student's own record: never post, rank or compare logs. A day with 0 minutes is fine to write down; the log is for noticing, not judging.

# 21. Why the Heart Gets Stronger
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how regular exercise makes the heart muscle stronger.
directions: Read the passage. Then answer the questions. Use details from the passage.
mistakes: Many students think a strong heart beats faster all the time. It is the other way around: at rest, a trained heart beats slower, because each beat pushes out more blood. Watch for answers that say exercise makes the heart "bigger" without saying why that helps.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). Facts it rests on: the heart is a muscle; during exercise working muscles need more oxygen, so heart rate rises; with regular aerobic training the heart pumps more blood per beat (stroke volume), resting heart rate tends to fall, and muscles grow more capillaries; the US physical activity guidelines call for at least 60 minutes of activity a day for ages 6 to 17. Keep the talk about the character and the class, not about any one student's body.

## Read
passage: A Muscle That Never Rests
  A heart is about the size of a fist, and it is a muscle. It works all day and all night, squeezing to pump blood to every part of the body. The blood carries oxygen, and muscles need oxygen to move.
  When Lena plays tag at recess, her leg muscles need much more oxygen than when she sits. Her heart beats faster to send more blood to them. She can feel it as a quick pulse in her wrist and neck.
  Like the muscles in the arms and legs, the heart changes when you train it. When a person is active most days, the heart muscle grows stronger. A stronger heart pushes out more blood with each beat. Because each beat moves more blood, the heart does not need to beat as often when the body is resting. That is why many active people have a lower resting heart rate.
  Training helps in other ways too. The muscles grow more tiny blood vessels, so oxygen reaches them more easily. After a few weeks of regular play, Lena notices that running across the field feels easier, and she can keep going longer before she needs a break.
  Health experts say kids should be active for at least 60 minutes every day. Activities that make you breathe hard for a while, like jumping rope, biking and dancing, train the heart best.

## Questions
choice: What kind of body part is the heart?
  - A bone
  * A muscle
  - A blood vessel
  - A lung
choice: Why does Lena's heart beat faster when she plays tag?
  - Her heart is getting tired.
  * Her leg muscles need more oxygen, so her heart sends more blood.
  - Her heart has stopped training.
  - She is resting between turns.
choice: Why do many active people have a lower resting heart rate?
  - Their hearts are weaker, so they beat less.
  - They never feel their pulse.
  * Their stronger heart pushes out more blood with each beat, so it does not need to beat as often.
  - Exercise makes the heart beat fast all day long.
fill: Kids should be active for at least {60} minutes every day.
write: Name two activities from the passage that train the heart best. What do they have in common?
  lines: 3
  key: Any two of jumping rope, biking and dancing; they make you breathe hard for a while (they keep the body moving long enough to work the heart).

# 33. Catch Streak Log
minutes: 15 to start, then 5 a day for 2 weeks
materials: pencil, 2 or 3 juggling scarves
ican: I can log my best catch streak each day and see whether practicing with a focus cue helps.
directions: Read Kit's sample log and answer the questions. Then practice each day for 2 weeks. Write your best streak (catches in a row before a drop). If you practiced with one focus cue in mind, circle yes and write the cue.
mistakes: Students count all the catches in a practice instead of the best streak in a row. Others mark yes for a focus cue without naming one. A focus cue is one thing you think about the whole practice, such as "toss to the high point" or "claw catch from above."
notes: Kit's key: best streak 14 on day 10; focus-cue days 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10 average (6 + 9 + 8 + 11 + 12 + 14) / 6 = 60 / 6 = 10; other days 1, 3, 6, 8 average (4 + 5 + 7 + 8) / 4 = 24 / 4 = 6; change from day 1 to day 10 is 14 - 4 = 10. Kit's streaks were higher on focus days, but they also rose over time, so practice and focus both may have helped; a strong stretch answer notices this. Each log is a student's own record; do not compare streaks between students. Days with no practice can be left blank.
