# 1. Give and Go
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can use a give-and-go to get past a defender and receive the ball in open space.
directions: Put the steps of a give-and-go in order. Then, on each diagram, draw a solid arrow for the pass, a dashed arrow for the cut, and a solid arrow for the return pass. Label the passer P and the receiver R, and write the number of the space the passer should cut to.
mistakes: Students often draw the cut backward or sideways, away from the goal. The cut goes past the defender toward the goal, into a space no other defender is touching. Watch for passers who stand still after passing: in a give-and-go the passer moves the moment the ball leaves their hands.
notes: The goal is on the right in every diagram. The player with the ball (marked with a dot) is the passer; the teammate is the receiver. Key: Diagram 1, A passes to B, A cuts to space 1, B passes back to A. Diagram 2, B passes to A, B cuts to space 3, A passes back to B. Diagram 3, C passes to A, C cuts to space 3, A passes back to C. The wrong spaces are either behind the passer (away from the goal) or touched by another defender. Walk it in the gym with three players and a cone as the defender.

## Stretch
write: In diagram 3, why is space 1 a poor place to cut to, even though it is toward the goal?
  lines: 2
  key: A defender is right next to space 1 (the X just above it), so the return pass could be stolen.
write: Why does a give-and-go work so well against a defender who watches the ball?
  lines: 3

# 21. Why the Heart Gets Stronger
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how regular exercise makes the heart muscle stronger.
directions: Read the passage. Then answer the questions. Use details from the passage.
mistakes: Many students think a strong heart beats faster all the time. It is the other way around: at rest, a trained heart beats slower, because each beat pushes out more blood. Watch for answers that say exercise makes the heart "bigger" without saying why that helps.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 230 words). Facts it rests on: the heart is a muscle; during exercise working muscles need more oxygen, so heart rate rises; with regular aerobic training the heart pumps more blood per beat (stroke volume), resting heart rate tends to fall, and muscles grow more capillaries; the US physical activity guidelines call for at least 60 minutes of activity a day for ages 6 to 17. Keep the talk about the character and the class, not about any one student's body.

## Read
passage: A Muscle That Never Rests
  A heart is about the size of a fist, and it is a muscle. It works all day and all night, squeezing to pump blood to every part of the body. The blood carries oxygen, and muscles need oxygen to move.
  When Lena plays tag at recess, her leg muscles need much more oxygen than when she sits. Her heart beats faster to send more blood to them. She can feel it as a quick pulse in her wrist and neck.
  Like the muscles in the arms and legs, the heart changes when you train it. When a person is active most days, the heart muscle grows stronger. A stronger heart pushes out more blood with each beat. Because each beat moves more blood, the heart does not need to beat as often when the body is resting. That is why many active people have a lower resting heart rate.
  Training helps in other ways too. The muscles grow more tiny blood vessels, so oxygen reaches them more easily. After a few weeks of regular play, Lena notices that running across the field feels easier, and she can keep going longer before she needs a break.
  Health experts say kids should be active for at least 60 minutes every day. Activities that make you breathe hard for a while, like jumping rope, biking and dancing, train the heart best.
