# 1. Beowulf Boast Builder
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, two colors of pen
ican: I can analyze how a poem's language, including kennings and caesuras, reveals a culture's values.
directions: Read Beowulf's two speeches before he fights Grendel, in Francis Gummere's translation. Underline every kenning, mark the caesura in the lines named below with a slash (/), and answer the questions about what the boast reveals about heroic values.
mistakes: Students often call any compound word a kenning (Grendel-deeds, home-land). A kenning is a figurative name that stands in for a noun: "Warriors'-shield" is not a shield but the king who protects his warriors. Also watch for caesura marks placed by counting syllables: the pause falls between the two half-lines, usually at a punctuation mark or where the alliterating sounds split into two groups.
notes: Gummere's translation (first published 1909, in The Oldest English Epic) keeps the Old English pattern of two half-lines joined by alliteration. Text follows Project Gutenberg eBook #981, with Gummere's dashes printed as they appear there (--) and [...] marking a cut. In the poem a vaunt (boast) is a public promise, not bragging: a warrior who boasts is bound to make his words good or die trying.

## Read: Beowulf speaks
source: Beowulf, translated by Francis B. Gummere (first published 1909): [1] and [2] from Beowulf's greeting to King Hrothgar; [3] from his vaunt before the fight
  [1] "Thou Hrothgar, hail! Hygelac's I,
  kinsman and follower. Fame a plenty
  have I gained in youth! These Grendel-deeds
  I heard in my home-land heralded clear. [...]
  [2] thou sovran of the Shining-Danes,
  Scyldings'-bulwark, a boon I seek, --
  and, Friend-of-the-folk, refuse it not,
  O Warriors'-shield, now I've wandered far, --
  that I alone with my liegemen here,
  this hardy band, may Heorot purge!
  More I hear, that the monster dire,
  in his wanton mood, of weapons recks not;
  hence shall I scorn -- so Hygelac stay,
  king of my kindred, kind to me! --
  brand or buckler to bear in the fight,
  gold-colored targe: but with gripe alone
  must I front the fiend and fight for life,
  foe against foe. [...]"
  [3] "Of force in fight no feebler I count me,
  in grim war-deeds, than Grendel deems him.
  Not with the sword, then, to sleep of death
  his life will I give, though it lie in my power.
  No skill is his to strike against me,
  my shield to hew though he hardy be,
  bold in battle; we both, this night,
  shall spurn the sword, if he seek me here,
  unweaponed, for war. Let wisest God,
  sacred Lord, on which side soever
  doom decree as he deemeth right."

## Warm up: Word help
match: Match each old word to its meaning.
  - boon = a favor asked of someone
  - recks not = does not care about
  - brand = a sword
  - targe = a small round shield
  - gripe = grip, the hands' hold

## Main: Kennings
fill:
  - Three kennings in passage 2 that name King Hrothgar: {Scyldings'-bulwark}, {Friend-of-the-folk}, {Warriors'-shield}
write: Choose one kenning. Explain what it literally says and what it reveals about what a king should be.
  lines: 3
  key: "Warriors'-shield": a king is pictured as the shield that protects his fighters, so a good king guards his people. ("Scyldings'-bulwark" works the same way: he is the wall of his people. "Friend-of-the-folk": he is generous and loyal to them.)

## Main: Caesuras
text: Mark the caesura with a slash in each of these lines from passage 3. Write the word the pause falls after and the sound that alliterates across the two halves.
fill:
  - "Of force in fight no feebler I count me," pause after {fight}; alliteration on {f}
  - "in grim war-deeds, than Grendel deems him." pause after {war-deeds}; alliteration on {g}
  - "No skill is his to strike against me," pause after {his}; alliteration on {s}
  - "bold in battle; we both, this night," pause after {battle}; alliteration on {b}
write: Why would a poem first performed aloud use a pause in the middle of every line and repeated first sounds?
  lines: 3
  key: The pause and alliteration give each line a steady, chant-like beat that a scop (poet) could perform and an audience could follow and remember; they also make key words, like force and fight, stand out.

## Main: What the boast reveals
write: In passage 1, how does Beowulf introduce himself? What does this show the culture valued?
  lines: 3
  key: He names his lord and kinsman, Hygelac, and his "Fame." Identity comes from loyalty to a lord and family and from a reputation earned by deeds.
write: In passage 2 and 3, Beowulf vows to fight without a sword or shield. Why? What value does this show?
  lines: 3
  key: Because Grendel "of weapons recks not," Beowulf will meet him "foe against foe" and "spurn the sword." It shows that honor demands a fair fight and that glory is greater when won by one's own strength.
write: The vaunt ends by leaving the outcome to God ("doom decree as he deemeth right"). What does this add to the picture of a hero?
  lines: 3
  key: A hero is confident but accepts that fate or God decides who lives; courage means fighting anyway. The poem mixes the warrior code with Christian faith.

## Stretch: Build a boast
write: Write a four-line boast in the Beowulf style for a modern challenge (a final exam, a big game, a hard climb). Use one kenning and alliteration in every line, and mark each caesura.
  lines: 5
  key: Example: Hear me, test-giver, / I take your trial; / the night-lamp's friend / has fought these figures; / no notes, no net, / my knowledge alone; / let the grade-giver / judge as is just. (Kennings: night-lamp's friend for a student who studies late; grade-giver for the teacher.)
# 22. Doublets: Kingly, Royal, Regal
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, a dictionary with etymologies (print or class set)
ican: I can sort related words by their origin and explain how origin affects a word's register.
directions: Each set has three words with similar meanings. Sort each set by origin: Old English (native English), French (arrived mostly after the Norman Conquest of 1066) or Latin (borrowed later, often by scholars). Then explain how origin changes register.
mistakes: Students may sort by length or spelling alone. Length is a clue, not a rule: the Latin word is usually longest and most formal, the Old English word shortest and plainest. Point out that French itself came from Latin, so the French and Latin words in a set are often cousins (royal and regal both trace to Latin regalis); the column shows the route the word took into English.
notes: The sets follow the well-known English, French and Latin triplets listed in Baugh and Cable's A History of the English Language (for example rise, mount, ascend and fear, terror, trepidation). Some Latin-column words, such as regal and secure, also passed partly through French; the column reflects the usual classroom grouping. Check doubtful cases in a dictionary's etymology notes.

## Warm up
example: kingly (Old English cyning, king) | royal (Old French roial) | regal (Latin regalis). All three mean "of a king," but each feels different: a kingly gift, the royal family, a regal bearing.
choice: Which word from each set is usually the most formal?
  - The Old English word
  - The French word
  * The Latin word

## Main: Register
write: Describe the pattern. How does each origin tend to sound: everyday, polished, or technical and formal?
  lines: 4
  key: Old English words are short, plain and everyday (ask, fire, end); French words, from the language of the Norman court, sound more polished or elevated (royal, question, commence); Latin words, borrowed by scholars, lawyers and the church, are long, formal or technical (interrogate, conflagration, conclude).
write: Why might a police report say "interrogate" while a parent says "ask"? Use the history of the words.
  lines: 3
  key: Interrogate came into English from Latin, the language of law and learning, so it sounds official and precise; ask is native everyday English, so it sounds friendly and plain.
write: Choose one set. Write three sentences, each using one word in a setting where its register fits.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: The house caught fire. The candle's flame flickered. The newspaper reported a conflagration that destroyed three city blocks.

## Stretch
write: After 1066, the English words for farm animals (cow, pig, sheep) stayed English, while the words for their meat (beef, pork, mutton) came from French. What does this pattern suggest about who spoke which language?
  lines: 3
  key: English-speaking farmers raised the animals, while the French-speaking Norman nobles ate the meat at their tables, so the French words became the words for food served to the upper class; the split shows language following social rank.
