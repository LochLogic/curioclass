# 1. Beowulf Boast Builder
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, two colors of pen
ican: I can analyze how a poem's language, including kennings and caesuras, reveals a culture's values.
directions: Read Beowulf's two speeches before he fights Grendel, in Francis Gummere's translation. Underline every kenning, mark the caesura in the lines named below with a slash (/), and answer the questions about what the boast reveals about heroic values.
mistakes: Students often call any compound word a kenning (Grendel-deeds, home-land). A kenning is a figurative name that stands in for a noun: "Warriors'-shield" is not a shield but the king who protects his warriors. Also watch for caesura marks placed by counting syllables: the pause falls between the two half-lines, usually at a punctuation mark or where the alliterating sounds split into two groups.
notes: Gummere's translation (first published 1909, in The Oldest English Epic) keeps the Old English pattern of two half-lines joined by alliteration. Text follows Project Gutenberg eBook #981, with Gummere's dashes printed as they appear there (--) and [...] marking a cut. In the poem a vaunt (boast) is a public promise, not bragging: a warrior who boasts is bound to make his words good or die trying.

## Read: Beowulf speaks
source: Beowulf, translated by Francis B. Gummere (first published 1909): [1] and [2] from Beowulf's greeting to King Hrothgar; [3] from his vaunt before the fight
  [1] "Thou Hrothgar, hail! Hygelac's I,
  kinsman and follower. Fame a plenty
  have I gained in youth! These Grendel-deeds
  I heard in my home-land heralded clear. [...]
  [2] thou sovran of the Shining-Danes,
  Scyldings'-bulwark, a boon I seek, --
  and, Friend-of-the-folk, refuse it not,
  O Warriors'-shield, now I've wandered far, --
  that I alone with my liegemen here,
  this hardy band, may Heorot purge!
  More I hear, that the monster dire,
  in his wanton mood, of weapons recks not;
  hence shall I scorn -- so Hygelac stay,
  king of my kindred, kind to me! --
  brand or buckler to bear in the fight,
  gold-colored targe: but with gripe alone
  must I front the fiend and fight for life,
  foe against foe. [...]"
  [3] "Of force in fight no feebler I count me,
  in grim war-deeds, than Grendel deems him.
  [...] No skill is his to strike against me,
  my shield to hew though he hardy be,
  bold in battle; we both, this night,
  shall spurn the sword, if he seek me here,
  unweaponed, for war. Let wisest God,
  sacred Lord, on which side soever
  doom decree as he deemeth right."

## Warm up: Word help
match: Match each old word to its meaning.
  - recks not = does not care about
  - brand = a sword
  - targe = a small round shield
  - gripe = grip, the hands' hold

## Main: Kennings
fill:
  - Three kennings in passage 2 that name King Hrothgar: {Scyldings'-bulwark}, {Friend-of-the-folk}, {Warriors'-shield}
write: Choose one kenning. Explain what it literally says and what it reveals about what a king should be.
  lines: 2
  key: "Warriors'-shield": a king is pictured as the shield that protects his fighters, so a good king guards his people. ("Scyldings'-bulwark" works the same way: he is the wall of his people. "Friend-of-the-folk": he is generous and loyal to them.)

## Main: Caesuras
text: Mark the caesura with a slash in each of these lines from passage 3. Write the word the pause falls after and the sound that alliterates across the two halves.
fill:
  - "Of force in fight no feebler I count me," pause after {fight}; alliteration on {f}
  - "in grim war-deeds, than Grendel deems him." pause after {war-deeds}; alliteration on {g}
  - "No skill is his to strike against me," pause after {his}; alliteration on {s}
  - "bold in battle; we both, this night," pause after {battle}; alliteration on {b}
write: Why would a poem first performed aloud use a pause in the middle of every line and repeated first sounds?
  lines: 2
  key: The pause and alliteration give each line a steady, chant-like beat that a scop (poet) could perform and an audience could follow and remember; they also make key words, like force and fight, stand out.

## Main: What the boast reveals
write: In passage 1, how does Beowulf introduce himself? What does this show the culture valued?
  lines: 2
  key: He names his lord and kinsman, Hygelac, and his "Fame." Identity comes from loyalty to a lord and family and from a reputation earned by deeds.
write: In passage 2 and 3, Beowulf vows to fight without a sword or shield. Why? What value does this show?
  lines: 2
  key: Because Grendel "of weapons recks not," Beowulf will meet him "foe against foe" and "spurn the sword." It shows that honor demands a fair fight and that glory is greater when won by one's own strength.
write: The vaunt ends by leaving the outcome to God ("doom decree as he deemeth right"). What does this add to the picture of a hero?
  lines: 2
  key: A hero is confident but accepts that fate or God decides who lives; courage means fighting anyway. The poem mixes the warrior code with Christian faith.

# 5. Romantic Poets Station Cards
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, scissors and tape for the teacher (to post the five cards around the room)
ican: I can analyze how Romantic poets present nature, power and childhood.
directions: Your teacher will post the five station cards. At each station, read the poem twice and fill in its row: which idea it is most about (nature, power or childhood), what the poem says about that idea, and the words that show it. Then answer the questions that compare the poems.
mistakes: Students often label a poem by its surface subject (a rainbow, so "nature") and miss the claim: "My Heart Leaps Up" is as much about childhood and the link between the child and the adult. Accept any of the three ideas with a good reason. Watch also for "Ozymandias" read as praise of the king: the sneer and the "lone and level sands" mock his power.
notes: Texts follow Project Gutenberg: Blake, Songs of Innocence and of Experience (eBook #1934); Wordsworth, Poems in Two Volumes, Volume 2 (eBook #8824), where "My Heart Leaps Up" first appeared in 1807 without a title; Shelley, Complete Poetical Works (eBook #4800); Keats, Poems 1817 (eBook #8209), where the sonnet is dated December 30, 1816 and "--" is the edition's dash. A slash marks a line break. "Infant Joy" (1789) is from Songs of Innocence and "Infant Sorrow" (1794) from Songs of Experience; Blake meant the two books to be read as "contrary states."

## Station cards: cut apart and post
cards:
  plain: yes
  - **Station 1. William Blake, "Infant Joy" (1789)** "I have no name; / I am but two days old." / What shall I call thee? / "I happy am, / Joy is my name." / Sweet joy befall thee! // Pretty joy! / Sweet joy, but two days old. / Sweet joy I call thee: / Thou dost smile, / I sing the while; / Sweet joy befall thee!
  - **Station 2. William Blake, "Infant Sorrow" (1794)** My mother groaned, my father wept: / Into the dangerous world I leapt, / Helpless, naked, piping loud, / Like a fiend hid in a cloud. // Struggling in my father's hands, / Striving against my swaddling bands, / Bound and weary, I thought best / To sulk upon my mother's breast.
  - **Station 3. William Wordsworth, "My Heart Leaps Up" (1807)** My heart leaps up when I behold / A Rainbow in the sky: / So was it when my life began; / So is it now I am a Man; / So be it when I shall grow old, / Or let me die! / The Child is Father of the Man; / And I could wish my days to be / Bound each to each by natural piety.
  - **Station 4. Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818)** I met a traveller from an antique land / Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone / Stand in the desert...Near them, on the sand, / Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown, / And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command, / Tell that its sculptor well those passions read / Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things, / The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed: / And on the pedestal these words appear: / 'My name is Ozymandias, king of kings: / Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!' / Nothing beside remains. Round the decay / Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare / The lone and level sands stretch far away.
  - **Station 5. John Keats, "On the Grasshopper and Cricket" (1817)** The poetry of earth is never dead: / When all the birds are faint with the hot sun, / And hide in cooling trees, a voice will run / From hedge to hedge about the new-mown mead; / That is the Grasshopper's--he takes the lead / In summer luxury,--he has never done / With his delights; for when tired out with fun / He rests at ease beneath some pleasant weed. / The poetry of earth is ceasing never: / On a lone winter evening, when the frost / Has wrought a silence, from the stove there shrills / The Cricket's song, in warmth increasing ever, / And seems to one in drowsiness half lost, / The Grasshopper's among some grassy hills.

## Main: Station notes
chart: Nature, power or childhood? | What the poem says about it | Words that show it
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5
  height: 0.7
  key: 1 childhood: a newborn is pure happiness, named by its own joy; "I happy am, / Joy is my name," "Thou dost smile." 2 childhood: birth is a struggle into a "dangerous world" and the baby is already held down and resisting; "Struggling," "Bound and weary," "sulk." 3 childhood and nature: the wonder a child feels at nature should last a whole life, and the child shapes the adult; "The Child is Father of the Man," "natural piety." 4 power: even the mightiest ruler's power and pride are erased by time, while art and the desert outlast him; "Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!" "Nothing beside remains." 5 nature: nature's music never stops, from summer's grasshopper to winter's cricket; "The poetry of earth is never dead," "ceasing never."

## Compare the stations
choice: How do stations 1 and 2 differ?
  - Both show childhood as purely happy.
  - Station 1 is about nature and station 2 is about power.
  - Station 2 is written from a parent's point of view only.
  * Station 1 shows a child's innocent joy; station 2 shows a child already struggling against a hard world.
choice: In station 4, what finally outlasts the king's power?
  - his statue, still standing whole
  * the sculptor's art and the empty desert
  - his kingdom and its great buildings
  - the traveller who tells the story
write: Wordsworth writes, "The Child is Father of the Man." Explain the paradox and how it fits the Romantic view of childhood.
  lines: 3
  key: A child cannot literally be a man's father, but the child we were shapes the adult we become. Romantics prized childhood as a time of pure feeling and closeness to nature, and Wordsworth hopes to keep that childlike wonder all his life.

# 16. Talk Blueprint: Perspective and Line of Reasoning
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, a timer for rehearsal
ican: I can plan a formal presentation with a clear perspective, a line of reasoning, alternate views and strategic media.
directions: Choose a topic you can argue in a 6-minute formal talk. Budget your time, state your perspective, build a line of reasoning, plan how you will address alternate views, and choose media that does a specific job.
mistakes: Students often plan slides first and the argument second, or use media as decoration (a title photo that proves nothing). Every visual should do a job: show data, show a place, or show a contrast. Watch also for talks that list three reasons with no order; a line of reasoning says why reason 2 comes after reason 1. Six minutes is about 750 to 900 spoken words.
notes: The key models a talk on replacing lawn strips in a town's road medians with native plants; the model's claims are for planning practice, not facts to present. In rehearsal, students should time each section and cut, not speed up, if they run long.

## Warm up: Budget the time
text: A 6-minute talk has an opening of 0:45, alternate views of 1:00 and a conclusion of 0:45.
fill:
  - Time left for the reasons: {3:30}
  - Split evenly among three reasons, each reason gets: {1:10}
  - Spoken at about 140 words a minute, one reason is about {163} words.
choice: Which media choice is most strategic?
  - A title slide with a photo of the speaker's favorite flower
  * A before-and-after photo pair of one median, shown exactly when the speaker claims the change is visible
  - Ten slides of bullet points that repeat the script word for word
  - A video that plays during the whole talk

## Main: The blueprint
write: Your perspective in one sentence (the claim and why it matters to this audience).
  lines: 2
  key: Our town should replace the grass in its road medians with native plants, because it would cut water use and mowing costs and give pollinators a home, all on land we already own.
chart: Time | What I will say | Media and the job it does
  labels: Opening; Reason 1; Reason 2; Reason 3; Alternate views; Conclusion
  height: 0.75
  key: Opening 0:45: a story of the crew mowing in July heat; claim. Media: one photo of a dry, brown median (shows the problem). Reason 1 1:10: water. Media: simple bar chart of water used by grass vs. native plants (shows data). Reason 2 1:10: cost of mowing and watering over ten years. Media: one number on a slide. Reason 3 1:10: pollinators and habitat. Media: before-and-after photos of a planted median elsewhere (shows the result). Alternate views 1:00: "Native plants look messy" and "Planting costs money up front." Responses: designs with edges look tended; savings cover the cost in a few years. Media: none, eye contact. Conclusion 0:45: return to the July mowing story, call to action for the town meeting. Total 6:00.
write: Explain your line of reasoning: why are your reasons in this order?
  lines: 3
  key: I start with water because it is the most concrete problem the audience has seen; cost comes next because it answers "can we afford it?"; pollinators come last because it is the most inspiring reason and leaves the audience with a hopeful picture before I answer objections.

## Stretch: Answer the strongest alternate view
write: Write the sentence you will say to answer the strongest alternate view. Begin by granting what is true in it.
  lines: 2
  key: It is true that planting costs more than mowing this year, but at the town's own rates the savings on water and mowing would repay that cost within a few years and keep saving after that.

# 22. Doublets: Kingly, Royal, Regal
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, a dictionary with etymologies (print or class set)
ican: I can sort related words by their origin and explain how origin affects a word's register.
directions: Each set has three words with similar meanings. Sort each set by origin: Old English (native English), French (arrived mostly after the Norman Conquest of 1066) or Latin (borrowed later, often by scholars). Then explain how origin changes register.
mistakes: Students may sort by length or spelling alone. Length is a clue, not a rule: the Latin word is usually longest and most formal, the Old English word shortest and plainest. Point out that French itself came from Latin, so the French and Latin words in a set are often cousins (royal and regal both trace to Latin regalis); the column shows the route the word took into English.
notes: The sets follow the well-known English, French and Latin triplets listed in Baugh and Cable's A History of the English Language (for example rise, mount, ascend and fear, terror, trepidation). Some Latin-column words, such as regal and secure, also passed partly through French; the column reflects the usual classroom grouping. Check doubtful cases in a dictionary's etymology notes.

## Warm up
example: kingly (Old English cyning, king) | royal (Old French roial) | regal (Latin regalis). All three mean "of a king," but each feels different: a kingly gift, the royal family, a regal bearing.
choice: Which word from each set is usually the most formal?
  - The Old English word
  - The French word
  * The Latin word

## Main: Register
write: Describe the pattern. How does each origin tend to sound: everyday, polished, or technical and formal?
  lines: 4
  key: Old English words are short, plain and everyday (ask, fire, end); French words, from the language of the Norman court, sound more polished or elevated (royal, question, commence); Latin words, borrowed by scholars, lawyers and the church, are long, formal or technical (interrogate, conflagration, conclude).
write: Why might a police report say "interrogate" while a parent says "ask"? Use the history of the words.
  lines: 3
  key: Interrogate came into English from Latin, the language of law and learning, so it sounds official and precise; ask is native everyday English, so it sounds friendly and plain.
write: Choose one set. Write three sentences, each using one word in a setting where its register fits.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: The house caught fire. The candle's flame flickered. The newspaper reported a conflagration that destroyed three city blocks.

## Stretch
write: After 1066, the English words for farm animals (cow, pig, sheep) stayed English, while the words for their meat (beef, pork, mutton) came from French. What does this pattern suggest about who spoke which language?
  lines: 3
  key: English-speaking farmers raised the animals, while the French-speaking Norman nobles ate the meat at their tables, so the French words became the words for food served to the upper class; the split shows language following social rank.

# 27. Theme Web: Macbeth
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, three colors of highlighter, your copy of Macbeth
ican: I can analyze how several themes interact and build on one another across a work.
directions: Read the quotation bank. Highlight words of ambition, guilt and fate in three colors. For each act, choose the quotation where two of the forces meet, name the link (for example, fate feeds ambition), and explain how they interact. Then decide which force drives the tragedy.
mistakes: Students often treat the three themes separately ("Act 1 is ambition, Act 5 is guilt"). The task is to find the links: the witches' prophecy (fate) wakes ambition; ambition's crimes produce guilt; guilt drives Macbeth back to the witches, whose new prophecies feed false confidence. Watch also for the claim that fate makes Macbeth kill Duncan: in 1.3 he hopes "chance may crown me / Without my stir," and in 1.7 he admits his only spur is ambition.
notes: Quotations follow Project Gutenberg eBook #1533 (The Tragedy of Macbeth), with curly apostrophes made straight; act and scene numbers follow that edition. More than one link per act is possible; accept any well-argued pair. The quotation bank lists more than one choice for most acts.

## Quotation bank
source: William Shakespeare, Macbeth (first performed about 1606)
  [1] Witches (1.3): "All hail, Macbeth! that shalt be king hereafter!"
  [2] Macbeth, aside (1.3): "If chance will have me king, why, chance may crown me / Without my stir."
  [3] Macbeth, aside (1.4): "Stars, hide your fires! / Let not light see my black and deep desires."
  [4] Macbeth (1.7): "I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on th' other"
  [5] Macbeth (2.2): "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?"
  [6] Banquo (3.1): "Thou hast it now, King, Cawdor, Glamis, all, / As the Weird Women promis'd; and, I fear, / Thou play'dst most foully for't"
  [7] Macbeth (3.2): "O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!"
  [8] Macbeth (3.4): "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er."
  [9] Apparition (4.1): "none of woman born / Shall harm Macbeth."
  [10] Lady Macbeth (5.1): "all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand."
  [11] Macbeth (5.5): "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day"

choice: In quotation 2, what does Macbeth first hope fate will do?
  - Make Banquo king instead of him
  * Make him king without his having to act
  - Punish him for his desires
  - Warn Duncan of danger
choice: Which quotation shows guilt most directly?
  - Quotation 1
  - Quotation 9

  - Quotation 4

## Main: The web, act by act
chart: The link (which forces meet) | Quotation number and key words | How the two forces interact
  labels: Act 1; Act 2; Act 3; Act 4; Act 5
  height: 0.9
  key: Act 1: fate feeds ambition. [1] "shalt be king" then [3] "black and deep desires" (or [4] "Vaulting ambition"). The prophecy names a future, and ambition decides not to wait for it. Act 2: ambition produces guilt. [5] "wash this blood / Clean." The murder ambition demanded leaves a stain no ocean can clean; guilt starts the moment ambition succeeds. Act 3: guilt and ambition drive each other, while fate still haunts him. [8] "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far" (or [7] scorpions; [6] Banquo's suspicion of the prophecy). Guilt does not stop him; it pushes him to more murder to stay safe. Act 4: fate feeds false confidence. [9] "none of woman born." Guilt and fear send him back to the witches, and their riddling prophecy makes his ambition reckless. Act 5: guilt destroys, and fate closes in. [10] the perfumes of Arabia (Lady Macbeth's guilt), [11] "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" (Macbeth's emptiness). The ambition that started with a prophecy ends in meaninglessness.

## Stretch: Which force drives the tragedy?
write: Is Macbeth's fall caused mainly by fate, ambition or guilt? Take a position and support it with two quotation numbers.
  lines: 4
  key: Ambition drives it. Fate only names a future [1], and Macbeth first hopes "chance may crown me / Without my stir" [2]; he admits his only spur is "Vaulting ambition" [4]. Guilt follows his choices rather than causing them, and the later prophecies matter only because ambition makes him trust them.

# 38. Voice from an Old Photograph
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, a printed or projected public domain street photograph from the early 1900s (teacher chooses)
ican: I can write a reflective narrative scene with deliberate pacing and a consistent voice.
directions: Study the photograph your teacher shows. Choose one figure in it. Plan a scene in that person's voice built around one small action (tying a shoe, lifting a basket, counting coins), slowing the action down so thoughts and memories fit inside it. Then write about 300 words.
mistakes: Students often write a whole day or a life story instead of one moment, or describe the photo from outside ("In this picture there is a man"). The scene should be in first person, in the present moment of the photograph, and the small action should frame it: start it at the beginning, return to it in the middle, finish it at the end. Watch for invented facts presented as history; this is imagined voice, so the figure needs no real name.
notes: A good source: the Library of Congress Detroit Publishing Company Collection, street scenes from roughly 1880 to 1920; for example, its photographs of Mulberry Street, New York City, around 1900. Check the rights statement on the item page (the Library lists no known restrictions on publication for that collection). Avoid photographs of children at work or of people in distress unless the class has studied the context; a market, a crowded street or a shop door works well. The model in the key is for a market-street photograph.

## Warm up: Look closely
chart: What I see | What I imagine (sound, smell, temperature)
  labels: People and what they wear; The place and the weather; One small object; The figure I choose
  height: 0.55
  key: Example: People: women in long skirts, a boy in a cap, a vendor in an apron. Place: a narrow street, awnings, wet cobblestones, a cart. Object: a scale on the fruit cart. Figure: the vendor, mid-forties, looking toward the camera. Imagined: wheels on stone, shouting in two languages, the smell of peaches going soft, cool morning air.

## Main: Plan the action
chart: Beat of the small action | What I think or remember during it
  labels: The action starts; Middle; Almost done; The action ends
  height: 0.6
  key: Model (the vendor weighs peaches): Starts: she lifts the first peach onto the scale; she thinks the stranger with the camera should buy something. Middle: the needle wobbles; she remembers her mother's orchard and the first peach she ever weighed. Almost done: she adds one more peach for the boy who cannot pay; she thinks no one will see. Ends: the needle settles; she looks up and meets the camera, as if to say "now you know."
write: Write your scene in the figure's voice (about 300 words). Keep it to one moment; let the small action pace it.
  lines: 16
  key: Model opening: The first peach goes on the scale, and the man with the box on legs is still looking at me. Buy something, I want to tell him. The needle shivers, the way my mother's hands shivered when she taught me to read it, back when the peaches were ours... (Strong responses stay in first person and in one moment, return to the action at least three times, slow down at the most important beat, and end when the action ends.)

## Stretch: Check your pacing
write: Underline the sentence where your scene moves slowest. Why did you choose to slow down there?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: I slowed down at "Almost done," when she adds the extra peach, because it reveals who she is, and it is the one choice in the scene.
