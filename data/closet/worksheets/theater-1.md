# 1. Who, Where, What? Story Starter
minutes: 30
materials: crayons, pencil
ican: I can plan a pretend story with a character, a setting and a problem.
directions: (Read aloud.) A story needs a who (a character), a where (a setting) and a what (a problem). Draw one of each in the boxes. Then act out a 1-minute pretend story with a partner.
mistakes: The "what" box is the hard one: children draw a thing (a ball) instead of a problem (the ball rolled into the pond). Ask "What goes wrong?" Some children draw three characters; steer them to one who, one place, one problem.
notes: Model it first with your own three drawings (a dragon, a library, the dragon is too big to fit through the door). Time the stories with a one-minute sand timer if you have one. A good pretend story has the problem fixed or changed by the end; praise any ending.

## Warm up: Who, where or what?
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHO, a character?
  * A dragon
  - A beach
  - The kite is stuck in a tree.
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHERE, a setting?
  - A puppy
  * A beach
  - The puppy is lost.
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHAT, a problem?
  - A park
  - A girl
  * The kite is stuck in a tree.

## Main: Draw your story starter
draw: WHO: my character | 1.8
draw: WHERE: my setting | 1.8
draw: WHAT: the problem | 1.8

## Stretch: Act it out
choice: (Read aloud.) You are acting out your story. What should happen at the end?
  - The character falls asleep in the middle.
  * The problem gets fixed or changes.
  - You start a new story.

# 21. Same Line, Three Feelings
minutes: 20
materials: crayons, pencil; for the teacher: one puppet (a sock puppet works)
ican: I can show a feeling with my face, my voice and my body.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the puppet say one line three different ways. After each one, draw the puppet's face and arms to show how it feels. Then circle the feeling.
mistakes: Children often draw only the face. Ask "What were the puppet's arms doing?" and act it again. Some children circle the feeling of the words ("a new hat" sounds happy) instead of the voice; the point is that the same words can carry different feelings.
notes: The line for all three: "I got a new hat." Version 1, happy: big bright voice, the puppet bounces, arms (or your hand) up high. Version 2, sad: slow, quiet voice, head down, arms hanging. Version 3, mad: loud, sharp voice, the puppet stomps and crosses its arms. Say each version twice, and let the class copy it with their own voices and bodies before they draw. Even a sock puppet whose face cannot move shows feelings through voice and body: that is the big idea.

## Version 1
draw: The puppet's face and arms, version 1 | 1.6
choice: (Read aloud.) How did the puppet feel?
  * Happy
  - Sad
  - Mad

## Version 2
draw: The puppet's face and arms, version 2 | 1.6
choice: (Read aloud.) How did the puppet feel?
  - Happy
  * Sad
  - Mad

## Version 3
draw: The puppet's face and arms, version 3 | 1.6
choice: (Read aloud.) How did the puppet feel?
  - Happy
  - Sad
  * Mad

## Stretch
choice: (Read aloud.) A sock puppet's face cannot move. How can it still show a feeling?
  * With its voice and its body
  - With its name
  - With its color
