# 1. Who, Where, What? Story Starter
minutes: 30
materials: crayons, pencil
ican: I can plan a pretend story with a character, a setting and a problem.
directions: (Read aloud.) A story needs a who (a character), a where (a setting) and a what (a problem). Draw one of each in the boxes. Then act out a 1-minute pretend story with a partner.
mistakes: The "what" box is the hard one: children draw a thing (a ball) instead of a problem (the ball rolled into the pond). Ask "What goes wrong?" Some children draw three characters; steer them to one who, one place, one problem.
notes: Model it first with your own three drawings (a dragon, a library, the dragon is too big to fit through the door). Time the stories with a one-minute sand timer if you have one. A good pretend story has the problem fixed or changed by the end; praise any ending.

## Warm up: Who, where or what?
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHO, a character?
  * A dragon
  - A beach
  - The kite is stuck in a tree.
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHERE, a setting?
  - A puppy
  * A beach
  - The puppy is lost.
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a WHAT, a problem?
  - A park
  - A girl
  * The kite is stuck in a tree.

## Main: Draw your story starter
draw: WHO: my character | 1.8
draw: WHERE: my setting | 1.8
draw: WHAT: the problem | 1.8

## Stretch: Act it out
choice: (Read aloud.) You are acting out your story. What should happen at the end?
  - The character falls asleep in the middle.
  * The problem gets fixed or changes.
  - You start a new story.

# 7. Make the Storm: Sound Plan
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan body sounds that match the parts of a story.
directions: (Read aloud.) Learn the 4 body sounds. Listen to the storm story. Under each picture, write the body sound you will make for that part. Then we will make the storm together.
mistakes: Children may choose the loudest sound for every part. Ask, "Is this part soft or loud? Small or big?" Wind is a soft swishing sound (rub), the first drops are small (snap), heavy rain is busy and loud (tap), and thunder is the biggest (stomp). Some children cannot snap yet; a soft click of the tongue works.
notes: Teach the sounds first: rub (rub palms together), tap (fingertips tap the desk), snap (snap fingers), stomp (stomp feet). The key shows the usual plan, from soft to loud; accept another sound if the child can say why it fits the picture. Perform it as a class: you read, the class makes each sound, growing louder to the thunder. For the ending, do the sounds backward (tap, snap, rub, then silence) as the storm rolls away.

## Warm up: Our body sounds
picword:
  - rub | rub
  - tap | tap
  - snap | snap
  - stomp | stomp

## Listen
passage: The Storm
  The wind starts to blow. Whoosh, whoosh! (1)
  A few raindrops fall. Drip, drop. (2)
  Now the rain falls faster and faster. It drums on the roof. (3)
  CRASH! Thunder shakes the sky. (4)
  Then the storm rolls away, and everything is quiet.

## Main: My sound plan
picword:
  - wind | 1. {rub}
  - cloud | 2. {snap}
  - rain | 3. {tap}
  - lightning | 4. {stomp}

## Stretch
choice: (Read aloud.) The storm rolls away. What should our sounds do?
  - Get louder and louder
  * Get softer and softer, then stop
  - Stay the same
draw: Draw the sky after the storm. | 1.6

# 20. Why Does the Puppet Feel That Way?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons; for the show: one puppet, a balloon (or a picture of one), a small toy
ican: I can tell what made a puppet feel the way it does.
directions: (Read aloud.) Watch the puppet in 3 moments. For each one, draw what happened in the "Why" box. Draw the puppet's face in the "Feeling" box and write the feeling word.
mistakes: Children often draw the feeling twice (a sad face in both boxes). The "Why" box shows what happened: the balloon floating away, two puppets playing, the toy in pieces. Some write a feeling that does not fit the cause; ask, "If that happened to the puppet, how would it feel?"
notes: Act each moment with the puppet before children draw. 1. The puppet holds a balloon; the string slips and it floats up and away: "My balloon!" (sad). 2. A new puppet says, "Want to play?" and they play tag (happy or excited). 3. The puppet's toy falls and breaks: "Oh no! My toy!" (sad, upset or mad). Accept any feeling that fits the cause and the puppet's face and voice. Feeling words: happy, sad, mad, scared, excited.

## Warm up: Feeling faces
picword:
  - face-happy | happy
  - face-sad | sad
  - face-mad | mad
  - face-scared | scared
choice: (Read aloud.) The puppet gets a big hug from a friend. How does it feel?
  - Mad
  * Happy
  - Scared

## Main: Why and feeling
chart: Why: what happened (draw) | Feeling (draw the face, write the word)
  labels: 1. Lost balloon; 2. New friend; 3. Broken toy
  height: 1.35
  key: 1. The balloon floats away: sad. 2. A new puppet asks to play: happy or excited. 3. The toy falls and breaks: sad, upset or mad.

## Stretch
write: (Read aloud.) What could the puppet do to feel better after the toy breaks?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary: ask a grown-up to help fix it, play with a different toy, tell a friend how it feels.

# 21. Same Line, Three Feelings
minutes: 20
materials: crayons, pencil; for the teacher: one puppet (a sock puppet works)
ican: I can show a feeling with my face, my voice and my body.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the puppet say one line three different ways. After each one, draw the puppet's face and arms to show how it feels. Then circle the feeling.
mistakes: Children often draw only the face. Ask "What were the puppet's arms doing?" and act it again. Some children circle the feeling of the words ("a new hat" sounds happy) instead of the voice; the point is that the same words can carry different feelings.
notes: The line for all three: "I got a new hat." Version 1, happy: big bright voice, the puppet bounces, arms (or your hand) up high. Version 2, sad: slow, quiet voice, head down, arms hanging. Version 3, mad: loud, sharp voice, the puppet stomps and crosses its arms. Say each version twice, and let the class copy it with their own voices and bodies before they draw. Even a sock puppet whose face cannot move shows feelings through voice and body: that is the big idea.

## Version 3
draw: The puppet's face and arms, version 3 | 1.6

## Stretch
choice: (Read aloud.) A sock puppet's face cannot move. How can it still show a feeling?
  * With its voice and its body
  - With its name
  - With its color

# 33. My Role Card
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can make a character and tell how it helps the group.
directions: (Read aloud.) We are going to the Moon Base. Choose a role. Draw your character. Write its name and its job. Then write one thing your character knows that helps the crew.
mistakes: Children often pick a job but no knowledge ("I am a pilot"). Ask, "What does your character know that the crew needs?" Some write facts that are not true of the moon (there is rain); check them against the True or Not sheet.
notes: Use after True or Not? Moon Base Facts. Roles to offer: pilot, scientist, doctor, plant grower, robot fixer, cook, radio talker. Things the character might know: the moon has no air, so we wear space suits; there is no rain, so we bring water; nights on the moon are long and very cold; the moon has dusty ground and craters. Children wear or hold their cards during the drama and speak in role: "I am Dr. Lin, the crew doctor. I know..."

## Warm up
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one could a moon crew member know that helps the group?
  * The moon has no air, so we wear space suits.
  - The moon is made of cheese.
  - It rains on the moon every day.
choice: (Read aloud.) Which one is a job?
  - Happy
  - Blue
  * Pilot

## Main: My role card
draw: My character | 2.4
write: My name in role:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example: Captain Rosa, Dr. Lin, Robo-Fixer Sam.
write: My job:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary: pilot, scientist, doctor, plant grower, robot fixer, cook.
write: I know ... (one thing that helps the crew)
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary; a true moon fact or a job skill, for example: The moon has no air, so we must keep our helmets on. Plants need water and light to grow.
