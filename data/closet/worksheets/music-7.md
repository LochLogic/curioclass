# 1. Major, Minor and Perfect Intervals
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; a keyboard picture or real keyboard helps
ican: I can name an interval's quality and size by counting half steps.
directions: Use the reference chart. For each interval, count the letter names for its size, count the half steps on a keyboard, and then write its quality and label (M3, m6, P5).
pairs: chord-explorer
mistakes: Students forget that E to F and B to C are half steps, so they count D to F as 4 half steps (a major 3rd) instead of 3 (a minor 3rd). Have them touch every key, black and white, on a keyboard picture. Watch for "minor 5th" or "major 4th": 4ths, 5ths and octaves are perfect, never major or minor (except when augmented or diminished, which this sheet does not use).
notes: Half steps: m2 1, M2 2, m3 3, M3 4, P4 5, P5 7, m6 8, M6 9, m7 10, M7 11, P8 12 (6 half steps is the tritone, not on this sheet). All notes on the staffs are naturals; read each pair left to right, both notes going up. The keyboard: every key to the very next key, black or white, is one half step; E to F and B to C have no black key between them.

  - major 7th | 11 | M7
  - perfect octave | 12 | P8
example: D up to F#: D, E, F is 3 letters, so a 3rd. D to F# is 4 half steps (D, D#, E, F, F#), so it is a major 3rd (M3).

## Stretch: Explain
write: Intervals 1 and 2 are both 3rds, but one is major and one is minor. Explain why, using the keyboard.
  lines: 2
  key: C to E covers 4 half steps (C, C#, D, D#, E), a major 3rd. D to F covers only 3 (D, D#, E, F), because E to F is a half step with no black key between, so it is a minor 3rd.

# 7. Harmonize Simple Gifts
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; a keyboard, guitar, ukulele or barred instruments for the chords D, G and A
ican: I can choose I, IV or V for each measure of a melody by matching melody notes to chord tones.
directions: List the notes of the I, IV and V chords in D major. For each measure of Simple Gifts, circle the melody notes on the strong beats, find the chord that contains them, and write it in. Then play the melody with your chords.
pairs: chord-explorer
mistakes: Students often try to match every note in a measure, including quick passing notes, and decide that nothing fits. Check the notes on the strong beats (beats 1 and 3) and the longest notes first. Watch for measure 3, where E fits only the V chord, and for measure 7, which needs two chords. In D major, IV is G (G B D), not G minor.
notes: Simple Gifts is a Shaker song from 1848, traditionally credited to Joseph Brackett; it is in the public domain. Melody here: the first 8 measures as commonly taught in D major (4/4, starting with two pickup notes A A), adapted from a public domain violin method. Answers: 1 I, 2 I, 3 V, 4 V, 5 I, 6 I, 7 V then I, 8 I. In measures 1 and 5, E and G are passing notes between chord tones; in measure 2, G and E pass between A, F♯ and D. A V7 chord (A C♯ E G) also works in measures 3, 4 and 7. Students may notice that IV is not needed in this half of the song: that is a fine discovery, and the Stretch shows where IV can fit.

## Warm up: Chord tones in D major
table: Chord | Root | Chord tones
  - I | D | {D F♯ A}
  - IV | G | {G B D}
  - V | A | {A C♯ E}
fill:
  - The note D is in two of these chords: {I} and {IV}.
  - The note E is only in the {V} chord.

## Main: Choose the chords
text: Melody (letter names; F♯ and C♯ are sharp in D major). The song starts with two pickup notes, A A, before measure 1.
table: Measure | Melody notes | Chord tones on strong beats | Chord
  - 1 | D D E F♯ D F♯ G | {D, F♯} | {I}
  - 2 | A A G F♯ E D | {A, F♯, D} | {I}
  - 3 | E E E D | {E} | {V}
  - 4 | E F♯ E C♯ A (A A pickup) | {E, A} | {V}
  - 5 | D D E F♯ D F♯ G | {D, F♯} | {I}
  - 6 | A A G F♯ E D | {A, F♯, D} | {I}
  - 7, beats 1-2 | E E | {E} | {V}
  - 7, beats 3-4 | F♯ F♯ E | {F♯} | {I}
  - 8 | D D D | {D} | {I}
choice: Why does measure 3 need the V chord?
  - Its notes are all D.
  * Its main note, E, is in V (A C♯ E) but not in I or IV.
  - V is always the third chord of a song.
  - E is in all three chords.
choice: Which chord ends the song sounding finished?
  * I
  - IV
  - V
  - none of them

## Main: Play it
write: Play the melody while a partner plays your chords (or play both). Which measure sounded wrong at first, and what did you change?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For example: "Measure 7 sounded wrong with only V, because the F♯ clashed on beat 3. Switching to I on beats 3 and 4 fixed it."

## Stretch: Where IV fits
write: A measure in the second half of the song holds the note D for 3 beats. Which two chords could go there? How would you choose?
  lines: 2
  key: I (D F♯ A) or IV (G B D), because both contain D. Play both and choose by ear, or pick IV for variety after a lot of I. Many arrangements use IV there.

# 20. Dynamic Range Lab
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; your ensemble and instruments; optional sound level meter or meter app on the teacher's device
ican: I can test my ensemble's dynamic range and find where control slips.
directions: Play one long tone at six dynamic levels, from pp to ff. Listeners rate each level for control, record the data and graph it. Then find where control slips and plan how to fix it.
mistakes: Students often rate "loud" as "good." Control means the level is clearly different from its neighbors and the tone stays steady and in tune. Watch for pp rated well when players are actually just stopping the sound: a pp that breaks up or drops in pitch is out of control. Keep the listener group the same for every level so ratings are fair.
notes: Procedure: the ensemble plays a comfortable long tone (for example concert F for band, an "oo" on a middle note for choir) for 8 counts at each level, pp, p, mp, mf, f, ff, with a short rest between. Three to five listeners (or the class from a recording) rate each level 0 to 4: 4 clearly different from the level before, steady and in tune; 3 small wobble; 2 hard to tell from the level before; 1 tone breaks up, goes out of tune or cracks; 0 the sound stops or is painful. Most groups lose control at the extremes: pp tones that crack or go flat, ff tones that turn harsh or sharp. The sample data in the key is one possible result.

## Warm up: Dynamics in order
fill:
  - From softest to loudest: pp, {p}, {mp}, mf, {f}, ff.
  - "pp" stands for {pianissimo}, which means very soft.
choice: What does "control" mean at a dynamic level?
  - the loudest sound possible
  * a level clearly different from its neighbors, with steady, in-tune tone
  - everyone playing the same rhythm
choice: What usually happens to a wind player's pitch at a very soft level with too little air?
  * it goes flat
  - it goes sharp
  - nothing changes

## Main: Question and prediction
text: **Question:** How many dynamic levels can our ensemble play with control, from pp to ff?
write: **Prediction:** At which level do you think control will slip first? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For example: "At pp, because people stop supporting the air and the sound cracks."

## Main: Data
chart: Rating 0-4 | What we heard (tone, pitch, difference from the level before)
  labels: 1 pp; 2 p; 3 mp; 4 mf; 5 f; 6 ff
  height: 0.4
  key: Answers vary. Sample: pp 1, cracks and goes flat; p 3, steady but close to pp; mp 4, clear; mf 4, clear and full; f 3, a little harsh; ff 1, harsh and sharp, no different from f.
text: Graph your ratings: plot a point for each level and connect the points.
graph: Level (1 pp to 6 ff) | 1 6 1 | Control rating | 0 4 1
  minor: off
  key: 1,1 2,3 3,4 4,4 5,3 6,1

## Main: Conclusion
write: How many levels did your ensemble play with control (rating 3 or 4)? Where did control slip?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. With the sample data: 4 levels (p, mp, mf, f) were controlled; control slipped at both ends, pp cracked and ff got harsh and sharp.
write: Choose one level where control slipped. Plan one practice step to fix it.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For pp: keep air support steady and play with smaller, faster air, practicing a long tone that starts at p and fades to pp. For ff: play big but not harsh by opening the sound, and stop at the loudest level that still has a good tone.

## Stretch: Real music
write: Find a pp or ff marking in a piece your ensemble is learning. Using your data, what will you need to remember at that spot?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For example: "At the pp ending of our march, we lose pitch, so we need extra air support and listening to the tuning note."

# 21. Seating and Roles Chart
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler, two pieces your ensemble is rehearsing
ican: I can show where each section sits and which role it plays in a piece.
directions: Review the jobs a part can do in an ensemble. Then draw your ensemble's seating chart and fill in who plays melody, harmony and bass in two pieces you are rehearsing.
mistakes: Students often decide a section's role once for the whole year ("trumpets play melody"). Roles change from piece to piece and even within a piece, so ask for the measures. Watch for "harmony" used for anything that is not the melody: a bass line and a rhythm part are different jobs.
notes: Seating varies by director and ensemble; there is no single right chart. In one common orchestra setup, first violins sit to the conductor's left, with cellos and basses to the right; in concert bands, flutes and clarinets usually sit in front and low brass and percussion in back. Students with no ensemble can use a piece the class has listened to and a standard orchestra or band layout. Roles: melody (the main tune), harmony (notes that support the melody, often chords or a countermelody), bass (the lowest line, the foundation of the harmony), rhythm (a steady pattern that drives the beat).

## Warm up: Jobs in an ensemble
match: Match each role to its job.
  - Melody = the main tune you would hum afterward
  - Harmony = notes that support the tune, often chords
  - Bass = the lowest line, the foundation of the chords
  - Countermelody = a second tune that weaves around the main one
  - Rhythm part = a steady pattern that drives the beat
choice: In a concert band, which section usually plays the bass line?
  - flutes
  - trumpets
  * tubas
choice: In a common orchestra seating, where do the first violins sit?
  * on the conductor's left
  - in the back row
  - behind the percussion

## Main: Our seating chart
draw: Draw your ensemble's seating chart. Put the conductor at the bottom. Label every section. | 3.4
chart: Piece | Melody (section, measures) | Harmony (section, measures) | Bass (section, measures)
  rows: 2
  key: Answers vary by piece. Check that each role names a section and measures, that the bass role goes to a low section (tubas, basses, bass clarinets, cellos, baritone voices), and that a role can move: for example, the melody passes from clarinets in measures 1 to 16 to trumpets in measures 17 to 32.

## Stretch: Why sit there?
write: Pick one section in your chart. Explain why it sits where it does, thinking about what it needs to hear and how its sound reaches the audience.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer links position to listening or sound, for example: "The tubas sit in the back center so the whole band can hear the bass line it tunes to, and their sound carries forward without covering the flutes."

# 33. Bach Prelude in C: Harpsichord or Piano?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; two recordings of Bach's Prelude in C major, BWV 846, one on harpsichord and one on piano
ican: I can compare two performances of the same piece on different instruments and support a preference.
directions: Read about the piece and the two instruments. Listen to each performance two times and fill the chart. Then state which you prefer and support it with evidence.
mistakes: Students often say the harpsichord "can't play loud or soft" and stop there. It cannot change volume by touch, but players create contrast in other ways: timing, adding notes, or switching to a different set of strings. Watch for preferences with no evidence ("I like piano better"); every claim needs a detail from the chart.
notes: Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750) finished Book 1 of The Well-Tempered Clavier in 1722: a prelude and fugue in each of the 24 major and minor keys. "Clavier" means keyboard instrument; in Bach's time that meant the harpsichord, clavichord or organ. The piano was invented by Bartolomeo Cristofori in Italy around 1700, but it was not common until after Bach. The Prelude in C major is a chain of broken chords, each pattern played twice. A harpsichord plucks its strings with small plectra (quills), so the sound is bright, starts crisply and fades fast, with little change in volume from touch. A piano strikes its strings with felt hammers, so the player controls loudness with touch and can hold notes with the sustain pedal. Play about 1 minute of each.

## Warm up: The facts
fill:
  - A harpsichord {plucks} its strings; a piano {hits} its strings with felt hammers.
  - Bach finished Book 1 of The Well-Tempered Clavier in {1722}.
  - The piano was invented around the year {1700} by Bartolomeo Cristofori.
choice: Why can a pianist make each note louder or softer by touch, but a harpsichordist cannot?
  - The piano has more keys.
  * Pressing a piano key harder makes the hammer hit the string harder; a harpsichord's pluck is about the same however you press.
  - Harpsichords have no strings.

## Main: Compare the performances
chart: Harpsichord | Piano
  labels: Tone (timbre); Dynamics (changes in loud and soft?); Tempo (fast, medium, slow; steady or flexible?); How the notes connect (crisp, blended, ringing?)
  height: 0.6
  key: Answers vary. Example: Tone: harpsichord bright and metallic; piano warm and round. Dynamics: harpsichord nearly the same level throughout; piano swells and fades with the chords. Tempo: harpsichord quicker and steady; piano slower and flexible at the end. Connection: harpsichord crisp, each note clear and fading fast; piano notes ring together into a cloud of sound with the pedal.

## Main: State a preference
write: Which performance do you prefer? Support your claim with two pieces of evidence from your chart.
  lines: 4
  starter: I prefer the ___ performance because ...
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer makes a claim and cites two chart details, for example: "I prefer the harpsichord because the crisp, separate notes let me hear every note of the broken chords, and its steady tempo makes the pattern feel like clockwork."
write: Bach wrote for keyboard instruments of his time, not the modern piano. Should that decide which performance is "right"? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A thoughtful answer weighs both sides: the harpsichord shows the sound Bach knew, but the music works on piano too, and performers today choose instruments that bring out different things in the piece.

## Stretch: Your own performance
write: If you played this prelude on a piano, what is one thing you would borrow from the harpsichord performance, and why?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For example: "I would use less sustain pedal so each note of the broken chords stays clear, like on the harpsichord."
