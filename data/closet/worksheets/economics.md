# 1. What Did It Really Cost?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can identify the opportunity cost of a choice: the value of the next best alternative given up.
directions: Read the worked example. For each choice, the options are listed from the chooser's favorite to least favorite. Name the opportunity cost: the next best alternative they gave up, not every option and not the money spent.
mistakes: The two classic errors: listing every option not chosen (opportunity cost is only the single next best alternative), and naming the price paid instead of what else the money or time could have done. Remind students that time has an opportunity cost too, and that a "free" choice is never free if it uses time.
notes: Rankings are given so each item has one answer. Item 8 asks for a number: the gap year's cost in money terms is the $28,000 in wages Maya would have earned from her next best option, the full-time job. Discuss after: in real life people rarely rank options this cleanly, which is why opportunity costs are often hidden.

## Warm up: Worked example
example: Jordan has a free Saturday. Ranked choices: (1) work a shift for $90, (2) go to a concert with friends, (3) study for a test.
  Jordan chooses to work. Opportunity cost: **the concert with friends**, the next best alternative. Studying is not part of the cost, because Jordan would not have chosen it anyway.

## Main: Find the opportunity cost
fill:
  - Aisha has $40. Ranked: (1) new running shoes, (2) a concert ticket, (3) a video game. She buys the shoes. Opportunity cost: {the concert ticket}
  - A farmer can plant one field. Ranked: (1) corn, (2) soybeans, (3) wheat. He plants corn. Opportunity cost: {the soybean crop}
  - Leo has one evening. Ranked: (1) study for a chemistry test, (2) babysit for $50, (3) watch a movie. He studies. Opportunity cost: {the $50 from babysitting}
  - A school has $20,000. Ranked: (1) new laptops, (2) a field trip for every grade, (3) new library books. It buys laptops. Opportunity cost: {the field trips}
  - A city has $500 million. Ranked: (1) a new stadium, (2) a light rail line, (3) school repairs. It builds the stadium. Opportunity cost: {the light rail line}
  - A country can put its workers into (1) cars or (2) computers. It makes cars. Opportunity cost: {the computers it could have made}
  - Sam can (1) take a free online course or (2) work 10 hours at $15 an hour. He takes the free course. Opportunity cost in dollars: {150}
  - Maya, after high school, ranks: (1) a gap year volunteering (unpaid), (2) a full-time job paying $28,000, (3) starting college at once. She takes the gap year. Opportunity cost in dollars: {28,000}

## Apply: Hidden costs
choice: A concert ticket is "free" from a contest, but you must skip a $60 shift at work to go. What is the opportunity cost of using the free ticket?
  - $0, because the ticket was free
  * The $60 you could have earned, or whatever else you would have done with that time if it is worth more
  - The face value of the ticket
  - Nothing, because you won the contest
write: The city in item 5 says the stadium "will pay for itself." Even if that is true, why should voters still think about opportunity cost?
  lines: 4
  key: Because the $500 million and the land could have built the light rail line, the next best use, and its benefits (faster commutes, cleaner air, access to jobs) are given up. A project can earn back its money and still be a worse choice than the alternative; the real question is which use creates more value for the city.

## Stretch: Sunk costs
write: Priya paid $200 for a nonrefundable camp, then got a summer job offer paying $1,200 for the same weeks. Her friend says, "You already paid $200, so you have to go to camp." Use opportunity cost and the idea of sunk cost to advise her.
  lines: 5
  key: The $200 is a sunk cost: it is gone whichever she chooses, so it should not decide anything. The real comparison is camp versus the job: going to camp now costs her the $1,200 she could earn (plus the job experience). If she values camp more than that, she should go; if not, she should take the job.

# 5. Economic Freedom Data
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator
ican: I can read economic data on a scatter plot and explain why a correlation does not prove that one thing causes another.
directions: Read the table of economic freedom scores and income per person for 10 countries. Plot each country on the graph, describe the pattern, and then decide what the data can and cannot prove.
mistakes: The big error is reading a correlation as proof: "more freedom makes countries rich." The data fit that story, but they also fit reverse causation (rich countries can afford strong courts and stable rules) and third factors (rule of law, education, history). Watch for students who plot GDP on the x-axis, and for students who skip Chile and Japan, the two points that weaken the pattern.
notes: The figures are rounded approximations for classroom use: freedom scores from the Heritage Foundation's 2024 Index of Economic Freedom (0 to 100) and GDP per person for 2023 in current US dollars, as reported by the World Bank. Both change every year, and the two major indexes (Heritage and the Fraser Institute's Economic Freedom of the World) use different methods, so check the current editions before quoting a number. Ireland's GDP per person is inflated by the profits of multinational companies booked there; Ireland's own statistics office publishes an adjusted measure (modified GNI) for that reason. Key math: top five scores (Switzerland, Ireland, Singapore, Germany, Chile) average (100 + 104 + 85 + 53 + 17) / 5 = 71.8 thousand dollars; bottom five (United States, Japan, Mexico, India, China) average (82 + 34 + 14 + 2.5 + 13) / 5 = 29.1 thousand dollars.

  - The index was made up by chance.
  - Freedom scores are measured in dollars.
  * Rich countries can afford well-run courts, low inflation and other things the index rewards.
write: Use Chile and Japan to explain why the columnist's claim that "any country that raises its score will grow" goes beyond what this data can show.
  lines: 4
  key: Chile's score (71) is about the same as Germany's (72) and the United States' (70), yet its income is far lower; Japan has a similar score and less than half the US income. A similar score does not bring a similar income, so third factors (rule of law, history, education, technology, natural resources) matter a lot. A snapshot of 10 countries can show that scores and income tend to go together, not that raising a score causes growth.

# 16. The Prisoner's Dilemma of Pricing
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a payoff matrix to find each firm's best strategy and explain why cartels tend to break down.
directions: Two airlines fly the same route. Each chooses a high or a low fare without knowing what the other will choose. Each box shows yearly profit in millions of dollars: Airline A's profit first, then Airline B's. For each matrix, find each airline's best move and the outcome.
mistakes: Students compare the wrong pair of boxes: to find A's best response, hold B's choice fixed (one column) and compare A's two profits in that column. Another common slip is reading B's number as A's. A prisoner's dilemma needs both: each player has a dominant strategy, and the outcome they reach is worse for both than another outcome they could have chosen together.
notes: Key: Matrix 1, low is dominant for both, outcome low and low (30, 30), a dilemma because high and high (50, 50) is better for both. Matrix 2, high is dominant (60 beats 55 and 35 beats 25), outcome high and high, no dilemma: there is no gain from undercutting. Matrix 3, low is dominant (90 beats 80, 40 beats 10), outcome (40, 40) versus (80, 80) together, a dilemma with a bigger cost. Matrix 4, A: 120 beats 100 and 80 beats 70, so low; B: 50 beats 40 and 30 beats 20, so low; outcome (80, 30), total 110, versus high and high (100, 40), total 140: a dilemma even though the airlines are different sizes. In the United States, agreements between competitors to fix prices are illegal under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. The numbers are made up.

## Warm up: Read a matrix
example: Matrix 1: A charges high and B charges low. Find that box: A earns $20 million, B earns $70 million. B steals customers by undercutting.
table: Airline A's choice | Airline B charges high | Airline B charges low
  - A charges high | A 50, B 50 | A 20, B 70
  - A charges low | A 70, B 20 | A 30, B 30
fill:
  - If B charges high, A earns more by charging {low} (70 instead of 50).
  - If B charges low, A earns more by charging {low} (30 instead of 20).
  - So A's dominant strategy is to charge {low}. The matrix is the same for B, so B charges {low} too.
  - Outcome: each airline earns {30} million. Together they could have earned {50} million each by both charging high.

## Main: Solve three more
text: **Matrix 2**
table: Airline A's choice | Airline B charges high | Airline B charges low
  - A charges high | A 60, B 60 | A 35, B 55
  - A charges low | A 55, B 35 | A 25, B 25
fill:
  - A's dominant strategy: charge {high}. Outcome: A {60}, B {60}. Is this a prisoner's dilemma? {no}
text: **Matrix 3**
table: Airline A's choice | Airline B charges high | Airline B charges low
  - A charges high | A 80, B 80 | A 10, B 90
  - A charges low | A 90, B 10 | A 40, B 40
fill:
  - A's dominant strategy: charge {low}. Outcome: A {40}, B {40}. Is this a prisoner's dilemma? {yes}
text: **Matrix 4:** a big airline (A) and a small one (B). Now the two players are different, so check each one.
table: Airline A's choice | Airline B charges high | Airline B charges low
  - A charges high | A 100, B 40 | A 70, B 50
  - A charges low | A 120, B 20 | A 80, B 30
fill:
  - A's dominant strategy: charge {low}. B's dominant strategy: charge {low}.
  - Outcome: A {80}, B {30}. Total profit {110}, compared with {140} if both charged high.
choice: Which matrix is **not** a prisoner's dilemma, and why?
  - Matrix 1, because the payoffs are the same for both airlines
  - Matrix 3, because the losses are too large
  * Matrix 2, because charging high is best for each airline no matter what the other does, so there is nothing to gain by cheating
  - Matrix 4, because the airlines are different sizes

## Apply: Why cartels break down
text: Suppose the two airlines in Matrix 3 secretly agree to both charge high. (This is illegal in the United States and many other countries.)
write: Use the matrix to explain why each airline is tempted to break the agreement.
  lines: 3
  key: If B keeps its promise and charges high, A can earn 90 instead of 80 by secretly cutting its fare. B faces the same temptation. Each gains by cheating whatever the other does, so the agreement is unstable.
write: Name two things that make a cartel more likely to break down in the real world.
  lines: 3
  key: Any two: many members (harder to watch and agree); cheating is hard to detect (secret discounts); members have different costs or sizes and want different prices; demand falls and each member wants more sales; new firms can enter; price fixing is illegal, so the agreement cannot be enforced in court and a member may report it for leniency.

## Stretch: Playing again and again
write: Real airlines set fares every week, not just once. How could repeating the game change the outcome? Use the idea of retaliation.
  lines: 4
  key: In a repeated game, cheating today can be punished tomorrow: if A undercuts, B can cut fares in every later round, so A gains 90 once but then earns 40 instead of 80 for many rounds. The threat of a price war can keep both at high prices without any formal agreement (tacit collusion), especially with few firms and visible prices. It can still collapse if one firm expects the game to end or cannot see the other's prices.

# 22. Tragedy of the Commons
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain why shared resources are overused and evaluate rules that can protect them.
directions: Read the case study of the Newfoundland cod collapse. Explain why it happened using the idea of the tragedy of the commons. Then evaluate three possible fixes, citing Elinor Ostrom's research on how communities manage shared resources.
mistakes: Students often blame individual fishers for greed. The point of the model is that each person acts sensibly for themselves: if I leave a fish, someone else catches it, so restraint helps others but costs me. Also watch for students who think Ostrom proved that government or markets never work; she showed that communities can succeed under certain conditions, and that no single fix fits every resource.
notes: Case facts: northern cod catches peaked at about 810,000 tons in 1968, when large factory trawlers fished the Grand Banks; Canada's fisheries minister John Crosbie declared a moratorium on July 2, 1992, when the stock had fallen to about 1 percent of historic levels; over 35,000 fishers and plant workers lost work; the moratorium was later extended indefinitely and the stock recovered only slowly. Garrett Hardin popularized "the tragedy of the commons" in a 1968 article in Science. Elinor Ostrom, the first woman to win the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (2009), studied commons around the world in Governing the Commons (1990), including the inshore fishery of Alanya, Turkey, where fishers drew lots each year for named fishing spots and rotated through them. The case study passage is original to Curio Class.

## Read: The case
passage: Empty Nets on the Grand Banks
  For almost five hundred years, the cold waters off Newfoundland held so many cod that early explorers reported catching them in baskets lowered over the side. Families in hundreds of small coastal towns lived by fishing them, and no one owned the fish in the sea.
  In the 1950s and 1960s, huge factory trawlers from many countries arrived. They dragged enormous nets along the sea floor and froze their catch on board. In 1968 the catch of northern cod peaked at about 810,000 tons. Then it fell, and kept falling, even as boats searched harder and used better sonar to find the fish that were left.
  Canada pushed foreign fleets out to 200 miles from shore in 1977 and set yearly catch limits, but scientists overestimated how many fish remained and the limits stayed high. Fishing communities depended on the jobs, and each boat that held back simply left more fish for others.
  On July 2, 1992, Canada closed the northern cod fishery. The stock had fallen to about 1 percent of its historic size. Over 35,000 fishers and fish plant workers lost their jobs. Decades later, the cod had still not fully returned.

## Analyze
choice: Why is the cod fishery a "commons"?
  - The government owned every fish and sold them.
  * No one owned the fish until they were caught, and it was hard to keep anyone from fishing.
  - The fish were raised on farms and shared equally.
  - Only one company was allowed to fish.
write: Explain the tragedy of the commons using the cod case. Why did each fisher's sensible choice lead to a bad result for everyone?
  lines: 5
  key: Each boat gained the full value of every fish it caught, while the cost of overfishing (fewer fish next year) was shared by everyone. A fisher who held back just left fish for others, so each kept fishing hard. Added together, these individually sensible choices took fish faster than the stock could reproduce, and the fishery collapsed, hurting everyone.
write: The catch limits Canada set before 1992 did not save the cod. Use the passage to explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Scientists overestimated how many fish remained and the limits stayed high, partly because communities depended on fishing jobs; a quota only works if it is set at a level the stock can sustain and is enforced.

## Evaluate three fixes
table: Fix | How it works | One strength | One weakness
  - Quotas (total catch limits) | A government sets a total yearly catch and closes the fishery when it is reached. | {simple to set and directly limits the catch} | {boats race to catch fish before the limit is hit, and limits can be set too high}
  - Tradable permits (individual quotas) | Each fisher owns a share of the total catch and can buy or sell shares. | {no race; owners gain when the stock grows, so they want it protected} | {shares can end up with a few large companies, pushing out small fishers}
  - Community management | Local fishers make, watch and enforce their own rules. | {users know local conditions and watch each other closely} | {works best in small groups with clear boundaries; hard against outside fleets}
write: Elinor Ostrom found that communities in places like Alanya, Turkey, managed fisheries well for generations. Using her findings, explain which conditions help community management succeed, and whether they existed on the Grand Banks.
  lines: 5
  key: Ostrom found success where boundaries and members are clearly defined, users help make the rules, rules fit local conditions, users monitor each other, penalties start small and grow, and there are cheap ways to settle conflicts. In Alanya a small, known group drew lots for fishing spots and rotated. The Grand Banks lacked these: huge fleets from many countries, no clear membership and no shared rules before 1977, so community management alone could not have worked there.

## Stretch: Your recommendation
write: Recommend a plan for a fishery that is starting to decline today. Combine at least two fixes and explain why your plan addresses the weaknesses you listed.
  lines: 6
  key: Strong answers combine tools: for example, a science-based total catch set below the sustainable level, divided into tradable shares with limits on how much one owner may hold (to protect small fishers), plus local councils that help set rules for their area and monitor catches, with penalties that increase for repeat violations. They connect each piece to a weakness from the table.

# 26. Building a CPI Basket
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can calculate a price index and inflation rate from a market basket and explain why a fixed basket can overstate the cost of living.
directions: A fictional town tracks the cost of the same basket of 5 goods for 3 years. Price the basket each year, turn the costs into a price index with Year 1 as the base, and find the inflation rate. Then think about what a fixed basket misses.
mistakes: The quantities stay the same every year: that is what makes it an index of prices, not of spending. Students often divide by the wrong year for inflation; the change is always measured from the earlier year (Year 2 to Year 3 divides by Year 2's index). Watch for students who subtract index numbers and call it a percent: 113.4 minus 108 is 5.4 points, but inflation is 5.4 / 108 = 5 percent.
notes: Key check: Year 1 total 100 + 600 + 100 + 100 + 100 = 1,000. Year 2: 110 + 660 + 100 + 105 + 105 = 1,080. Year 3: 120 + 690 + 110 + 110 + 104 = 1,134. Index 100, 108.0, 113.4; inflation 8.0 percent then 5.0 percent; 13.4 percent over two years. Gasoline rose from $600 to $690, so $90 of the $134 increase (about 67 percent) came from gas. Real wage: $16 / 1.134 = $14.11 in Year 1 dollars. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the real CPI from a basket of thousands of items and updates its weights regularly, partly to reduce substitution bias.

## Warm up: Price the basket
example: Year 1, bread: 50 loaves x $2.00 = $100. The basket's quantities never change; only prices do.
table: Item (yearly amount) | Year 1 price | Year 1 cost | Year 2 price | Year 2 cost | Year 3 price | Year 3 cost
  - Bread (50 loaves) | $2.00 | $100 | $2.20 | {$110} | $2.40 | {$120}
  - Gasoline (200 gallons) | $3.00 | {$600} | $3.30 | {$660} | $3.45 | {$690}
  - Movie tickets (10) | $10.00 | {$100} | $10.00 | {$100} | $11.00 | {$110}
  - Haircuts (5) | $20.00 | {$100} | $21.00 | {$105} | $22.00 | {$110}
  - Coffee (50 cups) | $2.00 | {$100} | $2.10 | {$105} | $2.08 | {$104}
  - **Total basket cost** | | {$1,000} | | {$1,080} | | {$1,134}

## Main: Index and inflation
example: Price index = (cost of the basket this year / cost in the base year) x 100. Inflation rate = (this year's index - last year's index) / last year's index x 100.
fill:
  - Year 1 index (base year): {100}
  - Year 2 index: {108.0}
  - Year 3 index: {113.4}
  - Inflation from Year 1 to Year 2: {8.0} percent
  - Inflation from Year 2 to Year 3: {5.0} percent
  - Total price increase from Year 1 to Year 3: {13.4} percent
choice: From Year 2 to Year 3, the inflation rate fell from 8 percent to 5 percent. What happened to prices?
  - Prices fell on average.
  - Prices stayed the same.
  * Prices still rose on average, just more slowly.
  - Only gasoline prices changed.
fill:
  - Of the $134 rise in the basket's cost from Year 1 to Year 3, gasoline added {$90}, which is about {67} percent of the increase.
  - A worker earned $16 an hour in Year 3. In Year 1 dollars, that is worth about {$14.11} an hour.

## Apply: Substitution bias
text: In Year 3, bread rose 20 percent but coffee barely changed. Suppose many people in town switched from bread to cheaper tortillas, and drove less as gas rose. The index still prices 50 loaves and 200 gallons.
choice: Because the basket stays fixed, the index probably...
  * overstates how much the cost of living rose, because people switched to cheaper choices
  - understates how much the cost of living rose
  - measures it exactly, because the basket is fixed
  - has no connection to the cost of living
write: Explain substitution bias in your own words, using one item from this basket as an example.
  lines: 3
  key: A fixed basket assumes people keep buying the same amounts even when one item gets much more expensive. In real life they substitute: when bread rose 20 percent, people bought cheaper tortillas or rice instead. Their real cost of living rose less than the index shows, so a fixed-basket index overstates inflation.

## Stretch: Beyond substitution
write: Name one other way a CPI can misjudge the cost of living (not substitution), and explain it.
  lines: 3
  key: Quality change: a $1,000 phone today does far more than one from years ago, so part of a price rise is really a better product. New goods: new products (streaming services, new medicines) enter the basket late. Outlet bias: people shift to discount stores. Different households: a family that drives a lot feels gas prices more than the average basket shows.

# 37. Comparative Advantage
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can calculate opportunity costs and use comparative advantage to decide who should specialize in which good.
directions: Study the worked example. For each pair of countries, find the opportunity cost of each good, then decide which country should specialize in which good. Watch whether the table gives output (how much can be made) or input (hours needed to make one).
mistakes: Absolute advantage (who can make more) is not the same as comparative advantage (who gives up less). A country can have the absolute advantage in both goods and still gain from trade. With input tables (hours per unit), students often flip the fraction: the opportunity cost of one car is the hours for a car divided by the hours for a ton of rice, which tells how much rice those hours could have made.
notes: Key summary: Northland and Southland, cloth 2 versus 3 wheat, so Northland makes cloth and Southland wheat, and a price between 2 and 3 wheat per cloth helps both. Alta and Baja: a car costs Alta 10 / 5 = 2 tons of rice and Baja 30 / 10 = 3, so Alta makes cars and Baja rice. Lumeria and Corvia: a fish costs Lumeria 2 bananas and Corvia 1, so Corvia makes fish and Lumeria bananas, and each also has the absolute advantage in its good. Pellia and Rova have the same opportunity costs (2 hats per pair of shoes), so neither has a comparative advantage and there is no gain from specializing and trading. All countries are fictional. Gains from trade are drawn on PPFs in sheet 38.

## Warm up: Worked example
example: With all its workers, **Northland** can make 120 tons of wheat or 60 bolts of cloth. **Southland** can make 90 tons of wheat or 30 bolts of cloth.
  Northland: 1 cloth costs 120 / 60 = **2 wheat**; 1 wheat costs 60 / 120 = **0.5 cloth**.
  Southland: 1 cloth costs 90 / 30 = **3 wheat**; 1 wheat costs 30 / 90 = **1/3 cloth**.
  Northland gives up less wheat for cloth (2 is less than 3), so it should make **cloth**. Southland gives up less cloth for wheat (1/3 is less than 0.5), so it should make **wheat**.
fill:
  - Which country has the absolute advantage in both goods? {Northland}
  - Does Southland still have a comparative advantage? In which good? {yes, wheat}
  - A trade price that helps both: 1 bolt of cloth for between {2} and {3} tons of wheat.
  - If they trade 1 cloth for 2.5 wheat, Southland saves {0.5} ton of wheat per bolt compared with making cloth itself.

## Main: Output tables
text: **Lumeria and Corvia** (most each could make in a month, using all workers)
table: Country | Bananas (tons) | Fish (tons) | Opportunity cost of 1 fish | Opportunity cost of 1 banana
  - Lumeria | 50 | 25 | {2 bananas} | {0.5 fish}
  - Corvia | 40 | 40 | {1 banana} | {1 fish}
fill:
  - Comparative advantage in fish: {Corvia}. Comparative advantage in bananas: {Lumeria}.
text: **Pellia and Rova** (most each could make in a month)
table: Country | Hats | Shoes (pairs) | Opportunity cost of 1 pair of shoes | Opportunity cost of 1 hat
  - Pellia | 20 | 10 | {2 hats} | {0.5 pair}
  - Rova | 40 | 20 | {2 hats} | {0.5 pair}
choice: What should Pellia and Rova do?
  - Pellia should make hats because it makes fewer.
  - Rova should make both goods and Pellia neither.
  * Neither has a comparative advantage, so specializing and trading gains them nothing.
  - Rova should make shoes because it has the absolute advantage.

## Main: An input table
text: **Alta and Baja** (hours of work needed to make one unit)
table: Country | Hours per car | Hours per ton of rice | Opportunity cost of 1 car | Opportunity cost of 1 ton of rice
  - Alta | 10 | 5 | {2 tons of rice} | {0.5 car}
  - Baja | 30 | 10 | {3 tons of rice} | {1/3 car}
fill:
  - Alta should specialize in {cars}. Baja should specialize in {rice}.
  - Who has the absolute advantage in both goods? {Alta}

## Stretch: Why trade at all?
write: A classmate says, "Alta is better at making everything. Trading with Baja can only hurt it." Use opportunity cost to explain why both countries can gain.
  lines: 4
  key: Being better at everything is absolute advantage; what matters is opportunity cost. Each hour Alta spends on rice could make half a car instead, while Baja gives up only a third of a car for each ton of rice. If Alta makes cars and buys rice from Baja at a price between 1/3 and 1/2 car per ton, Alta gets rice more cheaply than making it itself and Baja gets cars more cheaply too, so total output rises and both gain.
