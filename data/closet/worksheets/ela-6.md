# 1. Plot Diagram: Rikki-Tikki-Tavi
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, a copy of the full story
ican: I can map how a story's plot unfolds and cite a line that shows each stage.
directions: After reading "Rikki-Tikki-Tavi" by Rudyard Kipling, match the plot terms. Then read the quotation bank, decide which stage each line belongs to, and build a plot diagram with an event and a cited line for every stage.
mistakes: Students often call the first exciting event the climax (meeting Nag in the garden). The climax is the turning point where the main conflict is decided: Rikki follows Nagaina into her hole, and the snakes' threat ends. Some readers argue for the fight with Nag in the bathroom; accept it only if the student explains why it decides the conflict. Watch for resolution written as more action: the resolution is the new normal, Rikki guarding the garden.
notes: Students need the whole story (from The Jungle Book, 1894; free at Project Gutenberg, eBook #236). The quotations were checked word for word against that edition, with curly quotation marks made straight. The story is set in a British family's bungalow in colonial India; a short note on that setting helps students with words like bungalow, veranda and cantonment.

## Warm up: Plot words
match: Match each stage of a plot to what happens in it.
  - Exposition = We meet the characters and the setting.
  - Rising action = The conflict builds through a series of events.
  - Climax = The turning point, where the conflict is decided.
  - Falling action = Events that follow the turning point and wrap things up.
  - Resolution = How things end up; the new normal.

## Quotation bank
source: Rudyard Kipling, "Rikki-Tikki-Tavi," from The Jungle Book (1894)
  [1] "One day, a high summer flood washed him out of the burrow where he lived with his father and mother, and carried him, kicking and clucking, down a roadside ditch."
  [2] "It is the hardest thing in the world to frighten a mongoose, because he is eaten up from nose to tail with curiosity."
  [3] "Then inch by inch out of the grass rose up the head and spread hood of Nag, the big black cobra, and he was five feet long from tongue to tail."
  [4] "I will kill the big man and his wife, and the child if I can, and come away quietly."
  [5] "Rikki-tikki put his paws one on each side of the egg, and his eyes were blood-red."
  [6] "his little white teeth were clenched on her tail, and he went down with her"
  [7] "Rikki-tikki, covered with dirt, dragged himself out of the hole leg by leg, licking his whiskers."
  [8] "he kept that garden as a mongoose should keep it, with tooth and jump and spring and bite, till never a cobra dared show its head inside the walls."

write: Exposition: who, where, and how the story starts. Cite a line.
  lines: 2
  key: A flood washes young Rikki-tikki from his burrow; an English family in India takes him in and he decides to stay. Line: quotation 1 (or 2, which shows his curious character).

  lines: 3
  key: Rikki meets Nag and Nagaina in the garden; he kills Karait; he overhears the plan to kill the family and fights Nag in the bathroom; he destroys the eggs and corners Nagaina on the veranda. Line: quotation 3, 4 or 5.

  lines: 2
  key: Rikki chases Nagaina and follows her down into her hole; the fight underground decides whether the cobras will rule the garden. Line: quotation 6.

  lines: 2
  key: Rikki comes out of the hole alive, Darzee stops his death song, and the Coppersmith tells the garden that Nagaina is dead. Line: quotation 7.

  lines: 2
  key: The family is safe and grateful, and Rikki guards the garden so well that no cobra comes back. Line: quotation 8.

## Stretch
write: How does Rikki change from the exposition to the resolution? Use two quotation numbers as evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: At first he is a young, curious, half-drowned mongoose (quotations 1 and 2) who has never fought a live cobra. By the end he has proved his courage and become the confident protector of the whole garden (quotation 8).
# 6. Point of View Switch
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can retell a scene from a different character's point of view and explain how the new narrator changes what readers learn.
directions: Read the scene, told by Leo. Then plan and rewrite it in first person as his sister Ana. Ana sees and knows things Leo does not. Finish by explaining what new information your version reveals.
mistakes: Many students switch names but keep Leo's thoughts ("I felt my stomach drop" in Ana's voice). Ana cannot know what Leo feels inside; she can only see his face and guess. Others forget to use Ana's clues: her smile, sitting on the cushion and "Same as last time" must make sense in her version.
notes: The scene is original to Curio Class (about 240 words). The intended secret, which Leo cannot know: Ana broke the vase herself the week before and glued it back together, so it broke along her glue lines. Accept any explanation that fits all three clues. This is a good moment to name limited first person: the narrator only knows their own mind.

## Read
passage: The Blue Vase
  I knew the rule: no ball games in the living room. But it was raining, and my new foam ball was too bouncy to resist. I tossed it at the ceiling, caught it and tossed it again. On the fourth throw, it bounced off the lamp and hit Grandma's blue vase on the shelf.
  The vase wobbled. I lunged, but I was too slow. It hit the floor and split into three neat pieces.
  My stomach dropped. Grandma had carried that vase all the way from her old house. I scooped up the pieces and pushed them under a couch cushion. My heart was pounding so loudly I was sure the whole house could hear it.
  Then my sister Ana walked in. She looked at the empty shelf. She looked at me. She looked at the couch cushion, which was not quite flat.
  "Where's the vase?" she asked.
  "What vase?" I said. My voice came out squeaky.
  Ana did not yell or run to tell Mom. Instead, she smiled a strange little smile, picked up her book and sat down right on top of the cushion.
  "Huh," she said, turning a page. "Three pieces. Same as last time."
  I had no idea what she meant.

## Warm up
choice: Who narrates the scene, and what kind of point of view is it?
  - Ana, in first person
  * Leo, in first person
  - A narrator outside the story, in third person
  - Grandma, in third person
choice: Which detail best suggests that Ana knows something Leo does not?
  - "She looked at the empty shelf."
  - "My voice came out squeaky."
  * "Three pieces. Same as last time."
  - "It was raining."

## Plan: Inside Ana's head
chart: What Ana sees and hears | What Ana knows that Leo does not | How Ana feels, and why she smiles
  rows: 1
  height: 1.3
  key: Sees: the empty shelf, Leo's red face, the lumpy cushion, his squeaky "What vase?" Knows: she broke the vase last week and glued it back together, so it broke along the glue lines into the same three pieces. Feels: relieved and amused; she smiles because now nobody will ever find out about her accident, and she may help Leo glue it again.

## Write: Ana's version
write: Rewrite the scene in first person as Ana. Keep the same events, but show only what Ana can see, hear, think and know.
  starter: I came into the living room to get my book, and ...
  lines: 11
  key: Model: I came into the living room to get my book, and the shelf was empty. Leo stood by the couch, red as a tomato, and one cushion was sitting a little too high. I knew that lump. Last Tuesday I had knocked Grandma's vase off the shelf while dusting, and I had spent an hour gluing its three pieces back together. Nobody ever noticed. "Where's the vase?" I asked. "What vase?" Leo squeaked. I almost laughed. If it broke now, it broke on my glue lines, and no one would ever know about my accident. I sat right on the cushion and opened my book. "Three pieces. Same as last time," I said. Leo had no idea what I meant, and I wasn't going to tell him. Not yet.
write: What new information appears in Ana's version that Leo's version could not include? Why could Leo not know it?
  lines: 3
  key: Ana's version reveals that she broke and glued the vase before, which explains her smile and "Same as last time." Leo could not know because the story was told only through his own eyes and thoughts.

## Stretch
write: How does switching the narrator change how the reader feels about Leo?
  lines: 3
  key: In Leo's version we share his panic and feel sorry for him. In Ana's version he looks a little funny and the reader is in on a secret he does not know, so the scene becomes comic instead of tense.
# 18. Who and Whom: Pronoun Case
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose pronouns in the right case and fix pronouns whose meaning is unclear.
directions: For each sentence, write the correct pronoun and name its case: subjective (does the action), objective (receives the action or follows a preposition), possessive (shows ownership) or intensive (a -self word that adds emphasis). Then rewrite four sentences so each pronoun clearly points to one noun.
mistakes: The classic trap is a compound: "Coach gave it to Jamal and I." Cover up "Jamal and" and the right choice is clear: "gave it to me." For who and whom, try answering the question with he or him: "Him should I give it to?" sounds wrong, but "Give it to him" works, so whom. Watch for "it's" (it is) used as a possessive, and for "theirselves," which is not standard English.
notes: Cases in the key: subjective 1, 3, 7; objective 2, 4, 8, 11; possessive 5, 9, 12; intensive 6, 10. In sentence 6 "themselves" is intensive because the sentence still makes sense without it. For the vague pronoun fixes, accept any version where each pronoun has one clear antecedent, or where the pronoun is replaced with a noun.

## Warm up
example: Maya and (I / me) built the model. Cover "Maya and": **I** built the model. Answer: **I**, subjective.

## Main: Choose the pronoun and name its case
table: Sentence | Pronoun | Case
  - Maya and (I / me) built the model. | {I} | {subjective}
  - Coach gave the trophy to Jamal and (I / me). | {me} | {objective}
  - (Who / Whom) left this jacket on the bus? | {Who} | {subjective}
  - To (who / whom) should I give the permission slip? | {whom} | {objective}
  - The dog wagged (its / it's) tail. | {its} | {possessive}
  - The students painted the mural (theirselves / themselves). | {themselves} | {intensive}
  - (We / Us) sixth graders planned the food drive. | {We} | {subjective}
  - The principal thanked (we / us) volunteers. | {us} | {objective}
  - Is this notebook (yours / your's)? | {yours} | {possessive}
  - I (myself / me) checked every answer twice. | {myself} | {intensive}
  - Between you and (I / me), the quiz was easy. | {me} | {objective}
  - (Whose / Who's) bike is chained to the fence? | {Whose} | {possessive}

## Fix the vague pronouns
write: When Tara talked to Jen, she was upset.
  lines: 1
  key: Tara was upset when she talked to Jen. (or: Jen was upset when Tara talked to her.)
write: Take the books off the shelves and dust them.
  lines: 1
  key: Take the books off the shelves and dust the shelves.
write: They say the new skate park will open in May.
  lines: 1
  key: The city council says the new skate park will open in May.
write: Marcus told Leo that his bike had a flat tire.
  lines: 1
  key: Marcus told Leo, "Your bike has a flat tire." (or: Marcus told Leo that Leo's bike had a flat tire.)

## Stretch
write: Write one question using "who" and one using "whom." Show how you checked each with he or him.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Who wrote this poem? (He wrote this poem, so who.) Whom did you invite? (You invited him, so whom.)
# 25. Comparing Theme Across Genres
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter if you have one
ican: I can compare how a poem and a story develop the same theme, citing evidence from each.
directions: Read the poem and the story. Both share a theme about perseverance. Fill in the Venn diagram to show how each one develops the theme, then answer the questions with evidence from both texts.
mistakes: Students often stop at the topic ("both are about not giving up"). A theme is a full statement: "Steady effort, even after failure, can break through obstacles." Watch for comparisons that only list plot events; ask how each genre shows the theme: the poem through an image, rhyme and a direct message, the story through a character's actions, dialogue and change.
notes: The poem (12 lines) and the story (about 260 words) are original to Curio Class. Highlighting lines that show the theme in two colors, one per text, makes the Venn diagram faster.

## Read: The poem
source: "Stone and Seed," an original poem written for Curio Class
  The seed pushed up against the stone
  and found the stone would not be moved.
  It did not quit. It did not groan.
  It bent, and turned, and slowly proved
  that roots can find a crack of light
  where none was offered from the start.
  It grew a little every night,
  a green and stubborn little heart.
  Now look: the stone is split in two,
  and in the crack a flower stands.
  Whatever wall is blocking you,
  keep reaching, slow, with patient hands.

## Read: The story
passage: The Eleventh Try
  Rosa's robot arm swung, opened its claw, and dropped the paper cup full of paper clips. Again. It was the tenth time that afternoon.
  "The contest is Friday," said her brother Mateo from the doorway. "Maybe build something easier?"
  Rosa didn't answer. She picked up the cup and set it back on the table. Her arm was made of cardboard, straws and string, and when she pulled the string, the claw was supposed to close. Instead, it flopped open like a tired hand.
  That night she drew the arm in her notebook, every piece of it. She noticed that the string ran straight down from the claw, so when she pulled, the whole arm bent instead of the fingers. She frowned, erased, and drew the string through a straw on the inside of each finger.
  The next day she rebuilt the claw. It closed halfway, then slipped. She added rubber bands to the fingertips for grip. It closed, lifted the cup an inch, and dropped it.
  "Ten tries," Mateo said. "Want me to call it?"
  "Eleven," Rosa said. "I'm not done learning from it."
  She tightened the string, pulled slowly, and watched. The claw closed. The cup rose off the table, wobbled, and stayed. Rosa carried it across the room and set it down without spilling a single paper clip inside it.
  On Friday, the judges asked her to explain her design. Rosa opened her notebook to the page covered in erased lines. "This is the most important part," she said. "It's all the ways it didn't work."

## Warm up: The shared theme
choice: Which statement best expresses the theme the two texts share?
  - Robots and plants are both useful.
  - It is important to win contests.
  * Steady effort, even after many failures, can break through an obstacle.
  - Family members should help each other.

## Main: Compare
venn: The poem | The story
  both: The theme in both
  key: Poem only: the theme is shown through an image (a seed splitting a stone), rhyme and short lines, and a direct message to the reader in the last two lines. Story only: shown through a character (Rosa), her repeated tries, her thinking (redrawing the design), dialogue with Mateo, and her change. Both: an obstacle that will not move at first, small changes and patience, slow progress, success that grows out of struggle.
write: Cite a line from the poem that shows the theme, and explain how it shows it.
  lines: 3
  key: "It did not quit. It did not groan." The seed keeps pushing without complaint, which shows steady effort. Also accept "It grew a little every night" (slow progress) or "keep reaching, slow, with patient hands" (the message stated directly).
write: Cite a line from the story that shows the theme, and explain how it shows it.
  lines: 3
  key: "Eleven," Rosa said. "I'm not done learning from it." She refuses to quit after ten failures and treats each one as a lesson. Also accept the last line: "It's all the ways it didn't work."
write: The poem tells the reader the lesson directly in its last two lines. Does the story ever state the lesson directly? How does the reader learn it instead?
  lines: 3
  key: The story never states it outright; readers infer the theme from Rosa's actions (rebuilding, adding rubber bands), her dialogue with Mateo, and her pride in the notebook of failures at the end.
# 31. Information from Media
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain how information in a graphic and in a spoken summary each add to my understanding of a topic.
directions: Study the infographic and answer the data questions. Then listen as your teacher reads a short spoken summary about the same topic, and take notes. Finish the chart and explain what each format adds.
mistakes: Students may read each bar as one gallon; check the legend (each block is 5 gallons). In the comparison, watch for "the infographic has numbers and the speech has words." Push further: the graphic lets you compare amounts at a glance, while the speaker makes you care through a story and tells you what to do.

# 43. Same Scene, Two Orders
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell the same events in two different orders and explain how the order changes the effect on the reader.
directions: Read the event notes for a scene about a lost key. Write the scene in time order. Then write it again, starting from the end and going back to show how it happened. Finish by comparing the effect of each order.
mistakes: In the second version, students often start at the end and then simply retell everything in order with no clear shift back in time. Teach a signal: a time phrase ("Three hours earlier,") or the past perfect ("He had dropped the key in his pocket that morning"). Watch for versions that change the events; only the order should change.
notes: Starting at the end and then going back is often called a frame or a flashback structure. It trades suspense about what happens for curiosity about how and why. The model paragraphs on the key are original to Curio Class.

## Warm up: The event notes
example: 1. Before school, Jonah locks the front door and drops the key in his jacket pocket.
  2. At recess, he hangs his jacket on the fence to play soccer.
  3. After school, at his front door, he finds his pocket empty.
  4. He searches his backpack, then walks back to school in the rain.
  5. He spots the key shining in the grass under the fence.
  6. He sits on his porch, soaked, holding the key and laughing.
choice: Where does the key probably fall out of the pocket?
  - At the front door before school
  * By the fence at recess
  - Inside his backpack
  - On the porch

## Version 1: Time order
write: Write the scene in time order, from event 1 to event 6. Use at least two time words (first, later, after school).
  lines: 7
  key: Model: Before school, Jonah locked the front door and dropped the key into his jacket pocket. At recess, he tossed his jacket over the fence and ran to play soccer. After school, he stood at his front door and reached into his pocket. Empty. He dug through his backpack twice, then trudged back to school in the rain. There, under the fence, something shone in the wet grass: his key. Twenty minutes later he sat on his porch, soaked, holding the key and laughing.

## Version 2: Start from the end
write: Start with event 6. Then go back in time to show how Jonah got there. Use a signal such as "Two hours earlier," or "had" to shift back.
  starter: Jonah sat on his porch, soaked to the skin, holding a small silver key and laughing.
  lines: 7
  key: Model: Jonah sat on his porch, soaked to the skin, holding a small silver key and laughing. Two hours earlier, nothing had been funny. He had reached his front door after school and found his pocket empty. That morning he had dropped the key in that pocket, and at recess he had thrown his jacket over the fence. So he searched his backpack, walked back through the rain, and finally spotted the key shining in the grass under the fence.

## Compare the effect
chart: Time order | Starting from the end
  labels: What the reader wonders; When the reader feels tension; How the ending feels
  height: 0.65
  key: Wonders: time order makes the reader wonder what will go wrong and whether he will find the key; starting at the end makes the reader wonder why he is soaked and laughing. Tension: in time order it builds at the empty pocket and the search; starting at the end, the tension is mostly curiosity, since we know it ends well. Ending: time order ends with relief and a payoff; the second version ends by answering the opening mystery.
write: Which order works better for this scene? Explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Starting from the end works better, because the strange opening image of a soaked boy laughing hooks the reader, and the funny ending would be wasted if readers only feel suspense. (Time order is also a fine answer if the student explains it builds more suspense about whether he finds the key.)
