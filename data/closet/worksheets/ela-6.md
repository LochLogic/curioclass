# 1. Plot Diagram: Rikki-Tikki-Tavi
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, a copy of the full story
ican: I can map how a story's plot unfolds and cite a line that shows each stage.
directions: After reading "Rikki-Tikki-Tavi" by Rudyard Kipling, match the plot terms. Then read the quotation bank, decide which stage each line belongs to, and build a plot diagram with an event and a cited line for every stage.
mistakes: Students often call the first exciting event the climax (meeting Nag in the garden). The climax is the turning point where the main conflict is decided: Rikki follows Nagaina into her hole, and the snakes' threat ends. Some readers argue for the fight with Nag in the bathroom; accept it only if the student explains why it decides the conflict. Watch for resolution written as more action: the resolution is the new normal, Rikki guarding the garden.
notes: Students need the whole story (from The Jungle Book, 1894; free at Project Gutenberg, eBook #236). The quotations were checked word for word against that edition, with curly quotation marks made straight. The story is set in a British family's bungalow in colonial India; a short note on that setting helps students with words like bungalow, veranda and cantonment.

## Warm up: Plot words
match: Match each stage of a plot to what happens in it.
  - Exposition = We meet the characters and the setting.
  - Rising action = The conflict builds through a series of events.
  - Climax = The turning point, where the conflict is decided.
  - Falling action = Events that follow the turning point and wrap things up.
  - Resolution = How things end up; the new normal.

## Stretch
write: How does Rikki change from the exposition to the resolution? Use two quotation numbers as evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: At first he is a young, curious, half-drowned mongoose (quotations 1 and 2) who has never fought a live cobra. By the end he has proved his courage and become the confident protector of the whole garden (quotation 8).
# 25. Comparing Theme Across Genres
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter if you have one
ican: I can compare how a poem and a story develop the same theme, citing evidence from each.
directions: Read the poem and the story. Both share a theme about perseverance. Fill in the Venn diagram to show how each one develops the theme, then answer the questions with evidence from both texts.
mistakes: Students often stop at the topic ("both are about not giving up"). A theme is a full statement: "Steady effort, even after failure, can break through obstacles." Watch for comparisons that only list plot events; ask how each genre shows the theme: the poem through an image, rhyme and a direct message, the story through a character's actions, dialogue and change.
notes: The poem (12 lines) and the story (about 260 words) are original to Curio Class. Highlighting lines that show the theme in two colors, one per text, makes the Venn diagram faster.

## Read: The poem
source: "Stone and Seed," an original poem written for Curio Class
  The seed pushed up against the stone
  and found the stone would not be moved.
  It did not quit. It did not groan.
  It bent, and turned, and slowly proved
  that roots can find a crack of light
  where none was offered from the start.
  It grew a little every night,
  a green and stubborn little heart.
  Now look: the stone is split in two,
  and in the crack a flower stands.
  Whatever wall is blocking you,
  keep reaching, slow, with patient hands.

## Read: The story
passage: The Eleventh Try
  Rosa's robot arm swung, opened its claw, and dropped the paper cup full of paper clips. Again. It was the tenth time that afternoon.
  "The contest is Friday," said her brother Mateo from the doorway. "Maybe build something easier?"
  Rosa didn't answer. She picked up the cup and set it back on the table. Her arm was made of cardboard, straws and string, and when she pulled the string, the claw was supposed to close. Instead, it flopped open like a tired hand.
  That night she drew the arm in her notebook, every piece of it. She noticed that the string ran straight down from the claw, so when she pulled, the whole arm bent instead of the fingers. She frowned, erased, and drew the string through a straw on the inside of each finger.
  The next day she rebuilt the claw. It closed halfway, then slipped. She added rubber bands to the fingertips for grip. It closed, lifted the cup an inch, and dropped it.
  "Ten tries," Mateo said. "Want me to call it?"
  "Eleven," Rosa said. "I'm not done learning from it."
  She tightened the string, pulled slowly, and watched. The claw closed. The cup rose off the table, wobbled, and stayed. Rosa carried it across the room and set it down without spilling a single paper clip inside it.
  On Friday, the judges asked her to explain her design. Rosa opened her notebook to the page covered in erased lines. "This is the most important part," she said. "It's all the ways it didn't work."

## Warm up: The shared theme
choice: Which statement best expresses the theme the two texts share?
  - Robots and plants are both useful.
  - It is important to win contests.
  * Steady effort, even after many failures, can break through an obstacle.
  - Family members should help each other.

## Main: Compare
venn: The poem | The story
  both: The theme in both
  key: Poem only: the theme is shown through an image (a seed splitting a stone), rhyme and short lines, and a direct message to the reader in the last two lines. Story only: shown through a character (Rosa), her repeated tries, her thinking (redrawing the design), dialogue with Mateo, and her change. Both: an obstacle that will not move at first, small changes and patience, slow progress, success that grows out of struggle.
write: Cite a line from the poem that shows the theme, and explain how it shows it.
  lines: 3
  key: "It did not quit. It did not groan." The seed keeps pushing without complaint, which shows steady effort. Also accept "It grew a little every night" (slow progress) or "keep reaching, slow, with patient hands" (the message stated directly).
write: Cite a line from the story that shows the theme, and explain how it shows it.
  lines: 3
  key: "Eleven," Rosa said. "I'm not done learning from it." She refuses to quit after ten failures and treats each one as a lesson. Also accept the last line: "It's all the ways it didn't work."
write: The poem tells the reader the lesson directly in its last two lines. Does the story ever state the lesson directly? How does the reader learn it instead?
  lines: 3
  key: The story never states it outright; readers infer the theme from Rosa's actions (rebuilding, adding rubber bands), her dialogue with Mateo, and her pride in the notebook of failures at the end.

## Stretch: Write the comparison
write: Write a paragraph comparing how the two texts develop the theme of perseverance. Use at least one quotation from each text.
  lines: 7
  starter: Both "Stone and Seed" and "The Eleventh Try" show that ...
  key: Both texts show that steady effort through failure can break through an obstacle. The poem develops this through one image: a seed that "did not quit" and "grew a little every night" until the stone split; its last line speaks to the reader directly. The story develops the theme through Rosa's ten failed tries, her careful redesigns, and her answer to Mateo, "I'm not done learning from it." The poem compresses the idea into a symbol, while the story lets readers watch a character earn it.
