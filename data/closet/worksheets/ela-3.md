# 1. Chunk It: Reading Long Words
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can split a long word into syllables to read it.
directions: Every syllable has one vowel sound. Split each word into syllables with slashes, and put a dot under the vowel in each part. Then use the words to finish the sentences.
mistakes: Students often split after every consonant or break up a blend or digraph (pu/mpkin). Remind them each part needs one vowel sound, and two consonants in the middle usually split between them (nap/kin). Robot and music split after the vowel (ro/bot, mu/sic) because the first vowel is long. In umbrella, the double l splits (um/brel/la).
notes: Splits follow a standard dictionary. Have students clap each word before splitting it. Count the vowel sounds, not the vowel letters: in the last part of remember, -ber, the e is the only vowel sound.

## Warm up: How it works
example: fantastic: find the vowels (a, a, i), then split: **fan / tas / tic** = 3 syllables

## Main: Split and count
table: Word | Split it | Syllables
  - napkin | {nap / kin     } | {2}
  - rabbit | {rab / bit     } | {2}
  - basket | {bas / ket     } | {2}
  - problem | {prob / lem    } | {2}
  - robot | {ro / bot      } | {2}
  - music | {mu / sic      } | {2}
  - pumpkin | {pump / kin    } | {2}
  - remember | {re / mem / ber} | {3}
  - fantastic | {fan / tas / tic} | {3}
  - hospital | {hos / pi / tal} | {3}
  - important | {im / por / tant} | {3}
  - umbrella | {um / brel / la} | {3}

## Read them in sentences
text: Word bank: napkin, robot, umbrella, hospital, pumpkin, remember
fill:
  - It is raining, so open your {umbrella}.
  - The nurse works at the {hospital}.
  - We carved a big orange {pumpkin}.
  - Wipe your mouth with a {napkin}.

## Stretch: Longer words
fill:
  - invitation: {in / vi / ta / tion  }
  - hippopotamus: {hip / po / pot / a / mus}
write: Explain how you split one of the stretch words.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: In invitation I found four vowel sounds (i, i, a, io), so it has four parts: in / vi / ta / tion. I kept -tion together because it is one chunk.
# 6. Choices and Consequences: The Ant and the Grasshopper
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can describe each character's choices and explain how those choices shape what happens.
directions: Read the fable. Fill in the chart about each character's choice. Then answer the questions, using details from the story.
mistakes: Students often describe what a character is like ("the grasshopper is lazy") instead of the choice he makes ("he chooses to sing instead of storing food"). Ask "What did he decide to do?" Watch for consequence answers that skip the link: "In winter he is hungry" needs "because he stored no food in summer."
notes: This retelling is original to Curio Class, adapted from Aesop's fable (public domain; in the oldest versions the grasshopper is a cicada). The traditional ending is stern: the ant does not share. Many students will want her to; the stretch question invites that debate, and there is no single right answer.

## Read
passage: The Ant and the Grasshopper (adapted from Aesop)
  All summer long, the sun warmed the green field. A grasshopper hopped through the tall grass. He chirped and sang from morning until night.
  Nearby, an ant worked hard. Each day she dragged grains of wheat and corn back to her nest. The grain was heavy and the path was long, but she did not stop.
  "Why are you working on such a beautiful day?" asked the grasshopper. "Come and sing with me!"
  "I am storing food for the winter," said the ant. "You should do the same."
  The grasshopper laughed. "Winter is far away. There is plenty of food right now." And he went back to his song.
  Summer ended. The leaves turned brown and fell. Then snow covered the field, and there was nothing left to eat.
  The cold, hungry grasshopper knocked on the ant's door. "Please," he said, "may I have some food?"
  "What did you do all summer?" asked the ant.
  "I sang," said the grasshopper.
  "Then you sang all summer, and now you must go hungry all winter," said the ant. She had worked hard for her food, and she closed her door.
  The grasshopper learned a hard lesson: it is wise to get ready today for what you will need tomorrow.

## Warm up
choice: What does the grasshopper choose to do all summer?
  - Store grain in his nest
  * Sing and play in the grass
  - Help the ant carry food
  - Build a warm house
choice: Why does the ant keep working on a beautiful day?
  - She does not like to sing.
  - The grasshopper asked her to work.
  - She is lost and cannot find her nest.
  * She is storing food for the winter.

## Main: Choices and what they cause
chart: The choice they make | Why they make it | What happens because of it
  labels: The ant; The grasshopper
  height: 0.9
  key: The ant: chooses to carry grain all summer, because she knows winter is coming, so she has food in winter. The grasshopper: chooses to sing instead of storing food, because he thinks winter is far away and food is plentiful now, so in winter he is cold and hungry with nothing to eat.
choice: Which sentence shows that the ant thinks ahead?
  - "The grain was heavy and the path was long."
  * "I am storing food for the winter."
  - "What did you do all summer?"
  - "Come and sing with me!"
write: The grasshopper says, "Winter is far away." How does this choice shape what happens to him later?
  lines: 3
  key: Because he thinks winter is far away, he does not store any food. When the snow comes there is nothing left to eat, so he ends up cold and hungry and has to ask the ant for help.
write: How are the two characters different? Use one detail about each.
  lines: 3
  key: The ant plans ahead and works hard even on a sunny day ("she did not stop"). The grasshopper only thinks about today and sings "from morning until night."
choice: What lesson does the fable teach?
  - Singing is a waste of time.
  - Ants are stronger than grasshoppers.
  * It is wise to get ready today for what you will need tomorrow.
  - Never knock on a neighbor's door.

## Stretch
write: The ant does not share her food. Do you agree with her choice? Give a reason from the story.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers will vary. Agree: she worked hard all summer and warned the grasshopper to store food, so he had his chance. Disagree: he is cold and hungry, and she could share a little and still teach him to plan next time.
# 18. Dragon Color by Code: Parts of Speech
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; crayons in green, yellow, red, orange and blue
ican: I can tell what part of speech a word is by how it is used in a sentence.
directions: Read the story. Each numbered word is a noun, pronoun, verb, adjective or adverb. In the dragon grid, write the code for each word (N, P, V, Adj or Adv). Then color each box with the code's color. Leave the empty boxes white for the sky.
mistakes: Students may call every action-sounding word a verb or every describing word an adjective. Adverbs often end in -ly and tell how (slowly, loudly). "storm" (8) can be a verb in other sentences (Do not storm out!), but here "a storm" is a thing, so it is a noun. Remind students that "she" and "him" stand in for a noun, so they are pronouns.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. The answer key shows the code letters in the boxes; the colors follow the color key (N green, P yellow, V red, Adj orange, Adv blue). There are 4 words of each part of speech, so a finished dragon has 4 boxes of each color. Unnumbered words such as "slippery" and "home" are left out on purpose; they make good extra challenges.

## Warm up: The color key
example: **N** noun (a person, place, animal or thing) = green · **P** pronoun (she, he, him, we) = yellow · **V** verb (an action) = red · **Adj** adjective (tells what kind) = orange · **Adv** adverb (tells how) = blue
fill:
  - In "The wind howled," howled is a {verb}.
  - In "a tiny crab," tiny is an {adjective}.

## Read: Ember and the Storm

  When the clouds drifted away, Ember grinned and flew home happily **(20)**.

## Stretch: A tricky word
write: Some words can be different parts of speech in different sentences. Pick one numbered word. Tell what part of speech it is in the story and how you know.
  lines: 3

# 25. Science Word Map: Habitat
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can figure out what science words mean from the text and show what they mean.
directions: Read the text. Then make a word map for each bold word: write its meaning, draw a picture, give an example and use it in your own sentence.
mistakes: Students often copy the whole sentence from the text as the meaning. Ask them to say it in their own words, as if explaining to a younger student. For camouflage, watch for "hiding behind something": camouflage is blending in by color or pattern, not hiding behind an object.
notes: The text is original to Curio Class (about 190 words). The text defines each word right after using it, a common pattern in science books: point this out in the warm up so students learn to look for it.

## Read
passage: Life in a Pond
  A pond is a **habitat**. A habitat is the place where a plant or animal lives and finds everything it needs: food, water, shelter and space.
  Many animals share a pond. Frogs sit on lily pads and catch insects with their sticky tongues. Dragonflies zip over the water. Turtles rest on logs in the warm sun.
  Some animals in the pond are **predators**. A predator is an animal that hunts and eats other animals. A heron is a predator. It stands very still in the shallow water, then grabs a fish with its long, sharp beak. A big fish can be a predator too when it eats tadpoles.
  How do small animals stay safe? Many of them use **camouflage**. Camouflage is a color or pattern that helps an animal blend in with the place around it. A green frog is hard to see among green plants. A brown turtle shell can look like a muddy rock. When a predator cannot see an animal, it cannot catch it.
  Every part of the pond matters. If the water dries up, the plants and animals lose their habitat and must find a new home.

## Warm up: Find the clue
choice: Where in the text did you find what predator means?
  - In the title
  * In the sentence right after the word
  - In the last paragraph
  - The text does not say
choice: Which animal in the text is a predator?
  - A turtle resting on a log
  * A heron catching a fish
  - A dragonfly zipping over water
  - A lily pad

## Word map 1: habitat
write: Meaning in my own words
  lines: 1
  key: The home where a plant or animal lives and gets food, water, shelter and space.
draw: Picture of a habitat | 1.0
write: Example (one habitat or one animal and its habitat)
  lines: 1
  key: A pond is a frog's habitat (also: a forest, a desert, the ocean, a field).
write: My sentence
  lines: 2
  key: The desert is a habitat for lizards and cactus plants.

## Word map 2: predator
write: Meaning in my own words
  lines: 1
  key: An animal that hunts and eats other animals.
draw: Picture of a predator | 1.0
write: Example
  lines: 1
  key: A heron (also: a hawk, a big fish, a fox, a frog that eats insects).
write: My sentence
  lines: 2
  key: The hawk is a predator that hunts mice in the field.

## Word map 3: camouflage
write: Meaning in my own words
  lines: 1
  key: A color or pattern that helps an animal blend in so it is hard to see.
draw: Picture of camouflage | 1.0
write: Example
  lines: 1
  key: A green frog among green plants (also: a brown turtle shell like a rock, a white rabbit in snow).
write: My sentence
  lines: 2
  key: The brown moth's camouflage made it look just like the tree bark.
# 30. Timeline Read: Building a Bridge
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a text and a timeline to tell when and why things happened.
directions: Read the history of the Millbrook Bridge. Write each event's letter at the right year on the timeline. Then use the text and the timeline to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students may place 1898 on the 1900 tick or the 1903 event on 1905. Show them how to count the small spaces: each tick is 5 years, so 1898 sits just before 1900. For "why" questions, watch for answers that only restate the event; push for the word because.
notes: The town of Millbrook and its bridge are made up for this sheet (about 210 words), but the story follows how many real towns built bridges around 1900: ferries, floods, money raised by the town, stone piers, and later widening for cars. Point out that the text is in time order, with signal words such as "In 1898" and "Three years later."

## Read
passage: The Millbrook Bridge
  In 1890, the town of Millbrook had no bridge. To cross the wide Clear River, people rode a small ferry boat. On windy days the trip was slow and scary.
  In the spring of 1898, a big flood swept down the river and sank the ferry. Now farmers could not take their apples and corn to the market on the other side. Families were cut off from their friends.
  In 1900, the people of Millbrook voted to build a bridge. They chose stone instead of wood, because stone would not wash away in the next flood. To raise money, they held bake sales and a town fair.
  Workers began building in 1903. First they built thick stone piers, or posts, that stood in the river to hold up the bridge. Three years later, in 1906, the bridge was finished. The whole town marched across it in a parade.
  As years went by, more people drove cars and trucks. The bridge was too narrow for them. In 1925, workers made it wider so two cars could pass at once. The old stone piers were strong enough to hold the new road, and they still stand in the river today.

## Main: Put the events on the timeline
timeline: 1890 1930 5
  - 1890 | People cross the river on a small ferry.
  - 1898 | A flood sinks the ferry.
  - 1900 | The town votes to build a stone bridge.
  - 1903 | Workers start building the stone piers.
  - 1906 | The bridge opens with a parade.
  - 1925 | The bridge is made wider for cars.

## Questions: When and why
fill:
  - The flood sank the ferry in the year {1898}.
  - The bridge took {3} years to build, from 1903 to 1906.
choice: Why did the town decide to build a bridge?
  - The ferry was too fast.
  * The flood sank the ferry, and people could not get across the river.
  - The farmers wanted a parade.
  - The river dried up.
write: Why did the people choose stone instead of wood?
  lines: 2
  key: Because stone would not wash away in the next flood.
choice: Which event happened first?
  - The parade across the bridge
  - The bridge is made wider
  * The bake sales and town fair
  - Cars and trucks cross the new road
write: What happened in 1925, and why did it happen?
  lines: 3
  key: Workers made the bridge wider, because more people drove cars and trucks and the bridge was too narrow for two to pass.

## Stretch
write: How many years passed between the flood and the bridge opening? Show how you know.
  lines: 2
  key: 8 years: 1906 - 1898 = 8.
# 42. Speaking Clearly Self-Check
minutes: 15
materials: pencil
ican: I can check how clearly I speak and set a goal to get better.
directions: Use this sheet right after you give your report. First answer the warm up. Then circle one box in each row that tells how you did. Finish by writing what went well and one speaking goal.
mistakes: Third graders often circle the top box in every row. Before they rate, ask them to think of one moment in their report as proof. Watch for goals that are too big ("be perfect") or vague ("do better"); a good goal names one skill and one thing to try.
notes: Pair this with any short report or show and tell. If time allows, have a partner also circle the chart for the speaker in a different color, then compare. The chart is a self-check, not a grade. The warm up and model answers on the key are examples; students' own ratings and goals will vary.

## Warm up: What clear speaking sounds like
choice: Which shows a good **pace**?
  - Talking as fast as you can so you finish first
  * Talking at a steady speed, with short pauses between ideas
  - Stopping for a long time after every word
choice: Which shows good **volume**?
  - Whispering so only the front row hears
  - Shouting every word
  * Speaking loudly enough for the back of the room to hear
choice: Which shows good **eye contact**?
  * Looking up at different people in the audience
  - Looking at your paper the whole time
  - Looking at the ceiling while you talk

## Main: Rate yourself
text: Circle one box in each row.
table: Skill | Not yet | Getting there | Got it!
  - **Pace** (speed) | I went too fast or too slow. | I was mostly steady but rushed a little. | I spoke at a steady speed and paused between ideas.
  - **Volume** (loudness) | People had trouble hearing me. | Most people heard me most of the time. | Everyone could hear me the whole time.
  - **Eye contact** | I looked at my paper or the floor. | I looked up a few times. | I looked at my audience often.
write: Which skill went best? Tell one moment from your report that proves it.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Volume went best. When I told the part about the lion, I spoke loudly enough that my friend in the back row nodded.
write: Which skill do you want to grow most? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Eye contact, because I looked at my paper for most of my report.

## My speaking goal
write: Next time I speak, I will ...
  starter: Next time I speak, I will
  lines: 2
  key: Example: Next time I speak, I will look up at the audience after every sentence.
write: One thing I will do to practice my goal:
  lines: 2
  key: Example: I will practice my report in front of a mirror and look up at the end of each sentence.
