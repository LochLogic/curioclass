# 1. Vowel Team Sort: ai, ay, ee, ea, oa
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can read words with the vowel teams ai, ay, ee, ea and oa.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read each word card and find its vowel team. Cut out the cards and glue each one under its team. Then answer the questions at the bottom.
mistakes: Students mix up ai and ay because both say long a. Point out where each team sits: ai is usually in the middle of a word (rain), ay at the end (play). Bread is the surprise: ea says short e in bread but long e in leaf, so bread still goes under ea.
notes: Read all 15 words aloud together before cutting so no one sorts by guessing. Have students underline the two letters of the team on each card before they glue. Early finishers can add a word of their own to each column on the back.

## Warm up: Find the team
example: In **boat**, the letters **o** and **a** work as a team. They say long o, the name of the letter O.
fill:
  - rain: the vowel team is {ai}
  - feet: the vowel team is {ee}
  - day: the vowel team is {ay}

## Main: Sort the words
sort: Glue each word under its vowel team.
  - ai: rain, tail, paint
  - ay: play, tray, stay
  - ee: tree, feet, seed
  - ea: leaf, team, bread
  - oa: boat, soap, road

## Stretch: A team with two sounds
choice: (Read aloud.) Which vowel team can say two different sounds?
  - ai
  - oa
  * ea
  - ee
text: Circle that team at the top of its column. Then read: In **leaf**, ea says {long e}. In **bread**, ea says {short e}.
write: Write one more word where ea says short e, like bread.
  lines: 1
  key: head, read (past tense), dead, deaf, sweat, breath, spread or thread

# 6. Fable Lesson Match
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell the lesson, or moral, of a fable.
directions: (Read aloud.) A fable is a short story that teaches a lesson. Read each fable. Then read the six lessons in the box. Write the letter of the lesson that each fable teaches. Two lessons will be left over.
mistakes: Students pick a lesson because it names something in the story (the race, the food) rather than what the character learns. Ask, "What did the character do, and what happened because of it?" Some choose "Tell the truth" for The Fox and the Grapes because the fox fibs; the fox's fib is about pretending he never wanted the grapes, which is lesson C.
notes: The four fables are short retellings by Curio Class of fables told by Aesop, an ancient Greek storyteller; the old fables are in the public domain and appear in many versions. Key: The Ant and the Grasshopper, D; The Lion and the Mouse, F; The Fox and the Grapes, C; The Tortoise and the Hare, A. The leftover lessons fit other Aesop fables: B (The Milkmaid and Her Pail) and E (The Boy Who Cried Wolf); students who know those stories can say so.

## Read: Four fables
passage: 1. The Ant and the Grasshopper
  All summer, Grasshopper sang and played in the warm sun. Ant worked hard. She carried seeds to her home, one by one. "Come and play!" said Grasshopper. "I am saving food for winter," said Ant. Soon winter came, and snow covered the ground. Grasshopper had no food, and he was cold and hungry. Ant was warm at home with plenty to eat.
passage: 2. The Lion and the Mouse
  A lion caught a little mouse under his big paw. "Please let me go," squeaked the mouse. "Someday I will help you." The lion laughed, but he let her go. Later, the lion was caught in a hunter's net. He roared and roared. The mouse heard him. She chewed the ropes until the lion was free.
passage: 3. The Fox and the Grapes
  A hungry fox saw a bunch of grapes hanging high on a vine. He jumped for them, but he missed. He jumped again and again, but he could not reach them. At last he walked away. "Those grapes are sour anyway," he said. "I did not want them."
passage: 4. The Tortoise and the Hare
  Hare laughed at Tortoise for being so slow. "Let's race," said Tortoise. Hare ran far ahead. He was so sure he would win that he lay down for a nap. Tortoise kept going, step by step. When Hare woke up, Tortoise was crossing the finish line.

## Main: Match the lessons
table: Letter | Lesson
  - A | Slow and steady wins the race.
  - B | Do not count your chickens before they hatch.
  - C | It is easy to say you do not want something you cannot get.
  - D | Work now so you are ready later.
  - E | Tell the truth, or people may not believe you.
  - F | A kind act is never wasted. Even a small friend can help.
fill:
  - The Ant and the Grasshopper: {D}
  - The Lion and the Mouse: {F}
  - The Fox and the Grapes: {C}
  - The Tortoise and the Hare: {A}

## Stretch: Think about it
write: Pick one fable. Tell what the character did that taught the lesson.
  lines: 3
  key: For example: In The Tortoise and the Hare, Tortoise kept going step by step and did not stop, so he won even though he was slow. (Any fable, with what the character did and how it shows the lesson.)
choice: Why does the fox say the grapes are sour?
  - He tasted them and they were sour.
  - He does not like grapes.
  * He could not get them, so he pretends he did not want them.
  - A friend told him they were sour.

# 18. Story Paragraph Planner
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan and write a story paragraph with actions, thoughts and feelings.
directions: (Read aloud.) Make up a story about a brave squirrel. First plan it: who, where and what problem. Then fill the chart with what your squirrel does, thinks and feels at the beginning, middle and end. Last, write your story as one paragraph.
mistakes: Students fill only the Actions column, so the story reads like a list of events. Ask, "What is your squirrel thinking right now? How does it feel?" Watch for a paragraph that skips the end or stops at the problem. Thoughts can be written like talking to yourself: Squirrel thought, "I can do this."
notes: Model with the sample in the key, or brainstorm brave things a squirrel might do: cross a busy branch, scare off a hawk, rescue a baby bird, find food in the snow. Feeling words to post: scared, nervous, worried, brave, proud, happy, surprised, calm. A paragraph for grade 2 is about 5 to 8 sentences with a beginning, a middle and an end. The story is made up, so any brave act is fine.

## Plan: The start
write: Who is your squirrel? Give it a name.
  lines: 1
  key: Nutmeg, a small gray squirrel.
write: Where does the story happen?
  lines: 1
  key: In a tall oak tree next to a pond.
write: What is the problem? What brave thing will your squirrel do?
  lines: 2
  key: A baby bird falls out of its nest onto a high branch, and Nutmeg must climb out on a thin, shaky branch to help it.

## Plan: Actions, thoughts and feelings
text: **Feeling words:** scared, nervous, worried, brave, proud, happy, surprised, calm
chart: Actions (what it does) | Thoughts (what it thinks) | Feelings (how it feels)
  labels: Beginning; Middle; End
  height: 0.9
  key: Beginning: Nutmeg hears a peep and sees the baby bird on a thin branch; thinks "That branch is so high"; feels worried. Middle: she creeps out on the shaky branch and nudges the bird back to its nest; thinks "Do not look down"; feels scared but brave. End: the mother bird sings and Nutmeg climbs home; thinks "I did it!"; feels proud.

## Write: Your story paragraph
write: Write your story as one paragraph. Use your chart. Start with a sentence that tells who and where.
  lines: 9
  key: Nutmeg was a small gray squirrel who lived in an oak tree by a pond. One morning she heard a tiny peep. A baby bird had fallen onto a thin branch high above the water. Nutmeg felt worried, and she thought, "That branch is so shaky!" She took a deep breath and crept out, one paw at a time. "Do not look down," she told herself. She gently nudged the baby bird back into its nest. The mother bird sang a happy song, and Nutmeg felt proud all the way home.

# 25. Prefix Meaning Clues
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a prefix to figure out what a new word means.
directions: (Read aloud.) Learn what un-, re- and pre- mean. Read each sentence. Find the prefix in the bold word. Write what the word means.
mistakes: Students may write a meaning that leaves out the base word ("again") instead of the whole word ("fill again"). Have them say it as prefix meaning plus base word. Watch for words that only look like they have a prefix: uncle and read have no prefix to take off.
notes: Accept any meaning that joins the prefix and the base word correctly ("not locked," "open"). Meanings: un- means not or the opposite of; re- means again; pre- means before.

## Warm up: What do the prefixes mean?
match: Match each prefix to its meaning.
  - un- = not, or the opposite of
  - re- = again
  - pre- = before

## Main: Use the clue
example: I had to **redo** my picture. re (again) + do = **do again**
fill:
  - Dad will **preheat** the oven for the bread. preheat means {heat before}
  - The box is **unlocked**, so we can open it. unlocked means {not locked}
  - I **reread** the page to find the answer. reread means {read again}
  - The ice on the pond is thin and **unsafe**. unsafe means {not safe}
  - We **unpacked** our bags at Grandma's house. unpacked means {took things out of the bags}
  - Please **refill** the dog's water bowl. refill means {fill again}
  - We watched a **preview** of the new movie. preview means {a look before}
  - Mia will **retell** the story in her own words. retell means {tell again}

## Stretch: Make a word
write: Add un-, re- or pre- to one of these words: tie, kind, build, pay. Write the new word and use it in a sentence.
  lines: 2
  key: Possible answers: untie (I untie my shoes), unkind (It is unkind to tease), rebuild (We rebuild the tower), prepay (We prepay for the trip). Any correct prefix word in a sentence that shows its meaning.

# 31. How Does the Character Respond?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell how a character responds to events in a story.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read the story. For each event, tell how Rosa responds: what she does, says or feels. Use details from the story.
mistakes: Students retell the event instead of the response ("A wave washed it away"). Ask, "And then what did Rosa do?" Some say Rosa is just "sad" for the whole story; point out that her feelings change from the first event to the last.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 115 words). Each paragraph holds one event, numbered in the margin. A response can be an action (stomped her foot), words ("It took me so long!") or a feeling (sad, upset). Strong answers name a detail from the text.

## Read: The story
passage: The Sandcastle | numbered
  Rosa spent all morning on her sandcastle. It had four towers and a moat. Then a big wave rushed up the beach. Whoosh! The castle melted into a lump of wet sand. Rosa stomped her foot, and her eyes filled with tears. "It took me so long!" she cried.
  Her brother Leo walked over with two buckets. "We can build a new one together," he said. Rosa wiped her eyes and took a bucket.
  This time they built far from the water. When the waves came in, they stopped short of the castle. Rosa clapped and put a shell on the top tower. "Now it is safe," she said with a big smile.

## Main: Event and response
write: Event 1: A wave washes the sandcastle away. How does Rosa respond?
  lines: 2
  key: She is upset. She stomps her foot, her eyes fill with tears, and she cries, "It took me so long!"
write: Event 2: Leo says they can build a new one together. How does Rosa respond?
  lines: 2
  key: She wipes her eyes and takes a bucket, so she agrees to try again.
write: Event 3: The waves stop short of the new castle. How does Rosa respond?
  lines: 2
  key: She claps, puts a shell on the top tower, and says, "Now it is safe," with a big smile. She feels happy and proud.
choice: How do Rosa's feelings change from the beginning to the end?
  - From happy to sad
  * From upset to happy
  - From scared to angry
  - They do not change.

## Stretch: Your idea
write: What did Rosa learn? Use a detail from the story.
  lines: 2
  key: She learned to try again and to build far from the water, because the second castle was safe from the waves.

# 43. Talk Like a Reporter: Formal and Informal
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell formal from informal language and use formal language when I report to a class.
directions: (Read aloud.) Informal language is how we talk with friends. Formal language is how a reporter talks to an audience. Sort the sentences. Then rewrite each informal sentence so it sounds like a class report.
mistakes: Students only remove "hey" and leave the rest the same. Look for slang (super, way, gonna, like) and short forms (it's got). Some make the sentence formal but change its meaning ("This bug is gross" for "This bug is super cool"); keep the same idea.
notes: Formal language uses complete sentences, no slang, and polite openings and endings. Accept any rewrite that keeps the meaning and drops the slang. Contractions such as it's are fine in a grade 2 report; the goal is clear, polite, complete sentences. Let students read their formal sentences aloud like a TV reporter.

## Warm up: Formal or informal?
table: Sentence | Formal or informal?
  - Good morning. Today I will tell you about frogs. | {formal}
  - Dude, frogs are awesome. | {informal}
  - Thank you for listening to my report. | {formal}
  - Yep, that's all I got. | {informal}

## Main: Rewrite like a reporter
example: Informal: Look, it's, like, a huge leaf! Formal: Please look at this very large leaf.
write: Hey, look at this!
  lines: 1
  key: Please look at this picture.
write: This bug is super cool.
  lines: 1
  key: This insect is very interesting.
write: It's got, like, six legs.
  lines: 1
  key: It has six legs.
write: Gonna tell you about my rock now.
  lines: 1
  key: Now I am going to tell you about my rock.
write: Yeah, it's way old.
  lines: 1
  key: Yes, it is very old.
write: That's it, bye!
  lines: 1
  key: That is the end of my report. Thank you for listening.

## Stretch: Start a report
write: Write two formal sentences to begin a report about any animal.
  lines: 3
  key: Good morning, everyone. Today I will tell you three facts about owls.
