# 1. Reading Symbols in The Ambassadors
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Holbein's The Ambassadors, large
ican: I can decode symbols in a Renaissance painting and use them to interpret its meaning.
directions: Read the reference sheet. Find each object in the painting your teacher shows. Match each object to its meaning, then answer the questions about the painting as a whole.
mistakes: Students sometimes decide each object has one fixed meaning. Symbols depend on context, and scholars disagree about some of these: say "likely" or "may suggest" and give evidence. The skull is hard to see straight on; if possible, let students view the image from a sharp angle at the side, or stretch the image, to make it pop into shape.
notes: Hans Holbein the Younger, The Ambassadors (1533), National Gallery, London; public domain, and the National Gallery website has a zoomable image. The two men are Jean de Dinteville, the French ambassador to England (left), and Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur (right). The stretched shape across the floor is an anamorphic skull. The open hymnbook shows hymns in Martin Luther's German translation, and the lute has a broken string; many scholars read these together as the religious conflict of the Reformation. A small crucifix is half hidden behind the green curtain at the top left.

## Reference sheet
text: **Skull:** death; a memento mori, a reminder that everyone dies. **Broken string or broken instrument:** discord, things falling out of harmony. **Hymnbook or prayer book:** faith; a hymnbook open to shared songs can suggest hope for unity. **Crucifix:** Christian faith and the hope of salvation. **Globes, sundials and star instruments:** knowledge, science, exploration and the heavens. **Rich clothing, fur and silk:** wealth and status.

## Warm up: Find it
fill:
  - The two men are standing on either side of a {two}-shelf stand covered with objects.
  - The strange stretched shape across the floor is a {skull}.
  - The objects on the top shelf deal with the {sky} (heavens), the objects on the bottom shelf with the {earth}.

## Main: Decode six symbols
match: Match each object in the painting to its likely meaning.
  - the stretched skull across the floor = death comes for everyone, however rich or learned
  - the lute with one broken string = discord, perhaps the religious conflict dividing Europe
  - the open hymnbook = faith and a hope for unity
  - the crucifix half hidden behind the curtain = the hope of salvation, easy to miss
  - the celestial globe and sundials on the top shelf = knowledge of the heavens and of time
  - the terrestrial globe and arithmetic book on the bottom shelf = knowledge of the earth, trade and exploration

## Interpret
write: The skull can only be seen properly from a sharp angle at the side. Why might Holbein hide death in a painting so full of riches and learning?
  lines: 3
  key: To surprise the viewer: as you walk past admiring the wealth and knowledge, death suddenly appears. It suggests that all worldly success is temporary, a memento mori built into the viewing experience.
write: Put two symbols together. What message could they make as a pair?
  lines: 3
  key: Examples: the broken lute string next to the Lutheran hymnbook suggests religious discord and a hope to heal it; the skull and the crucifix together set death against the hope of salvation; the globes and the skull together say knowledge cannot defeat death.
write: In one or two sentences, state an interpretation of the whole painting, using at least three symbols as evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample: The painting celebrates two powerful, learned men but warns that wealth and knowledge are fragile. The globes and instruments show their learning, the broken string shows a divided world, and the hidden skull and crucifix remind viewers of death and of faith.

## Stretch
write: If a painter made a portrait of a famous person today using symbols, name three objects you would include and what each would mean.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers choose objects tied to the person's achievements and values, and at least one that adds a second layer of meaning, as the skull does.

# 5. A Critic in 1874
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; the teacher shows Claude Monet's Impression, Sunrise and, if possible, Monet's Boulevard des Capucines and Camille Pissarro's Hoarfrost
ican: I can identify the criteria a critic uses and judge whether they fit the work being judged.
directions: Read the background and the excerpt from Louis Leroy's review. Name what the critic attacks in each paragraph and the standard behind it. Then decide whether his criteria were fair to what the painters were trying to do, and write a fairer critique of your own.
mistakes: Students often call the review simply "mean" and stop there. The task is to name the criterion under each joke: finish, clear drawing, legible forms. Others assume Leroy was writing a serious review; it is satire, a comic dialogue with an imaginary painter, which is part of why it is funny and part of why it is unfair. Remind students that a criterion can be reasonable in general and still be the wrong test for a particular work.
notes: The excerpt is adapted and translated in our own words from Louis Leroy, "L'Exposition des impressionnistes," Le Charivari (Paris), April 25, 1874; it is a paraphrase, not an exact translation. In the review, Leroy tours the show with an imaginary academic landscape painter he calls Joseph Vincent, who grows more upset at each painting and by the end has lost his mind. Paragraph 1 is about Pissarro's Hoarfrost (1873), paragraph 2 about Monet's Boulevard des Capucines (1873), paragraph 3 about Monet's Impression, Sunrise (1872). All three paintings are in the public domain. Hoarfrost is in the Musée d'Orsay and Impression, Sunrise in the Musée Marmottan Monet, both in Paris; Monet painted two versions of Boulevard des Capucines (now in Kansas City and Moscow), and scholars debate which one hung in the show. The show opened April 15, 1874, in the former studio of the photographer Nadar on the boulevard des Capucines.

## Background
text: Each year the official Salon in Paris decided which paintings the public would see. Its juries prized smooth, carefully finished surfaces, clear drawing and serious subjects. In April 1874 about 30 artists, including Claude Monet, Camille Pissarro, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Edgar Degas, Berthe Morisot, Alfred Sisley and Paul Cézanne, organized their own independent show instead. Many of them painted outdoors in quick, visible strokes to catch changing light and a passing moment. The humor magazine Le Charivari sent the critic Louis Leroy.

## The source
source: Adapted and translated from Louis Leroy, "The Exhibition of the Impressionists," Le Charivari, Paris, April 25, 1874. Leroy wrote the review as a comic dialogue with an imaginary academic painter.
  [1] We stopped in front of a painting of a frosty plowed field. My companion stared and asked whether those lines were furrows and that white was frost. To him they looked like scrapings from a palette, spread evenly over a dirty canvas. The picture, he said, had no head and no tail, no top and no bottom, no front and no back.
  [2] At a view of a crowded boulevard, he pointed to the many black dabs at the bottom of the picture and asked what they were. When I told him they were people walking, he asked whether he himself looked like that when he walked along the boulevard.
  [3] At last we reached a harbor at sunrise. The catalog called it Impression, Sunrise. "Impression," he said; he had been sure of it. Since he was impressed, there had to be some impression in it. And what freedom, what ease in the brushwork! Wallpaper in its first, unfinished stage, he said, was more finished than this seascape.

## Warm up
choice: What kind of writing is Leroy's review?
  - a news report listing the prices of the paintings
  * a comic dialogue that mocks the paintings through an imaginary painter
  - a letter from Monet defending his work
  - an official judgment from the Salon jury
choice: Why does this review still matter today?
  - It ended the careers of the painters it mocked.
  - It was the only review of the exhibition.
  * Its title helped give the group the name Impressionists.
  - It convinced the Salon to show the paintings.

## Main: Find the criteria
table: Paragraph | What the critic attacks | The criterion (standard) behind it
  height: 0.75
  - 1 | {the marks do not clearly show furrows or frost; no clear structure} | {a painting should be clearly drawn and organized, with forms you can read}
  - 2 | {the people are only black dabs} | {figures should be carefully drawn with accurate detail}
  - 3 | {it looks less finished than wallpaper} | {a painting should have a smooth, complete, polished finish}
write: What were these painters trying to capture, and why did quick, visible brushstrokes suit that goal?
  lines: 3
  key: They wanted to capture changing light, weather and a passing moment, often painting outdoors. Light changes in minutes, so quick, broken strokes let them record the overall effect before it vanished; from a distance the strokes blend into the impression of mist, crowds or sunlight.
write: Were Leroy's criteria fair to these paintings? Give one way they were fair and one way they were not.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Fair: finish and clear drawing were the accepted standards of the day, and viewers had a right to ask what a painting showed. Not fair: he judged the works by goals the artists had rejected, and satire let him mock instead of explaining. In paragraph 2, for example, the dabs actually capture how a crowd looks from a high window in a glance.

## Stretch: A fairer critic
write: Write a three-sentence critique of Impression, Sunrise that uses criteria suited to what Monet was trying to do. Include one honest criticism.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Model: "Monet captures the moment the sun burns through the morning haze: the orange disc and its broken reflection are the only strong colors in a gray-blue world. The quick, loose strokes make the water seem to move and the harbor seem to dissolve in mist. Some viewers may still want more detail in the boats, which are only dark shapes."

# 15. Figures in Perspective Space
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler, eraser
ican: I can place figures in a two-point perspective scene at the right size using the horizon line.
directions: Read the rule and the example. Complete the size chart. Then build your own two-point scene and place 5 figures in it, checking each one with the rule.
mistakes: The most common mistake is drawing distant figures smaller but floating: their feet must sit on the ground plane, and their size comes from how far their feet are below the horizon, not from guessing. Students also place every head exactly on the horizon even for children or seated people; that only works for standing figures about as tall as the viewer's eye height.
notes: The rule: when the viewer stands on the same level ground as the figures, the horizon line is at the viewer's eye height everywhere in the scene. So for any standing figure, the distance from its feet up to the horizon on the page stands for the viewer's eye height (here 5 feet). Drawn height = (distance from feet to horizon) x (real height / 5). A 5-foot figure's head touches the horizon; taller figures rise above it; shorter figures stay below it. In the chart, 6-foot adults are crossed by the horizon five sixths of the way up, near the chin.

## The rule
example: Eye level is 5 feet, so the horizon line is 5 feet above the ground everywhere in the scene. A 6-foot adult stands with feet 2 inches below the horizon on your paper. Those 2 inches stand for 5 feet, so the adult is drawn 2 x 6 / 5 = 2.4 inches tall, and the horizon crosses near the chin.

## Warm up
choice: The viewer's eye level is 5 feet. Where does the horizon line cross a standing figure who is exactly 5 feet tall, no matter how near or far?
  - at the waist
  - at the feet
  * at the top of the head
  - it depends on the distance
choice: A figure's feet are drawn very close to the horizon line. What does that tell you?
  * The figure is far away and will be drawn small.
  - The figure is very close and will be drawn large.
  - The figure is floating above the ground.
  - The figure must be sitting down.

## Main: Size chart
text: Eye level is 5 feet. Use: drawn height = (inches from feet to horizon) x (real height / 5).
table: Figure | Real height | Feet below horizon | Drawn height | Horizon crosses it at
  - Adult | 6 ft | 2.5 in | {3 in} | {near the chin}
  - Adult | 5 ft | 1.5 in | {1.5 in} | {the top of the head}
  - Child | 4 ft | 2 in | {1.6 in} | {above the head}
  - Adult, far away | 6 ft | 0.5 in | {0.6 in} | {near the chin}

## Main: Your scene
text: Draw a horizon line, two vanishing points near the edges and one building or block in two-point perspective. Then add 5 standing figures: one near, two in the middle, two far.
draw: My two-point scene | 4.0

  labels: Figure 1; Figure 2; Figure 3; Figure 4; Figure 5
  key: Answers vary. For each figure, drawn height should equal the measured distance from feet to horizon times real height divided by 5, within about a tenth of an inch, and every figure's feet should sit on the ground plane below the horizon.

## Stretch
write: Your viewer now sits on a bench, so eye level is 3.5 feet. Where will the horizon cross the 6-foot adults in your scene? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Lower on their bodies, a little above the waist: the horizon is at 3.5 feet on every standing figure, so their heads rise above the horizon. 3.5 / 6 is about 58 percent of their height.

# 21. Finding Your Concentration
minutes: 40
materials: pencil; your working portfolio or sketchbook (at least 8 pieces)
ican: I can review my own work to find recurring themes and choose a concentration with reasons.
directions: Lay out at least 8 of your pieces. Fill in the chart for each one. Look for subjects, materials and ideas that keep coming back. Name 3 recurring themes, then choose one as your concentration and explain why.
mistakes: Students often name a theme that is only a subject ("dogs"). A strong concentration is a subject plus an idea or question ("how pets show what their owners care about"). Others choose a theme that sounds impressive but does not appear in their work; the evidence must come from the chart.
notes: A concentration is a sustained body of work around one theme or question. If a student has fewer than 8 pieces, sketchbook pages count. Students choose which pieces to review; nothing personal needs to be shared beyond the art itself.

## Warm up: Subject or idea?
choice: Which is the strongest concentration?
  - Trees
  - My best drawings
  * How trees in the city survive in small spaces
  - Pencil
choice: A student notices that 5 of their 8 pieces show hands. What should they ask next?
  - How can I stop drawing hands?
  * What do the hands in my work seem to be about: work, care, connection?
  - Which piece is the best?
  - Who else draws hands?

## Main: Review your work
chart: Piece | Subject | Material or method | Mood or idea | Repeats?
  rows: 8
  key: Answers vary. Each row should name a concrete subject, the medium, and a mood or idea; the last column notes whether any of these appear in other pieces.
chart: Recurring theme | Pieces that show it (by number) | What idea or question is behind it?
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary. Each theme should be supported by at least two pieces from the first chart and be phrased as an idea, not just a subject.

## Choose
write: My concentration: write it as one sentence that includes a subject and an idea.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Model: "I explore how hands show care and work in everyday tasks."
write: Give two reasons this theme is the right choice for you. Use evidence from your charts.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Strong reasons cite how often the theme appears, the student's interest, and room to grow (many possible pieces, materials and angles).

## Stretch
write: List three new pieces you could make for this concentration, each trying a different material or point of view.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong lists vary the medium (charcoal, collage, clay), scale and viewpoint while keeping the same idea.

# 25. Concentration Statement
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; your portfolio or sketchbook; your concentration from Finding Your Concentration
ican: I can write a clear statement that explains the question, approach and growth in a body of my work.
directions: Read the model and label its parts. Fill in the planning frame. Then write a concentration statement of about 200 words in the space given, and check it against the list.
mistakes: Students often write a list of their pieces ("First I made... then I made...") instead of explaining the idea that connects them. Others use big words that say nothing ("my art explores the human condition"). Push for concrete evidence: name a piece, a material change or a discovery. A statement over 250 words usually means it is retelling instead of explaining.
notes: A concentration statement (sometimes called an artist statement for a body of work) introduces a sustained investigation to a viewer or reviewer. Requirements vary by program; AP Art and Design, for example, asks students to identify their inquiry question and describe how their investigation evolved through practice, experimentation and revision, in short written responses with strict limits. Have students check any specific program's rules. The model is original to Curio Class.

## Read the model
example: My work asks how ordinary objects hold the memory of the people who use them. I began by drawing worn shoes and tools in graphite, focusing on scuffs and repairs as records of use. After four drawings I realized close detail was not enough, so I started pairing each object with a second image: a coffee mug with the hand that holds it each morning. I tested charcoal, ink washes and finally cyanotype prints, which let me trace real objects onto paper with sunlight. The prints gave the objects a ghostly, faded look that fit my idea of memory better than sharp realism. My biggest growth came when I stopped choosing pretty objects and began choosing broken ones, because a crack or a mend tells a stronger story. My latest pieces combine cyanotype silhouettes with pencil detail, so each work moves from a faint memory to a sharp present.
table: Part of a statement | Words from the model that show it
  height: 0.55
  - The question | {how ordinary objects hold the memory of the people who use them}
  - The approach (materials and methods) | {graphite drawings, pairing objects with hands, charcoal, ink washes, cyanotype prints}
  - A change and why | {close detail was not enough, so pairing images; cyanotype fit the idea of memory better}
  - Growth | {stopped choosing pretty objects and began choosing broken ones}

## Plan
write: My question (one open sentence):
  starter: My work asks ...
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. An open question that combines a subject and an idea, such as "how light changes the way a city corner feels."
write: My approach: the materials and methods I have used, with two pieces as examples.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Names at least two media or methods and two specific pieces.
write: A change I made and why I made it:
  starter: At first I ... but then I ... because ...
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A real turning point tied to the question, such as a new material, scale or subject that served the idea better.
write: How I have grown, and where the work is going next:
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A specific growth in skill or thinking, and one next step.

## Write your statement (about 200 words)
write: Combine your plan into one paragraph. Open with your question; end with where the work is going.
  lines: 12
  key: Answers vary. Rubric: (1) opens with a clear, open question; (2) names materials and methods with at least two specific pieces; (3) explains at least one change and the reason for it; (4) shows growth and a next step; (5) about 180 to 220 words, first person, no list of every piece.

## Check
chart: Checklist item | Yes or not yet
  labels: My question is open, not yes or no; I name at least two real pieces; I explain why I changed something; I show growth; It is about 200 words
  key: Answers vary. Every row should be "yes" before the statement is final.

# 35. Technique Trials
minutes: 60
materials: pencil; for your technique: soft carving block, lino cutters, bench hook and water-based ink; or paper or precut tiles, scissors and glue; or egg yolk, water, school-safe pigment or powdered tempera, small brushes and a gessoed board or heavy card
ican: I can test a traditional technique through controlled trials and record what I learn.
directions: Match the terms. Choose one traditional technique. Plan 3 trials that each change one thing, carry them out, and record your results in the table. Then explain what you learned and how you will use the technique.
mistakes: Students often change several things at once (a new tool, a new ink and a new paper), so they cannot tell what caused the result. One change per trial. Others write "it was good" as a result: push for what they see (crisp line, ragged edge, streaks, gaps). For safety, students carve away from the hand holding the block, with the block against a bench hook.
notes: Safety: carve away from your body and your other hand, always with a bench hook, and keep cutters capped when not in use. Use only school-safe pigments; never dry artist pigments that contain lead, cadmium or cobalt, which are hazardous as dust. Check for egg allergies before tempera; students wash hands after handling egg and never eat it. For tesserae, use paper, foam or precut tiles; if cutting ceramic tile, use tile nippers with safety glasses. Background: egg tempera (pigment bound with egg yolk) was the main panel-painting medium in medieval and early Renaissance Europe and dries fast, so painters build it up in thin hatched strokes; Cennino Cennini described it in his handbook of about 1400. Mosaics of small tesserae covered Roman floors and the walls of Byzantine churches such as San Vitale in Ravenna (sixth century). Relief carving for printing is the basis of the woodcut and of Japanese ukiyo-e prints; linocut became popular in the twentieth century.

## Warm up: Know the terms
match: Match each term to its meaning.
  - egg tempera = paint made by binding pigment with egg yolk
  - gesso = a chalky white ground painted on a panel to take tempera
  - hatching = building tone with many thin parallel strokes, used because tempera dries fast
  - tesserae = small pieces of stone, glass, tile or paper set side by side in a mosaic
  - andamento = the visual flow of the rows of tesserae
  - relief print = a print made by cutting away everything that should stay white

## Plan
text: My technique (circle one): relief carving, tesserae, egg tempera
write: Question: What do I want to find out in my trials?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A testable question, such as "How does the depth of my cuts change how crisp the printed line is?" or "How does the ratio of yolk to pigment change how smoothly tempera flows?"
write: Prediction:
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A specific prediction with a reason.
text: The one thing I will change in each trial: ________________ What I will keep the same: ________________

  lines: 3

  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a specific use and connects a quality of the technique (the bold contrast of relief, the light-catching gaps of tesserae, the luminous layers of tempera) to the idea.

## Stretch
write: Find one historical work made with your technique. What does it do that your trials could not yet do?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: Botticelli's tempera paintings show fine hatched modeling; the mosaics of San Vitale use gold glass tesserae angled to catch light; Hokusai's prints use separate blocks for each color.
