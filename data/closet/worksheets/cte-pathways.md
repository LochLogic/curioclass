# 1. Four Paths Compared
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can compare education and training paths by time, cost, pay while training and outcomes.
directions: Read the four path cards. Fill in the comparison chart. Then use it to advise three invented students.
mistakes: Students often assume every path after high school costs the same or that only college leads to good pay. Apprenticeships and military service pay you while you train; certificates are short and lead to specific jobs. Watch for "free" used for the military; training is paid, but it comes with a service commitment and real risks.
notes: Figures are typical ranges in plain words, not promises: costs vary widely by school, state and financial aid, and apprenticeship and military terms vary by program and branch. Sources to check before teaching: the U.S. Department of Labor's Apprenticeship.gov, the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, and each military branch's official recruiting site. A strong student answer to the advice questions matches the path to the student's goal and situation, not to a single "best" path.

## The four path cards
text: **Four-year college.** Time: about 4 years for a bachelor's degree. Cost: tuition varies from a few thousand to tens of thousands of dollars a year; grants and scholarships can lower it. Pay while training: usually none, though many students work part time. Outcomes: a bachelor's degree, required for many professional jobs such as engineer or teacher; on average, the highest lifetime earnings of the four.
text: **Registered apprenticeship.** Time: usually 1 to 6 years. Cost: little or no tuition; often the employer pays for classes. Pay while training: yes, a paycheck from day one that rises as skills grow. Outcomes: a nationally recognized credential in a skilled job such as electrician, plumber, or IT support.
text: **Certificate program.** Time: a few weeks to 2 years at a community or technical college. Cost: usually much less than a four-year degree. Pay while training: usually none. Outcomes: a certificate or license for a specific job, such as welder, medical assistant or HVAC technician; a fast way into work.
text: **Military service.** Time: usually a commitment of about 4 years of active duty, starting with basic training and then job training. Cost: no tuition; food, housing and health care are provided. Pay while training: yes, from the first day. Outcomes: job training and experience, plus education benefits for college later; requires meeting age, health, fitness and test standards and can include deployment.

## Main: Comparison chart
table: Path | Time | Cost | Paid? | Outcome
  - College | {about 4 years} | {high, varies} | {no} | {bachelor's degree}
  - Apprenticeship | {1 to 6 years} | {little or none} | {yes} | {trade credential}
  - Certificate | {weeks to 2 years} | {low} | {no} | {job certificate}
  - Military | {about 4 years} | {no tuition} | {yes} | {training, benefits}

## Questions
choice: Which two paths pay you while you train?
  - College and certificate
  * Apprenticeship and military
  - Certificate and military
  - College and apprenticeship
choice: Which path is usually the fastest way into a specific job?
  - College
  - Apprenticeship
  * Certificate
  - Military

## Advise three invented students
write: Ivy wants to be an electrician and cannot afford to go without a paycheck. Which path fits her best? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: An apprenticeship: electricians are trained through apprenticeships, and she earns wages from the first day with little or no tuition.
write: Omar wants to become a high school chemistry teacher.
  lines: 2
  key: Four-year college: teaching requires a bachelor's degree (and a teaching license).
write: Bea wants to start working as a medical assistant within a year.
  lines: 2
  key: A certificate program: medical assistant certificates take about a year or less at a community or technical college.

## Stretch
write: Many people combine paths. Describe one way someone could combine two of these paths, and why it might make sense.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: serve in the military, then use education benefits for college; earn a certificate and work, then attend college part time with employer tuition help; finish an apprenticeship that also earns college credit toward an associate's degree.

# 7. Path to a Goal
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator, career information (the Occupational Outlook Handbook or a printed career profile)
ican: I can plan a route to a career goal with steps, a timeline, costs and a backup path.
directions: Study Rosa's invented plan to become a dental hygienist and answer the questions. Then plan your own route to one career goal, with steps, dates, costs and a backup path.
mistakes: Students often list a goal and a degree with nothing in between: prerequisites, applications, exams and licenses are real steps that take time. Watch for costs that include only tuition (books, tools, fees and exams add up) and for backup paths that are a completely different field instead of a related job that uses the same steps. Grants do not need to be repaid; loans do.
notes: Rosa and her costs are invented; real costs vary widely by school and state. The career facts follow the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook: dental hygienists typically need an associate's degree in dental hygiene, and every state requires them to be licensed, which includes passing exams. Dental assistants, the backup path, may train through a certificate program or on the job, depending on the state. Totals: $4,000 + $18,000 + $3,000 + $1,500 = $26,500; grants $4,000 x 3 = $12,000; Rosa pays $14,500; the path takes 3 years after high school.

## Model: Rosa's plan
table: Step | When | Cost | How Rosa will pay
  - 1. Graduate high school with biology and chemistry | June 2027 | $0 | none needed
  - 2. Take prerequisite classes (anatomy, chemistry, English) at the community college | Aug 2027 to May 2028 | $4,000 tuition | grant, part-time job
  - 3. Apply to the dental hygiene program | Jan 2028 | $50 fee | savings
  - 4. Complete the 2-year associate's degree in dental hygiene | Aug 2028 to May 2030 | $9,000 a year tuition | grant, job, small loan
  - 5. Buy books, instruments and uniforms | 2028 to 2030 | $3,000 | savings, job
  - 6. Pass the national and clinical exams and get a state license | summer 2030 | $1,500 | savings
text: **Backup path:** If Rosa is not accepted in 2028, she will work as a dental assistant (a shorter certificate, about a year) and apply again in 2029. That job keeps her in a dental office and builds her application.

## Warm up
fill:
  - Tuition for the 2-year program: 2 x $9,000 = {$18,000}
  - Total cost (prerequisites, program tuition, books and supplies, exams; leave out the $50 fee): $4,000 + $18,000 + $3,000 + $1,500 = {$26,500}
  - Rosa gets a grant of $4,000 a year for 3 years: {$12,000}. She still needs {$14,500}.
  - From high school graduation (June 2027) to her license (summer 2030) is about {3} years.
choice: Why is Rosa's backup path a strong one?
  - It is in a completely different field, so she has a fresh start.
  - It costs nothing and needs no training.
  * It keeps her in dental care and makes her a stronger applicant next time.
  - It means she never has to apply again.

## Main: My route to a goal
text: Career goal: {answers vary} Training it requires (from a career profile): {answers vary}
chart: Step | When (month and year) | Cost | How I could pay
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6
  height: 0.5
  key: Answers vary. A strong plan has steps in order from now to the first job, including requirements such as prerequisites, applications, exams or licenses; realistic dates; a cost for each step that includes more than tuition; and a way to pay (savings, work, grants, scholarships, employer help, loans).
fill: My total estimated cost: {answers vary} Estimated grants or scholarships: {answers vary} Amount left to pay: {answers vary}

## Main: Backup path
write: What is your backup path if your first plan is delayed? Choose a related job or route that uses some of the same steps.
  lines: 3
  starter: If ___, I will ___. This keeps me on track because ___.
  key: Answers vary. A strong backup names a realistic obstacle (not accepted, cost too high, a failed exam), a related job or training route that uses the same skills, and how it leads back toward the goal.
write: Which step in your plan is the riskiest or most expensive? What could you do now to prepare for it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: a competitive program application (take the prerequisite classes seriously, volunteer in the field); a large tuition bill (apply for scholarships and grants by their deadlines, consider starting at a community college).

## Stretch
write: Where did you find the training requirements for your career? Name one fact you learned that surprised you.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a reliable source (the Occupational Outlook Handbook "How to Become One" section, a school's program page, a licensing board) and a specific requirement.

# 20. Market Research Survey
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can design a fair survey, tally the results and use them to choose a product and a price.
directions: An invented student business wants to sell a snack at Saturday school games. Write six fair survey questions. Tally 30 invented responses, then use the data to choose a product and a price.
mistakes: Students often write leading questions ("Wouldn't you love our delicious fruit cups?") or questions with no clear answer choices. In the price table, a person who would pay $3 would also pay $1 or $2, so count everyone at that price or higher. Watch for picking the price with the most sales or the most revenue; the business wants the most profit, which subtracts costs.
notes: The business and all 30 responses are invented. Tallies: product A 8, B 15, C 7; maximum price $1: 7, $2: 12, $3: 9, $4: 2. Fruit cup (B) fans at each price or higher: $1: 15, $2: 13, $3: 8, $4: 2. With a $1 cost per cup, profit per 15 fans surveyed: $0, $13, $16, $6, so $3 earns the most profit even though $2 sells more cups.

## Warm up
choice: Which survey question is fair (not leading)?
  - Wouldn't you love our delicious fruit cups?
  - Everyone hates soggy chips, right?
  - Do you agree that trail mix is the healthiest snack?
  * Which of these three snacks would you most like to buy at a game?
choice: Which question gives answers that are easiest to tally?
  * What is the most you would pay for one snack: $1, $2, $3 or $4?
  - What do you think about snacks?
  - Tell us about your favorite foods.
  - How do you feel about prices?

## Main: Design six questions
chart: Question | Answer choices
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6
  height: 0.5
  key: Model: 1. Which snack would you most like to buy at a game: trail mix, fruit cup or veggie chips? 2. What is the most you would pay for one: $1, $2, $3 or $4? 3. How often do you come to Saturday games: every week, sometimes, rarely? 4. When do you usually buy snacks: before the game, at halftime, after? 5. Do you have any food allergies we should plan for: yes or no (and which)? 6. How would you rate the snacks sold now: 1 to 5? A strong set is fair (not leading), short, has clear answer choices and helps decide the product and the price.

## Main: Tally 30 responses
text: Each response shows the snack chosen (A = trail mix, B = fruit cup, C = veggie chips) and the most that person would pay.
table: 1-6 | 7-12 | 13-18 | 19-24 | 25-30
  - B $4 | B $1 | C $2 | B $3 | A $1
  - A $2 | A $1 | B $2 | C $2 | A $1
  - B $3 | B $2 | C $1 | B $3 | B $3
  - C $3 | B $1 | C $1 | B $2 | B $2
  - A $3 | C $2 | B $3 | A $2 | A $2
  - C $2 | B $4 | A $3 | B $3 | B $2
table: Snack | Tally | Total
  - A. Trail mix | | {8}
  - B. Fruit cup | | {15}
  - C. Veggie chips | | {7}
table: Most they would pay | $1 | $2 | $3 | $4
  - Number of people | {7} | {12} | {9} | {2}

## Main: Choose a price
text: The business picks the snack most people chose. It costs **$1** to make each one. Use only the people who chose that snack. Count everyone who would pay the price **or more**.
table: Price | Buyers at this price or more | Revenue (price x buyers) | Profit ((price - $1) x buyers)
  - $1 | {15} | {$15} | {$0}
  - $2 | {13} | {$26} | {$13}
  - $3 | {8} | {$24} | {$16}
  - $4 | {2} | {$8} | {$6}
write: Which snack and which price should the business choose? Use the data.
  lines: 3
  key: Fruit cups at $3. Fruit cups were chosen by 15 of 30 people, more than trail mix (8) or veggie chips (7). At $3, profit is $16 per 15 fans surveyed, more than $13 at $2, even though $2 sells 13 cups and brings in more revenue.

## Stretch
write: Name one weakness of this survey and how you would fix it.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Examples: 30 people is a small sample; ask more people at several games. People may say they would pay more than they really would; run a one-day test sale. The people surveyed may not represent everyone at the games; survey families, players and visiting fans.

# 21. Business Model Canvas
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can use a business model canvas to plan how a small business creates value and makes money.
directions: Read about the invented Saturday market booth, Spoke and Chain Bike Fix. Fill in the business model canvas, then do the math on costs and revenue.
mistakes: Students often confuse value (why customers come: fast, cheap repairs on the spot) with channels (how customers find and reach you: the booth, flyers). Revenue is all the money coming in; profit is what is left after costs. Watch for the $120 tool kit counted every week; it is a one-time cost.
notes: The booth is invented for this sheet. The business model canvas (developed by Alexander Osterwalder) has nine boxes: key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams. The canvas answers in the key are models; the math answers are exact.

## The booth
text: Sam and Lucia run Spoke and Chain Bike Fix at the town's Saturday market. They pump tires, oil chains and adjust brakes while customers shop. A tune-up costs **$12**. Supplies (oil, rags, patches) cost **$2** per tune-up. The market charges **$30** per Saturday for a booth. They bought a tool kit once for **$120**. They hand out flyers at the school and the library, and they give a free tire check to every kid who rides in. Last Saturday they did **15** tune-ups.

## Main: The canvas
chart: Key partners | Key activities | Value we offer | Customer relationships | Customer segments
  rows: 2
  key: Model: Key partners: the market, a bike shop that sells parts, the school and library (flyers). Key activities: tune-ups, tire pumping, brake adjustments. Value: quick, cheap repairs while you shop, no trip to a store. Customer relationships: friendly, free tire checks for kids, regulars by name. Customer segments: families who bike to the market, kids, commuters.
chart: Key resources | Channels | Cost structure | Revenue streams
  rows: 2
  key: Model: Key resources: the tool kit, Sam and Lucia's repair skills, the booth space. Channels: the market booth, flyers at the school and library, word of mouth. Costs: $30 booth fee each Saturday, $2 supplies per tune-up, the $120 tool kit once. Revenue: $12 per tune-up.

## Main: Do the math
fill:
  - Revenue last Saturday = 15 x $12 = {$180}
  - Supplies last Saturday = 15 x $2 = {$30}
  - Total costs last Saturday (booth and supplies) = {$60}
  - Profit last Saturday = {$120}
  - Each tune-up earns $12 - $2 = {$10} after supplies.
  - Tune-ups needed each Saturday to cover the booth fee: {3}
  - Saturdays like last one needed to pay back the $120 tool kit: {1}

## Stretch
write: Sam wants to add a second product. Suggest one, and say which boxes of the canvas it would change.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: selling bike bells or lights would add a revenue stream and a cost (buying stock) and might need a new key partner (a supplier); offering a bike safety class would add a key activity and a new customer segment.

# 33. Medical Word Parts
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can split medical terms into prefixes, roots and suffixes and use the parts to define them.
directions: Use the word part bank and the example. Split each medical term into its prefix, root and suffix (write a dash if there is no prefix or suffix). Then define the term in plain words.
mistakes: Students often treat the combining vowel (the o in cardiology) as a separate part; it joins the root to the suffix and adds no meaning. Watch for a missing prefix being forced (cardiology has none). Definitions usually read from the end: start with the suffix, then the prefix, then the root ("-itis, inflammation, of the stomach").
notes: Word parts follow standard medical terminology references. Meanings: cardi/o heart, gastr/o stomach, brady- slow, tachy- fast, hypo- below or low, glyc/o sugar, -emia blood condition, dermat/o skin, arthr/o joint, neur/o nerve, append/o or appendic/o appendix, hepat/o liver, peri- around, leuk/o white, -cyte cell, hemat/o blood, -algia pain, intra- within, ven/o vein, -ous pertaining to, -ectomy surgical removal, -itis inflammation, -logy study of, -ia condition. Stretch terms: neuritis, tonsillectomy, tachypnea (-pnea, breathing). Accept "card" or "cardi" as the heart root.

  - | leuk/o white; hemat/o blood | -cyte cell; -ous pertaining to

## Example
example: **bradycardia** = brady- (slow) + card (heart) + -ia (condition) = a condition of a slow heartbeat.
