# 1. After the French and Indian War
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils, a map of North America in 1754 and 1763 (textbook or atlas)
ican: I can compare who claimed North America before and after the French and Indian War and explain reactions to the Proclamation of 1763.
directions: Use your textbook maps of North America in 1754 and 1763 to fill in the claims chart. Sketch the Proclamation Line. Then read two short sources and explain why colonists and Native nations reacted the way they did.
mistakes: Students often treat European "claims" as if Europeans lived on or controlled that land. Most land between the Appalachians and the Mississippi was home to Native nations, who had not given it up; a claim was a promise between empires, not ownership. Students also think the Proclamation Line was permanent: settlers kept crossing it, and treaties such as Fort Stanwix (1768) moved the line west.
notes: Both sources are adapted (modern wording, shortened), not exact quotations. Source A paraphrases the Royal Proclamation of October 7, 1763. Source B paraphrases words that the trader Alexander Henry, in his memoir (1809), said the Ojibwe leader Minavavana spoke to him at Michilimackinac in 1761; check the original wording in Henry's Travels and Adventures before quoting it. Pontiac's War, led by the Odawa leader Pontiac and allied nations, began in the spring of 1763, months before the Proclamation, and helped push Britain to draw the line.

## Sources: Two points of view
source: Source A, adapted from the Royal Proclamation issued by King George III, October 7, 1763
  [1] The nations of Indians who live under our protection should not be disturbed on the lands that have not been sold to us or given up by them. These lands are reserved for them as their hunting grounds.
  [2] No governor may grant land beyond the heads of the rivers that flow into the Atlantic Ocean. Any settlers living on such lands must leave them.
source: Source B, adapted from words that Alexander Henry, a British trader, said the Ojibwe leader Minavavana spoke to him in 1761 (Henry's memoir, published 1809)
  [1] Englishman, you have conquered the French, but you have not conquered us. These lakes, these woods and these mountains were left to us by our ancestors. They are our inheritance, and we will give them up to no one.
write: In Source B, what does Minavavana say about the French defeat? Why would Native nations not accept that Britain now owned their land?
  lines: 3
  key: He says Britain beat the French but did not conquer the Ojibwe, and the land was left to them by their ancestors. France had no right to give away land Native nations lived on and had never given up, so a treaty between two empires did not make the land British.
write: Many colonists, including veterans and land companies, were angry about the Proclamation. Use Source A to explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: Source A banned land grants and settlement west of the mountains and ordered settlers there to leave. Colonists felt they had fought the war to win the Ohio Valley, and land speculators (George Washington among them) wanted to sell or farm that land.
write: Native nations did not all trust the Proclamation, even though it reserved land for them. Why might that be?
  lines: 3
  key: Britain had broken promises before, and settlers were already crossing the mountains; the Proclamation also said land could be sold to the Crown, so it could be taken later. Britain had also stopped giving gifts and supplies as the French had, which helped spark Pontiac's War in 1763.

## Stretch: Cause and effect
write: Historians call the Proclamation of 1763 one step on the road to revolution. Explain how a royal order meant to keep peace with Native nations added to colonists' anger at Britain.
  lines: 4
  key: Colonists saw it as Parliament and the king limiting their freedom and their reward for fighting the war, made far away without their say. Along with new taxes to pay for the war and for soldiers to guard the frontier, it convinced many colonists that Britain did not respect their rights.

# 31. What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can analyze a primary source for its audience, its argument and its tone.
directions: Read the background and the four excerpts. Mark words that show Douglass's feelings. Then answer the questions, citing the excerpt numbers.
mistakes: Students sometimes read Douglass as rejecting the Declaration itself. He holds the nation to its own founding words; he condemns the hypocrisy of celebrating liberty while slavery continues. Excerpt 4 matters: his tone is fierce but not hopeless. For question 4, also accept cruelty, vanity, unholy, mockery or any word from excerpt 3 that carries his anger.
notes: Hard history: keep the focus on Douglass's own words, his reasoning and his purpose. He had escaped slavery in 1838 and was one of the most famous speakers in the country. Excerpts follow the 1852 pamphlet text with cuts marked [...]; check the wording against a scan of the pamphlet (Library of Congress, Frederick Douglass Papers) before printing. Spelling ("to-day") is the original.

## Background
text: On July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass spoke at Corinthian Hall in Rochester, New York, to an audience gathered by the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. Douglass had escaped from slavery in Maryland fourteen years earlier. He was asked to speak about the Fourth of July.

## Read: Four excerpts
source: Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?", Rochester, New York, July 5, 1852
  [1] "Fellow-citizens, pardon me, allow me to ask, why am I called upon to speak here to-day? What have I, or those I represent, to do with your national independence? Are the great principles of political freedom and of natural justice, embodied in that Declaration of Independence, extended to us?"
  [2] "The rich inheritance of justice, liberty, prosperity and independence, bequeathed by your fathers, is shared by you, not by me. The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me. This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn."
  [3] "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July? I answer: a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim. To him, your celebration is a sham; your boasted liberty, an unholy license; your national greatness, swelling vanity [...]"
  [4] "Allow me to say, in conclusion, notwithstanding the dark picture I have this day presented of the state of the nation, I do not despair of this country. There are forces in operation, which must inevitably work the downfall of slavery."

## Analyze
choice: Who was Douglass's audience that day?
  - People who supported slavery
  * Northerners who opposed slavery, gathered by an anti-slavery society
  - Members of Congress in Washington
  - People who were enslaved in the South
write: In excerpt 1, Douglass asks about the Declaration of Independence. What founding ideals does he name, and what is he really asking?
  lines: 3
  key: Political freedom and natural justice; he is asking whether those ideals apply to Black Americans and the enslaved, pointing out that they do not.
write: In excerpt 2, Douglass repeats a pattern: "yours, not mine," "You may rejoice, I must mourn." Why do you think he uses this contrast?
  lines: 3
  key: The contrast shows the gap between free Americans and the enslaved; repeating it makes the audience feel the division in their own celebration.
fill:
  - Two words that show Douglass's tone in excerpt 3: {sham} and {injustice}
write: How does excerpt 4 change or add to the tone of the speech? Use a quotation.
  lines: 3
  key: It adds hope: "I do not despair of this country." He believes forces at work will end slavery, so his anger is aimed at change, not at giving up on the nation.

## Stretch
write: Why might Douglass have chosen to speak on July 5 instead of July 4? Use what you learned from the speech.
  lines: 2
  key: Possible answers: to set himself apart from a holiday that did not include enslaved people; to make his point that the Fourth was "yours, not mine." July 4, 1852 was also a Sunday; accept any reasoning supported by the text.
