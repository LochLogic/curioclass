# 6. Articles of Confederation Report Card
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, textbook section on the Articles of Confederation
ican: I can evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation using evidence.
directions: Read the facts about the Articles and the source. Then grade the national government on four subjects, A to F, and give two pieces of evidence for each grade. Finish with the teacher's comment: what one change would most improve this government?
mistakes: Students grade every subject F without evidence. Push for two specific facts per grade. Students also forget that the Articles had real successes: the government won the war, made the 1783 peace treaty and passed the Northwest Ordinance (1787), which set up a fair way for new states to join. Watch for "Congress could not make laws": it could, but it needed 9 of 13 states to pass important ones and had no way to make states obey.
notes: Grades are judgments; the key gives one model, and any grade backed by two accurate pieces of evidence earns full credit. The Articles were written in 1777 and took effect in 1781, when Maryland ratified last. Shays' Rebellion (1786 to 1787) was led by farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them war veterans, facing debts, high state taxes and the loss of their farms; a militia paid for by Boston merchants broke it up at the Springfield arsenal in January 1787. The source is adapted (shortened, modern wording) from Washington's letter to James Madison of November 5, 1786; check the exact wording at Founders Online before quoting it.

## Warm up: How the Articles worked
fill:
  - Under the Articles, each state had {one} vote in Congress, no matter how many people it had.
  - Important laws needed the votes of {9} of the 13 states.
  - Changing the Articles needed the votes of all {13} states.
  - There was no president to carry out laws and no national {courts} to settle disputes.

## Evidence bank
source: Facts about the national government, 1781 to 1787
  [1] Congress could ask the states for money but could not collect taxes. The states paid only a small part of what Congress asked for.
  [2] Congress could not regulate trade. States taxed goods from other states, and Britain closed many of its ports to American ships.
  [3] Congress had no power to draft soldiers and depended on the states for an army. British troops stayed in forts on US land in the Northwest.
  [4] In 1786 farmers in Massachusetts rose up against debts and taxes and shut down courts. Congress could not raise troops to stop them.
  [5] The Land Ordinance (1785) and the Northwest Ordinance (1787) set up a plan to survey western land and admit new states as equals.
source: George Washington, letter to James Madison, November 5, 1786 (adapted)
  [6] Thirteen states pulling against each other, all tugging at the national government, will soon bring ruin to the whole.

## Main: The report card
table: Subject | Grade | Evidence (use the bank and your textbook)
  height: 0.8
  - Raising money (taxes) | {F} | {Congress could only ask states for money (1) and they paid little, so it could not pay war debts or soldiers.}
  - Trade | {D} | {Congress could not regulate trade (2); states taxed each other's goods, and Britain shut ports to US ships.}
  - Defense | {D} | {No power to draft an army (3); British troops stayed in forts on US land.}
  - Keeping order: Shays' Rebellion | {F} | {Congress could not raise troops to stop the uprising (4); Massachusetts had to rely on a militia paid for by merchants. Washington warned of ruin (6).}
  - Extra credit: western lands | {A} | {The Land Ordinance and Northwest Ordinance (5) organized the West and let new states join as equals.}

## Teacher's comment
choice: Which problem does source 6 describe?
  - Congress had too much power over the states.
  - The president ignored Congress.
  * The states acted on their own instead of together.
  - Too many people could vote.
write: Write the teacher's comment: the one change that would most improve this government, and why. Use evidence from your report card.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample: give Congress the power to collect taxes. With its own money it could pay its debts, raise an army to defend the country and stop uprisings like Shays' Rebellion, instead of begging the states. (Also accept: power to regulate trade, an executive to enforce laws, or national courts, with evidence.)

## Stretch: From report card to Constitution
write: In 1787 delegates met in Philadelphia to fix the Articles and ended up writing a new Constitution. Name two powers the Constitution gave Congress that answer failing grades on your report card.
  lines: 3
  key: The power to lay and collect taxes (answers the F in raising money) and the power to regulate trade with other nations and among the states (answers trade). Also accept: the power to raise and support armies, or a president who is commander in chief (answers defense and Shays' Rebellion).

# 19. Who Can Act on This?
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can identify which level of government decides an issue and name ways citizens can take action.
directions: For each problem, decide who has the power to fix it and at what level (school, local, state or national). Then name two ways citizens, including students, could act. Use the example to get started.
mistakes: Students send every problem to the president or Congress. Most everyday problems (streets, schools, parks) are decided close to home, where one person's voice counts more. Students also list only "sign a petition." Push for a range: speak at a public meeting, write to the decision maker, gather evidence, work with a group, or vote. For the national park, both the National Park Service (which runs parks) and Congress (which funds them) are correct.
notes: Who decides can vary by place: a dangerous intersection on a city street is up to the city (often its traffic or public works department and the city council), but one on a state highway belongs to the state department of transportation. Accept any reasonable answer that names the right level and a real decision maker. Students under 18 cannot vote, but they can testify at public meetings, write letters, organize and help register voters.

## Warm up: Levels of government
match: Match each decision maker to its level.
  - School board = School district
  - City council = Local
  - State legislature = State
  - Congress = National
  - Governor = State, executive

## Main: Four problems
example: **Problem:** The library near school closes at 5 p.m., before many families can visit. **Who decides:** the city council, which sets the library's budget (local). **Ways to act:** 1. Speak during public comment at a council meeting. 2. Collect signatures from library users and deliver them to the council.
table: Problem | Who decides (and what level) | Two ways citizens can act
  height: 1.0
  - A busy intersection near school has no crosswalk, and two students were almost hit last month. | {The city council and city traffic department (local); the state transportation department if it is a state highway} | {1. Count near misses and present the data at a council meeting. 2. Ask the traffic department in writing for a crosswalk or signal study.}
  - The school's dress code bans hoodies, and students think the rule is unfair. | {The school board, which sets district policy, and the principal (school district)} | {1. Bring the issue to student council and propose a new rule. 2. Speak during public comment at a school board meeting.}
  - A state law says teens must wait until 16 to get a learner's driving permit, and some want it lowered to 15. | {The state legislature, which writes the law, and the governor, who signs it (state)} | {1. Write to or meet with your state representative. 2. Testify at a hearing on a bill to change the law.}
  - A national park plans to close its campground because its budget was cut. | {The National Park Service runs the park, and Congress sets its budget (national)} | {1. Write to your members of Congress about park funding. 2. Send a public comment to the park, or volunteer with a group that supports it.}

## Think it through
choice: Which problem could a student most likely help solve fastest, and why?
  - The national park, because Congress meets often
  - The driving permit law, because laws change quickly
  * The dress code, because the school board and principal are close by and students can speak to them directly
  - None, because students cannot vote
write: Pick one problem. Which of your two actions would work best? Explain why it would reach the person who decides.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: for the crosswalk, presenting near-miss counts at a council meeting works best, because council members vote on the city budget and traffic projects, and data plus parents' and students' stories in a public meeting make the danger hard to ignore.

## Stretch: Same problem, many levels
write: Some problems are shared by several levels of government. Choose one problem from the table and explain how a second level of government could also play a part.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: the crosswalk is local, but if the road is a state highway the state transportation department decides, and federal safety grants from Congress can pay for crosswalks. (Also accept: states set some rules for school dress codes; states run their own parks.)
