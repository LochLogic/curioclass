# 1. The Stress Response in the Body
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain what happens in the body during the stress response and describe fight, flight and freeze.
directions: Draw a simple body outline for a character and label six changes the stress response causes. Fill in the chart, then decide which response each character shows and explain fight, flight and freeze.
mistakes: Students say digestion speeds up because of the "butterflies" feeling. It slows down: blood and energy move away from the stomach toward the muscles. Another mix-up: freeze is not the same as calm. The body is still, but it is on high alert.
notes: The outline is of a character, not the student, so nobody shares anything about their own body. Science behind the key: when the brain senses danger, the sympathetic nervous system and the hormone adrenaline (epinephrine, from the adrenal glands) act within seconds, and cortisol follows over minutes. The same response can switch on for things that are not physical danger, like a test or a game. A stretch extension: discuss why slow breathing helps the body switch the response off.

## Warm up
text: Word bank: **faster**, **tense**, **slows down**, **widen**, **more**, **more oxygen**, **ready to move**, **more blood to the muscles**, **saves energy for the muscles**, **lets in more light**, **cools the body**
example: When the brain senses danger, it signals the body to get ready, fast. Adrenaline rushes into the blood within seconds. This is the stress response.

## Main: Label the body
draw: Draw a simple body outline for a character named Alex. Label six changes: heart, breathing (lungs), muscles, stomach (digestion), eyes (pupils) and skin (sweat). | 3.6
table: Body part | What changes | How it helps in danger
  - Heart | beats {faster} | sends {more blood to the muscles}
  - Lungs | breathing gets {faster} | brings in {more oxygen}
  - Muscles | get {tense} | body is {ready to move}
  - Stomach | digestion {slows down} | {saves energy for the muscles}
  - Eyes | pupils {widen} | {lets in more light}
  - Skin | {more} sweat | {cools the body}

## Fight, flight or freeze?
choice: A dog suddenly barks at Kenji, and he steps back and runs across the street. Which response is this?
  - Fight
  * Flight
  - Freeze
choice: Lucia hears a crash in the dark hallway and goes completely still, holding her breath. Which response is this?
  - Fight
  - Flight
  * Freeze
choice: A goose charges at Omar's little sister, and Omar steps in front of her, shouting and waving his arms. Which response is this?
  * Fight
  - Flight
  - Freeze

## Stretch
write: Explain fight, flight and freeze in your own words. Then explain why Alex's body might show the same changes before a big exam, even though an exam is not dangerous.
  lines: 6
  key: Fight means facing the threat and pushing back; flight means getting away from it; freeze means going still, often so a threat won't notice you. The brain reacts to a stressful situation, like an exam, with the same alarm system it uses for physical danger, so the heart races and muscles tense even though there is nothing to fight or run from.

# 7. Sleep and Stress Data
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can read sleep data and explain how sleep, stress and focus affect each other.
directions: Read the CDC table of recommended sleep and the graph of one week of sleep for Jordan, a fictional 16-year-old. Answer the questions, then explain how sleep and stress are linked.
mistakes: Students read Jordan's weekend nights (9 hours) and decide he sleeps enough "on average." Averaging hides the school nights, which are the ones that matter for school focus. Also watch students who give the adult recommendation (7 or more hours) for teens. The teen row is 13 to 17 years.
notes: Table adapted from the CDC page "About Sleep" (cdc.gov/sleep), which lists recommended hours of sleep per 24 hours by age; the infant through preschool ranges include naps. The CDC lists teens as 13 to 17 years. Jordan's data is made up. The stress fact comes from the American Psychological Association's Stress in America: Generation Z report (October 2018): 68 percent of Gen Z adults (ages 18 to 21) said they had lain awake at night because of stress in the past month. Keep the conversation about Jordan; students do not report their own sleep.

## Questions
fill:

  - As people get older, the recommended hours of sleep {go down}.
  - Jordan got at least 8 hours on {2} of the 7 nights.

choice: Night 4 (Wednesday) was Jordan's shortest night. What is most likely on Thursday?
  - He will focus better because he had more time to study.
  * He will have a harder time focusing and may feel more irritable.
  - Nothing, because he will sleep 9 hours on the weekend.
write: In a 2018 survey by the American Psychological Association, 68 percent of 18- to 21-year-olds said they had lain awake at night because of stress in the past month. Use this fact and Jordan's graph to explain how stress and sleep can form a cycle.
  lines: 4
  key: Stress can keep people awake, like the 68 percent who lay awake from stress. Then, short on sleep, a person like Jordan has a harder time focusing and handling problems the next day, so schoolwork piles up and stress goes up again, which makes it even harder to sleep. Each one makes the other worse.

## Stretch
write: Suggest two changes that could help Jordan get closer to 8 hours on school nights, and explain how each one could also lower his stress.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: Set a regular bedtime on school nights, so his body gets used to falling asleep at the same time; feeling rested makes stress easier to handle. Put his phone away 30 minutes before bed and write a to-do list for tomorrow, so worries are on paper instead of keeping him awake.

# 20. Club Meeting Agenda
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan and open a meeting with a clear agenda, group norms and a fair way to decide.
directions: Read about the Green Team's first meeting. Fill in the agenda times, choose a decision method, write three norms, and then write the opening script the club president will read aloud.
mistakes: Agendas often skip the clock: every item needs minutes, and the minutes must add up to the meeting length. Watch for norms that are vague ("be good"); a norm is a specific behavior ("one person talks at a time"). In the decision method, students often pick "vote" without saying what kind or what happens in a tie.
notes: The club and its members are fictional. Common decision methods: majority vote (more than half wins), consensus (everyone can live with it), fist to five (members show 0 to 5 fingers for support), dot voting (each member places a set number of dots on favorite options) and ranked choice. For a choice among three options with 18 people, dot voting or ranked choice avoids a winner with only 7 votes. In the agenda table, the start times are computed from the minutes: 3:15, 3:18, 3:22, 3:27, 3:39, 3:47, 3:52.

## Read
text: **The Green Team** is a new environmental club with 18 members. Its first meeting runs from 3:15 to 3:55 p.m. in Room 104. The members must choose **one spring project**: (1) plant trees at the city park, (2) run a school recycling drive or (3) clean up Willow Creek. Three members will each give a short pitch for one project. You are the club president.

## Warm up
fill:
  - The meeting lasts {40} minutes.
  - If each of the 3 project pitches gets 4 minutes, the pitches take {12} minutes in all.
choice: Three options and 18 members. Which decision method best avoids a winner that only a few people like?
  - Flip a coin
  - The president decides
  * Ranked choice or dot voting
  - Whoever talks loudest

## Main: The agenda
table: Agenda item | Minutes | Start time
  - Welcome and purpose of the meeting | 3 | {3:15}
  - Norms: agree on how we will work together | 4 | {3:18}
  - Quick introductions: name and one reason you joined | 5 | {3:22}
  - Three project pitches | 12 | {3:27}
  - Questions and discussion | 8 | {3:39}
  - Decide on one project | 5 | {3:47}
  - Next steps and wrap-up | 3 | {3:52}
fill: Check: the minutes add up to {40}, so the meeting ends at {3:55}.
write: Our decision method, and what we will do if there is a tie:
  lines: 2
  key: Model: Ranked choice. Everyone ranks the three projects 1, 2, 3 on a slip of paper. If no project has more than half the first-choice votes, we drop the last-place project and count again. If there is still a tie, the two tied projects get one more minute of discussion and we vote again.
write: Three norms for the club (each one a specific behavior):
  lines: 3
  key: Model: 1. One person talks at a time; raise a hand to speak. 2. Everyone gets a chance to talk before anyone speaks twice. 3. Disagree with ideas, not people.

## Write the opening script
write: Write what you will say from 3:15 to 3:22: welcome the members, state the purpose, introduce the norms and explain how the club will decide.
  lines: 10
  key: Model: Welcome to the first meeting of the Green Team! Thanks for coming. Today we have one job: to choose our spring project, either planting trees at the park, running a recycling drive or cleaning up Willow Creek. We will finish by 3:55. First, let's agree on our norms. I'm proposing three: one person talks at a time, everyone talks once before anyone talks twice, and we disagree with ideas, not people. Give me a thumbs up if you can live with those. After introductions, we will hear three four-minute pitches and have time for questions. Then we will decide with ranked choice, so the project we pick is one most of us actually support.

# 21. Team Trouble Moves
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose a leadership move that fits a problem on a team.
directions: Match each team problem to the leadership move that best fixes it. Then explain why each move fits its problem.
mistakes: Students match "silence" to the round-robin, since both are about talking. A round-robin evens out who talks; writing first helps quiet people who need time to think. Ask "What is the root of the problem?"
notes: The problems come from a fictional student council committee planning a food drive. Each move fits one problem best, though good leaders mix them. In explanations, look for the link between the cause of the problem and how the move changes it.

## Warm up
text: **The committee:** Five students are planning a school food drive. Meetings are not going well.

## Main: Match the move
match: Write the letter of the best leadership move next to each problem.
  - Dana talks for most of every meeting, and others stop sharing. = Set a talking order: "Let's hear from people who haven't spoken yet."
  - When the leader asks for ideas, nobody says anything. = Give two minutes for everyone to write one idea on a sticky note, then share.
  - Posters and sign-up sheets that people promised never get made. = Make a task chart with names and due dates, and check it at the start of each meeting.
  - Talk drifts to weekend plans and the meeting runs out of time. = Post an agenda and write off-topic ideas in a "parking lot" for later.
  - Ravi and Jess argue every time about where to put the collection boxes. = Meet with both and have each restate the other's point before deciding.

## Explain each choice
write: Problem 1: Why does the move fit?
  lines: 2
  key: Dana's voice fills the space; a talking order makes room for others without attacking Dana.
write: Problem 2: Why does the move fit?
  lines: 2
  key: People may be shy or need time to think; writing first gives everyone an idea to share and lowers the pressure.
write: Problem 3: Why does the move fit?
  lines: 2
  key: Tasks were not clear or tracked; a chart with names and dates makes each person responsible and shows progress.
write: Problem 4: Why does the move fit?
  lines: 2
  key: An agenda keeps focus, and the parking lot respects other ideas without letting them take over the time.
write: Problem 5: Why does the move fit?
  lines: 2
  key: Restating shows each person they were heard, which cools the argument and helps them find a spot that works for both.

# 33. Pause, Question, Reconsider
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can slow down a snap judgment by pausing, questioning it and reconsidering.
directions: Read the example. For each scenario, use the frame: **Pause** (name the assumption), **Question** (list other explanations or facts to check) and **Reconsider** (rewrite the thought so it is fairer).
mistakes: Students often skip Pause and jump straight to a nicer thought. Naming the assumption first is the step that makes the habit work. In Question, watch for explanations that just flip the judgment ("He's actually nice"); ask for possibilities the character could check. In Reconsider, the new thought should stay open ("I don't know yet"), not swap one guess for another.
notes: All characters are fictional. Scenario 4 touches on accent bias, the tendency to judge someone's intelligence or knowledge by how they speak; an accent shows the languages a person has learned, not how well they understand a topic. If students share personal experiences of being misjudged, thank them and keep the focus on the frame.

## Warm up
example: **Scenario:** Cam says hi to a classmate in the hall, and the classmate walks past without answering. Cam thinks, "She's ignoring me."
  **Pause:** My assumption is that she ignored me on purpose.
  **Question:** Maybe she didn't hear me, she was thinking about a test, or she didn't see me. Did she look at me?
  **Reconsider:** "She might not have heard me. I'll say hi again at lunch."
choice: What is the **first** step of the frame?
  - Decide what kind of person someone is
  * Notice and name the assumption you just made
  - Ask your friends what they think

## Main: Use the frame
text: **1.** Amir, a new student, sits alone at lunch every day and doesn't talk to anyone. Kayla thinks, "He's stuck-up. He thinks he's better than us."
text: **2.** Rosa has been ten minutes late to practice three times this month. The team captain thinks, "She doesn't care about this team."
text: **3.** In group discussions, Ethan almost never speaks. Mia thinks, "He doesn't have any ideas."
text: **4.** Lucia speaks with a strong accent during her history presentation. Jordan thinks, "She probably doesn't really understand the topic."
chart: Pause: the assumption | Question: other explanations or facts to check | Reconsider: a fairer thought
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4
  height: 1.05
  key: 1. Pause: Amir is stuck-up. Question: He is new and may not know anyone, he may be shy or nervous, he may not know the school's lunch routines. Has anyone invited him to sit with them? Reconsider: "I don't know him yet. Maybe I'll invite him to sit with us." 2. Pause: Rosa doesn't care. Question: She might have a bus or ride problem, a sibling to pick up or a class that runs late. Has anyone asked her? Reconsider: "Something might be making her late. I'll ask if there is a way to help." 3. Pause: Ethan has no ideas. Question: He may like to think before he speaks, others may talk over him, or he may be unsure his idea is welcome. Has anyone asked him directly? Reconsider: "He might have ideas we haven't heard. I'll ask what he thinks." 4. Pause: An accent means she doesn't understand. Question: An accent shows the languages she speaks, not what she knows. What did her content actually say? Reconsider: "I should judge her presentation by her ideas and evidence, not her accent."

## Stretch
write: Choose one scenario. What could happen if the character never paused and acted on the assumption?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: If Kayla keeps thinking Amir is stuck-up, she might tell friends, and soon nobody invites him to sit with them. Amir stays alone, which seems to "prove" the assumption, even though it was never true.
