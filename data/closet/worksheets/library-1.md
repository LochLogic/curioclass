# 1. Title Page Treasure Hunt
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, a real picture book with a title page
ican: I can find the title, author, illustrator and publisher on a title page.
directions: (Read aloud.) A title page is inside the book, near the front. Read each title page with me. Find the title, the author, the illustrator and the publisher. Copy each one onto its line.
mistakes: Children often copy the author's name on the illustrator line. Point out the clue words: "by" or "written by" comes before the author, and "pictures by" or "illustrated by" comes before the illustrator. The publisher is usually small, at the bottom of the page.
notes: The three books are made up for this sheet, so children cannot look them up. Show a real title page first and find the four parts together. Long titles are fine to copy slowly; early writers can copy just the first word of the title and the last names. The stretch title page has an author who also drew the pictures.

## Warm up: The clue words
example: Title: the big words at the top.
  Author: the name after **by** or **written by**.
  Illustrator: the name after **pictures by** or **illustrated by**.
  Publisher: the company name at the bottom.

# 7. Five-Finger Just-Right Test
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, a real book to try the test on
ican: I can use the five-finger test to tell if a book is just right.
directions: (Read aloud.) Max is trying three books. Read each page with me. The **dark words** are words Max does not know. Hold up one finger for each dark word. Write how many fingers. Then circle: too easy, just right, or too hard for Max.
mistakes: Children may count every long word, not only the dark ones: remind them the dark words are the ones Max does not know. Some think "too easy" is bad. An easy book is great for fun reading; the test is about finding a book for learning to read better. Others count a word twice when it appears twice; on these pages each dark word appears once.
notes: The five-finger test used here: 0 or 1 unknown words on a page, too easy; 2 or 3, just right; 4 or 5, too hard for now. Some schools use slightly different cutoffs; use your school's. The three pages are original to Curio Class. Key: Book 1 has 1 dark word (meadow): too easy. Book 2 has 3 (heron, snatches, giggles): just right. Book 3 has 5 (expedition, explorers, glacier, spectacular, valley): too hard. After the sheet, children try the test on a real book from the classroom library.

## Warm up: The rule
example: Read one page. Hold up a finger for each word you do not know.
  **0 or 1 finger:** too easy. **2 or 3 fingers:** just right. **4 or 5 fingers:** too hard for now.

## Main: Max tries three books
passage: Book 1: Sam's Red Kite
  Sam has a red kite. He runs up the hill. The wind lifts the kite up, up, up! It flies over the **meadow**. Sam holds on tight. The kite dips and spins. Sam laughs and laughs. "Go, kite, go!" he yells to the sky.
fill:
  - Max holds up {1} finger.
choice: Book 1 is
  * too easy for Max.
  - just right for Max.
  - too hard for Max.
passage: Book 2: Rosa at the Pond
  Rosa and her dog walk to the pond. A **heron** stands in the water on long legs. It waits very still. Then it **snatches** a fish! The dog barks. The heron flaps its big wings and flies away. Rosa **giggles** all the way home.
fill:
  - Max holds up {3} fingers.
choice: Book 2 is
  - too easy for Max.
  * just right for Max.
  - too hard for Max.
passage: Book 3: The Ice Climb
  The **expedition** set out at dawn. The **explorers** carried heavy packs up the **glacier**. Ice cracked under their boots. At the top, they saw a **spectacular** view of the **valley** below. They rested, ate a big lunch, and took lots of pictures before they climbed back down.
fill:

choice: Book 3 is

  - just right for Max.
  * too hard for Max.

## Stretch: Which book?
write: Max wants to get better at reading. Which book should he take home? Why?
  lines: 2
  starter: Max should take Book ... because ...
  key: Book 2, because it is just right: it has a few new words to learn but Max can still read it.

# 20. Moon Landing Question Starters
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, a printed photo of the 1969 Moon landing if you have one
ican: I can use question starters to ask good questions about a picture.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at the Moon landing pictures and listen to the facts. Use each question starter to write a question you wonder about. Begin with the starter word and end with a question mark.
mistakes: Children often write a statement after the starter ("Who the astronaut.") or forget the question mark. Have them say the question out loud first, with a questioning voice. Some write the same question four times with a new first word; each starter should ask for something different (a person, a thing, a place, a reason).
notes: Facts are checked: on July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon and walked on it; Michael Collins stayed in the spacecraft circling the Moon. They set up a flag, left footprints and brought Moon rocks back to Earth. NASA photos of the landing are public domain; showing one (for example, Buzz Aldrin standing on the Moon) makes this sheet much richer. Questions are open; the key gives models. Good questions can be answered later in a book or on a trusted site, which leads into the next sheets.

## Warm up: Look and listen
picword:
  - astronaut | astronaut
  - flag | flag
  - footprint-left | footprint
  - moon-full | Moon
text: (Read aloud.) On July 20, 1969, two astronauts, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, walked on the Moon. They put up a flag and left footprints. A third astronaut, Michael Collins, flew around the Moon and waited for them.
fill:
  - The first two people to walk on the Moon were Neil Armstrong and Buzz {Aldrin}.

## Main: Write your questions
write: Who
  lines: 2
  starter: Who ...?
  key: Model: Who was the first person to step on the Moon?
write: What
  lines: 2
  starter: What ...?
  key: Model: What did the astronauts eat on the Moon?
write: Where
  lines: 2
  starter: Where ...?
  key: Model: Where did the spaceship land when they came home?
write: Why
  lines: 2
  starter: Why ...?
  key: Model: Why do the footprints stay on the Moon?

## Stretch: Best question
write: Circle your best question. Where could you find the answer?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary: a library book about the Moon landing, a grown-up, or a trusted space website.

# 21. Answer From a Diagram
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can use the labels on a diagram to answer a question.
directions: (Read aloud.) This diagram shows the parts of a tree. Each arrow points to a part, and its label names it. I will read each question. Point to the part on the diagram, then write its name on the line.
mistakes: Children may write "leaf" on the branch line because the branch is near the leaves: have them follow the arrow with a finger to its label. Some think roots are on top of the ground; point to the ground line in each picture.
notes: The four pictures show the same tree, each with one arrow and its label, so together they work like one labeled diagram. Science check: roots hold the tree in the ground and take in water; the trunk holds the tree up and carries water up to the leaves; leaves use sunlight to make food for the tree; branches grow out from the trunk and hold the leaves. Spelling help is on the diagram, so expect correct copying.

## Warm up: Read the diagram
text: (Read aloud.) Touch each arrow. Read its label with me: leaves, branch, trunk, roots.
picword:
  - tree-leaves | leaves
  - tree-branch | branch
  - tree-trunk | trunk
  - tree-roots | roots

## Main: Find the part
fill:
  - Which part grows under the ground? The {roots}
  - Which part makes food from sunlight? The {leaves}
  - Which part holds the tree up tall? The {trunk}
  - Which part grows out from the trunk and holds the leaves? A {branch}
  - Which part takes in water from the soil? The {roots}

draw: Draw a tree. Draw an arrow to one part and write its label. | 2.2

# 33. Parts Our Book Needs
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can name the parts our class book needs: cover, title page, dedication and about the authors.
directions: (Read aloud.) Our class book needs four parts. Look at each page. Use the word bank to label it. Then number the parts in the order they come in a book.
mistakes: Children often mix up the cover and the title page because both show the title. The cover is the outside; the title page is the first page inside. A dedication is short and says who the book is for. Some children label the dedication "about the authors" because both are about people; ask, "Is it about who wrote the book, or who the book is for?"
notes: Hold up a real picture book and find each part together: the cover, the title page inside, a dedication (often a short line after the title page), and an "about the author" page, usually at the back. The pages on this sheet are a model for the class book "Many Voices." Key: cover (the book picture), page A title page, page B about the authors, page C dedication. Order: cover 1, title page 2, dedication 3, about the authors 4.

## Warm up: The word bank
text: **Word bank:** cover, title page, dedication, about the authors
picword:
  - book-cover | {cover}

## Main: Label the pages

  height: 0.85

  - B | We are 22 kids in Room 12. We like soccer, art and frogs. We all wrote this book together. | {about the authors}
  - C | This book is for everyone who loves a good story. | {dedication}

## Order the parts
table: Part | Order in the book
  - about the authors | {4}

  - dedication | {3}

## Stretch: Write a dedication
write: Who should our class book be for?
  lines: 2
  starter: This book is for ...
  key: Answers vary. Example: This book is for our school and everyone who reads it.
