# 1. Claim, Evidence and Strength
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can rate the evidence for a claim as strong, weak or missing and explain why.
directions: Read each claim and the evidence given for it. Circle the rating: strong, weak or missing. Then explain your rating in one or two sentences, using the checklist in the example.
mistakes: Students often rate evidence "strong" because it comes from someone famous or because they agree with the claim. Rate the evidence, not the claim: a true claim can still have weak evidence. Others call a feeling or a single story "missing" evidence; it is evidence, just weak.
notes: All six claims and their evidence are made up for practice. The ratings in the key are the clearest reading; accept a different rating only with a strong explanation (for example, claim 4 could be called strong but limited to one class's garden). Claim 1 check: 300 kg down to 200 kg is a drop of 100 kg, one third of 300.

## Warm up: The checklist
example: **Strong** evidence: measured facts or a fair test, enough of it, and it connects directly to the claim.
  **Weak** evidence: there is some, but it is one opinion, one story, one person or one day, or a famous name instead of facts.
  **Missing** evidence: only the claim itself, or "everyone knows."
choice: "The new lunch menu is popular. Evidence: Everyone knows it is." How strong is the evidence?
  - Strong
  - Weak
  * Missing

## Main: Rate six claims
choice: Claim 1: The school's new recycling bins cut the trash we throw away by one third. Evidence: The custodian weighed the trash every week for 4 weeks before and 4 weeks after the bins arrived. It fell from about 300 kg to about 200 kg a week.
  * Strong
  - Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 1.
  lines: 2
  key: Strong: the trash was measured the same way for several weeks before and after, and a drop from 300 kg to 200 kg is one third, which matches the claim.
choice: Claim 2: Ten-minute brain breaks help students focus. Evidence: My friend said she feels more focused after a break.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 2.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: it is one person's feeling, not a measurement, and one student cannot show what happens for students in general.
choice: Claim 3: The new bike lane made Oak Street safer. Evidence: None is given. The flyer says, "Everyone can see it worked."
  - Strong
  - Weak
  * Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 3.
  lines: 2
  key: Missing: there are no crash numbers or observations, only the claim repeated and "everyone can see," which is not evidence.
choice: Claim 4: Bean plants grow taller with more sunlight. Evidence: A class grew 20 bean plants in a sunny window and 20 in a shady corner, with the same soil, water and seeds. After 3 weeks the sunny plants averaged 24 cm and the shady plants 15 cm.
  * Strong
  - Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 4.
  lines: 2
  key: Strong: it is a fair test with many plants, only sunlight changed, and the measured difference supports the claim.
choice: Claim 5: These running shoes make you faster. Evidence: A famous sprinter wears them in ads.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 5.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: a famous person in an ad is paid to appear and is not a test; the sprinter was already fast, so it shows nothing about what the shoes do.
choice: Claim 6: The library's longer hours brought in more visitors. Evidence: The librarian says it seemed busier on one Tuesday in October.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 6.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: it is one day and an impression, not a count; the door counter numbers for months before and after would be stronger.

## Stretch: Make it stronger
write: Pick claim 2, 5 or 6. Describe evidence that would make it strong.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample for claim 6: Compare the door counter's visitor totals for several months before the change with the same months after, and check that nothing else (like a new program) changed at the same time.

# 7. Deepfake Red Flags
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can spot red flags in a video that may be a deepfake and name checks that could confirm whether it is real.
directions: Read the two kinds of red flags. Then read the four video scenarios. For each, list the red flags you notice and the checks that could confirm whether the video is real. Then give your verdict. Not every video is fake.
mistakes: Students often rely only on "it looks weird." As AI video improves, the picture may look perfect, so context red flags (who posted it, when, whether trusted sources report it) matter more than glitches. Others decide Scenario 3 is fake because it is shaky and low quality; real eyewitness video is often shaky, and the checks confirm it. Push for checks that go outside the video itself.
notes: Every person, account and organization is made up and described generically (Athlete A, Mayor A). A deepfake is a video or audio clip in which a real person's face or voice has been changed or created with AI. Visual red flags fact checkers mention: lips out of sync with words, blurry or warping edges around the face and hair, odd lighting or shadows, details that change between frames (earrings, teeth, a logo), hands or backgrounds that look wrong, a flat or robotic voice. Context red flags: a brand-new or unknown account, an urgent or emotional message, a request for money or clicks, no trusted outlet reporting it. Verdicts in the key: 1 likely fake, 2 likely fake, 3 likely real, 4 likely fake.

## Warm up: Two kinds of red flags
example: **In the video:** lips out of sync, blurry edges around the face, odd shadows, details that change from frame to frame, a flat or robotic voice.
  **Around the video:** who posted it, when, and why; an urgent or emotional message; a request for money; no trusted source reporting the same thing.
choice: Which check goes outside the video itself?
  - Watching it again in slow motion
  - Turning the volume up
  * Seeing whether the person's official account or trusted news outlets report the same thing
  - Looking for blurry edges

## Scenario 1
text: A video shows a famous basketball player, Athlete A, saying, "I doubled my money in one week with this app. Click the link to start!" It was posted yesterday by a brand-new account. The player's lips seem slightly out of step with the words.
write: Red flags:
  lines: 2
  key: Brand-new account; a request to click and a money promise; lips out of sync with the words; a famous person selling a get-rich-quick app.
write: Checks that could confirm it, and your verdict:
  lines: 2
  key: Check Athlete A's official accounts and team site; search news for the endorsement; look up the app with fact checkers. Verdict: likely fake (a deepfake ad scam).

## Scenario 2
text: At 11 p.m., a video in a group chat shows Mayor A saying all schools will close tomorrow because of a gas leak. The voice sounds flat, and the mayor never blinks. The city's website and local news say nothing about it.
write: Red flags:
  lines: 2
  key: Shared late at night in a group chat with no source; an urgent message; flat voice and no blinking; no report from the city or local news.
write: Checks that could confirm it, and your verdict:
  lines: 2
  key: Check the city and school district websites and official accounts; call or check the school's alert system; look for local news. Verdict: likely fake.

## Scenario 3
text: A shaky phone video shows a tornado crossing a field near a small town. It was posted by a local resident. That afternoon, the National Weather Service confirmed a tornado touched down there, and the local TV station showed the same video with the resident's permission.
write: Red flags:
  lines: 2
  key: Few or none: it is shaky and low quality, but that is normal for eyewitness video; the poster is a real local resident.
write: Checks that could confirm it, and your verdict:
  lines: 2
  key: The National Weather Service report and the TV station confirm the event, time and place; a reverse search of a frame could show where it first appeared. Verdict: likely real.

## Scenario 4
text: A clip of a news anchor reports that an asteroid will hit Earth next week. The station logo is slightly different from the real one, the anchor's earrings change between shots, and no other news outlet or space agency mentions the asteroid.
write: Red flags:
  lines: 2
  key: Logo slightly wrong; earrings change between frames; a scary, emotional claim; no other outlet or space agency reports it.
write: Checks that could confirm it, and your verdict:
  lines: 2
  key: Check the station's real website and official accounts; check NASA or other space agencies' asteroid news; look for fact checks. Verdict: likely fake.

## Stretch
write: As AI video gets better, which kind of red flag will matter more: in the video, or around it? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Around it. Glitches like odd blinking or blurry edges will become rare as tools improve, but a fake still has to come from somewhere, so checking who posted it, whether trusted sources confirm it and why it was made will still work.

# 20. Inquiry Pivot Log
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, your research notes and sources
ican: I can log the moments when a source changed my research question and explain how my question changed.
directions: Read the four kinds of pivots and the model. Then, during your research project, log 4 moments when a source made you change your question. For each, record the source, what it showed you, the kind of pivot and your new question.
mistakes: Students often log every new fact as a pivot. A pivot is a moment the question itself changes. Others write a new question that ignores the source ("I just felt like changing it"); the new question should come from what the source showed. Some think changing a question means they failed; strong researchers pivot often.
notes: Use with any research project; students can fill it in over several sessions. The model is made up, but its facts are checked: the US Census Bureau counts many US counties that lost population since 2010, and rural hospital closures are a real and studied problem. The kinds of pivots: narrow (a smaller part), broaden (a wider view), shift (a new angle on the topic), correct (the source showed a starting idea was wrong). Grade on whether each new question follows from the source and is researchable.

## Warm up: Kinds of pivots
example: **Narrow:** focus on a smaller part. **Broaden:** take a wider view. **Shift:** look at the topic from a new angle. **Correct:** a source shows your starting idea was wrong.
  Model: Question before: Why are small towns shrinking? Source: a Census Bureau report showing many rural counties lost people, while some grew. New question: Why do some rural counties grow while others shrink? (a **correct** pivot: not all small towns are shrinking)
choice: Ravi asked, "How do bees make honey?" An article showed him that many wild bees do not make honey at all. He changed his question to "What jobs do wild bees do in nature?" What kind of pivot is this?
  - narrow
  - broaden
  * correct
  - shift
choice: Maya asked, "What causes air pollution?" A city report about asthma rates near highways led her to ask, "How does traffic pollution affect kids who live near highways?" What kind of pivot is this?
  * narrow
  - broaden
  - correct

## Main: My pivot log
write: My starting question:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Example: Why are small towns shrinking?
chart: Source (title, author or site) | What it showed me | Kind of pivot | My new question
  labels: Pivot 1; Pivot 2; Pivot 3; Pivot 4
  height: 1.1
  key: Sample path: 1 Census Bureau report; some rural counties grew; correct; Why do some rural counties grow while others shrink? 2 news story on a town that grew after a new factory; jobs pull people; narrow; How do new jobs change a rural town? 3 study on rural hospital closures; losing services pushes people out; shift; What happens to a town when its hospital closes? 4 report on rural internet access; remote work lets people move to small towns; broaden; How is technology changing where Americans choose to live?

## Look back
write: Which pivot changed your research the most? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: Pivot 1, because I found out my starting idea was wrong: not all small towns are shrinking. That made me ask why, which became my real question.
write: Write your final question. Is it better than your starting question? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: What happens to a rural town when its hospital closes? Yes: it is narrower, I can find data and stories about it, and it comes from evidence instead of my first guess.

# 21. Question Web
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, 5 colored pencils (blue, green, red, orange, purple)
ican: I can grow a web of related questions and sort them by type to plan my research.
directions: Learn the five question types and their colors. Sort the warm-up questions. Then write one starting question in the center of the web and grow 10 related questions around it. Color each one by its type, and list them in the table.
mistakes: Students often grow ten fact questions, which makes a list, not a web. Aim for at least three types. Others confuse cause and process: "How does a hurricane form?" is a process (how it happens); "Why are hurricanes getting stronger?" asks for causes.
notes: The five types used here (fact, process, cause, prediction, judgment) are a working set for this sheet, not a fixed standard; some teachers use "open and closed" or "thick and thin" questions instead. Fact questions are answered by looking something up; the others need explaining, reasoning or weighing evidence. Students can keep their webs in a research notebook and return to them when picking a project.

## Warm up: Five types
example: **Fact** (blue): who, what, when, where. When did the Titanic sink?
  **Process** (green): how something happens. How do icebergs form?
  **Cause** (red): why something happened. Why did the Titanic sink so fast?
  **Prediction** (orange): what if, what will. What if the ship had seen the iceberg sooner?
  **Judgment** (purple): should, which is best, how well. Should ships still sail in iceberg areas in spring?
fill:
  - Where do monarch butterflies spend the winter? Type: {fact}
  - How do monarchs find the same forests every year? Type: {process}
  - Why are monarch numbers lower than they were 30 years ago? Type: {cause}
  - What will happen to monarchs if milkweed keeps disappearing? Type: {prediction}
  - Should towns plant milkweed along roadsides? Type: {judgment}

## Main: Grow the web
draw: Write your starting question in a circle in the middle. Draw lines out to 10 related questions, then color each one by type. | 3.6
chart: # | My question | Type
  rows: 10
  key: Sample web for "How do bats find food in the dark?": 1 What do most bats eat? (fact) 2 Where do bats roost in the day? (fact) 3 How does echolocation work? (process) 4 How do moths try to escape bats? (process) 5 Why do some bats hibernate? (cause) 6 Why are some bat species in decline? (cause) 7 What if all the bats in an area disappeared? (prediction) 8 How will wind turbines affect bats in the future? (prediction) 9 Should farmers put up bat houses? (judgment) 10 Is echolocation better than eyesight for hunting at night? (judgment)

## Stretch: Pick a path
write: Which colors does your web have the most of? Which question would make the best research project, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer notices if the web leans on fact questions, and picks a cause, prediction or judgment question because it needs evidence from several sources and cannot be answered in one line.

# 33. Research Ethics Cases
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can decide whether a researcher used sources and data ethically and explain how to fix a problem.
directions: Read each case. Decide whether the student acted ethically. If not, name the problem and explain how to fix it. If yes, explain what the student did right.
mistakes: Students often decide by whether the student "got caught" or whether the result was good. Ethics is about honesty and fairness to sources, readers and the people studied. Others call every use of AI cheating; Case 3 shows an approved use that the student reported. Case 5 is tricky: leaving out evidence that disagrees misleads readers even when every fact shown is true.
notes: All students and cases are made up. Key: Case 1 not ethical (changing data is falsification); Case 2 ethical (charts made by US federal agencies are usually in the public domain, and she gave clear credit; with a copyrighted chart, one chart on one slide of a class presentation, credited, is a likely fair use); Case 3 ethical (the teacher allowed AI for brainstorming search terms, and she logged it); Case 4 not ethical (sharing a person's name and health details without consent); Case 5 not ethical (cherry-picking). AI rules differ by school; tell students your own class policy. Good fixes: report the real data and explain it; ask permission and remove identifying details; acknowledge and answer the opposing studies.

## Warm up
choice: Which is an example of falsifying data?
  - Reporting a survey result you did not expect
  * Changing survey answers so the results look stronger
  - Leaving a blank survey out because it was not filled in, and saying so
  - Rounding 47.6% to 48%

## The cases
text: **Case 1.** Leo surveyed 30 classmates about later school start times. Only 14 wanted a later start, so he changed 3 answers to make it a majority.
text: **Case 2.** Priya put a chart from a government website on one slide of her class presentation, with the title, the agency's name and the link under it.
text: **Case 3.** Mei's teacher allowed AI for brainstorming search terms. Mei asked a chatbot for keywords, used them in the library database, and noted it in her research log. She wrote the paper herself from her sources.
text: **Case 4.** Jordan interviewed a neighbor about living with diabetes. He quoted her by full name, with details of her medicine, in a report posted on the school website. He did not ask whether he could use her name.
text: **Case 5.** Ava's paper argues that video games improve memory. She found five studies: three agreed, two did not. She mentioned only the three that agreed.
table: Case | Ethical? | The problem (or what was done right) | How to fix it
  - 1 | {no} | {falsified data} | {report the real result, 14 of 30, and discuss it honestly}
  - 2 | {yes} | {used one chart for a class project and gave clear credit} | {nothing to fix}
  - 3 | {yes} | {followed the class rule and recorded the AI use} | {nothing to fix}
  - 4 | {no} | {shared private health details and a name without consent} | {ask permission first; use no name or a changed name and remove details}
  - 5 | {no} | {cherry-picked evidence; hid the studies that disagree} | {include all five and explain why the evidence is mixed}
  height: 0.65

## Think about it
write: Case 5 uses only true facts. Explain why it is still unethical.
  lines: 3
  key: Leaving out the two studies that disagree gives readers a false picture of what the research shows; they think the evidence is all on one side, so they are misled even though each fact is true.
write: Case 4's report was about a real problem. Why does consent still matter?
  lines: 2
  key: The neighbor's health information is private; she should decide who knows it. Posting her name online could harm or embarrass her, no matter how good the report is.

## Stretch
write: Write one rule for ethical research that would have prevented two of the problems above. Name the cases.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Show your readers the whole truth about your evidence and your sources. This would prevent Case 1 (changing data) and Case 5 (hiding studies). Another: Get permission and protect people's privacy (Case 4).
