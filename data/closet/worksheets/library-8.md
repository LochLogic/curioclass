# 1. Claim, Evidence and Strength
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can rate the evidence for a claim as strong, weak or missing and explain why.
directions: Read each claim and the evidence given for it. Circle the rating: strong, weak or missing. Then explain your rating in one or two sentences, using the checklist in the example.
mistakes: Students often rate evidence "strong" because it comes from someone famous or because they agree with the claim. Rate the evidence, not the claim: a true claim can still have weak evidence. Others call a feeling or a single story "missing" evidence; it is evidence, just weak.
notes: All six claims and their evidence are made up for practice. The ratings in the key are the clearest reading; accept a different rating only with a strong explanation (for example, claim 4 could be called strong but limited to one class's garden). Claim 1 check: 300 kg down to 200 kg is a drop of 100 kg, one third of 300.

## Warm up: The checklist
example: **Strong** evidence: measured facts or a fair test, enough of it, and it connects directly to the claim.
  **Weak** evidence: there is some, but it is one opinion, one story, one person or one day, or a famous name instead of facts.
  **Missing** evidence: only the claim itself, or "everyone knows."
choice: "The new lunch menu is popular. Evidence: Everyone knows it is." How strong is the evidence?
  - Strong
  - Weak
  * Missing

## Main: Rate six claims
choice: Claim 1: The school's new recycling bins cut the trash we throw away by one third. Evidence: The custodian weighed the trash every week for 4 weeks before and 4 weeks after the bins arrived. It fell from about 300 kg to about 200 kg a week.
  * Strong
  - Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 1.
  lines: 2
  key: Strong: the trash was measured the same way for several weeks before and after, and a drop from 300 kg to 200 kg is one third, which matches the claim.
choice: Claim 2: Ten-minute brain breaks help students focus. Evidence: My friend said she feels more focused after a break.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 2.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: it is one person's feeling, not a measurement, and one student cannot show what happens for students in general.
choice: Claim 3: The new bike lane made Oak Street safer. Evidence: None is given. The flyer says, "Everyone can see it worked."
  - Strong
  - Weak
  * Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 3.
  lines: 2
  key: Missing: there are no crash numbers or observations, only the claim repeated and "everyone can see," which is not evidence.
choice: Claim 4: Bean plants grow taller with more sunlight. Evidence: A class grew 20 bean plants in a sunny window and 20 in a shady corner, with the same soil, water and seeds. After 3 weeks the sunny plants averaged 24 cm and the shady plants 15 cm.
  * Strong
  - Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 4.
  lines: 2
  key: Strong: it is a fair test with many plants, only sunlight changed, and the measured difference supports the claim.
choice: Claim 5: These running shoes make you faster. Evidence: A famous sprinter wears them in ads.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 5.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: a famous person in an ad is paid to appear and is not a test; the sprinter was already fast, so it shows nothing about what the shoes do.
choice: Claim 6: The library's longer hours brought in more visitors. Evidence: The librarian says it seemed busier on one Tuesday in October.
  - Strong
  * Weak
  - Missing
write: Explain your rating for claim 6.
  lines: 2
  key: Weak: it is one day and an impression, not a count; the door counter numbers for months before and after would be stronger.

## Stretch: Make it stronger
write: Pick claim 2, 5 or 6. Describe evidence that would make it strong.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample for claim 6: Compare the door counter's visitor totals for several months before the change with the same months after, and check that nothing else (like a new program) changed at the same time.

# 21. Question Web
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, 5 colored pencils (blue, green, red, orange, purple)
ican: I can grow a web of related questions and sort them by type to plan my research.
directions: Learn the five question types and their colors. Sort the warm-up questions. Then write one starting question in the center of the web and grow 10 related questions around it. Color each one by its type, and list them in the table.
mistakes: Students often grow ten fact questions, which makes a list, not a web. Aim for at least three types. Others confuse cause and process: "How does a hurricane form?" is a process (how it happens); "Why are hurricanes getting stronger?" asks for causes.
notes: The five types used here (fact, process, cause, prediction, judgment) are a working set for this sheet, not a fixed standard; some teachers use "open and closed" or "thick and thin" questions instead. Fact questions are answered by looking something up; the others need explaining, reasoning or weighing evidence. Students can keep their webs in a research notebook and return to them when picking a project.

## Warm up: Five types
example: **Fact** (blue): who, what, when, where. When did the Titanic sink?
  **Process** (green): how something happens. How do icebergs form?
  **Cause** (red): why something happened. Why did the Titanic sink so fast?
  **Prediction** (orange): what if, what will. What if the ship had seen the iceberg sooner?
  **Judgment** (purple): should, which is best, how well. Should ships still sail in iceberg areas in spring?
fill:
  - Where do monarch butterflies spend the winter? Type: {fact}
  - How do monarchs find the same forests every year? Type: {process}
  - Why are monarch numbers lower than they were 30 years ago? Type: {cause}
  - What will happen to monarchs if milkweed keeps disappearing? Type: {prediction}
  - Should towns plant milkweed along roadsides? Type: {judgment}

## Main: Grow the web
draw: Write your starting question in a circle in the middle. Draw lines out to 10 related questions, then color each one by type. | 3.6
chart: # | My question | Type
  rows: 10
  key: Sample web for "How do bats find food in the dark?": 1 What do most bats eat? (fact) 2 Where do bats roost in the day? (fact) 3 How does echolocation work? (process) 4 How do moths try to escape bats? (process) 5 Why do some bats hibernate? (cause) 6 Why are some bat species in decline? (cause) 7 What if all the bats in an area disappeared? (prediction) 8 How will wind turbines affect bats in the future? (prediction) 9 Should farmers put up bat houses? (judgment) 10 Is echolocation better than eyesight for hunting at night? (judgment)

## Stretch: Pick a path
write: Which colors does your web have the most of? Which question would make the best research project, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer notices if the web leans on fact questions, and picks a cause, prediction or judgment question because it needs evidence from several sources and cannot be answered in one line.
