# 1. Lateral Reading Practice
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can read laterally, checking what other sources say about a website, to decide whether to trust it.
directions: Read the website summary. Then read the three outside sources about it, the way a fact checker opens new tabs instead of staying on one page. Answer the questions and decide whether the site is trustworthy.
mistakes: Students often judge the site by how it looks or by its own About page ("dedicated to pet wellness" sounds trustworthy). The point of lateral reading is that a site's description of itself is the least useful clue. Others decide the claim is false just because a company is involved: the stronger reason is that independent experts (Source B) disagree and the site shows no evidence.
notes: The website, the newspaper, the veterinary school, the consumer group and the company are all made up, and the companies have letter names so they cannot be confused with real brands for this sheet. The veterinary advice in Source B reflects common guidance from veterinarians: most healthy dogs digest grains well, true grain allergies are uncommon, and diet changes are best made with a veterinarian. Professional fact checkers call this method lateral reading: leave the site quickly and see what trusted sources say about it.

## The website
source: Summary of petinstitute-q.example.org, home page (made up)
  Headline: "Grain-Free Is the Only Healthy Choice for Dogs." The page says grains are "cheap fillers that harm dogs" and that "our experts agree every dog should eat grain-free." No experts are named. There is no date. A big button says "Shop Our Favorite Foods." The About page says the institute is "a group dedicated to pet wellness."

## Three outside sources
source: Source A: The Millbrook Ledger (a made-up newspaper), "Who Is Behind Pet Advice Sites?", March 2026
  The Pet Institute Q website is owned by Company G, a pet food company that sells grain-free dog food. The "Shop Our Favorite Foods" button links only to Company G products.
source: Source B: State University College of Veterinary Medicine (made up), "Pet Health Basics: Grains in Dog Food"
  Most healthy dogs digest grains well, and grains can be part of a balanced diet. True grain allergies in dogs are uncommon. A grain-free diet is not needed for most dogs. Talk with your veterinarian before changing your dog's food.
source: Source C: Consumer Group W (a made-up nonprofit), profile of the Pet Institute Q
  Founded in 2020. The site lists no veterinarians or scientists on its staff. Its articles do not cite studies.

  - Yes, because it looks professional and has an About page
  - Yes, because it says experts agree
  - We cannot know anything without visiting the site for an hour

  lines: 3

## Stretch

# 7. Altered Image Scenarios
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can decide whether an image was altered, cropped or taken out of context, and name a way to check.
directions: Read the three definitions. Then read each scenario about an image shared online. Decide which problem it shows, write the clue that tells you, and name one way to check.
mistakes: Students often call every misleading image "fake" or "photoshopped." Only an altered image has had its pixels changed. A cropped image is real but cut to hide part of the scene, and an out-of-context image is real and complete but paired with a false caption about when, where or what. Others think a reverse image search proves an image is true; it shows where else the image has appeared, which is a clue, not a verdict.
notes: All five scenarios, people and accounts are made up, though each follows a common pattern fact checkers report (a shark pasted into a flood photo is a hoax that resurfaces in many hurricanes). Answers: 1 altered, 2 cropped, 3 out of context, 4 cropped, 5 altered. Ways to check: reverse image search to find the earliest and original version; find the original photographer or news outlet; compare with other photos of the same event; check the date and location; look closely at edges, shadows, lighting and reflections. Accept any reasonable check.

## Warm up: Three ways an image can mislead
example: **Altered:** the image itself was changed: something added, removed or edited.
  **Cropped:** the image is real, but its edges were cut to hide part of the scene.
  **Out of context:** the whole image is real and unchanged, but the caption is wrong about when, where or what.
choice: A real photo from a 2015 parade is shared with the caption "Huge crowd at today's parade!" Which problem is this?
  - altered
  - cropped
  * out of context
choice: What does a reverse image search do?
  - Proves an image is real
  * Shows where else the image has appeared online, including earlier versions
  - Removes edits from an image
  - Tells you who is in the photo

## Main: Five scenarios
text: **1.** During a hurricane, a photo spreads of a shark swimming down a flooded highway. A reverse image search finds the same highway photo from two years earlier, with no shark, and the shark matches a well-known ocean photo.
text: **2.** A post shows a photo of a few dozen people at a town concert, captioned "Nobody showed up." The photographer's full photo shows the same people in front of a crowd of thousands.
text: **3.** A photo of flooded streets is shared as "Our city right now!" The image is unchanged, but a news site published it six years ago, from a flood in another country.
text: **4.** A photo shows a hiker standing alone at the very edge of a cliff, captioned "No railings, no rules!" The park's own photo of the same spot shows a wide safety railing and a viewing deck just outside the frame.
text: **5.** A photo shows a student holding a giant first-place trophy at a science fair. The school's photo of the same moment shows the student holding a paper certificate, and the edges around the trophy look blurry.
table: Scenario | Altered, cropped or out of context? | Clue | One way to check
  - 1 | {altered} | {shark added to an older photo} | {reverse image search; find both originals}
  - 2 | {cropped} | {full photo shows a big crowd} | {find the photographer's full photo}
  - 3 | {out of context} | {same photo, six years old, another country} | {check date and place with a reverse image search}
  - 4 | {cropped} | {railing and deck just outside the frame} | {compare with the park's own photos}
  - 5 | {altered} | {trophy pasted in; blurry edges} | {compare with the school's original photo}
  height: 0.6

## Think about it
write: Which kind of problem do you think is hardest to catch, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: Out of context, because the image is real and has not been changed, so looking closely at it shows nothing wrong; you have to search for where and when it first appeared.
write: Scenario 2's caption and image are both "real." Explain how the post still misleads.
  lines: 2
  key: Cutting out the crowd hides the part of the scene that matters, so the caption's claim seems true when the full photo shows the opposite.

## Stretch
write: Write two questions you will ask before sharing a surprising image.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Where did this image first appear, and who took it? Do trusted news sources show the same event, at the same time and place?

# 20. Inquiry Path Log
minutes: 80
materials: pencil, this log, your research sources
ican: I can keep a log of my research path: what I searched, what I found, and the new questions it raised.
directions: Keep this log over 4 research sessions. Each session, record where you searched and the words you used, the most useful thing you found (with its source), and the new question it raised. At the end, look back at your path.
mistakes: Students often log only the topic ("searched sharks") instead of the exact words and the place they searched, so they cannot retrace or improve a search. Others write a new question that is not connected to what they found; each new question should grow out of the finding. Check that every finding names its source.
notes: Plan for 4 sessions of about 20 minutes each. Use this log with any research topic. The model row is made up for practice; the shark fact in it is checked: some sharks, such as the Greenland shark, live for centuries, and scientists have estimated ages using the eye lens. Grade the log on completeness (all columns, every session), specific search words and sources, and new questions that follow from the findings. The reflection asks students to compare their first and last questions to see how the inquiry grew.

## Warm up: A model session
example: Session 1. **Searched:** library database, "shark lifespan." **Found:** Some sharks, like the Greenland shark, may live for hundreds of years (from a science magazine article, 2023). **New question:** How do scientists figure out how old a shark is? **Next step:** search "how scientists measure shark age."

  - a lot of websites

# 21. Question Ladder
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can climb from fact questions to how and why questions that lead to deeper research.
directions: Read the article. Then climb the ladder from the bottom rung up: write one question for each rung, and answer the lower rungs from the article. The top rungs will need more research.
mistakes: Students often write a "why" question that the article already answers in one sentence, so it is really a fact question. A good top-rung question cannot be answered by pointing to one line; it needs reasons from several sources. Others skip answering the bottom rungs: the answers show they read closely before climbing.
notes: The article is original to Curio Class (about 300 words). Facts are checked: a honey bee colony has one queen, thousands of female workers and, in warm months, male drones; workers turn nectar into honey by adding enzymes and fanning it until much of the water evaporates; honeycomb cells are six-sided and made of wax; foragers share where food is with the waggle dance, decoded by Karl von Frisch, who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize; many crops depend on bees and other pollinators. Summer colonies often hold tens of thousands of bees. Rungs 1 and 2 have answers in the article; rungs 3 to 5 have sample questions in the key.

## Read
passage: Inside the Hive
  A honey bee hive is a busy city. In summer, a single colony can hold tens of thousands of bees, but only one of them is the queen. Her job is to lay eggs, sometimes more than a thousand in a day. Most of the other bees are workers, and all of them are female. A smaller number of male bees, called drones, live in the hive during the warm months.
  Workers do almost every job. Young workers clean cells, feed the young and build honeycomb from wax that their own bodies make. The cells of the comb are six-sided, a shape that packs tightly with no wasted space. Older workers fly out to gather nectar and pollen from flowers.
  Honey begins as nectar, a thin, sugary liquid. Back at the hive, workers pass the nectar from bee to bee, adding enzymes that change the sugars. They store it in the comb and fan it with their wings until most of the water evaporates. Then they cap each cell with wax. The thick, sweet result is honey, the colony's food for winter.
  How does a forager tell her sisters where the flowers are? She dances. In the waggle dance, the direction of her run on the comb shows the direction of the food compared to the sun, and the length of her waggle shows how far away it is. A scientist named Karl von Frisch spent years decoding this dance and shared a Nobel Prize in 1973 for his work on animal behavior.
  As bees move from flower to flower, pollen sticks to their fuzzy bodies and rubs off on the next flower. This pollination helps plants make fruit and seeds. Apples, almonds, blueberries and many other foods depend on bees and other pollinators.

text: **Rung 5 (top), what if or should:** a question about the future or a choice people must make.
write: My rung 5 question:
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: What would happen to farms if honey bee colonies kept shrinking? / Should towns plant more wildflowers for bees?

write: My rung 4 question:
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Why are some honey bee colonies dying out? / Why did bees evolve a dance instead of another way to share directions?
text: **Rung 3, how:** a question about a process.
write: My rung 3 question:
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: How does a new queen take over a hive? / How do bees keep the hive warm in winter?
text: **Rung 2, when or where:** a fact question the article answers. Write the question and its answer.
write: My rung 2 question and answer:
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: When do drones live in the hive? In the warm months. / Where do workers store honey? In the wax comb.
text: **Rung 1 (bottom), who or what:** a fact question the article answers. Write the question and its answer.
write: My rung 1 question and answer:
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Who lays the eggs in a hive? The queen. / What is honey made from? Nectar.

## Check the ladder
choice: Which question belongs on rung 1?
  * What is the waggle dance?
  - Why might bee colonies be shrinking?
  - Should cities keep beehives on rooftops?
  - How would farming change without pollinators?
write: Which rung's question would make the best start for a research project? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Rung 4 or 5, because those questions cannot be answered by one line in one article; they need reasons and evidence from several sources, which is what a research project explores.

# 33. Research Calendar
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a school calendar
ican: I can plan a research project on a calendar with checkpoints for questions, notes, drafting and revision.
directions: First place Ana's checkpoints on her 20-day timeline and answer the questions. Then plan your own 4-week research calendar: write a task for each school day and star the checkpoints.
mistakes: Students often save all the writing for the last few days and leave no time to revise. A good plan spreads the work out and leaves at least a few days between the draft and the final. Others plan research to go on until the draft is due; notes should be mostly done before drafting begins, with a day or two for filling gaps.
notes: Ana's plan is made up for practice. Her checkpoints by school day: 2 question approved, 5 at least four sources found, 9 notes check, 11 outline, 14 full draft, 16 peer review, 18 revision, 20 final paper and works cited. The calendar key shows a model 4-week plan; accept any plan that keeps the four checkpoints in order (question, notes, draft, revision) and leaves revision time before the due date. If your school calendar has a break, have students cross out those days first.
