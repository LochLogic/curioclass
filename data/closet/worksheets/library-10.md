# 1. Questions That Last
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify the enduring question a historical speech addresses and explain why it still matters.
directions: Read each excerpt and its background. Highlight the words that point to a big, lasting question. Answer the questions, then name the enduring question each speaker is wrestling with.
mistakes: Students often write the topic ("the Civil War," "voting") instead of a question. An enduring question is open and could be asked in any era: "Who counts as 'the people'?" rather than "Did women get the vote?" Others write a question the speaker already answers; the enduring question is the one beneath the argument.
notes: All three speeches are in the public domain. Excerpt 1 follows the Bliss copy of the Gettysburg Address (November 19, 1863). Excerpt 2 is from the speech Susan B. Anthony gave in 1873 after her arrest for voting in the 1872 election, as printed in her published text; check the wording against a scan before printing. Excerpt 3 is from Franklin D. Roosevelt's first inaugural address, March 4, 1933, cut at the end of the clause (marked [...]). Model enduring questions are in the key; accept others that are open, lasting and supported by the text.

## Background
text: **1.** Abraham Lincoln spoke at the dedication of a soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, about four and a half months after the battle there. **2.** Susan B. Anthony was arrested for voting in the 1872 election, when women could not legally vote; before her trial in 1873, she gave this speech in towns across her county. **3.** Franklin D. Roosevelt took office as president in the depths of the Great Depression, when about one in four American workers had no job.

## Read: Three excerpts
source: Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address, Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, November 19, 1863
  [1] "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. Now we are engaged in a great civil war, testing whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure."
source: Susan B. Anthony, speech after her arrest for voting, Monroe County, New York, 1873
  [2] "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union."
source: Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, Washington, D.C., March 4, 1933
  [3] "So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself [...]"

## Questions
choice: In excerpt 1, what does Lincoln say the Civil War is "testing"?
  - Whether the Union army is stronger than the Confederate army
  - Whether Pennsylvania will stay in the Union
  * Whether a nation founded on liberty and equality can last
  - Whether the Declaration of Independence was written correctly
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 1, and explain why it was urgent in 1863.
  lines: 3
  key: Can a nation built on liberty and equality survive? In 1863 the country was at war over slavery and union, so whether a democracy could hold together was being decided on battlefields.
choice: In excerpt 2, why does Anthony repeat "we, the people" three ways?
  * To narrow it step by step and show that "the people" must mean everyone, not only white men or men
  - To list the three branches of government
  - To show that she agrees with the people who arrested her
  - To quote three different founders
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 2. Give one example of how Americans have asked it since 1873.
  lines: 3
  key: Who counts as "the people," with full rights as citizens? Since then it has been asked in the fight for the 19th Amendment (1920), the voting rights movement and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and debates over the voting age (lowered to 18 in 1971).
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 3. Why would a leader raise it during a depression?
  lines: 3
  key: How should people face fear in hard times? With banks failing and millions out of work, panic itself made things worse (people pulled money from banks), so Roosevelt argued that fear was the greatest danger.

## Compare
write: Which of the three questions do you think matters most today? Support your answer with a current example.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks one question, connects it to a specific current debate (for example, who may vote, or how a country stays united when divided), and explains why it is still unresolved.

## Stretch
write: Write your own enduring question that connects at least two of the excerpts.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: What must citizens do to keep a democracy alive in a crisis? (connects Lincoln and Roosevelt) / Does a nation keep its founding promises to all of its people? (connects Lincoln and Anthony)

# 21. Lateral Reading for Authority
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can check an unfamiliar source's authority by reading what other sources say about its author and publisher.
directions: Read each source excerpt, then the outside information a fact checker found about it. Rate each source's authority on this topic as strong, moderate or weak, and justify each rating with specific evidence from the outside information.
mistakes: Students often accept a title like "Dr." as proof of authority. Authority is topic-specific: a doctorate in marketing gives no special authority on water chemistry. Others rate the journalist as weak because she is not a scientist; a careful science reporter who names her experts and links to studies can be a solid source, though she is one step removed from the research.
notes: Every source, person, organization and fact on this sheet is made up for practice; the topic (microplastics in drinking water) is real and still being studied, so the sheet does not ask students to settle the science. Ratings in the key are the clearest reading: Source 1 weak, Source 2 strong, Source 3 moderate. Accept moderate-to-strong for Source 3 with a solid reason. Authority here means expertise on this topic plus a track record that others in the field recognize.

## Warm up
choice: Which question best tests a source's authority on a topic?
  - Does the website look professional?
  - Does the source agree with what I already think?
  - Is the source easy to read?
  * Does the author have recognized expertise in this specific topic, according to others?

## Source 1
source: Dr. Ellen Marsh, "Microplastics: Much Ado About Nothing," Policy Review P (made up), 2025
  [1] "Tiny plastic particles in water have been blown out of proportion. There is no reason for families to worry, and new rules on plastic would cost jobs."
text: **What a fact checker found:** A university faculty page lists Marsh's doctorate as being in marketing. Policy Review P is published by the Trade Group T (made up), a trade group whose members make plastic packaging. The article cites no studies.

## Source 2
source: Great Lakes Water Lab (made up), "Microplastic Levels in Great Lakes Tap Water, 2024," a state university report
  [2] "We found microplastic particles in 34 of 40 tap water samples. Health effects at these levels are not yet known, and more study is needed."
text: **What a fact checker found:** The lab is part of a state university. Its director is an environmental chemist with more than 40 published research papers. The report was reviewed by other scientists before it was published in a research journal. The state environmental agency cites it. It was paid for by state and federal research grants.

## Source 3
source: Jordan Lee, "What We Know, and Don't, About Microplastics," Harbor Point Courier (made up), 2025
  [3] "Researchers agree that microplastics are widespread. They disagree about how much harm they cause. Here is what three scientists told us."
text: **What a fact checker found:** Lee has reported on the environment for 12 years. The newspaper publishes a corrections policy and has corrected errors in her stories when found. The article names three university researchers and links to their studies.

## Rate each source
table: Source | Authority on this topic | Best evidence for your rating
  - 1. Marsh | {weak} | {marketing degree, not science; published by a plastics trade group; cites no studies}
  - 2. Water Lab | {strong} | {expert chemist; peer reviewed; cited by state agency; public research funding}
  - 3. Lee | {moderate} | {experienced reporter, names experts and links studies, but not a scientist herself}
write: Source 1's author is called "Dr." Why doesn't that settle her authority?
  lines: 2
  key: Her doctorate is in marketing, not a science related to water or plastics, so it gives her no special expertise on whether microplastics are harmful.
write: Besides expertise, what else in the outside information should make a reader cautious about Source 1?
  lines: 2
  key: It is published by a plastics trade group, which benefits if people believe microplastics are harmless and plastic rules are unnecessary (a conflict of interest), and it cites no studies.
write: Source 2 says health effects "are not yet known." Does admitting uncertainty weaken or strengthen its authority? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: It strengthens it: the lab reports only what its data show and says what is still unknown, which is what careful experts do; Source 1's confident "no reason to worry" goes beyond any evidence it gives.
choice: You can quote only one source for a fact about how common microplastics are in tap water. Which is best?
  - Source 1
  * Source 2
  - Source 3

## Stretch
write: When would Source 3 be more useful to you than Source 2?
  lines: 3
  key: When you need an overview of what several experts think and where they disagree, explained in plain language, or leads to more studies; Source 2 is better for one specific, original finding.
