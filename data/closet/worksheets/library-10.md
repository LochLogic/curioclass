# 1. Questions That Last
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify the enduring question a historical speech addresses and explain why it still matters.
directions: Read each excerpt and its background. Highlight the words that point to a big, lasting question. Answer the questions, then name the enduring question each speaker is wrestling with.
mistakes: Students often write the topic ("the Civil War," "voting") instead of a question. An enduring question is open and could be asked in any era: "Who counts as 'the people'?" rather than "Did women get the vote?" Others write a question the speaker already answers; the enduring question is the one beneath the argument.
notes: All three speeches are in the public domain. Excerpt 1 follows the Bliss copy of the Gettysburg Address (November 19, 1863). Excerpt 2 is from the speech Susan B. Anthony gave in 1873 after her arrest for voting in the 1872 election, as printed in her published text; check the wording against a scan before printing. Excerpt 3 is from Franklin D. Roosevelt's first inaugural address, March 4, 1933, cut at the end of the clause (marked [...]). Model enduring questions are in the key; accept others that are open, lasting and supported by the text.

## Background
text: **1.** Abraham Lincoln spoke at the dedication of a soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, about four and a half months after the battle there. **2.** Susan B. Anthony was arrested for voting in the 1872 election, when women could not legally vote; before her trial in 1873, she gave this speech in towns across her county. **3.** Franklin D. Roosevelt took office as president in the depths of the Great Depression, when about one in four American workers had no job.

## Read: Three excerpts
source: Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address, Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, November 19, 1863
  [1] "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. Now we are engaged in a great civil war, testing whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure."
source: Susan B. Anthony, speech after her arrest for voting, Monroe County, New York, 1873
  [2] "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union."
source: Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, Washington, D.C., March 4, 1933
  [3] "So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself [...]"

## Questions
choice: In excerpt 1, what does Lincoln say the Civil War is "testing"?
  - Whether the Union army is stronger than the Confederate army
  - Whether Pennsylvania will stay in the Union
  * Whether a nation founded on liberty and equality can last
  - Whether the Declaration of Independence was written correctly
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 1, and explain why it was urgent in 1863.
  lines: 3
  key: Can a nation built on liberty and equality survive? In 1863 the country was at war over slavery and union, so whether a democracy could hold together was being decided on battlefields.
choice: In excerpt 2, why does Anthony repeat "we, the people" three ways?
  * To narrow it step by step and show that "the people" must mean everyone, not only white men or men
  - To list the three branches of government
  - To show that she agrees with the people who arrested her
  - To quote three different founders
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 2. Give one example of how Americans have asked it since 1873.
  lines: 3
  key: Who counts as "the people," with full rights as citizens? Since then it has been asked in the fight for the 19th Amendment (1920), the voting rights movement and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and debates over the voting age (lowered to 18 in 1971).
write: Name the enduring question in excerpt 3. Why would a leader raise it during a depression?
  lines: 3
  key: How should people face fear in hard times? With banks failing and millions out of work, panic itself made things worse (people pulled money from banks), so Roosevelt argued that fear was the greatest danger.

## Compare
write: Which of the three questions do you think matters most today? Support your answer with a current example.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks one question, connects it to a specific current debate (for example, who may vote, or how a country stays united when divided), and explains why it is still unresolved.

## Stretch
write: Write your own enduring question that connects at least two of the excerpts.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: What must citizens do to keep a democracy alive in a crisis? (connects Lincoln and Roosevelt) / Does a nation keep its founding promises to all of its people? (connects Lincoln and Anthony)

# 5. Enduring Questions Check
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can refine a question into an enduring one, connect a past issue to today, and judge how researchable a question is.
directions: Part 1: revise a weak question into an enduring question. Part 2: read about a past issue and connect its enduring question to today. Part 3: rank three questions from most to least researchable and explain your ranking.
mistakes: In Part 1, students often make the question longer instead of deeper; an enduring question is open, lasting and arguable, not just wordier. In Part 3, students often rank the biggest question as most researchable. Researchable means evidence exists to answer it in the time and sources available: a big philosophical question can be researched through arguments, but a precise question with data behind it is easier to answer well.
notes: Facts are checked. San Francisco passed an ordinance requiring masks in public in October 1918, during the influenza pandemic; it lapsed and was brought back, and in January 1919 opponents formed the Anti-Mask League. Many sources report that Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from under 7 percent in 1964 to about 60 percent by 1967 after the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (US Commission on Civil Rights figures); students do not need the numbers to rank the question. Ranking in the key: B (most), A, C (least). Accept A first only with a strong reason tied to available evidence.

## Warm up
choice: Which is an enduring question?
  - When was the printing press invented?
  - Was the printing press important?
  * How do new ways of sharing information change who holds power?
  - Which is better, books or e-books?

## Part 1: Refine a question
text: **Weak question:** Was the Erie Canal a good idea?
write: Explain what makes this question weak.
  lines: 2
  key: It asks for a yes-or-no judgment about one project and points to no lasting issue; it is easy to answer with a quick opinion and does not invite argument across time.
write: Rewrite it as an enduring question that the Erie Canal could help answer.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Who should pay for projects that connect a nation, and who benefits from them? / How do new transportation routes change which places grow and which decline?

## Part 2: Link one issue to today
source: Background (adapted from city records and newspaper accounts)
  In October 1918, as influenza spread through San Francisco, the city passed a law requiring people to wear masks in public. The law lapsed and was brought back as cases rose again. In January 1919, a group called the Anti-Mask League formed to fight it, arguing that the rule went too far.
write: Name the enduring question behind this conflict.
  lines: 2
  key: How much may a government limit individual freedom to protect public health? / When does the common good outweigh personal choice?
write: Connect the question to a debate in the last few years. Name the debate and explain the link.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: During the COVID-19 pandemic, communities argued over mask rules and school closings; like San Francisco in 1918, people weighed individual freedom against protecting others, and courts and voters had to decide where the limits are.

## Part 3: Rank by researchability
text: **A.** Is it ever right to break the law? **B.** How did the Voting Rights Act of 1965 change Black voter registration in Mississippi? **C.** What will schools look like in 100 years?
table: Rank | Question letter | Reason
  - 1 (most researchable) | {B} | {specific place, time and law; data and primary sources exist}
  - 2 | {A} | {researchable through philosophy, history and court cases, but too broad to settle}
  - 3 (least researchable) | {C} | {about the future; no evidence yet, only predictions}
  height: 0.55

## Stretch
write: Question B is researchable but not enduring. Write an enduring question it could help answer.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: What does it take for a democracy to make voting rights real for everyone? / Can laws change who holds power in a community?

# 15. Copyright and Music Sampling
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how copyright law treats music sampling and weigh the rights of original creators against new ones.
directions: Read the passage about music sampling and copyright. Highlight each court decision. Then answer the five questions, using evidence from the passage.
mistakes: Students often think only the songwriter must give permission for a sample. A recording usually carries two copyrights, the composition and the sound recording, so a sample of a record can need permission from both. Others think "it was only two seconds" settles the matter; courts have disagreed about very short samples.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 500 words). Facts are checked against the court opinions: Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. Records (S.D.N.Y. 1991) found that a rap album's unlicensed sample of the 1972 song "Alone Again (Naturally)" infringed; Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films (6th Cir. 2005) held that even a two-second guitar chord, lowered in pitch and looped, needed a license; VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone (9th Cir. 2016) held that a horn hit of less than a quarter second was too small to infringe; Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music (1994) held that a commercial parody can be fair use. The passage paraphrases the courts; it does not quote them. Federal copyright for sound recordings began with recordings fixed on or after February 15, 1972.

## Read
passage: Borrowed Beats | numbered
  In the 1970s, DJs at parties in the Bronx, New York, discovered that crowds went wild for the short instrumental "breaks" in funk and soul records. Using two turntables and two copies of the same record, a DJ could repeat a break again and again. That idea grew into hip hop, and when digital samplers arrived in the 1980s, musicians could capture any slice of a recording and build a new song on top of it. This practice is called sampling.
  Sampling raised a hard question: who owns a sound? Under US law, a recorded song usually carries two separate copyrights. One protects the composition, the melody and lyrics, which usually belongs to the songwriter or a music publisher. The other protects the sound recording itself, the particular performance captured on the record, which usually belongs to the record label. A producer who samples a record may need permission, called "clearing" the sample, from both owners, and often pays a fee or a share of the profits.
  In the 1980s, many artists sampled freely. That changed in 1991. In Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. Records, a federal judge ruled that a rapper's album had used part of a 1972 pop song without permission. The judge treated the case as simple theft and sent it to prosecutors to consider criminal charges. After the ruling, record labels began insisting that every sample be cleared.
  Courts did not agree on how small a sample could be before it no longer mattered. In 2005, in Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films, a federal appeals court ruled that copying even a two-second guitar chord from a sound recording required a license. The court wanted a clear rule: get permission or do not sample. In 2016, another appeals court disagreed. In VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone, it held that a horn blast lasting less than a quarter of a second, copied into a hit song, was too small for listeners to recognize and did not infringe.
  Sampling can also be defended as fair use. In 1994, in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, the Supreme Court ruled that a rap group's parody of a 1960s rock song could be fair use even though the group sold it for profit. A parody, the Court said, must borrow from the original to comment on it.
  People still argue about where the line should be. Supporters of strict rules say original artists deserve control over their work and a share of the money it earns, especially musicians who were paid little when their records were new. Critics say that requiring a license for every tiny sound blocks creativity, since every musical tradition borrows from the ones before it, and that clearing samples is so expensive that only artists with big budgets can afford it. Both sides agree on one thing: a sample is a piece of someone's work, and the law has to decide what that piece is worth.

## Questions
choice: Why might a producer need permission from two different owners to sample one record?
  - Because every song has two songwriters
  * Because the composition and the sound recording are separate copyrights
  - Because the law requires two licenses for hip hop only
  - Because the record label and the radio station both own the song
write: How did the 1991 Grand Upright decision change the music industry? Cite the paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: Before it, many artists sampled without permission; after the judge treated an unlicensed sample as theft, record labels began requiring that every sample be cleared (paragraph 3).
choice: How do the 2005 and 2016 decisions differ?
  - Both said any sample needs a license.
  - Both said short samples are always fair use.
  - The 2016 court said only songwriters can sue.
  * The 2005 court required a license for a two-second sample; the 2016 court said a sample under a quarter second was too small to infringe.
write: Why did the Supreme Court say a parody may borrow from an original song?
  lines: 2
  key: A parody has to copy enough of the original to comment on it or make fun of it, so borrowing is part of its purpose; that can make it fair use even when it is sold (paragraph 5).
write: Whose rights do you think should matter more in sampling, the original creator's or the new artist's? Use one argument from paragraph 6 and answer one argument from the other side.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Sample: The original creator's, because musicians who were paid little for old records deserve a share when their sound earns money again. Critics say licenses block creativity, but a fair, affordable licensing system could protect both, rather than letting anyone take a recording for free.

## Stretch
write: Write an enduring question about creativity and ownership that this passage raises.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Can anyone own a sound or an idea, when all art builds on what came before? / How should a society reward creators without blocking new creators?

# 21. Lateral Reading for Authority
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can check an unfamiliar source's authority by reading what other sources say about its author and publisher.
directions: Read each source excerpt, then the outside information a fact checker found about it. Rate each source's authority on this topic as strong, moderate or weak, and justify each rating with specific evidence from the outside information.
mistakes: Students often accept a title like "Dr." as proof of authority. Authority is topic-specific: a doctorate in marketing gives no special authority on water chemistry. Others rate the journalist as weak because she is not a scientist; a careful science reporter who names her experts and links to studies can be a solid source, though she is one step removed from the research.
notes: Every source, person, organization and fact on this sheet is made up for practice; the topic (microplastics in drinking water) is real and still being studied, so the sheet does not ask students to settle the science. Ratings in the key are the clearest reading: Source 1 weak, Source 2 strong, Source 3 moderate. Accept moderate-to-strong for Source 3 with a solid reason. Authority here means expertise on this topic plus a track record that others in the field recognize.

## Warm up
choice: Which question best tests a source's authority on a topic?
  - Does the website look professional?
  - Does the source agree with what I already think?
  - Is the source easy to read?
  * Does the author have recognized expertise in this specific topic, according to others?

## Source 1
source: Dr. Ellen Marsh, "Microplastics: Much Ado About Nothing," Policy Review P (made up), 2025
  [1] "Tiny plastic particles in water have been blown out of proportion. There is no reason for families to worry, and new rules on plastic would cost jobs."
text: **What a fact checker found:** A university faculty page lists Marsh's doctorate as being in marketing. Policy Review P is published by the Trade Group T (made up), a trade group whose members make plastic packaging. The article cites no studies.

## Source 2
source: Great Lakes Water Lab (made up), "Microplastic Levels in Great Lakes Tap Water, 2024," a state university report
  [2] "We found microplastic particles in 34 of 40 tap water samples. Health effects at these levels are not yet known, and more study is needed."
text: **What a fact checker found:** The lab is part of a state university. Its director is an environmental chemist with more than 40 published research papers. The report was reviewed by other scientists before it was published in a research journal. The state environmental agency cites it. It was paid for by state and federal research grants.

## Source 3
source: Jordan Lee, "What We Know, and Don't, About Microplastics," Harbor Point Courier (made up), 2025
  [3] "Researchers agree that microplastics are widespread. They disagree about how much harm they cause. Here is what three scientists told us."
text: **What a fact checker found:** Lee has reported on the environment for 12 years. The newspaper publishes a corrections policy and has corrected errors in her stories when found. The article names three university researchers and links to their studies.

## Rate each source
table: Source | Authority on this topic | Best evidence for your rating
  - 1. Marsh | {weak} | {marketing degree, not science; published by a plastics trade group; cites no studies}
  - 2. Water Lab | {strong} | {expert chemist; peer reviewed; cited by state agency; public research funding}
  - 3. Lee | {moderate} | {experienced reporter, names experts and links studies, but not a scientist herself}
write: Source 1's author is called "Dr." Why doesn't that settle her authority?
  lines: 2
  key: Her doctorate is in marketing, not a science related to water or plastics, so it gives her no special expertise on whether microplastics are harmful.
write: Besides expertise, what else in the outside information should make a reader cautious about Source 1?
  lines: 2
  key: It is published by a plastics trade group, which benefits if people believe microplastics are harmless and plastic rules are unnecessary (a conflict of interest), and it cites no studies.
write: Source 2 says health effects "are not yet known." Does admitting uncertainty weaken or strengthen its authority? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: It strengthens it: the lab reports only what its data show and says what is still unknown, which is what careful experts do; Source 1's confident "no reason to worry" goes beyond any evidence it gives.
choice: You can quote only one source for a fact about how common microplastics are in tap water. Which is best?
  - Source 1
  * Source 2
  - Source 3

## Stretch
write: When would Source 3 be more useful to you than Source 2?
  lines: 3
  key: When you need an overview of what several experts think and where they disagree, explained in plain language, or leads to more studies; Source 2 is better for one specific, original finding.

# 26. Inquiry Design Plan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can design an inquiry with a compelling question, supporting questions, sources and a final product.
directions: Study the model plan. Check two compelling questions against the criteria. Then design your own inquiry: a compelling question, 4 supporting questions with a source for each, and a final product that answers the compelling question.
mistakes: Students often write supporting questions that are only facts with no path to the compelling question. Each supporting question should give students a piece they need to build the final argument. Others plan a final product that is a display of facts (a poster about the topic) instead of an argument that answers the compelling question.
notes: The structure follows the Inquiry Design Model used with the C3 Framework for Social Studies: a compelling question (open, worth arguing, connected to students), supporting questions (more specific, answerable with sources), featured sources, a summative argument, and a chance to take informed action. The model is made up for practice; its facts are general and checked: many US cities have added bike lanes and green roofs, and city heat studies show neighborhoods with fewer trees run hotter. Criteria check in the key: question 1 does not meet the criteria (it has a single factual answer); question 2 does.

## Warm up: A model plan
example: **Compelling question:** Can a city grow and stay green?
  **Supporting questions:** 1. How has our city's population changed since 1990? 2. What happens to temperatures in neighborhoods with fewer trees? 3. What have other cities done to add green space as they grow? 4. Who pays for green projects, and who benefits?
  **Sources:** census tables; a city heat map; case studies of two cities; a city budget and interviews with residents.
  **Final product:** an argument essay or a proposal to the city council that answers the compelling question with evidence.
table: Criterion | Q1: How many parks does our city have? | Q2: Who should decide how a growing city uses its land?
  - Open (more than one defensible answer) | {no} | {yes}
  - Worth arguing about, with real stakes | {no} | {yes}
  - Answerable with evidence from sources | {yes} | {yes}

## Main: My inquiry
write: My compelling question:
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Sample: Should our state pay college athletes?
chart: Supporting question | Source I will use (title or type) | What it adds to my answer
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4
  height: 0.85
  key: Sample for "Should our state pay college athletes?": 1 How much money do college sports bring in? / NCAA and university revenue reports / shows who earns the money. 2 How have the rules on athlete pay changed since 2021? / news coverage and the court ruling / shows the current law. 3 How do athletes describe their time demands? / athlete interviews / adds athletes' own voices. 4 What happened in states that allowed name, image and likeness deals? / state laws and data / shows effects.
write: My final product (form and audience). How will it answer the compelling question?
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: A 3-minute policy speech for the school's debate night arguing yes or no, using evidence from each supporting question and answering the strongest counterargument.

## Stretch
write: Name one way to take informed action after your inquiry.
  lines: 2
  key: Sample: Write to a state legislator with my findings, or present the argument to the student council, citing my sources.

# 36. Source Judgment Reflection
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, your research notes (optional)
ican: I can reflect on how my judgment of a source changed and what made me question it.
directions: Read the model reflection. Then think of a source you trusted at first and later questioned: from a research project, the news or anywhere. Rate it before and after, explain what changed your mind, and write a rule you will use next time.
mistakes: Students often describe a source that was obviously bad from the start; the point is a source that seemed trustworthy. Others say they "just had a feeling"; push for the specific evidence that changed their judgment (who funds it, what other sources said, a missing study). Some decide the source was useless; often it is still useful for some purpose once its limits are clear.
notes: The model is made up; the organization and study are fictional. Students may choose a source from any part of their lives, but not anything private or personal about themselves or their families. Strong reflections name the source, describe the first impression, name the evidence that changed it, and state a lasting habit. Pair with the Source Portfolio Tracker (sheet 21 in grade 9) if students have one.

## Warm up: A model reflection

I still used the report, but only to show what the industry argues, not as evidence about teen health.
