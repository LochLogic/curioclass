# 1. Passive, Aggressive or Assertive?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell passive, aggressive and assertive responses apart and speak up assertively.
directions: Read each situation and the response. Label the response passive, aggressive or assertive. Then rewrite three responses so they are assertive.
mistakes: Students label sarcasm (row 9) as assertive because it is not shouting. Sarcasm and put-downs attack the person, so they are aggressive. Another mix-up: polite but silent giving in (row 8) feels nice, yet it is passive because the speaker's own need disappears.
notes: Definitions used here. **Passive:** gives up your own needs or stays silent to avoid trouble. **Aggressive:** gets your needs met by attacking, blaming, threatening or putting down the other person. **Assertive:** states your needs clearly and calmly while respecting the other person, often with an "I" statement and a request. The situations are about characters, so nobody has to share their own.

## Warm up
example: Someone keeps kicking your chair. Passive: say nothing and get upset. Aggressive: "Stop it, you weirdo!" Assertive: "**Please stop kicking my chair. It's distracting me.**"

## Main: Label each response
table: # | Situation | Response | Label
  - 1 | Someone cuts in front of Jae in the lunch line. | "Hey, the line starts back there. Please wait your turn." | {assertive }
  - 2 | Someone cuts in front of Rosa in the lunch line. | She sighs, says nothing and stews about it all afternoon. | {passive   }
  - 3 | Someone cuts in front of Tom in the lunch line. | "Move, or you'll be sorry." | {aggressive}
  - 4 | A group member has not started their slides. | "I need your slides by Thursday so we can practice. Can you do that?" | {assertive }
  - 5 | A friend borrows Mina's hoodie without asking. | Mina says nothing, even though she is upset. | {passive   }
  - 6 | A friend borrows Leo's hoodie without asking. | "You're so selfish. You always take my stuff." | {aggressive}
  - 7 | A teammate never passes to Kofi. | "I'm open a lot. Can you pass to me when I'm free?" | {assertive }
  - 8 | A classmate asks Ivy to do his share of the poster, and she doesn't want to. | "Um, sure, I guess I can do it." | {passive   }
  - 9 | A classmate shares an idea Ben doesn't like. | He rolls his eyes and says, "Great, another genius idea." | {aggressive}
  - 10 | A sibling reads Zara's journal. | "I don't like it when you read my journal. Please ask first." | {assertive }

## Stretch: Make it assertive
write: Rewrite response 3 so it is assertive.
  lines: 2
  key: Excuse me, I was in line. The end of the line is back there.
write: Rewrite response 5 so it is assertive.
  lines: 2
  key: Mina says, "I'd like you to ask before you borrow my hoodie. Can you give it back after school?"
write: Rewrite response 6 so it is assertive.
  lines: 2
  key: I felt upset when you took my hoodie without asking. Please ask me first next time.

# 7. Group Project Rescue
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can solve group project problems with fair solutions that give every member a clear role.
directions: Match each role to its job. Then read each group's problem, name what each person needs, and write a fair solution that uses roles from the role menu.
mistakes: Students "solve" the problem by removing or blaming someone ("kick him out," "tell the teacher he's lazy"). A fair fix keeps everyone in the group with a clear job and a check-in. Another miss: splitting the work into equal pieces without asking about time, strengths or schedules. Fair does not always mean identical.
notes: Role menu used here: facilitator, timekeeper, researcher, designer, presenter, checker. All groups and names are fictional, so nobody has to describe their own group. Accept any solution that (1) gives every member a named role, (2) meets the main need of each person, and (3) includes a check-in or deadline so the problem does not come back. If a real group in your class has one of these problems, use the sheet as a model for a private conversation, not a public example.

## Warm up: The role menu
match: Match each role to its job.
  - Facilitator = keeps the group on task and makes sure everyone gets a turn to talk
  - Timekeeper = sets mini deadlines and checks progress against the calendar
  - Researcher = finds facts and sources and takes notes
  - Designer = plans how the poster, slides or model will look
  - Checker = compares the finished work with the rubric before it is turned in
example: **Problem:** Ana and Ben both want to give the whole presentation. Cy never says anything.
  **Needs:** Ana and Ben each want a chance to speak; Cy needs a job that fits a quieter person and a way to be heard.
  **Fair, role-based fix:** Ana presents the introduction and Ben presents the findings. Cy is the designer who builds the slides, and the group asks Cy's opinion first at each meeting. They practice once together on Wednesday.

## Main: Four groups in trouble
text: **Group 1: One person doing it all.** Maya's group of four is making a poster about water pollution. Maya is worried about her grade, so she did most of the research and redid the poster at home. Jordan and Eli say, "Why bother? She'll change it anyway." Priya missed two class periods and doesn't know what to do.
write: What does each person need?
  lines: 1
  key: Maya needs to know the work will be good enough for a strong grade. Jordan and Eli need real jobs and to be trusted with them. Priya needs to catch up on what she missed.
write: Write a fair, role-based solution.
  lines: 2
  key: Make a task list together. Maya becomes the checker, so she reviews everyone's work against the rubric instead of redoing it. Jordan is the designer, Eli writes the captions as a second researcher, and Priya is the timekeeper; Maya gives her a five-minute catch-up on the research. They check in at the start of every class.
text: **Group 2: A missed deadline.** Deshawn promised to finish his three slides by Monday so the group could practice Tuesday. On Monday his slides are not done because he had a soccer tournament all weekend. The presentation is Thursday, and his group is angry.
write: What does each person need?
  lines: 1
  key: The group needs time to practice and needs to be able to count on each other. Deshawn needs a deadline that fits his schedule and a chance to fix the problem without being shamed.
write: Write a fair, role-based solution.
  lines: 2
  key: Deshawn finishes his slides by Tuesday night and the group practices Wednesday. A timekeeper sets a halfway check-in for each person's part, and the group writes known events (like tournaments) on the calendar when they plan. New rule: if you will miss a deadline, tell the group at least a day early.
text: **Group 3: Ideas ignored.** In a science model group, Lina has offered three ideas. Each time, Marco and Tess talk over her and vote 2 to 1 before she finishes explaining.
write: What does each person need?
  lines: 1
  key: Lina needs to be heard all the way through. Marco and Tess want to make decisions quickly and get building.
write: Write a fair, role-based solution.
  lines: 2
  key: Rotate the facilitator role, starting with Lina. Before any vote, the facilitator runs a round where each person gets one uninterrupted minute to explain an idea. A timekeeper keeps the round short, so Marco and Tess still get fast decisions.
text: **Group 4: Stuck on a topic.** Sam wants to research volcanoes. Riley wants to research space. They have argued for two class periods. Ivy just wants to start, and the topic is due tomorrow.
write: What does each person need?
  lines: 1
  key: Sam wants a topic about dramatic Earth events. Riley wants a topic about exploring space. Ivy needs the group to stop arguing and get started. The group needs a fair way to decide.
write: Write a fair, role-based solution.
  lines: 2
  key: Ivy facilitates: each person pitches a topic for one minute, then the group looks for a topic that meets both interests, such as volcanoes on other planets (Mars has the giant volcano Olympus Mons). If they still disagree after ten minutes (the timekeeper watches the clock), they vote, and the person whose topic loses chooses their role first.

## Check
choice: Which fix is both fair and role-based?
  - The two hardest workers do the project, and the others get a lower grade.
  * Each member takes a named job, and the group checks progress at the start of each class.
  - The group leader decides everything so there are no arguments.

## Stretch
write: Write three rules for a group agreement that would have prevented all four problems.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: 1. Everyone has a named role, and we write it at the top of our plan. 2. If you will miss a deadline, tell the group a day early. 3. Before any vote, every person gets one minute to explain without being interrupted.

# 20. Conflict Resolution Review
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can analyze a conflict for response styles, interests and escalation, and write a fair agreement.
directions: Read the story; its paragraphs are numbered. Label each moment's response style, track how the conflict escalated, find each side's position and interests, and then write the agreement the mediator could help Nadia and Theo sign.
mistakes: Students label Nadia's silence in paragraph 3 as "calm" or "assertive." Saying nothing while upset is passive. Students also list positions ("Theo wants a collage") as interests. Ask "Why does he want that?" The answer is the interest. Watch agreements that only say "be nice": a good agreement names who does what by when.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. It reviews the module's tools: passive, aggressive and assertive responses; positions and interests; escalating and de-escalating moves; and a mediated agreement. Paragraph 8 ends with a collaborative idea, a solution that meets both sides' interests instead of splitting the difference. Students who finish early can role-play the mediation with three people (Jada, Nadia, Theo) using the agreement they wrote.

## Read
passage: The Wall | numbered
  Ms. Ortega's art club had finally won permission to paint the long wall outside the cafeteria. The club had three weeks and one rule: the design had to show what made their school special.
  Nadia came to the first meeting with a sketch she had worked on all weekend, a timeline of the school's history from its first small building to the new gym. Theo glanced at it and said, "That's kind of boring. Nobody reads a timeline at lunch." He wanted a giant collage of student hobbies, with music, sports and art bursting across the wall.
  Nadia's face went hot. She said nothing, rolled up her sketch and spent the rest of the meeting staring at the table. That night she texted her friend Kim, "Theo thinks he's the boss of the whole club."
  Kim forwarded the message to two other members. By Wednesday the club had split into "Team Timeline" and "Team Collage." At the next meeting Theo said loudly, "Some people would rather complain in group chats than actually paint." Nadia shot back, "Some people think their ideas are the only ones that count." Two members walked out.
  On Friday, Theo came in early and began sketching his collage on the wall in pencil, right over the space Nadia had measured for her timeline. When Nadia saw it, she erased half of it.
  Ms. Ortega asked Jada, a trained peer mediator, to meet with them. Jada set the ground rules: one person talks at a time, no put-downs, and both people work on the problem together. Then she asked each of them what they really needed.
  Nadia said she wanted people to learn that the school was built by families who raised the money for it. She also admitted that she wanted her weekend of work to be respected. Theo said he wanted students to see themselves on the wall, so they would feel the school belonged to them. He was also worried the club would run out of time.
  Jada wrote both lists on a whiteboard. Nadia and Theo looked at it for a long minute. Then Theo said, "What if the timeline ran along the bottom, and the student pictures grew up out of it, like the school today grew out of its past?"

## Response styles
table: Moment | Passive, aggressive or assertive? | Evidence
  - Nadia at the first meeting (paragraph 3) | {passive} | {She says nothing and rolls up her sketch, though she is upset.}
  - Theo at the next meeting (paragraph 4) | {aggressive} | {He uses sarcasm to put Nadia down in front of everyone.}
  - Nadia's answer (paragraph 4) | {aggressive} | {She fires a put-down back instead of saying what she needs.}
  - Theo with the whiteboard (paragraph 8) | {assertive} | {He calmly offers an idea that meets both people's needs.}

## Escalation
chart: What happened | How it made the conflict worse
  labels: Step 1; Step 2; Step 3; Step 4
  height: 0.4
  key: Step 1: Theo calls Nadia's sketch boring (paragraph 2), which dismisses her work. Step 2: Nadia complains by text instead of to Theo, and Kim forwards it (paragraphs 3 and 4), which pulls in other people and splits the club. Step 3: public sarcasm and put-downs at the meeting (paragraph 4), and two members walk out. Step 4: Theo sketches over Nadia's space and she erases it (paragraph 5), so the fight moves from words to actions.
choice: Which move at the first meeting would have **de-escalated** the conflict?
  - Nadia tells Theo his collage idea is worse.
  * Nadia says, "I worked hard on this. Can we look at both ideas before we decide?"
  - Nadia quits the club.

## Positions and interests
table: Person | Position (what they say they want) | Interests (why they want it)
  - Nadia | {a timeline of the school's history} | {people learn that families built the school; her weekend of work is respected}
  - Theo | {a collage of student hobbies} | {students see themselves and feel the school belongs to them; finish on time}

## The agreement
write: Write the agreement Nadia and Theo could sign. Include the design, who does what, a deadline and what they will do if they disagree again.
  lines: 5
  starter: We agree that ...
  key: Model: We agree that the mural will have the school's history as a timeline along the bottom, with student hobby pictures growing up out of it. Nadia will lead the timeline section using her measurements, and Theo will lead the collage section. Both of us will present the combined sketch to the whole club next Tuesday, and the club will finish painting by the end of week three. If we disagree again, we will talk to each other first, face to face, not in group chats, and ask Jada to help if we get stuck. Signed, Nadia and Theo.

# 21. Decision Tree Deep Dive
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can map options and possible outcomes in a decision tree and judge how likely each outcome is.
pairs: decision-tree
directions: Read about Sofia's choice. Build a three-level decision tree: the decision, the three options, and two possible outcomes for each option. Rate how likely each outcome is, using Sofia's facts.
mistakes: Students rate outcomes by what they hope will happen. Ask "Which fact supports that rating?" Also watch for outcomes that are really options ("take band") instead of results ("plays in the spring concert").
notes: Use after the Decision Tree tool. Level 1 is the decision, level 2 the options, level 3 the outcomes. The tree is drawn by hand in the box, then the chart holds the ratings and reasons. Likelihood ratings: likely, possible or unlikely. The key gives model outcomes and ratings; accept others that cite the facts.

## Read
text: **Sofia's choice.** Sofia is in seventh grade and must pick one elective for next semester: **Art**, **Band** or **Journalism**.
text: **Fact 1.** Sofia has never played an instrument. Beginning band students rent an instrument and are asked to practice 20 minutes a day at home.
text: **Fact 2.** Sofia draws in a sketchbook almost every day. The art class ends with a show in the front hall.
text: **Fact 3.** Journalism writes the school newspaper. Sofia's friend Ana is an editor, and every student must write one article each month.
text: **Fact 4.** Sofia says she gets bored when a class is "too easy" and loves learning new skills.

## Main: Build the tree
draw: Draw your tree. Level 1 (left): the decision. Level 2: three branches, one per elective. Level 3: two outcomes on each branch. | 3.4
chart: Elective | Possible outcome (what might happen) | Likely, possible or unlikely? | Fact that supports your rating
  rows: 6
  key: Model answers. Art: her work is in the art show (likely, she draws every day); she gets bored because she already draws a lot (possible). Band: she learns to read music and play a new instrument (likely, she loves new skills and practice is built in); she plays a solo at the first concert (unlikely, she has never played). Journalism: she publishes an article each month (likely, it is required); she works with Ana on stories (likely or possible, Ana is an editor).

## Check the facts
choice: Which outcome is the least likely, based on the facts?
  - Sofia's drawing is in the art show.
  * Sofia plays a solo at the first band concert.
  - Sofia writes an article for the newspaper.
choice: Which fact best supports choosing Band?
  - Sofia's friend Ana is an editor.
  - Sofia draws almost every day.
  * Sofia loves learning new skills.

## Stretch
write: Which elective would you advise Sofia to take? Use your tree's ratings to explain.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: Band, because her most likely outcome is learning a brand new skill, which she loves, and the daily practice keeps it from being too easy. A strong answer cites at least one likely outcome and one risk.

# 33. Decision Words in Use
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use the words influence, ethical, risk, alternative and integrity to talk about decisions.
directions: Fill in each definition sentence with a word from the word bank. Then read each scenario and write a sentence about it that uses the word given.
mistakes: Students use "risk" as only a bad thing ("Jae will get hurt"). A risk is the chance that something bad could happen, so good sentences say "could" or "might." Watch "alternative" used to mean "the bad option"; an alternative is simply another choice. "Ethical" and "integrity" often get swapped: ethical describes a choice or action, integrity describes a person.
notes: Accept any sentence that uses the word correctly and is about the scenario. A quick oral check: have students read their integrity sentence to a partner, who says whether it describes the person or the action. The scenarios are fictional and do not ask students about their own choices.

## Warm up: What the words mean
text: **Word bank:** influence, ethical, risk, alternative, integrity
fill:
  - An {influence} is a person or thing that changes how you think or what you do.
  - An {ethical} choice is one that is fair, honest and right.
  - A {risk} is the chance that something bad might happen.
  - An {alternative} is another choice you could make instead.
  - A person with {integrity} does what is right, even when no one is watching.

## Main: Use the words
text: **1. Carmen** wants to spend all her birthday money on the newest sneakers because her older cousin and his friends all wear them.
write: Use **influence** in a sentence about Carmen.
  lines: 2
  key: Carmen's cousin is a big influence on what she wants to buy.
text: **2. Omar** finds the answer key to Friday's quiz left on the copier in the library.
write: Use **ethical** in a sentence about Omar.
  lines: 2
  key: The ethical choice is for Omar to give the answer key back to the teacher without looking at it.
text: **3. Jae** wants to skateboard down the steep hill by the library without a helmet.
write: Use **risk** in a sentence about Jae.
  lines: 2
  key: Riding down the steep hill without a helmet is a big risk because Jae could fall and hurt his head.
text: **4. Ruby's** friends are going to the mall on Saturday, but her science project is due Monday and she has not started.
write: Use **alternative** in a sentence about Ruby.
  lines: 2
  key: One alternative is for Ruby to work on her project Saturday morning and meet her friends at the mall later.
text: **5. Leo's** team won because the referee missed a foul Leo made. A reporter for the school paper asks him about the game.
write: Use **integrity** in a sentence about Leo.
  lines: 2
  key: If Leo has integrity, he will admit that he fouled even though it might make the win look less impressive.

## Check
choice: Which sentence uses **integrity** correctly?
  - The integrity of the test was 20 questions.
  - Maria took an integrity to the store.
  * Maria showed integrity when she returned the extra change the cashier gave her.

## Stretch
write: Write advice to Omar that uses at least three of the five words.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: Omar, your friends might influence you to peek, but the ethical choice is to return the key. Peeking is a risk because you could get caught, and it would hurt your integrity even if you didn't. A better alternative is to hand it to the librarian right away.
