# 1. AND, OR, NOT Practice
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can use AND, OR and NOT to make a search wider or narrower.
directions: Study the three Venn diagrams in the warm up. For each question, write a search using AND, OR or NOT. Then shade the part of its Venn diagram that your search finds. The first word goes in the left circle, the second word in the right circle.
mistakes: Students often use AND when they mean OR ("frogs AND toads" finds only pages about both). Remind them: AND narrows (fewer results, both words), OR widens (more results, either word), NOT removes. With NOT, the word after NOT is the one taken away, so students shade only the part of the left circle outside the overlap.
notes: Library databases and catalogs understand AND, OR and NOT (often typed in capitals). Many web search engines treat a space as AND and use a minus sign instead of NOT (bats -baseball); quotation marks keep a phrase together ("mountain lions"). Accept quotation marks around two-word terms. Shading key: AND shades only the middle; OR shades both whole circles; NOT shades the left circle without the middle.

## Warm up: What each word finds
picword:
  - venn-and | AND
  - venn-or | OR
  - venn-not | NOT
example: cats AND dogs: only pages about both (the middle).
  cats OR dogs: pages about either one or both (both whole circles).
  cats NOT dogs: pages about cats that do not mention dogs (the left circle without the middle).

## Main: Write it, then shade it
write: You want pages about pandas that also talk about bamboo.
  lines: 1
  key: pandas AND bamboo. Shade only the middle.
write: You want every page about frogs and every page about toads.
  lines: 1
  key: frogs OR toads. Shade both whole circles.
write: You want pages about bats, the animals, without pages about baseball.
  lines: 1
  key: bats NOT baseball. Shade the left circle (bats) without the middle.
write: You want pages about volcanoes in Hawaii.
  lines: 1
  key: volcanoes AND Hawaii. Shade only the middle.
write: The same big cat is called a cougar or a mountain lion. You want pages that use either name.
  lines: 1
  key: cougars OR "mountain lions" (or cougar OR mountain lion). Shade both whole circles.
write: You want pages about dinosaurs, but not about movies.
  lines: 1
  key: dinosaurs NOT movies. Shade the left circle (dinosaurs) without the middle.

## Stretch: Most and fewest
choice: Which search finds the most pages?
  - cats AND dogs
  * cats OR dogs
  - cats NOT dogs
write: Explain why. Use the Venn diagrams.
  lines: 3
  key: OR shades both whole circles, so it includes every page about cats, every page about dogs and the pages about both; AND keeps only the middle and NOT keeps only part of one circle.

# 7. Spot the Fake Animal Page
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can find clues that a web page is not reliable or not real.
directions: Read the printed web page about the "Sahara desert penguin." Highlight anything that makes you suspicious. Then list 5 clues that the page is not real, and tell how you could check.
mistakes: Students often list only "it looks weird." Push for specific clues: no author, no date, no sources, a fact that breaks what they know, a page that sells something. Others think a real-looking photo proves the animal is real; photos can be edited, so a photo alone is not evidence.
notes: The desert penguin, the website and the "scientist" are made up for practice; the example.org address leads nowhere. Real penguin facts used in the key: almost all penguins live in the Southern Hemisphere, and the Sahara is north of the equator; penguins hunt in the ocean for fish, squid and krill; their wings are flippers, so penguins cannot fly. A good stretch talk: some real penguins do nest on desert coasts (African penguins in Namibia, Humboldt penguins in Peru and Chile), but always right next to the ocean, where they find their food. Accept any 5 clues from the key list.

## The web page
source: sahara-penguins.example.org, home page. No author. No date.
  **THE AMAZING SAHARA DESERT PENGUIN: The Animal Scientists Do Not Want You to Know About!**
  The Sahara desert penguin lives in the middle of the Sahara Desert in Africa, hundreds of miles from any ocean. It stays cool by sleeping in the hot sand all day. At night it flies from dune to dune, hunting sand beetles. Its feathers are orange so it can hide in the sand.
  "I saw thousands of them!" says a famous scientist. Only 12 people have ever seen one, but there are millions of them.
  Adopt your own desert penguin today! Only 29.99 for a certificate and a photo. Click here to buy. Share this page before it gets deleted!

## Warm up
choice: Penguins hunt for food in the:
  - sand
  - treetops
  * ocean
  - desert caves
choice: Which question should you ask first about any web page?
  * Who made this page, and how do they know?
  - Does the page have a lot of exclamation points?
  - Is the animal cute?

## Main: Five clues
table: Clue | What makes it suspicious
  - 1 | {No author and no date: we cannot tell who made it or when.}
  - 2 | {Penguins live by the ocean and eat fish; a penguin far from any ocean eating beetles does not fit real facts.}
  - 3 | {Penguins cannot fly; their wings are flippers.}
  - 4 | {The "famous scientist" has no name, so we cannot check the quote.}
  - 5 | {It sells adoption certificates, so the page may exist to make money.}
  height: 0.55
note: Other clues to accept: the facts contradict each other (only 12 people have seen one, but there are millions; the scientist saw thousands); the headline says scientists are hiding it (a common trick); "share before it gets deleted" pushes you to hurry; no sources; almost all penguins live south of the equator, but the Sahara is north of it.
choice: The page says only 12 people have seen one, and also that there are millions. What kind of clue is this?
  - A clue about the date
  * The page contradicts itself
  - A clue about who made the page

## Check it
write: Name two ways you could check whether the desert penguin is real, without using this page.
  lines: 3
  key: Look up "desert penguin" on a site I trust, like a zoo, aquarium or science museum site; check a library book or encyclopedia about penguins; ask a librarian. If no trusted source mentions it, it is not real.

## Stretch
write: Some real penguins nest on dry, desert coasts in Africa and South America. Why does that not make the Sahara desert penguin real?
  lines: 3
  key: Those penguins live right next to the ocean, where they catch fish to eat. The page says this penguin lives hundreds of miles from any ocean, eats beetles and flies, which no real penguin does.

# 20. Citation Scramble
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors (optional)
ican: I can put the parts of a citation in the right order with the right punctuation.
directions: Each citation below was cut into pieces and mixed up, and its periods and commas fell off. Write the letters of the pieces in the right order. Find your answer in the decoder to spell a secret word. Then write two of the citations out in full, with every period and comma.
mistakes: Students often put the title first for every source. Only a source with no author starts with its title. Others write the date before the website name for a web page; in MLA style the website name comes first, then the date, then the address. Magazine page numbers come last.
notes: Citations follow MLA style (9th edition), as in sheet 21: Book: Last, First. *Title*. Publisher, Year. Web page: Last, First. "Title of Page." *Website*, day Month year, address. Magazine article: Last, First. "Title of Article." *Magazine*, Month year, pp. pages. The pieces keep italics and quotation marks so students can tell a book title from a page title. All sources are made up; the example.org addresses lead nowhere. Secret word: CITED. Full citations: 1 Nguyen, Linh. *Secrets of the Coral Reef*. Blue Wave Press, 2023. 2 Okafor, David. *Rockets and Robots*. Sky High Books, 2021. 3 Silva, Rosa. # 21. Report Planner With Citations
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, 3 sources on one topic (books or printed web pages), index cards or scrap paper (optional)
ican: I can plan a report from three sources and list them in a works cited list.
directions: Practice two works cited entries first. Then plan your own 3-paragraph report: write your question, record your 3 sources, take keyword notes, plan each paragraph, and write your works cited list.
mistakes: Students often write the URL alone as the citation, or put the first name first. In MLA style the author's last name comes first, then a comma, then the first name. A source with no author starts with its title in quotation marks. Works cited entries go in ABC order by their first word, not in the order students found them.
notes: Citations follow MLA style (9th edition). Book: Last, First. Title of Book. Publisher, Year. Web page: Last, First. "Title of Page." Name of Website, day Month year, URL. With no author, start with the title. MLA recommends leaving out https:// in URLs, abbreviates every month except May, June and July (Jan., Feb., Mar., Apr., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., Dec.), and alphabetizes by the first word, skipping A, An and The. Titles of whole works (books, websites) are in italics when typed; students underline them by hand, since this sheet cannot print italics. A typed list also gets a hanging indent (second and later lines indented). The books and websites in the practice are made up; the example.org addresses are reserved for examples and do not lead anywhere.

## Warm up: Build two entries
example: Book: Last name, First name. Title of Book. Publisher, Year.
  Web page: Last name, First name. "Title of Page." Name of Website, day Month year, address.
  No author? Start with the title. Underline the titles of books and websites when you write by hand (they are in italics when typed).
  Model: Kim, Sora. "How Sea Turtles Find Home." Ocean Kids Lab, 14 Apr. 2025, oceankidslab.example.org/turtles.
write: Book facts: author Jamal Rivers; title Volcanoes Up Close; publisher Stone Bridge Books; year 2022. Write the entry.
  lines: 2
  key: Rivers, Jamal. Volcanoes Up Close. Stone Bridge Books, 2022. (Volcanoes Up Close underlined or in italics.)
write: Web page facts: no author; page title How Lava Cools; website Earth Kids Lab; posted 5 May 2025; address earthkidslab.example.org/lava. Write the entry.
  lines: 2
  key: "How Lava Cools." Earth Kids Lab, 5 May 2025, earthkidslab.example.org/lava. (Earth Kids Lab underlined or in italics.)
choice: In a works cited list with these two entries, which comes first?
  * "How Lava Cools."

# 33. Windows and Mirrors Check
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, a world map or globe (optional)
ican: I can talk about a book in a book club role, place stories on a map, and check whether a book collection shows many kinds of lives.
directions: Part 1: read the story and do one book club job. Part 2: name the country and continent for three book settings. Part 3: check five book listings for a class shelf and decide what the shelf is missing.
mistakes: In Part 1, students often write questions with one-word answers ("What is the bus called?"). A Discussion Director asks questions that start a talk ("Why do you think..."). In Part 3, students may stop at "they are all good books." An audit is about the whole shelf: where and when the stories happen and what kinds of lives they show.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class (about 170 words); the book listings are made up. Matatus are shared minibuses that many people ride in Kenya, and Nairobi is Kenya's capital. Part 2 settings: Nairobi is in Kenya, Africa; Kyoto is in Japan, Asia; Oaxaca is in Mexico, North America. If you have a world map, students can number the three places on it. Part 3 audit: four of the five books happen in the United States and one in a make-believe world, so the shelf has no stories set in Africa, Asia, Europe, South America or Australia, and only one set in the past.

## Part 1: Book club role
passage: The Morning Matatu
  Wanjiru heard the matatu before she saw it. Music thumped from its open windows, and its sides were painted with bright stars and a roaring lion.
  "Hurry!" called her brother Kamau. They squeezed onto the minibus with their school bags on their laps. The conductor leaned out the door and shouted the stops as the matatu wove through the Nairobi traffic.
  Wanjiru loved the ride. She counted the jacaranda trees, purple with blossoms, and waved to the woman who sold roasted maize at the corner. But today she held her bag tightly. Inside was the bead necklace she had made for the school art show.
  At the next stop, the matatu jerked forward. Her bag slipped, and the beads spilled across the floor. Wanjiru froze. Then a boy in a green sweater knelt down. So did an old man with a cane, and a girl in a nurse's uniform. By the time the matatu reached the school gate, every bead was back in her hands.
  "Asante sana," Wanjiru whispered. Thank you very much.
write: Choose one role and do its job. **Discussion Director:** write two questions that would start a good talk. **Word Wizard:** pick two interesting words, tell what they mean and why the author chose them. **Connector:** connect the story to another book or to something in the world.
  lines: 5
  key: Discussion Director sample: Why do you think the strangers stopped to help Wanjiru? How does the author make the ride feel busy and alive? Word Wizard sample: "wove" means moved in and out between things, which shows how the matatu squeezes through traffic; "froze" means stopped still, which shows her shock. Connector sample: like other stories where a community helps someone, the riders work together; it also shows how many people in cities around the world get to school on shared buses.

  lines: 3
