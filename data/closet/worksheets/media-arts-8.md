# 1. Exposure Triangle
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can explain how aperture, shutter speed and ISO control light and change the look of a photo.
directions: Complete the chart for the three settings of the exposure triangle. Draw and label the triangle. Solve the stop problems, then choose settings for each photo situation.
mistakes: Students mix up f-numbers: a SMALLER f-number (f/2) means a WIDER opening and more light. Shutter speeds are fractions of a second: 1/1000 is much faster than 1/30. A "stop" doubles or halves the light; to keep the same brightness, a stop more from one setting needs a stop less from another.
notes: Exposure is how bright the photo is. Each setting has a side effect: aperture changes depth of field, shutter speed changes motion blur, and ISO changes noise (grain). Standard full stops: f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16; shutter 1/30, 1/60, 1/125, 1/250, 1/500, 1/1000; ISO 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600. If cameras are available, students can test the stop problems, but the sheet works on paper alone.

## Warm up: The three settings
table: Setting | What it controls | More light when... | Side effect
  - Aperture | {the size of the lens opening} | {the opening is wider (smaller f-number)} | {depth of field: wider means shallower focus}
  - Shutter speed | {how long the sensor is exposed to light} | {the shutter stays open longer (slower speed)} | {motion blur: slower means more blur}
  - ISO | {how sensitive the sensor is to light} | {the ISO number is higher} | {noise or grain: higher means more grain}

## Main: Draw the triangle
draw: Draw a triangle. Label each corner with a setting and write its side effect next to it. | 2.6

## Main: Stops
example: f/8 to f/5.6 is one stop MORE light (wider opening). To keep the same brightness, make the shutter one stop faster: 1/125 becomes 1/250.
fill:
  - f/2.8 to f/5.6 lets in {2} stops less light.
  - ISO 100 to ISO 800 is {3} stops brighter.
  - Start at f/4, 1/60, ISO 400. You lower ISO to 200. To keep the same brightness, the shutter becomes 1/{30}.
  - Start at f/8, 1/250, ISO 100. You open to f/5.6. To keep the same brightness, the shutter becomes 1/{500}.

## Main: Choose the settings
choice: You want to freeze a soccer player in the middle of a kick on a sunny day. What matters most?
  - A slow shutter, like 1/15
  * A fast shutter, like 1/1000
  - The smallest possible aperture, like f/22
  - The highest ISO
choice: You want a waterfall to look silky and smooth, with the camera on a tripod. What do you choose?
  * A slow shutter, like 1/2 second
  - A fast shutter, like 1/2000
  - A high ISO, like 3200
  - A flash
choice: In a dark gym with no flash, your action photos are blurry. Which change helps, and what is the cost?
  * Raise the ISO; the cost is more grain
  - Slower shutter; the cost is less blur
  - Smaller aperture; the cost is more light
  - Lower the ISO; the cost is a brighter photo
choice: You want a portrait with a soft, blurred background. What do you choose?
  - f/16
  - ISO 3200
  - 1/15 second
  * f/2

## Stretch
write: A friend's night photo of a city street is too dark. Give two different ways to brighten it and the side effect of each.
  lines: 4
  key: Any two: open the aperture (shallower depth of field), use a slower shutter on a tripod (moving cars and people blur into streaks), or raise the ISO (more grain). A strong answer pairs each change with its side effect.

# 7. Six-Photo Portfolio
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan a photo portfolio with a clear theme, a strong sequence and honest captions.
directions: Answer the warm up about themes, sequence and captions. Write your theme statement, then plan six photos in order with a caption for each. Explain your sequence, and fix the weak captions in the stretch.
mistakes: Themes are often just topics ("my dog"). A theme says something about the topic ("Our old dog still runs the house"). Students also plan six photos that look alike; push for variety in shot size, angle and light. Captions that only say "Cool shot" or repeat what is visible add nothing; a good caption adds who, where, when or why. Captions must be honest: never caption a posed or staged photo as if it just happened.
notes: Students plan photos they can really take: places they are allowed to be and people who agree to be photographed (or no people at all). The model theme in the warm up is a sample. If cameras are available, students can shoot from this plan and bring back more than six photos, then choose; the plan is a starting point, not a cage.

## Warm up
example: **Theme statement (model):** *Hands at Work.* The hands of the people who keep our school running tell the story of a school day before anyone sees it.
choice: Which is a theme, not just a topic?
  - Trees
  - My neighborhood
  * Small things in our neighborhood that people walk past without seeing
  - Photos I took
choice: Which photo makes the best opener for a portfolio?
  * A strong, clear image that introduces the theme right away
  - The weakest photo, to save the best for later
  - A blurry test shot
  - Any photo; order does not matter
choice: Which caption is honest and adds information?
  - "Wow!"
  - "A rare wild owl" (it is a statue)
  * "The custodian unlocks the gym doors at 6:45 a.m., before the first bus arrives."
  - "Best photo ever taken"

## Main: Theme
write: Write your theme statement: a title and one sentence that says what the portfolio shows and why it matters.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong statement names a specific subject and an idea about it, such as "Repairs: our neighborhood fixes things instead of throwing them away."

## Main: Plan six photos
table: Order | Photo plan (subject, shot size, angle, light) | Role in the sequence | Caption draft
  height: 0.75
  - 1 | | Opener: strong, introduces the theme |
  - 2 | | Builds: a new view of the theme |
  - 3 | | Variety: a change in size or angle |
  - 4 | | Detail: a close-up |
  - 5 | | Surprise or turn |
  - 6 | | Closer: ends with a feeling or answer |
write: Explain your order. Why does your opener come first and your closer come last?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer explains that the opener sets up the theme clearly, the middle varies size and angle so the set does not repeat, and the closer leaves the viewer with a feeling or idea that answers the theme.

## Stretch: Fix the captions
write: Rewrite this weak caption so it adds who, where, when or why: "Cool picture of a guy fixing a bike."
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: "Every Saturday morning, a volunteer at the Elm Street bike co-op fixes donated bikes for kids who need one." Fictional details are fine for practice; real captions must be true.
write: A photographer asked a friend to pretend to cry for a photo, then captioned it "A fan's tears after the final loss." What is wrong, and how could the caption be honest?
  lines: 2
  key: The caption presents a staged photo as a real moment, which misleads viewers. An honest caption says it was staged, such as "A friend acts out a fan's heartbreak (staged photo)," or the photographer should use a real, unposed moment.

# 20. Animation Check
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain the animation pipeline, animatics and lip sync, and calculate timing in frames.
directions: Answer the 8 review items about the production pipeline, animatics and lip sync. Show your work for the frame problems. Then try the stretch.
mistakes: Students think the animatic comes after the animation. It comes before: it tests the timing with storyboard drawings so nobody animates a shot that will be cut. For lip sync, students draw a mouth shape for every letter; animators draw shapes for sounds, and several sounds share one shape.
notes: In most studio pipelines, dialogue is recorded before animation so animators can match mouth shapes and acting to the real voice performance. A common simplified pipeline: script, storyboard, voice recording, animatic, layout, animation, final sound mix. Studios vary in the exact order of the middle steps. The mouth shapes in the match are standard simplified lip sync shapes; studios use sets of about 6 to 12.

## Warm up: The pipeline
choice: Which step comes right after the storyboard in most animation pipelines?
  - The final sound mix
  - The premiere
  * The animatic (with the recorded or scratch voice track)
  - The script
choice: What is an animatic?
  - The finished, colored film
  * Storyboard drawings timed to a soundtrack, played like a rough movie
  - A list of all the animators
  - A computer program that draws inbetweens
choice: Why do studios usually record the dialogue before animating?
  - Actors are only available at the end
  - Sound is cheaper to make later
  - The animators need silence while they work
  * Animators match the mouth shapes and acting to the real voice

## Main: Animatic timing
fill: An animatic has 12 panels and runs 30 seconds at 24 fps. The animatic is {720} frames long.

## Main: Lip sync
match: Write the letter of the mouth shape for each sound.
  - M, B, P (as in "mom") = Lips pressed together
  - O, OO, W (as in "who") = Lips pushed out in a small round shape
  - F, V (as in "five") = Top teeth resting on the bottom lip
  - EE (as in "see") = Lips stretched wide, teeth close together

## Stretch
fill:
  - A character says "Mop!" starting 1.5 seconds into the shot. At 24 fps, the word starts {36} frames into the shot.
  - The first mouth shape for "Mop" is {lips pressed together}.
write: A director wants to skip the animatic to save time. Explain one risk of skipping it.
  lines: 3
  key: Without an animatic, timing problems (scenes too long, jokes that land late, shots that do not cut together) are found only after the expensive animation is done, so animators may spend weeks on shots that get cut or must be redone.

# 21. Where Will It Show?
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare ways to share media by how many people it reaches and how much control I keep.
directions: Read the four ways to share a student film. Answer the questions. Compare the places, then recommend where to show the film in the scenario and give reasons.
mistakes: Students often think "online" simply means "the most viewers." Many posted videos get few views, and once a video is online, others can copy, share or comment on it, so the maker has less control. Festivals reach fewer people but carefully chosen ones. Check that students consider permission: anyone shown in a film should agree to how it is shared, and schools have rules for showing students.
notes: Reach means how many people, and which people, can see it. Control means how much the maker decides: who watches, when, whether people can comment or copy it, and whether it can be taken down. Policies differ by festival and platform; students should always read the rules. Many festivals ask that a film has not been shown or posted publicly before, so posting first can rule out some festivals.

## Read: Four places to show a film
example: **Film festival:** a judged event; you submit your film and a jury picks what screens. **School broadcast:** the school's morning show or in-school channel. **Library screening:** an event at a public library, open to the community. **Online platform:** a video-sharing site or app where anyone with the link or the app might see it.

## Warm up
choice: Which place gives the maker the most control over who watches and when?
  - An online platform with comments on
  * A library screening the maker helps run
  - A video shared by strangers
  - None of them
choice: Why might posting a film online first be a problem for a student filmmaker?
  - Online videos are always low quality
  - Libraries will not show films
  * Some festivals only accept films that have not been shown publicly
  - It is against the law to post films
choice: Once a video is posted publicly online, what is true?
  * Others may copy, share or comment on it even if you take it down later
  - Deleting it always removes every copy
  - Only your friends can see it
  - It cannot be shared
choice: What should you have before sharing a film that shows other people?
  - A bigger screen
  - A festival award
  * Their permission, and the school's when classmates appear
  - A longer film

## Main: Compare
example: For each place, write its reach (who can see it), who controls it, what it is good for and what to watch out for. **Model, school broadcast:** reach: students and staff at one school; control: the school and the broadcast team decide; good for: quick feedback from peers; watch out for: only one school sees it, and it must follow school rules.
write: **Film festival**
  lines: 2
  key: Reach: a smaller audience of judges, filmmakers and fans, sometimes from far away. Control: the festival decides whether and when it screens. Good for: recognition and feedback from experts. Watch out for: entry rules, deadlines, fees and premiere rules.
write: **Library screening**
  lines: 2
  key: Reach: local families and community members who attend. Control: high; the makers and library choose the time, introduce the film and answer questions. Good for: a live audience and discussion. Watch out for: small audience; needs planning and publicity.
write: **Online platform**
  lines: 2
  key: Reach: possibly very large and worldwide, but many videos get few views. Control: low; comments, copying and sharing are hard to control and deleting does not remove copies. Good for: easy access for anyone, anytime. Watch out for: permission from people shown, privacy, platform rules and festival premiere rules.

## Main: Recommend
example: **Scenario:** Your class made a 6-minute documentary about the school garden. Classmates appear in it, and you want neighbors, families and garden volunteers to see it. You might also enter it in a youth festival next spring.
write: Where should the class show it first? Give at least two reasons using reach and control.
  lines: 5
  key: A strong answer chooses the library screening (or the school broadcast) first: it reaches the community who cares about the garden, the class controls the event, classmates' families can give permission, and it keeps the film unposted so the festival is still possible. A student who picks online should address permission, comments and the festival rule.

## Stretch
write: Design a release plan in three steps for the documentary, from first showing to widest audience.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: 1) school broadcast to classmates for feedback; 2) library screening for families and volunteers; 3) submit to the youth festival, then post online with permission after the festival if its rules allow. Each step widens reach while the class keeps control early.

# 33. The War of the Worlds Broadcast
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can analyze how news coverage reported an event and judge whether the coverage was exaggerated.
directions: Read the background and the three sources. Answer the questions about the newspaper coverage and the later evidence. Then rate how exaggerated the "panic" story was and defend your rating with evidence.
mistakes: Students jump to "it was all fake." The evidence supports a middle answer: many listeners were confused or scared and thousands called police, radio stations and newspapers, but the nationwide mass panic described in headlines did not happen. Watch for students who treat a big number of articles as proof of a big panic; it shows how big the story was in the papers, not how big the panic was.
notes: Facts here come from the historical record as summarized by media historians Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow (2013) and from Hadley Cantril's study The Invasion from Mars (1940). The broadcast said it was a drama at four points: the beginning, before and after the middle break, and the end. The headline and subheadline are quoted from the New York Times front page of October 31, 1938; the other sources are summaries, not quotations. Many papers ran the same Associated Press story, which was built from separate local anecdotes. Radio had taken advertising money from newspapers during the Great Depression, which historians see as one reason papers played up the story.

## Background
text: On Sunday night, October 30, 1938, CBS radio's *Mercury Theatre on the Air*, led by Orson Welles, performed a version of H. G. Wells's novel *The War of the Worlds*. The first half sounded like a music program interrupted by news bulletins about Martians landing in Grovers Mill, New Jersey. The show was announced as a drama at the start, around the middle and at the end.

## Sources
source: The New York Times, front page, October 31, 1938: the main headline, then the subheadlines
  "Radio Listeners in Panic, Taking War Drama as Fact"
  "Many Flee Homes to Escape 'Gas Raid From Mars'"
  "Phone Calls Swamp Police at Broadcast of Wells Fantasy"
source: Summary of the next-day coverage in many newspapers (not a quotation)
  Stories said "thousands" were frightened. They described police and newspaper phone lines jammed with calls, people leaving their homes, and scattered scenes in cities across the country. Most stories came from the same news service and were built from separate local anecdotes collected late on a Sunday night.
source: Summary of evidence gathered later (not a quotation)
  [1] That night a ratings company phoned about 5,000 homes. About 2 percent said they were listening to the play; a far more popular comedy show was on another network at the same time. [2] A 1940 Princeton study estimated about 6 million heard the show and 1.2 million were frightened or disturbed, but it counted "excited" and "disturbed" listeners as panicked. [3] The study's researchers found no admissions for shock at a Newark hospital during the broadcast, and New York hospitals reported no extra admissions that night. [4] Within three weeks, newspapers had printed at least 12,500 articles about the broadcast.

## Questions
choice: Which words in the headlines make the event sound biggest?
  - "Radio," "Broadcast," "Wells"
  * "Panic," "Flee," "Swamp"
  - "Listeners," "Fact," "Fantasy"
  - "Times," "Police," "Homes"
choice: What kind of evidence did most next-day stories use?
  * Separate local stories and anecdotes
  - National surveys of listeners
  - Hospital records
  - Recordings of the show
choice: Why might newspapers in 1938 have wanted to make radio look dangerous?
  - Newspapers owned the radio stations
  - Radio was not allowed to air news
  - Orson Welles worked for a newspaper
  * Radio was taking advertising money from newspapers
choice: What problem did historians find in the 1940 study?
  - It was written before the broadcast
  - It surveyed only Martians
  * It counted excited or disturbed listeners as panicked
  - It found no listeners at all
write: Compare evidence [1] with the headline "Radio Listeners in Panic." What does the ratings survey suggest?
  lines: 3
  key: Only about 2 percent of surveyed homes were listening, so the audience was small; most people were listening to something else or nothing. A small audience makes a nationwide panic unlikely, even if some listeners were scared. The headline turns some listeners into "radio listeners" in general.

## Judge it
text: **1** = the coverage was accurate. **3** = real fear, but its size was stretched. **5** = the panic was almost entirely invented.
table: Circle your rating | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5
  - How exaggerated was the panic story? | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5
write: Defend your rating with at least three pieces of evidence from the sources, including one that points the other way.
  lines: 6
  key: Answers vary; most evidence supports a 3 or 4. A strong answer: some fear was real (thousands of calls, some people left their homes, the study found many "frightened or disturbed"), but the coverage exaggerated it: the ratings survey found about 2 percent listening, the next-day stories were stitched-together anecdotes, no extra hospital admissions were found, the study counted "excited" as "panicked," and papers had a reason to make radio look irresponsible.
