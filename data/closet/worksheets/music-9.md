# 1. Circle of Fifths Builder
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, compass or a round object to trace
ican: I can build the circle of fifths with every major key, its relative minor and its key signature.
directions: Learn the order of sharps and flats and two shortcuts. Complete the key chart for all 15 major keys. Then draw the circle from your chart.
mistakes: Students mix up the order of sharps (F C G D A E B) and flats (B E A D G C F); they are the same letters in reverse. Watch for relative minors a minor 3rd up instead of down (the relative minor of C is A, not Eb). F major has one flat (Bb), so F is not on the sharp side even though it begins the order of sharps.
notes: The circle clockwise from 12 o'clock: C, G, D, A, E, B/Cb, F#/Gb, C#/Db, Ab, Eb, Bb, F. Three positions hold two names for the same sounding key (enharmonic keys): B (5 sharps) and Cb (7 flats), F# (6 sharps) and Gb (6 flats), C# (7 sharps) and Db (5 flats). That is how 12 positions hold 15 major keys. A relative minor starts on the 6th note of its major scale, 3 half steps below the tonic, and shares the key signature. Shortcuts: in a sharp key, the last sharp is a half step below the tonic; in a flat key, the next-to-last flat names the key (F major, with one flat, is the one to memorize).

## Warm up: Orders and shortcuts
fill:
  - Order of sharps: F C {G} D {A} E {B}
  - Order of flats: B {E} A {D} G C {F}
  - Each step clockwise around the circle goes up a perfect {5th} and adds one {sharp} (or takes away one flat).
  - A major key's last sharp is D#. Go up a half step: the key is {E} major.
  - A major key's flats are Bb Eb Ab Db. The next-to-last flat names the key: {Ab} major.
  - The relative minor of a major key starts {3} half steps below its tonic.

## Stretch: Neighbors
write: Why are keys next to each other on the circle called closely related? Use G major and D major as your example.
  lines: 3
  key: Neighbors differ by only one sharp or flat, so they share six of their seven notes. G major (F#) and D major (F# C#) differ only in C versus C#, which makes it easy to modulate between them.
write: Which three positions on the circle hold two keys, and why do both names work?
  lines: 3
  key: Positions 5 (B and Cb), 6 (F# and Gb) and 7 (C# and Db). The two keys in each pair sound the same on a keyboard (they are enharmonic) but are spelled with sharps on one side of the circle and flats on the other.

# 7. Closely Related Keys Grid
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; the circle of fifths from sheet 1 (optional)
ican: I can use the circle of fifths to find the five keys closely related to any major or minor key.
directions: Study the example. Fill the grid with the 5 closely related keys of each of 6 tonics. Then use the clues and your grid to solve the mystery keys.
mistakes: Students forget the relative keys of the neighbors (for G major, B minor and A minor) or write the parallel minor (G minor) instead of the relative minor. For a minor tonic, the dominant key counted here is minor (A minor's is E minor, not E major). Quick check: every closely related key has at most one sharp or flat more or fewer than the tonic key.
notes: The five closely related keys of a major key are its relative minor, its dominant and subdominant keys, and their relative minors: the keys of the chords ii, iii, IV, V and vi. For a minor key they are its relative major, its minor dominant and minor subdominant, and their relative majors: III, iv, v, VI and VII. On the circle they are the neighbors one step each way plus the relative key of all three. Mystery keys: B minor (2 sharps), C major (no sharps or flats), D minor (1 flat). Stretch 1 accepts E-flat major or C minor (both 3 flats).

## Warm up: How to find them
example: G major has 1 sharp. Its neighbors on the circle are D major (one step clockwise, the dominant) and C major (one step counterclockwise, the subdominant).
  Add the relative minor of all three: E minor, B minor and A minor.
  The 5 closely related keys of G major: **E minor, D major, C major, B minor, A minor.**
fill:
  - The relative minor of a major key starts on scale degree {6} of the major scale.
  - The dominant key is one step {clockwise} on the circle of fifths.
  - A closely related key has at most {one} sharp or flat more or fewer than the tonic key.

fill:
  - I am a minor key closely related to both G major and D major. I have 2 sharps. I am {B minor}.
  - I am a major key closely related to both A minor and D minor. I have no sharps or flats. I am {C major}.
  - I am a minor key closely related to both F major and Bb major. I have 1 flat. I am {D minor}.

  - E minor

  * A minor and G major

## Stretch
write: A piece in Bb major (2 flats) moves to a closely related key that has 3 flats. Name a key it could be and explain where it sits on the circle.
  lines: 2
  key: E-flat major, the subdominant, one step counterclockwise from Bb; or C minor, the relative minor of E-flat major. Both have 3 flats.
write: Why do Classical composers usually modulate to closely related keys?
  lines: 3
  key: Closely related keys share six of their seven notes and differ by only one sharp or flat, so a chord that belongs to both keys can act as a pivot and the move sounds smooth. The listener still hears the home key nearby, which makes the return feel natural.

# 20. The Rite of Spring Premiere
minutes: 40
materials: pencil; a recording of The Rite of Spring (the opening and "Augurs of Spring") if you can play one
ican: I can use primary sources to explain how The Rite of Spring broke with what audiences expected in 1913.
directions: Read the background and the three accounts, adapted from reviews written after the ballet was first performed. Answer the questions. Then connect what the audience heard to what was new in the music.
mistakes: Students often say the audience protested because the music was "loud." Push for specific features: the high, strained bassoon, the stamping chord with accents in unexpected places, two clashing chords at once, meters that change almost every measure. Others assume every critic hated the work; Linor praised the performance and blamed the noise on the audience.
notes: Facts: premiere 29 May 1913 at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in Paris, by the Ballets Russes, with choreography by Vaslav Nijinsky, conducted by Pierre Monteux. The three accounts are paraphrased from English translations of the reviews, not exact quotations; check a published translation before quoting them word for word. Historians still debate how much of the uproar was about the music: Richard Taruskin argued that Nijinsky's heavy, stamping choreography shocked people more than the score, and rival groups in the audience came ready to boo or to cheer. The opening bassoon melody comes from a published collection of Lithuanian folk songs, which Stravinsky acknowledged. The "Augurs of Spring" chord is usually described as an E-flat dominant seventh stacked on an F-flat major triad. If you can, play the opening and "Augurs of Spring" before the questions.

## Background
text: On 29 May 1913, the Ballets Russes danced a new ballet in Paris: The Rite of Spring, with music by Igor Stravinsky and dances by Vaslav Nijinsky. It shows an imagined ancient ritual to welcome spring, in which a chosen young woman dances until she dies. The first night became one of the most famous scandals in music history: parts of the audience laughed, shouted and argued so loudly that the dancers had trouble hearing the orchestra.

## Three accounts
source: Account A. Adapted from the review by Henri Quittard, Le Figaro (Paris), after the premiere. Paraphrased from an English translation.
  The critic called the new work a laborious and childish kind of barbarism. [1]
  He wrote that he was sorry to see an artist as gifted as Stravinsky take part in such a disconcerting adventure. [2]
source: Account B. Adapted from the review by Gustav Linor, Comoedia (Paris), after the premiere. Paraphrased from an English translation.
  Linor wrote that the theater had never been so full or so splendid, with crowds filling the stairways and corridors. [1]
  He praised the performance as superb. The noise, he wrote, was a rowdy debate between two badly behaved groups in the audience. [2]
source: Account C. Adapted from a review in The Times (London), after the ballet came to London later in 1913. Paraphrased.
  The critic thought the music, the story and the dancing fit together well, but was less impressed by the music itself. [1]
  If Stravinsky wanted to be truly primitive, the critic suggested, he would have been wiser to write the ballet for nothing but drums. [2]

## What the audience heard
table: Moment | What happens in the music
  - The opening | A lone bassoon plays a folk melody at the very top of its range, so high it sounds strained and strange.
  - "Augurs of Spring" | Strings stamp one harsh chord over and over, with loud accents landing where you do not expect them.
  - The harmony | Two chords from different keys sound at the same time (E-flat seventh on top of F-flat major), so they clash.
  - "Sacrificial Dance" (the end) | The meter changes almost every measure, so you cannot settle into a steady beat.

## Main: Read the sources
choice: Which account blames the uproar on the audience rather than on the music?
  - Account A
  * Account B
  - Account C
choice: In Account C, the joke about drums suggests the critic heard the music as mostly about ...
  - long, singing melodies
  - soft, delicate harmonies
  * rhythm and pounding accents
choice: What did the critic in Account A object to?
  * The music seemed crude and primitive on purpose.
  - The orchestra played out of tune.
  - The theater was too crowded.
write: Explain what musically shocked the audience in 1913. Use two features from the chart and connect each to one of the accounts.
  lines: 6
  key: Model: Listeners expected melodies, steady meters and chords that resolve. Instead the "Augurs of Spring" stamps one dissonant chord, two keys at once, with accents in unpredictable places, and the final dance changes meter nearly every bar, so there is no steady pulse to hold onto. That is why the Le Figaro critic heard "barbarism" (Account A) and the Times critic said the ballet might as well be scored for drums (Account C): rhythm and clashing sound had replaced the melody and harmony audiences valued.

## Stretch
write: Account B was written by a critic who praised the performance. Why is it still useful evidence, and what is one limit of it?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows that not everyone rejected the work, and it describes the crowded hall and two groups arguing, which suggests some people came ready to protest or to cheer. One limit: it is one critic's view, and he may have wanted to defend the company, so it tells us less about what the protesters actually heard.
write: Some historians argue that Nijinsky's dancing shocked the audience more than the music. What evidence would help you decide?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: accounts that say exactly when the noise started (during the orchestral opening, before the dancers appeared, or once the stamping dances began); later concert performances of the music alone and how audiences reacted to them (a 1914 Paris concert performance was a success); reviews that discuss the dancing in detail versus the music.

# 21. Music History Checkpoint 9
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can place works in their eras, recognize each era's style traits, and explain how music changed between eras.
directions: Write each work's letter on the timeline where you think it belongs, match each to its era, identify eras from short style descriptions, and then explain one change between eras with evidence.
mistakes: Students often memorize dates but cannot hear or describe the difference between eras; push for a trait (texture, instruments, form) in every answer. Watch for Baroque and Classical traits swapped: the basso continuo and terraced dynamics are Baroque; balanced phrases, crescendos and sonata form are Classical.
notes: Era dates are approximate and overlap: Medieval about 500 to 1400, Renaissance about 1400 to 1600, Baroque about 1600 to 1750, Classical about 1750 to 1820, Romantic about 1820 to 1900, then the 20th century. Timeline dates: Palestrina's Pope Marcellus Mass was published in 1567; Handel's Messiah was first performed in Dublin in 1742; Mozart finished Eine kleine Nachtmusik in 1787; Beethoven's Fifth Symphony premiered in 1808; Tchaikovsky's The Nutcracker premiered in 1892; Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring premiered in Paris in 1913. Students place the works from what they know of the eras, so accept any letter that lands in the right era rather than requiring the exact year. Beethoven's Fifth is left out of the era match on purpose: it is often taught as a bridge between Classical and Romantic, a good discussion point.

  lines: 6

# 33. Deliberate Practice Log
minutes: 15
materials: pencil, metronome or metronome app, your recital music
ican: I can practice deliberately: set a specific goal, measure tempo and accuracy, and choose a strategy on purpose.
directions: Do the warm up in class. Then log each practice session for one week: the section you worked on, your starting and ending tempo, the strategy you used, and how many of 5 tries were clean. Finish with the weekly review.
mistakes: Students log the whole piece every day ("played it through"). A deliberate session targets one short section with a measurable goal. Watch for ending tempos that jump far above the starting tempo with low accuracy: speed with 1 clean try of 5 is not progress. Accuracy means clean tries out of 5 at the ending tempo.
notes: Deliberate practice, in the research of Anders Ericsson and others, means focused work on a specific weakness with immediate feedback, rather than repeating what already sounds good. Keep the log to 5 minutes a day: students fill one row right after practicing. Collect it after a week and look for one pattern to praise (a strategy that clearly raised accuracy). The log feeds sheet 34, Tempo Progress Graph.
