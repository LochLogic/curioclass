# 1. Health-Related or Skill-Related?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can sort the components of fitness into health-related and skill-related and name a lifetime activity that builds each.
directions: Cut out the 11 component cards and glue each under health-related or skill-related. Then match each component to the lifetime activity that builds it best.
mistakes: Power and muscular strength get confused: strength is how much force a muscle can make, power is strength used fast (strength times speed). Muscular strength and muscular endurance also blur: strength is one big effort, endurance is many repeats without tiring. Agility is changing direction quickly, not just running fast.
notes: The two lists follow the standard fitness model used in US physical education: 5 health-related components (cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition) and 6 skill-related components (agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed). Body composition is discussed here only as a component of fitness; do not ask students about their own bodies. Many activities build several components; the match uses the clearest pairing for each.

## Warm up
choice: Health-related components are linked to ...
  * Staying healthy and lowering the risk of disease, for anyone
  - Winning games and playing sports well
  - Only athletes who compete
choice: Skill-related components help most with ...
  - Sleeping well
  * Performing sport and movement skills, such as returning a fast serve
  - Growing taller

## Main: Sort the components
sort: Glue each component card in its column.
  - Health-related: cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition
  - Skill-related: agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed

## Main: Match a lifetime activity
match: Write the letter of the activity that builds each component best.
  - cardiorespiratory endurance = Long, steady bike rides or jogs
  - muscular strength = Lifting heavy weights for a few repetitions
  - muscular endurance = A bodyweight circuit with many repetitions
  - flexibility = A yoga class
  - body composition = Regular aerobic and strength activity over months
  - agility = An ultimate game full of quick cuts and changes of direction
  - balance = Stand-up paddleboarding
  - coordination = Juggling three balls
  - power = Jumping to spike a volleyball
  - reaction time = Returning fast shots in table tennis
  - speed = Sprinting a 100-meter race

## Stretch
write: Choose one activity you could still enjoy at age 50. Name three components it builds and explain how.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: tennis builds cardiorespiratory endurance (long rallies keep the heart working), agility (quick changes of direction to reach the ball) and coordination (timing the racket with the ball).

# 7. Warm-Up Science
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain what a dynamic warm-up does to my heart, lungs, muscles, joints and nerves, and why that lowers injury risk.
directions: Draw a simple body outline and put each of the six numbered body parts where it belongs. Then match each body part to what a dynamic warm-up does to it. Then use the science to explain the risks of skipping a warm-up.
mistakes: Students often think a warm-up is the same as static stretching. A dynamic warm-up keeps the body moving (leg swings, high knees, skips, lunges with a twist) and builds from easy to close to game speed. Watch for "warm muscles are stronger" as the only reason: the bigger reasons are more blood and oxygen to the muscles, more elastic muscles and smoother joints. Some put the nerves label only on the brain; nerves run through the spinal cord to every muscle.
notes: The six changes are standard exercise physiology: heart rate and the heart's output rise; blood vessels in working muscles widen (vasodilation); breathing gets faster and deeper; muscle temperature rises, so muscles contract faster and are more extensible; synovial fluid in the joints becomes less thick and spreads over the joint surfaces; nerve signals travel faster in warmer tissue. Diagram key: 1 heart in the center-left of the chest; 2 a line along an arm or leg (blood vessels); 3 both sides of the chest (lungs); 4 a large muscle such as the thigh or calf; 5 the knee, hip or shoulder; 6 the brain and spinal cord, with lines out to a limb. Accept any sensible placement. Keep the diagram generic: students draw an outline, not their own body.

## Warm up
choice: A dynamic warm-up is ...
  * Moving through active stretches and gradually faster movements that rehearse the activity
  - Holding each stretch still for 30 seconds before playing
  - Sprinting all-out right away to get loose quickly
choice: Which move belongs in a dynamic warm-up for soccer?
  - A seated hamstring stretch held for 45 seconds
  - Full-speed shots on goal in the first minute
  * Leg swings, high knees and side shuffles that build to a jog with the ball

## Main: Label the diagram
example: The six body parts to place on your diagram: **1** heart, **2** blood vessels in a working muscle, **3** lungs, **4** a large muscle, **5** a joint, **6** nerves (brain and spinal cord out to a limb).
draw: Draw a front-view body outline (head, trunk, arms, legs). Write each number 1 to 6 at its spot and draw a short line to it. | 3.4
match: Match each body part to what it does to get you ready.
  - Heart = Pumps more blood each minute, so more oxygen leaves for the muscles
  - Blood vessels in working muscles = Widen to let more blood and oxygen reach the muscle
  - Lungs = Take in more oxygen with faster, deeper breaths
  - Muscles = Warm up, so they contract faster and stretch further before they tear
  - Joints = Move smoothly through a fuller range as the fluid inside spreads over the joint surfaces
  - Nerves = Carry signals faster, so reactions and coordination sharpen

## Main: Why it matters
text: Word bank: **strain, range of motion, oxygen, reaction, gradually**
fill:
  - Wider blood vessels deliver more {oxygen} to working muscles.
  - Thinner, well-spread synovial fluid lets the knee move through its full {range of motion}.
  - Faster nerve signals improve {reaction} time.
  - A warm-up should raise intensity {gradually}, from easy to close to game speed.
  - A warm, elastic muscle is less likely to suffer a {strain} (a pulled muscle).

## Stretch
write: Kai sits on the bench for 20 minutes, then sprints at full speed in his first minute on the field. Use at least two body parts from your diagram to explain the risks.
  lines: 4
  key: Kai's muscles are still cool and less elastic, so a sudden full-speed stride could strain a hamstring. His heart rate and blood flow have not risen, so his muscles get less oxygen at first and he tires fast. His joints have not moved through their range, and his slower nerve signals make his reactions less sharp. A quick dynamic warm-up on the sideline (jog, leg swings, strides) would lower the risk.

# 20. Teamwork Observation
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, clipboard, a class game to watch (about 8 minutes)
ican: I can observe a team and record teamwork and sportsmanship behaviors as data, not opinions.
directions: First practice on a written play-by-play: make one tally mark for each behavior. Then watch one team during a real class game and tally every time you see each behavior. Use your tallies to give the team specific feedback.
mistakes: Observers drift to watching the ball and the best player; remind them to watch the whole team, including players away from the ball. Some tally a behavior twice when two things happen at once; one action, one tally, in the box that fits best. Watch for feedback that is opinion ("they were good teammates") instead of evidence ("4 tallies for encouraging after mistakes").
notes: Practice key: Communicates 2 (events 1 and 7), Encourages a teammate 2 (2 and 8), Shares the ball 1 (3), Accepts the call calmly 1 (4), Respects opponents 2 (5 and 9), Covers for a teammate 1 (6). Total 9. Event 10 (Dev argues the travel call) is the one moment that breaks sportsmanship and gets no tally. For the live game, give each observer one team and a set time (two 4-minute halves work well), and keep observers off the court and out of the way of play. The live tallies vary; check that totals add up and that written feedback cites numbers.

## Warm up: Practice tally
text: **Team Blue, 2 minutes of a class basketball game.** (1) Ana calls out "Screen left!" to warn Ben. (2) Ben misses a layup; Cara claps and says, "Good look, get the next one." (3) Dev drives, sees Eli open under the basket, and passes instead of forcing a shot. (4) The referee calls a foul on Ben; he raises his hand and jogs back. (5) A Red player trips; Cara helps her up. (6) Eli gets beaten on defense; Ana slides over to guard his player until he recovers. (7) Ana shouts, "Ball! I'm open!" (8) Dev high-fives Eli after Eli scores. (9) Ben tells a Red player "Nice shot" after she scores. (10) Dev argues with the referee about a traveling call.

  lines: 2

## Stretch: Feedback from data
write: Write two sentences of feedback to the team: one strength and one goal, each with a number from your tallies.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: "You encouraged each other 6 times, which kept everyone in the game after mistakes. Next game, try to share the ball more: we saw only 2 passes to the open teammate in 8 minutes."
write: Why is a tally sheet a fairer way to give feedback than your overall impression of the team?
  lines: 2
  key: Tallies record what actually happened, so feedback rests on evidence, not on who is loud or popular, and a team can compare the numbers next time to see if it improved.

# 21. Handling a Bad Call
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can respond respectfully to an official's call I disagree with.
directions: Read the scenario. Use the frames to write a response that stays calm, asks respectfully, and moves on. Then write your own response to a second call.
mistakes: Students often write a polite sentence that still argues ("With all due respect, you're wrong"). A respectful question asks for information, not a reversal. Watch for responses that ignore the next play: moving on is part of the skill.
notes: In volleyball, only the game captain may speak to the first referee, and only to ask about how a rule was applied; a player who argues can be warned or penalized. Most sports have a similar rule that one captain or the coach speaks for the team. Officials make mistakes; the point of the sheet is that a calm response protects the team and the game whether the call was right or wrong.

## Warm up
choice: A referee calls a foul you are sure you did not commit. Which response is most respectful?
  - Throw your hands up and shout, "That's terrible!"
  * Take a breath, nod, and let your captain ask about the call at the next break.
  - Tell your teammates the referee is against your team.
  - Argue until the referee changes the call.
choice: Why should only one player, usually the captain, talk to the official?
  - So the captain can argue louder
  * So the team speaks calmly with one voice and the game can keep moving
  - Because officials do not like other players

## Main: The scenario
text: **The call:** Maya serves in a close volleyball match. Her team is sure the ball landed on the line, which counts as in. The line judge signals out, and the point goes to the other team. Maya's teammates groan and one starts to walk toward the referee.
write: **Stay calm.** What should Maya and her teammates do in the next 10 seconds?
  lines: 3
  starter: First, I would ...
  key: Take a deep breath, keep hands down and face calm, and stop the teammate from walking over; say "We're fine, next point."
write: **Ask.** Write the exact words the team captain could use to ask the referee about the call.
  lines: 3
  starter: Excuse me, ...
  key: "Excuse me, could you tell us what the line judge saw on that serve? We thought it touched the line." (A calm question asking for information, at a break in play.)
write: **Move on.** What could Maya say to her team to get ready for the next point?
  lines: 2
  starter: Next point, ...
  key: "Next point, let's go. Same serve, I've got it." Any reset that turns attention to the next play.

## Stretch: Your turn
text: **The call:** In a basketball game, the referee calls a traveling violation on Jamal, who believes he took only two steps. The other team gets the ball.
write: Write Jamal's full response: what he does, what (if anything) he or his captain says, and how he moves on.
  lines: 5
  key: Jamal takes a breath and hands the ball to the referee or sets it down; he does not argue. At the next dead ball the captain or coach asks, "Could you tell us what you saw on the travel?" Jamal hustles back on defense and focuses on stopping the next play.

# 33. Coaching Conversation
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can coach a partner's forehand error with a question, a short cue and a drill.
directions: Read the model conversation about a different forehand error. Then read the scenario and use the frames to write your own coaching conversation for Jordan. In the stretch, write a shorter one for Sam.
mistakes: The most common slip is a cue that is a sentence ("make sure your racket starts low and then you swing up while keeping your eye on the ball"). A cue is 2 to 5 words a player can think mid-swing. Others ask a yes or no question ("Did you swing flat?"), which tells instead of asks. Watch for drills with no number: a drill needs a set number of reps and a success target.
notes: Jordan's error: a level swing with the racket face tilted down at contact and a low finish sends the ball into the net. The usual fix is a low-to-high swing path that brushes up the back of the ball and finishes high over the opposite shoulder. Sam's error: contact is late (beside or behind the body), which for a right-handed player sends the ball wide to the right; the fix is meeting the ball out in front of the front hip, helped by an earlier shoulder turn. Accept other accurate cues ("brush up," "finish high," "meet it out front"). The same frame works in pickleball or badminton.

## Warm up
choice: Which is the best coaching cue for Jordan to think about during the swing?
  - "Try not to hit the net so much."
  * "Low to high."
  - "Your racket path needs to go from below the ball to above it while you keep your wrist firm and watch the ball."
choice: Why start with a question instead of telling Jordan the error?
  * A question helps Jordan notice and feel the error, so Jordan can fix it without you later.
  - Questions are faster than giving a cue.
  - Telling a player the error is not allowed in tennis.

## Model: a different error
example: **Error seen:** Lee faces the net with square shoulders and swings with the arm only.
  **Praise:** "Your footwork to the ball was quick every time."
  **Question:** "Where were your shoulders pointing when the ball bounced?"
  **Cue:** "Turn early, shoulder to the ball."
  **Drill:** "I'll drop-feed 10 balls. Turn your shoulders before the bounce. Goal: 7 of 10 with a full turn."
  **Check in:** "What felt different on the good ones?"

## Main: Coach Jordan
text: **Scenario:** You watch Jordan hit 10 forehands from a feed; 6 go into the net. You notice Jordan's swing stays level, the racket face is tilted down at contact, and the swing finishes at chest height.
write: **Praise.** Start with one specific thing Jordan does well.
  lines: 2
  starter: I noticed that you ...
  key: "I noticed that you get set early and your eyes stay on the ball through contact."
write: **Question.** Ask an open question that helps Jordan notice the error.
  lines: 2
  starter: What did you notice about ...
  key: "What did you notice about where your racket started and finished on the ones that hit the net?"
write: **Cue.** Write a cue of 2 to 5 words.
  lines: 1
  key: "Low to high, finish high." (or "Brush up the back.")
write: **Drill.** Describe the drill: what Jordan does, how many reps, and the success target.
  lines: 3
  starter: For this drill, ...
  key: "For this drill, I drop-feed 10 balls from beside you. Start the racket below the ball and finish over your opposite shoulder. Goal: 7 of 10 clear the net and land in the court, then we try it from a partner's feed."
write: **Check in.** Write the question you ask after the drill.
  lines: 2
  key: "How did low to high feel compared with your old swing? Which balls cleared the net best?"

## Stretch: Coach Sam
text: Sam is right-handed. Sam's forehands keep going wide to the right, and you see contact happening beside or behind Sam's body.
write: Write Sam's question, cue and drill.
  lines: 4
  key: Question: "Where was the ball, compared with your front hip, when you hit it?" Cue: "Meet it out front." Drill: 10 drop-feeds with a cone on the court where contact should be, in front of the front hip; goal 7 of 10 land in the court, cross-court.
