# 1. Health-Related or Skill-Related?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can sort the components of fitness into health-related and skill-related and name a lifetime activity that builds each.
directions: Cut out the 11 component cards and glue each under health-related or skill-related. Then match each component to the lifetime activity that builds it best.
mistakes: Power and muscular strength get confused: strength is how much force a muscle can make, power is strength used fast (strength times speed). Muscular strength and muscular endurance also blur: strength is one big effort, endurance is many repeats without tiring. Agility is changing direction quickly, not just running fast.
notes: The two lists follow the standard fitness model used in US physical education: 5 health-related components (cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition) and 6 skill-related components (agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed). Body composition is discussed here only as a component of fitness; do not ask students about their own bodies. Many activities build several components; the match uses the clearest pairing for each.

## Warm up
choice: Health-related components are linked to ...
  * Staying healthy and lowering the risk of disease, for anyone
  - Winning games and playing sports well
  - Only athletes who compete
choice: Skill-related components help most with ...
  - Sleeping well
  * Performing sport and movement skills, such as returning a fast serve
  - Growing taller

## Main: Sort the components
sort: Glue each component card in its column.
  - Health-related: cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition
  - Skill-related: agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed

## Main: Match a lifetime activity
match: Write the letter of the activity that builds each component best.
  - cardiorespiratory endurance = Long, steady bike rides or jogs
  - muscular strength = Lifting heavy weights for a few repetitions
  - muscular endurance = A bodyweight circuit with many repetitions
  - flexibility = A yoga class
  - body composition = Regular aerobic and strength activity over months
  - agility = An ultimate game full of quick cuts and changes of direction
  - balance = Stand-up paddleboarding
  - coordination = Juggling three balls
  - power = Jumping to spike a volleyball
  - reaction time = Returning fast shots in table tennis
  - speed = Sprinting a 100-meter race

## Stretch
write: Choose one activity you could still enjoy at age 50. Name three components it builds and explain how.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: tennis builds cardiorespiratory endurance (long rallies keep the heart working), agility (quick changes of direction to reach the ball) and coordination (timing the racket with the ball).

# 21. Handling a Bad Call
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can respond respectfully to an official's call I disagree with.
directions: Read the scenario. Use the frames to write a response that stays calm, asks respectfully, and moves on. Then write your own response to a second call.
mistakes: Students often write a polite sentence that still argues ("With all due respect, you're wrong"). A respectful question asks for information, not a reversal. Watch for responses that ignore the next play: moving on is part of the skill.
notes: In volleyball, only the game captain may speak to the first referee, and only to ask about how a rule was applied; a player who argues can be warned or penalized. Most sports have a similar rule that one captain or the coach speaks for the team. Officials make mistakes; the point of the sheet is that a calm response protects the team and the game whether the call was right or wrong.

## Warm up
choice: A referee calls a foul you are sure you did not commit. Which response is most respectful?
  - Throw your hands up and shout, "That's terrible!"
  * Take a breath, nod, and let your captain ask about the call at the next break.
  - Tell your teammates the referee is against your team.
  - Argue until the referee changes the call.
choice: Why should only one player, usually the captain, talk to the official?
  - So the captain can argue louder
  * So the team speaks calmly with one voice and the game can keep moving
  - Because officials do not like other players

## Main: The scenario
text: **The call:** Maya serves in a close volleyball match. Her team is sure the ball landed on the line, which counts as in. The line judge signals out, and the point goes to the other team. Maya's teammates groan and one starts to walk toward the referee.
write: **Stay calm.** What should Maya and her teammates do in the next 10 seconds?
  lines: 3
  starter: First, I would ...
  key: Take a deep breath, keep hands down and face calm, and stop the teammate from walking over; say "We're fine, next point."
write: **Ask.** Write the exact words the team captain could use to ask the referee about the call.
  lines: 3
  starter: Excuse me, ...
  key: "Excuse me, could you tell us what the line judge saw on that serve? We thought it touched the line." (A calm question asking for information, at a break in play.)
write: **Move on.** What could Maya say to her team to get ready for the next point?
  lines: 2
  starter: Next point, ...
  key: "Next point, let's go. Same serve, I've got it." Any reset that turns attention to the next play.

## Stretch: Your turn
text: **The call:** In a basketball game, the referee calls a traveling violation on Jamal, who believes he took only two steps. The other team gets the ball.
write: Write Jamal's full response: what he does, what (if anything) he or his captain says, and how he moves on.
  lines: 5
  key: Jamal takes a breath and hands the ball to the referee or sets it down; he does not argue. At the next dead ball the captain or coach asks, "Could you tell us what you saw on the travel?" Jamal hustles back on defense and focuses on stopping the next play.
