# 1. Striking and Fielding Games Compared
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare striking and fielding games by how they bat, field, make outs and score.
directions: Fill in the blanks in the comparison chart. Then use the chart to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students assume every striking and fielding game works like softball. In cricket, batters bat in pairs, there is no foul territory, and a batter may hit and choose not to run. In rounders the ball is bowled underarm, and a "rounder" is the name of the score, not just the game.
notes: Rules summarized from fastpitch softball, common school kickball rules, the Laws of Cricket and the Rounders England rules. Kickball rules vary most: many leagues add a strikeout or foul-ball limit, so the chart lists only the outs every version shares. In cricket, an innings ends when 10 of the 11 batters are out, because the last batter has no partner, or when the agreed overs are used up.

## Warm up
choice: What is the main goal of the batting (or kicking) team in every striking and fielding game?
  * Hit the ball and run to score runs.
  - Send the ball over a net so it cannot be returned.
  - Get the ball into the other team's goal.
choice: What do fielders try to do?
  - Score as many runs as they can.
  * Catch, stop and throw the ball to get batters out and stop runs.
  - Guard a goal with their hands.

## Main: Comparison chart
table: Game | Batting | Fielding | Outs | Scoring
  - Softball | Batter hits a ball pitched {underhand} with a bat | 9 fielders | Strikeout, caught fly ball, tag or force out; {3} outs end a half-inning | A run when a runner touches all four bases, ending at home plate; 7 innings
  - Kickball | Kicker kicks a ball {rolled} by the pitcher | Fielders cover bases and the outfield, as in softball | Caught fly ball, tag or force out; 3 outs end a half-inning | A run for each runner who reaches home
  - Cricket | Two batters bat at once, in a {pair}; the bowler delivers the ball overarm without straightening the elbow, usually so it bounces before the batter | 11 fielders, including the bowler and the wicketkeeper | Bowled, caught, run out, stumped, leg before wicket; the innings ends when {10} batters are out | Runs by running between the wickets; {4} if the ball reaches the boundary along the ground, {6} if it clears it in the air
  - Rounders | Batter hits a ball bowled {underarm} with a short, round bat | 9 fielders, including a bowler, a backstop and post fielders | Caught ball; the runner is out if a fielder touches the post they are running to with the ball first | A {rounder} for getting all the way around the four posts after hitting the ball

## Main: Use the chart
write: Which two games on the chart are most alike? Give two reasons from the chart.
  lines: 3
  key: Softball and kickball: both have four bases, three outs a half-inning, outs by caught fly ball, tag or force, and a run for reaching home; the difference is kicking a rolled ball instead of hitting a pitched one.
write: In cricket there is no foul territory: the batter can hit the ball in any direction. How might that change where the fielders stand?
  lines: 3
  key: Fielders must spread all the way around the batter, including behind, instead of only in front as in softball.
write: Name one way scoring in rounders is different from scoring in softball.
  lines: 2
  key: In rounders a score is a "rounder," made by getting around all four posts after hitting; softball runners score a run by touching home plate, and can stop on a base and score later.

## Stretch
write: Cricket batters may hit the ball and decide not to run. Softball batters must run when they hit a fair ball. How does this rule change a batter's choices?
  lines: 3
  key: A cricket batter can play safe shots just to protect the wicket and wait for a good ball to score from; a softball batter has to run on every fair hit, so every swing puts them at risk of an out.

# 7. Scorebook Basics
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can score a half-inning of softball or baseball with position numbers and out symbols, and total the runs.
directions: Learn the position numbers and the scorebook marks. Then read the half-inning, one batter at a time, and fill in the scorebook: the mark for each play, the out number if the batter was out, and whether the batter scored. Total the runs, hits and runners left on base.
mistakes: Students write the fielder's name or position letter instead of the number, or write a groundout as one number. A groundout lists every fielder who touched the ball in order: shortstop to first base is 6-3. Students also forget that a runner who scores counts for the batter who is running, not the batter who hit them in.
notes: Key marks: Ava 6-3 (out 1), Ben 1B, Cora BB, Dev 2B (Ben and Cora score), Eli F8 (out 2), Fay 1B (Dev scores), Gus K (out 3). Runs 3 (Ben, Cora, Dev), hits 3 (Ben, Dev, Fay; a walk is not a hit), left on base 1 (Fay). Scorebooks vary: some write a flyout as just 8, circle the out number, or use a backward K for a called third strike; accept any common form. Stretch key: 4-6-3 is a double play: the second baseman fields the ball and throws to the shortstop covering second for one out, and the shortstop throws to first for a second out.

## Warm up: Position numbers
text: Write each position's scorebook number (1 to 9).
fill:
  - Shortstop {6}
  - Center field {8}
  - Catcher {2}
  - First base {3}
  - Right field {9}
  - Pitcher {1}
  - Third base {5}
  - Left field {7}
  - Second base {4}
example: **Marks:** 1B single, 2B double, 3B triple, HR home run, BB walk, K strikeout. **Groundout:** the fielders' numbers in order, such as 6-3 (shortstop throws to first). **Flyout:** F and the fielder's number, such as F7. **Outs:** write the out number (1, 2 or 3) and circle it.

## Main: The half-inning
text: 1. **Ava** hits a ground ball to the shortstop, who throws to first base. Out. 2. **Ben** hits a single to left field. 3. **Cora** walks. Ben goes to second. 4. **Dev** hits a double to right field. Ben and Cora both score. Dev stops at second. 5. **Eli** hits a fly ball, caught by the center fielder. Out. Dev stays at second. 6. **Fay** hits a single to center field. Dev scores. Fay stops at first. 7. **Gus** strikes out swinging. Out. The inning is over.
table: Batter | Scorebook mark | Out number | Scored a run?
  - Ava | {6-3} | {1} | {no}
  - Ben | {1B} | {none} | {yes}
  - Cora | {BB} | {none} | {yes}
  - Dev | {2B} | {none} | {yes}
  - Eli | {F8} | {2} | {no}
  - Fay | {1B} | {none} | {no}
  - Gus | {K} | {3} | {no}
fill:
  - Runs this half-inning: {3}
  - Hits this half-inning: {3}
  - Runners left on base: {1}
write: Cora reached base and scored, but she did not get a hit. Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: Cora walked (BB). A walk puts the batter on first base, but the batter did not hit the ball, so it is not counted as a hit. She scored later on Dev's double.

## Stretch
write: A scorebook shows 4-6-3 next to a batter, with two outs marked. What happened on that play?
  lines: 3
  key: A double play: the batter hit a ground ball to the second baseman (4), who threw to the shortstop (6) covering second base to force out the runner, and the shortstop threw to first base (3) to get the batter out.

# 20. Plan Peer Review
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can review a fitness plan against a rubric and suggest specific improvements.
directions: Read Sam's 8-week plan and goal. Score the plan on each rubric point from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong), and give evidence from the plan. Then write two suggestions that would raise the lowest scores.
mistakes: Students score the plan on how hard it is ("lots of running, so it is good") instead of each criterion. Evidence must point to a week or a line in the plan. Students also miss the safety problem in "push through any pain"; pain is a signal to stop, not a goal.
notes: Key (accept one point off with good evidence): balance 2 (cardio every phase and stretching after sessions, but strength is dropped in weeks 5 to 8); progression 1 (jumps from 25 minutes 3 days a week to 40 minutes 6 days a week; a common rule of thumb is to raise total time by about 10 percent a week); specificity 3 (each phase names the activity, days and minutes); rest 1 (no rest day in weeks 7 and 8); safety 1 (no warm-up, and "push through any pain"); goal fit 3 (the run-walk to steady jogging builds toward running a mile). Total 11 of 18. Suggestions should be specific changes, for example keep 2 rest days a week and increase jogging time by about 5 minutes a week.

## The plan
text: **Sam's goal:** run 1 mile without stopping by the end of week 8.
table: Weeks | Sam's plan
  - 1 and 2 | Walk-jog intervals, 20 minutes, Mon, Wed, Fri. Push-ups and squats, 10 minutes, Tue and Thu. Stretch for 5 minutes after each session.
  - 3 and 4 | Walk-jog intervals, 25 minutes (more jogging each week), Mon, Wed, Fri. Push-ups and squats, 10 minutes, Tue and Thu. Stretch after each session.
  - 5 and 6 | Jog 40 minutes, every day except Sunday. Stretch after each session.
  - 7 and 8 | Jog 45 minutes, every day. Push through any pain to stay on track.

## Main: Score the plan
table: Criterion | What a 3 looks like | Score (1 to 3) | Evidence from the plan
  height: 0.6
  - Balance | Cardio, strength and flexibility all included | {2} | {Strength dropped in weeks 5 to 8}
  - Progression | Small, steady increases | {1} | {25 to 40 minutes and 3 to 6 days at once}
  - Specificity | Activity, days and minutes for each week | {3} | {Every phase lists them}
  - Rest | Rest or easy days every week | {1} | {No rest day in weeks 7 and 8}
  - Safety | Warm-up, cool-down, stop when it hurts | {1} | {No warm-up; "push through any pain"}
  - Goal fit | The activities build toward the goal | {3} | {Jogging builds toward the mile run}
fill:
  - Sam's total score: {11} out of 18
write: Write two specific suggestions that would raise Sam's lowest scores.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: 1. Raise jogging time slowly, about 5 minutes a week (25, 30, 35, 40), and keep 3 or 4 days a week. 2. Add a 5-minute walking warm-up before every session, keep at least 2 rest days each week, and stop and tell an adult if anything hurts.

## Stretch
write: Sam says, "More is always better." Use the rubric to respond in two or three sentences, as a respectful peer reviewer.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: More can help, but only if your body has time to adapt. Rest days are when muscles recover and get stronger, and big jumps raise the risk of injury, which would stop your training altogether. A steady plan will get you to the mile.

# 21. Specific to the Sport
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can design exercises that follow the specificity principle for a sport.
directions: Read the example. Answer the warm-up questions. Then design 3 exercises for each sport that train the movements and energy that sport really uses, and explain the match.
mistakes: Students pick exercises that are healthy but not specific, such as long slow jogging for a sprinter or bicep curls for a soccer player. Ask: "Does this exercise use the same muscles, the same movement and the same kind of effort (short bursts or long and steady) as the sport?"
notes: The specificity principle: the body adapts to the exact kind of training it does, so training should match the sport's movements, muscles and energy demands. Model answers are in the key; accept any exercise whose match the student explains. Everyone should still keep general fitness; specificity is about making the training count for the sport.

## Warm up
example: **Basketball** needs jumping, quick sideways defense and repeated short sprints. Specific exercises: squat jumps or box jumps (jumping power), defensive slide drills (sideways speed), and shuttle sprints with short rests (repeated sprints).
choice: What does the specificity principle say?
  - Train every muscle the same amount, whatever your sport.
  * Your body adapts to the kind of training you do, so train the movements and energy your sport uses.
  - Do the hardest workout you can every day.
choice: Which exercise is most specific for a swimmer?
  - Running hills
  * Pulling resistance bands in the shape of the arm stroke
  - Bicep curls
  - Sitting toe touches
choice: Which exercise is most specific for a 100-meter sprinter?
  - A slow 5-mile jog
  * Short, all-out sprints with full rest between them
  - Yoga
  - Long bike rides

## Main: Design the exercises
table: Sport | What the sport demands | Exercise 1 | Exercise 2 | Exercise 3
  - Soccer | Repeated sprints, quick cuts, strong kicks, 90 minutes of running | | |
  - Volleyball | Jumping to spike and block, overhead arm swings, quick moves to the ball | | |
  - Distance running | Steady effort for a long time, strong calves and hips | | |
write: Pick one exercise for each sport and explain how it matches the sport's movement or energy.
  lines: 6
  key: Soccer: shuttle sprints with short recovery (repeated sprints), cone weave dribbling (quick cuts with the ball), single-leg lunges and hops (kicking and cutting strength). Volleyball: squat jumps and box jumps (jump height), band or medicine ball overhead throws (spike and serve arm swing), shuffle drills to a cone (quick moves to the ball). Distance running: long steady runs (endurance), tempo runs at race effort, calf raises and step-ups (calf and hip strength).

## Stretch
write: A soccer player only does heavy leg presses on a machine to get better at soccer. Using the specificity principle, explain what is missing and one exercise that would fix it.
  lines: 3
  key: Leg presses build leg strength, but soccer needs running, quick cuts, jumping and repeated sprints on one leg at a time while moving. Add shuttle sprints, cutting drills or single-leg hops so the training matches the game.

# 33. Coaching Dilemmas
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can choose a respectful, helpful coaching response for hard moments in peer coaching.
directions: You are coaching a partner or a small group in a skill drill. Read each dilemma card and choose the best coaching response. Then explain one choice.
mistakes: Students choose the response that ends the problem fastest (send the player away, ignore it) instead of the one that keeps the player learning. Ask: "Which response helps this player get better and keep trying?" For dilemma 6, any sign of injury goes to the teacher.
notes: Key: 1 B, 2 D, 3 A, 4 C, 5 A, 6 B. The best responses keep the focus on the skill, give the player a next step that fits them (easier or harder), protect respect in the group and send safety concerns to an adult. Discuss why each wrong choice falls short, for example ignoring a player who is ahead of the drill leads to boredom and off-task play.

## Main: Dilemma cards
choice: A player argues every time you give a cue: "I already know how!"
  - Tell them to sit out until they listen.
  * "Okay, show me three in a row with that cue. If you have it, we will move to a harder step."
  - Stop giving them cues.
  - Argue back until they agree.
choice: After missing twice, a player says, "I am terrible at this," and starts to walk away.
  - "Yeah, it is a hard skill. Maybe it is not for you."
  - "Just try harder."
  - Let them go and work with someone else.
  * "Your footwork was right both times. Let us try it from closer, then step back."
choice: One player is far ahead of the drill and has started goofing off.
  * Give them a harder version, such as using the weaker hand or adding a defender.
  - Tell them to slow down so the others can catch up.
  - Ignore it, since they already have the skill.
  - Send them to the side to wait.
choice: Two partners keep laughing at each other's misses.
  - Laugh along so they feel relaxed.
  - Split them up without saying why.
  * Remind them that feedback is about the skill, and have each give one specific tip.
  - Tell the teacher right away and stop the drill.
choice: A player is nervous to try in front of the whole group.
  * Let them practice first with one partner, then join the group.
  - Make them go first to get it over with.
  - Skip them for the whole drill.
  - Tell the group to cheer loudly when it is their turn.
choice: During the drill, a player says their wrist hurts.
  - Tell them to shake it off and keep going.
  * Have them stop and tell the teacher right away.
  - Have them switch to the other hand and keep going.
  - Tell them to rest for one turn, then keep going.

## Main: Explain
write: Pick one dilemma. Explain why your choice is better than one of the other choices.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: In dilemma 3, giving a harder version keeps the player learning and on task; telling them to slow down wastes their practice time and they will keep goofing off.

## Stretch
write: Write a new dilemma you have seen in a game or drill, and the best coaching response.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: A player always passes to the same friend. Response: "Nice passes. This round, try to pass to each teammate once."
