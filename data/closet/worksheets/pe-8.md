# 1. Striking and Fielding Games Compared
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare striking and fielding games by how they bat, field, make outs and score.
directions: Fill in the blanks in the comparison chart. Then use the chart to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students assume every striking and fielding game works like softball. In cricket, batters bat in pairs, there is no foul territory, and a batter may hit and choose not to run. In rounders the ball is bowled underarm, and a "rounder" is the name of the score, not just the game.
notes: Rules summarized from fastpitch softball, common school kickball rules, the Laws of Cricket and the Rounders England rules. Kickball rules vary most: many leagues add a strikeout or foul-ball limit, so the chart lists only the outs every version shares. In cricket, an innings ends when 10 of the 11 batters are out, because the last batter has no partner, or when the agreed overs are used up.

## Warm up
choice: What is the main goal of the batting (or kicking) team in every striking and fielding game?
  * Hit the ball and run to score runs.
  - Send the ball over a net so it cannot be returned.
  - Get the ball into the other team's goal.
choice: What do fielders try to do?
  - Score as many runs as they can.
  * Catch, stop and throw the ball to get batters out and stop runs.
  - Guard a goal with their hands.

## Main: Comparison chart
table: Game | Batting | Fielding | Outs | Scoring
  - Softball | Batter hits a ball pitched {underhand} with a bat | 9 fielders | Strikeout, caught fly ball, tag or force out; {3} outs end a half-inning | A run when a runner touches all four bases, ending at home plate; 7 innings
  - Kickball | Kicker kicks a ball {rolled} by the pitcher | Fielders cover bases and the outfield, as in softball | Caught fly ball, tag or force out; 3 outs end a half-inning | A run for each runner who reaches home
  - Cricket | Two batters bat at once, in a {pair}; the bowler delivers the ball overarm without straightening the elbow, usually so it bounces before the batter | 11 fielders, including the bowler and the wicketkeeper | Bowled, caught, run out, stumped, leg before wicket; the innings ends when {10} batters are out | Runs by running between the wickets; {4} if the ball reaches the boundary along the ground, {6} if it clears it in the air
  - Rounders | Batter hits a ball bowled {underarm} with a short, round bat | 9 fielders, including a bowler, a backstop and post fielders | Caught ball; the runner is out if a fielder touches the post they are running to with the ball first | A {rounder} for getting all the way around the four posts after hitting the ball

## Main: Use the chart
write: Which two games on the chart are most alike? Give two reasons from the chart.
  lines: 3
  key: Softball and kickball: both have four bases, three outs a half-inning, outs by caught fly ball, tag or force, and a run for reaching home; the difference is kicking a rolled ball instead of hitting a pitched one.
write: In cricket there is no foul territory: the batter can hit the ball in any direction. How might that change where the fielders stand?
  lines: 3
  key: Fielders must spread all the way around the batter, including behind, instead of only in front as in softball.
write: Name one way scoring in rounders is different from scoring in softball.
  lines: 2
  key: In rounders a score is a "rounder," made by getting around all four posts after hitting; softball runners score a run by touching home plate, and can stop on a base and score later.

## Stretch
write: Cricket batters may hit the ball and decide not to run. Softball batters must run when they hit a fair ball. How does this rule change a batter's choices?
  lines: 3
  key: A cricket batter can play safe shots just to protect the wicket and wait for a good ball to score from; a softball batter has to run on every fair hit, so every swing puts them at risk of an out.

# 21. Specific to the Sport
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can design exercises that follow the specificity principle for a sport.
directions: Read the example. Answer the warm-up questions. Then design 3 exercises for each sport that train the movements and energy that sport really uses, and explain the match.
mistakes: Students pick exercises that are healthy but not specific, such as long slow jogging for a sprinter or bicep curls for a soccer player. Ask: "Does this exercise use the same muscles, the same movement and the same kind of effort (short bursts or long and steady) as the sport?"
notes: The specificity principle: the body adapts to the exact kind of training it does, so training should match the sport's movements, muscles and energy demands. Model answers are in the key; accept any exercise whose match the student explains. Everyone should still keep general fitness; specificity is about making the training count for the sport.

## Warm up
example: **Basketball** needs jumping, quick sideways defense and repeated short sprints. Specific exercises: squat jumps or box jumps (jumping power), defensive slide drills (sideways speed), and shuttle sprints with short rests (repeated sprints).
choice: What does the specificity principle say?
  - Train every muscle the same amount, whatever your sport.
  * Your body adapts to the kind of training you do, so train the movements and energy your sport uses.
  - Do the hardest workout you can every day.
choice: Which exercise is most specific for a swimmer?
  - Running hills
  * Pulling resistance bands in the shape of the arm stroke
  - Bicep curls
  - Sitting toe touches
choice: Which exercise is most specific for a 100-meter sprinter?
  - A slow 5-mile jog
  * Short, all-out sprints with full rest between them
  - Yoga
  - Long bike rides

## Main: Design the exercises
table: Sport | What the sport demands | Exercise 1 | Exercise 2 | Exercise 3
  - Soccer | Repeated sprints, quick cuts, strong kicks, 90 minutes of running | | |
  - Volleyball | Jumping to spike and block, overhead arm swings, quick moves to the ball | | |
  - Distance running | Steady effort for a long time, strong calves and hips | | |
write: Pick one exercise for each sport and explain how it matches the sport's movement or energy.
  lines: 6
  key: Soccer: shuttle sprints with short recovery (repeated sprints), cone weave dribbling (quick cuts with the ball), single-leg lunges and hops (kicking and cutting strength). Volleyball: squat jumps and box jumps (jump height), band or medicine ball overhead throws (spike and serve arm swing), shuffle drills to a cone (quick moves to the ball). Distance running: long steady runs (endurance), tempo runs at race effort, calf raises and step-ups (calf and hip strength).

## Stretch
write: A soccer player only does heavy leg presses on a machine to get better at soccer. Using the specificity principle, explain what is missing and one exercise that would fix it.
  lines: 3
  key: Leg presses build leg strength, but soccer needs running, quick cuts, jumping and repeated sprints on one leg at a time while moving. Add shuttle sprints, cutting drills or single-leg hops so the training matches the game.
