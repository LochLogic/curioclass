# 1. Philosophers Ask
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify the enduring question about justice that a philosophical text explores and trace how thinkers answer it differently.
directions: Read the three public domain excerpts. Mark the key claim in each. Answer the questions, then name the enduring question each thinker explores and compare their answers.
mistakes: Students often treat Thrasymachus's claim as Plato's own view. In the Republic, Socrates spends the rest of the dialogue arguing against it; Plato puts the claim in a challenger's mouth so it can be tested. Others read Mill as saying government may never act; his limit is on power used against a person "against his will," and preventing harm to others is exactly when he says it may.
notes: All three texts are in the public domain. Excerpt 1 is from Benjamin Jowett's 1871 translation of Plato's Republic, Book I; translations differ, so check the wording against the Jowett edition you use. Excerpt 2 is from John Stuart Mill, On Liberty (1859), chapter 1, with the opening of the sentence cut ([...]). Excerpt 3 joins two sentences from Henry David Thoreau's essay published in 1849 as "Resistance to Civil Government," later known as "Civil Disobedience"; Thoreau spent a night in jail in 1846 for refusing to pay a poll tax, in protest against slavery and the war with Mexico.

## Read: Three excerpts
source: Plato, Republic, Book I (about 375 BCE), translated by Benjamin Jowett (1871). The speaker is Thrasymachus, who challenges Socrates.
  [1] "I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger."
source: John Stuart Mill, On Liberty, chapter 1 (1859)
  [2] "[...] the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."
source: Henry David Thoreau, "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849)
  [3] "Unjust laws exist: shall we be content to obey them, or shall we endeavor to amend them, and obey them until we have succeeded, or shall we transgress them at once?"
  [4] "Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison."

## Questions
write: In your own words, what does Thrasymachus claim justice is? Give a modern example of what he might point to.
  lines: 3
  key: Justice is whatever benefits those in power; rulers make rules that serve themselves and call obeying them "just." He might point to laws written to protect a powerful group's wealth or position.
write: Name the enduring question excerpt 1 explores.
  lines: 2
  key: Is justice a real standard of right, or just whatever the powerful decide? (Is justice more than the interest of the stronger?)
choice: According to Mill, when may power rightfully be used over a person against his will?
  - Whenever the majority votes for it
  - Whenever it would make the person happier or healthier
  * Only to prevent harm to others
  - Never, under any circumstances
write: Name the enduring question excerpt 2 explores, and give one current debate where it applies.
  lines: 3
  key: When may a community limit an individual's freedom? It applies to debates over seat belt or helmet laws, public health rules, or limits on speech that may cause harm.
write: In excerpt 3, Thoreau lists three choices. Name them in your own words.
  lines: 3
  key: Obey unjust laws and accept them; obey them while working to change them; or break them right away.
write: Name the enduring question excerpts 3 and 4 explore. What is Thoreau's own answer, based on excerpt 4?
  lines: 3
  key: What should a person do when the law is unjust? Thoreau's answer: a just person should refuse to go along, even if that means going to prison alongside those jailed unjustly.

## Compare
chart: Thinker | Enduring question | Answer the text gives | Who holds power in this view
  rows: 3
  key: Thrasymachus: what is justice? / it is the interest of the stronger / rulers. Mill: when may society limit a person? / only to prevent harm to others / the individual, except where others are harmed. Thoreau: what do we do about unjust laws? / resist them, even to prison / the individual conscience.
write: Mill and Thoreau both limit the power of government over individuals. How are their limits different?
  lines: 4
  key: Mill sets a rule for when power is legitimate (only to prevent harm to others); Thoreau focuses on the individual's duty when the law itself is unjust, saying conscience can require breaking it. Mill describes what government may do; Thoreau describes what a just person must do.

## Stretch
write: How might Thoreau answer Thrasymachus? Write two or three sentences in Thoreau's voice, using ideas from excerpts 3 and 4.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample student answer (not a Thoreau quotation): If justice were only what the strong command, no law could ever be unjust, yet unjust laws exist. The strong may imprison a just man, but they cannot make their rule right; his place in their prison shows where justice really stands.

# 7. Reading a Scholarly Abstract
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can read a scholarly abstract to find the research question, method, findings and limits before I decide to read the whole article.
directions: Read the three abstracts. Highlight the research question in each, then fill in the analysis chart. Answer the questions that follow.
mistakes: Students often copy the title as the research question; the question is what the study set out to find, usually in the purpose or objective sentence. Others read the findings of abstract A as proof that screens cause short sleep. A survey taken at one time can show a link, not a cause; abstract A says so in its own last sentence. Students also skip limits when an abstract does not label them; in abstract C the limits are in the last sentence, and in abstract B they follow the word "Limitations."
notes: All three studies, their numbers and the journals are made up for practice; they are written in the shape of real structured abstracts. Abstract A is a cross-sectional survey (one point in time), B is an experiment with classes randomly assigned, and C is a qualitative interview study. A follow-up: have students find one real abstract in a library database on their own topic and run it through the same chart.

## Read: Three abstracts
source: Abstract A. "Evening Screen Use and Sleep Duration in High School Students." *Journal of Youth Health Studies* (made up), 2025.
  **Purpose:** To examine whether screen use in the hour before bed is linked to shorter sleep on school nights. **Methods:** 1,214 students in grades 9 to 12 at six public high schools kept a daily online diary of bedtime screen use and sleep for one school week. **Results:** Students who reported screen use in the last hour before bed on all five nights slept on average 34 minutes less per night than students who reported none. The link held after accounting for grade level and school start time. **Conclusions:** Evening screen use is associated with shorter sleep. Because the data are self-reported and collected at one point in time, the study cannot show that screens cause shorter sleep.
source: Abstract B. "Practice Quizzing Versus Rereading in High School Biology." *Classroom Learning Research* (made up), 2024.
  **Objective:** To test whether short practice quizzes improve long-term memory of course material more than rereading. **Method:** Eight biology classes (186 students) at two high schools were randomly assigned, by class, to study one unit either by taking three short practice quizzes or by rereading notes for the same total time. All students took the same unit test one week and five weeks later. **Results:** Quizzing classes scored higher at one week (81% vs. 72%) and at five weeks (74% vs. 61%). **Conclusions:** Practice quizzing improved retention, and the gap grew over time. **Limitations:** One subject at two schools; teachers knew which method their classes used.
source: Abstract C. "'I Didn't Know I Could Ask': First-Generation Students and the Academic Library." *Library Practice Quarterly* (made up), 2026.
  **Aim:** To understand how first-generation college students describe their early experiences with the campus library. **Design:** Semi-structured interviews with 14 first-year students at one public university, analyzed for recurring themes. **Findings:** Three themes emerged: students were unsure what librarians do; most first used the library as a quiet place to study rather than for research help; and students who had one positive meeting with a librarian went back for help again. **Conclusions:** Early outreach that introduces students to librarians may increase research help-seeking. Findings come from a small group of volunteers on one campus and may not describe other students.

## Main: Analysis chart
chart: Research question | Method (and sample) | Key finding | Limit
  labels: A; B; C
  height: 0.7
  key: A: Is bedtime screen use linked to shorter school-night sleep? / survey diary, 1,214 students, grades 9 to 12, six schools, one week / screens on all five nights: about 34 minutes less sleep per night / self-reported, one point in time, cannot show cause. B: Do practice quizzes improve long-term memory more than rereading? / experiment, 8 classes (186 students) randomly assigned by class / quizzing scored higher, 81 vs. 72 at one week and 74 vs. 61 at five weeks / one subject, two schools, teachers knew the method. C: How do first-generation students describe early library experiences? / qualitative interviews, 14 students, one university / unsure what librarians do; library as study space; one good meeting leads to more help-seeking / small volunteer group, one campus.

## Questions
choice: Which study's design gives the strongest support for a claim that one thing causes another?
  - Abstract A, because it has the largest sample
  * Abstract B, because classes were randomly assigned to a method
  - Abstract C, because it heard students in their own words
  - None; abstracts cannot describe causes
choice: A news headline says: "Study Proves Phones Steal 34 Minutes of Teen Sleep." What is the main problem with this headline?
  - The number 34 does not appear in abstract A.
  - Abstract A studied college students, not teens.
  - Abstract A measured sleep with brain scans, not diaries.
  * Abstract A found a link, and its authors say it cannot show cause.
write: In abstract B, the gap between the two groups grew from 9 points to 13 points. Why does that detail matter for a student deciding how to study for a final exam?
  lines: 3
  key: A final exam comes weeks after learning, and the advantage of quizzing got bigger with time (9 points at one week, 13 at five weeks), so quizzing is the better choice for long-term memory.
choice: Abstract C interviewed only 14 students. What can its findings best be used for?
  - Proving what most first-generation students in the country think
  * Understanding experiences in depth and suggesting ideas to test in a larger study
  - Measuring how many hours students spend in the library
  - Showing that outreach programs cause better grades

## Stretch
write: Write a two-sentence annotation of one abstract for an annotated bibliography: what the study did and found, then how you would use it and with what caution.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample for A: A survey diary of 1,214 high school students found that those who used screens in the hour before bed every school night slept about 34 minutes less than those who did not. I would use it to show a link between bedtime screens and short sleep, but not as proof that screens cause it, since the data are self-reported and from one week.

# 20. Source Authority Audit
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can audit sources for the author's authority and expertise, the publisher, and who paid for the work, and summarize what the audit shows.
directions: Read the six source descriptions about one question: do four-day school weeks help or hurt students? Fill in the audit for each source and rate it high, medium or low. Then answer the questions and write a summary of your findings.
mistakes: Students often rate a source high because it sounds confident or low because they dislike its side. Rate the source, not the opinion: who wrote it, what they know, who published it, and who paid. Others treat "no funding listed" as "no interests"; source 3 has no funder but also no name, so no one can check the author's expertise. Some miss that a real expert can still have an interest: the superintendent knows the district well and also made the decision being judged.
notes: Every source, person, company and group on this sheet is made up. Four-day school weeks are real and are studied by researchers; this sheet is about judging sources, not settling the question. Ratings in the key: 1 high, 2 medium (knowledgeable, but a membership group speaking for its members), 3 low, 4 low to medium (an outside interest and no peer review), 5 medium (real expertise, but judging her own decision), 6 high (public data from an agency with no stake in the answer). Accept a neighboring rating when the student's reasons are sound.

## Read: Six sources
source: Source 1: Journal article, "Four-Day School Weeks and Achievement in Rural Districts," 2025
  Two education economists at a state university compared test scores in 40 districts that switched to four-day weeks with similar districts that did not. Peer reviewed. Funded by a federal education research grant; the authors list no conflicts. Found small drops in math scores after the switch.
source: Source 2: Newsletter article, "Four Days Works for Teachers," 2026
  Published by a statewide teachers' association, a membership group that speaks for its members. Written by the association's policy director, a former teacher. Argues that four-day weeks help districts hire and keep teachers. Cites two district surveys.
source: Source 3: Blog post, "Why the Four-Day Week Is Ruining Our Kids," 2026
  No author name; posted on a personal blog. Says test scores "crashed everywhere" after four-day weeks began. Gives no sources or data.
source: Source 4: Report, "Fill the Fifth Day," 2025
  Published by Learning Group L, a policy group funded mostly by Tutor Company T, which sells Friday tutoring programs to four-day districts. Not peer reviewed. Recommends that every four-day district buy a fifth-day program.
source: Source 5: Newspaper interview, "Superintendent Defends Schedule," 2026
  A local newspaper interviews the superintendent who led her district's switch to a four-day week. She has run the district for nine years. She says attendance rose and the district saved on buses and heating.
source: Source 6: State Department of Education data brief, 2026
  Written by agency staff, paid for by the state. Lists attendance, test scores and budgets for every district, with four-day and five-day districts side by side. Draws no conclusion.

## Main: The audit
chart: Author and expertise | Publisher | Who paid | Interest in the answer? | Rating
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6
  height: 0.5
  key: 1: education economists, research experts / peer-reviewed journal / federal research grant / none stated / high. 2: policy director, former teacher / teachers' association / member dues / speaks for teachers, who may like the schedule / medium. 3: unknown author, expertise cannot be checked / personal blog / no one stated / unknown; no evidence given / low. 4: policy group staff / Learning Group L, no peer review / Tutor Company T / its funder sells the fifth-day programs it recommends / low to medium. 5: superintendent, nine years running the district / local newspaper / not a study; her own statements / she made the decision being judged / medium. 6: agency staff / state Department of Education / the state / little; reports data without a conclusion / high.

## Questions
choice: Which two sources have the strongest authority with the least interest in a particular answer?
  - Sources 2 and 5
  - Sources 3 and 4
  * Sources 1 and 6
  - Sources 4 and 5
choice: What is the main problem with Source 4?
  - It was published in 2025.
  - It is a report instead of a book.
  * Its funder profits if readers follow its recommendation.
  - It is about rural districts only.
write: The superintendent in Source 5 has real expertise. Why should a researcher still check her claims, and where on this list could they be checked?
  lines: 3
  key: She led the switch, so she has a reason to show it worked. Her claims about attendance and savings can be checked against the state data brief (Source 6), which lists attendance and budgets for every district.

## Summary
write: Write a short summary of your audit (3 to 4 sentences): which sources you would rely on, which you would use with caution, and which you would leave out, with a reason for each.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample: I would rely on Source 1, a peer-reviewed study with federal funding and no stated conflicts, and Source 6, the state's own data. I would use Sources 2 and 5 with caution: both authors know schools, but the teachers' association and the superintendent each have a stake in the schedule, so their claims need checking against the data. I would use Source 4 only to find its data, since its funder sells the programs it recommends, and I would leave out Source 3, which has no author and no evidence.

# 21. Corroboration Matrix
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can check key claims across several sources and flag the claims that are weakly supported or contested.
directions: Read the four sources about one local event. For each claim in the matrix, mark each source Y (supports it), N (contradicts it) or 0 (does not mention it). Then mark the verdict: keep if independent sources back the claim, or flag if it rests on one source or the sources conflict.
mistakes: Students often count sources without weighing them: a claim backed by three sources that all copy one press release is weaker than it looks. Here A (official minutes) and B (a news report) are independent enough to corroborate each other. Others mark the resident's blog N on every claim, but it only contradicts some claims and does not mention others; read for what each source actually says.
notes: The town, people, newspaper, company and event are all made up. Verdicts in the key: claims 1 to 3 are corroborated (keep) by the official minutes, the newspaper and (for 1 and 2) the builder; claim 4 rests on one source and is contradicted by the blog, so flag it; claim 5 is contradicted by the builder's later press release, so flag it and look for a newer official update. Note for discussion: the builder's release is the most recent source and the builder knows its own schedule, but it also has reasons to promise a date it can meet.

## Read: Four sources
source: Source A: Carver Falls City Council, meeting minutes, May 12, 2026
  The council voted 5 to 2 to close the Elm Street Bridge on June 1, after a state inspection rated the bridge deck in poor condition. Repairs are estimated at $4.2 million, paid from the state bridge fund and city reserves. Work is scheduled to finish by December 2026.
source: Source B: Carver Falls Herald, "Elm Street Bridge to Close June 1," May 13, 2026
  The council voted 5 to 2 on Tuesday to close the Elm Street Bridge starting June 1. A state inspection last month found its deck in poor condition. The city estimates repairs at $4.2 million and hopes to reopen the bridge by the end of the year. Two marked detours will add about 10 minutes to most drives across town.
source: Source C: "Bridge Scam," a resident's blog post, May 20, 2026
  They're closing the Elm Street Bridge in July, and it's going to cost us $10 million! The real reason is that the mayor wants a new shopping center on the other side. The detours will add an hour to every trip.
source: Source D: Builder R, press release, June 3, 2026
  Builder R is proud to begin work on the Elm Street Bridge, which closed to traffic on June 1. The $4.2 million repair will be complete by spring 2027.

## Main: The matrix
table: Claim | A | B | C | D | Verdict
  - 1. The bridge closed on June 1. | {Y} | {Y} | {N} | {Y} | {keep}
  - 2. The repairs cost about $4.2 million. | {Y} | {Y} | {N} | {Y} | {keep}
  - 3. A state inspection found the deck in poor condition. | {Y} | {Y} | {0} | {0} | {keep}
  - 4. Detours add about 10 minutes. | {0} | {Y} | {N} | {0} | {flag}
  - 5. The bridge will reopen by the end of 2026. | {Y} | {Y} | {0} | {N} | {flag}
text: Note on claim 3: Source C gives a different reason (a shopping center) but does not deny the inspection.

## Questions
write: Why is claim 4 flagged even though no reliable source contradicts it?
  lines: 2
  key: Only one source (B) supports it, so it is not corroborated; the blog's "an hour" contradicts it, though weakly. It needs a second source, such as the city's traffic office.
write: Sources A and D disagree about claim 5. Give one reason to trust each, then say what you would do next.
  lines: 4
  key: A is the official record; D is newer (June 3) and comes from the company doing the work, which knows its schedule. Next, look for a newer city update or council minutes, or contact the city, before stating a reopening date.
write: Source C claims the "real reason" for the closing is a shopping center. How strong is that claim? Use the matrix.
  lines: 3
  key: Weak: no other source mentions a shopping center, C gives no evidence, and C is wrong about the date and the cost (claims 1 and 2), which lowers its credibility.
choice: Sources A and B agree on almost everything. What should a careful researcher ask before counting them as two separate confirmations?
  - Which source is longer?
  - Which source was written first in the morning?
  * Did the newspaper report on its own, or simply repeat the council's minutes?
  - Does the newspaper have a website?

## Stretch
write: Write two sentences for a report on the bridge that state only what the sources support, and signal any uncertainty.
  lines: 4
  key: Sample: The Elm Street Bridge closed on June 1, 2026, after a state inspection found its deck in poor condition, and repairs are estimated at $4.2 million. City records set a December 2026 finish, but the builder has since said work will last until spring 2027.

# 33. Citation Escape Challenge
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a style guide or the library's citation help page (optional)
ican: I can apply MLA and APA citation rules and fair use factors to solve citation problems.
directions: Solve the five problems. Each one gives you one digit. Write the digits in order in the lock at the bottom to escape. Check your code with the hint before you call your teacher over.
mistakes: Problem 1: students write every author's name; APA 7 uses the first surname and "et al." for three or more authors in every in-text citation. Problem 2: students carry the "et al." rule into the reference list, but the APA 7 reference list names up to 20 authors. Problem 3: students file "The Bridge Builders" under T; MLA alphabetizes a work with no author by its title, skipping A, An and The. Problem 4: a quote of exactly four lines stays in the paragraph; MLA sets off prose quotations that run more than four lines.
notes: Rules used: APA Publication Manual, 7th edition (in-text citations for three or more authors; up to 20 authors in a reference entry); MLA Handbook, 9th edition (alphabetizing titles; block quotations for prose longer than four lines); the four fair use factors in US copyright law, 17 U.S.C. section 107, in the order the law lists them. The code is 16423 and its digits add to 16. All names and titles are made up. For a real escape room feel, put the code on a small lock box or an envelope with a prize sheet inside.

## Warm up
example: Each answer is a single digit. Problem 1's answer is the first digit of the code, problem 2's is the second, and so on.

## Main: Five locks
text: **Problem 1 (APA 7, in-text).** An article by Chen, Ortiz, Walker and Ng was published in 2023. Write the parenthetical in-text citation: {(Chen et al., 2023)}
fill: How many author surnames appear in your citation? {1}
text: **Problem 2 (APA 7, reference list).** A journal article has six authors. In the APA 7 reference list entry, how many authors' surnames and initials are listed?
fill: Number of authors listed: {6}
text: **Problem 3 (MLA 9, works cited order).** Put these entries in works cited order: Bright, Ana. / Baker, Tom. / *The Bridge Builders* (no author). / Brown, Leo. / Bauer, Kim. / Bell, Sara.
fill: *The Bridge Builders* goes in position number {4}
text: **Problem 4 (MLA 9, block quotations).** Four quotations from a novel will take these lengths in your paper: 3 lines, 5 lines, 4 lines, 7 lines.
fill: How many of them must be set off as block quotations? {2}
text: **Problem 5 (fair use).** US law lists four fair use factors, in this order: (1) the purpose and character of the use, (2) the nature of the copyrighted work, (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used, (4) the effect on the market for the work. A student copies an entire three-minute song, start to finish, into a video she posts publicly.
fill: Which factor number most clearly weighs against fair use because of how much was taken? {3}

## The lock
table: Digit 1 | Digit 2 | Digit 3 | Digit 4 | Digit 5
  height: 0.5
  - {1} | {6} | {4} | {2} | {3}
text: Hint: the five digits add up to 16. If yours do not, recheck your work.
