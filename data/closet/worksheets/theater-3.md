# 1. Actor's Toolbox: Voice, Body, Imagination
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can name an actor's three tools and use each one to show a character.
directions: Match each actor's tool to what it does. Draw an actor and label where each tool lives. Then plan how you would use each tool to play the Hare from the fable The Tortoise and the Hare.
mistakes: Students often label imagination on the hands or feet. Imagination lives in the mind: picturing the character's world and believing it. Some students describe what the Hare does in the story (he naps) instead of how an actor would show it with voice or body; ask "What would the audience see and hear?"
notes: The Tortoise and the Hare is one of Aesop's fables (public domain): a boastful Hare races a slow Tortoise, naps halfway because he is sure he will win, and the Tortoise keeps going and wins. Tell or read it before the sheet. Labels on the drawing: voice at the mouth or throat, body at the arms, legs or whole figure, imagination at the head. Let three volunteers show their Hare plans at the end.

## Warm up: Match the tools
match: Match each tool to what an actor uses it for.
  - Voice = How loud, fast, high or low the character speaks
  - Body = How the character stands, walks and moves
  - Imagination = Picturing the character's world and believing it is real

## Main: Label the actor
draw: Draw an actor. Draw 3 arrows and label VOICE, BODY and IMAGINATION. | 2.6
fill:
  - The arrow for voice points to the {mouth} (or throat).
  - The arrow for body points to the arms, legs or {whole body}.
  - The arrow for imagination points to the {head} (the mind).

## Main: Plan your Hare
draw: Body: draw the Hare standing or moving. | 1.4
write: How does the Hare's body move?
  lines: 1
  key: Quick, bouncy hops; chest puffed out, chin up to show he is proud; a lazy stretch before the nap.
draw: Voice: draw a speech bubble with something the Hare says. | 1.2
write: How does the Hare's voice sound?
  lines: 1
  key: Fast, loud and bragging; a big yawn and a slow, sleepy voice at nap time.
draw: Imagination: draw what the Hare pictures in his mind. | 1.2
write: What do you imagine to help you play the Hare?
  lines: 1
  key: Picturing the finish line close by, the crowd cheering for him, the soft grass under a shady tree where he naps.

## Stretch
write: Now you play the Tortoise. Change one tool. What would you do differently?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: body moves slow and steady with heavy, careful steps; voice is calm and low; I imagine the long road and keep my eyes on the finish.

# 7. Inside and Outside Character Map
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can describe a character from the outside (looks, moves, sounds) and the inside (wants, feels).
directions: Read the fable. Fill the character map for the Crow: the outside (how it looks, moves and sounds) and the inside (what it wants and feels). Give a detail from the story for each, then add an actor's choice you would make.
mistakes: Students fill the "outside" with feelings ("she looks proud") and leave the "inside" empty. Outside is what an audience can see and hear; inside is what the Crow wants and feels, which an actor must show through the outside. Watch for "wants" written as an object only ("cheese"): push for the bigger want (to be admired, to be called the Queen of Birds).
notes: The Fox and the Crow is one of Aesop's fables, retold here in Curio Class's own words (about 190 words). The story links to sheet 2, where students marked the Fox's and Crow's lines for volume, pace and pitch. After the map, have pairs perform the moment the Crow opens her beak, using one outside choice from their map. Model answers are in the key; accept any choice supported by the story.

## Read
passage: The Fox and the Crow
  A black Crow found a piece of cheese on a windowsill. She flapped up to a high branch, held the cheese tight in her beak, and fluffed her shiny feathers. "Mine," she thought. "All mine."
  A hungry Fox trotted by and sniffed the air. He looked up and saw the cheese. He sat down below the tree and smiled his widest smile.
  "Good morning, beautiful bird," he called. "What glossy feathers you have! What bright eyes! Surely a bird so lovely must have the sweetest voice in the forest. If only I could hear you sing just one song, I would know that you are the Queen of all Birds."
  The Crow puffed out her chest. She hopped from one foot to the other. She lifted her head high. The Queen of all Birds! She wanted everyone to hear her.
  She opened her beak and cried, "CAW! CAW!"
  Down fell the cheese. The Fox snapped it up in one bite.
  "Thank you," said the Fox, licking his lips. "Your voice is fine, dear Crow. It is your good sense that is missing. Never trust someone who praises you only to get something."

## Main: The Crow's character map
table: Part of the map | Detail from the story | My actor's choice
  height: 0.75
  - OUTSIDE: How she looks | {black, shiny, glossy feathers; bright eyes} | {fluff my arms like feathers, stand tall on the branch}
  - OUTSIDE: How she moves | {flaps up to a branch, fluffs her feathers, hops from foot to foot, lifts her head high} | {quick, proud hops and a chin held high}
  - OUTSIDE: How she sounds | {a loud, harsh "CAW! CAW!"} | {a big, scratchy caw that surprises the audience}
  - INSIDE: What she wants | {to keep the cheese, then to be admired as Queen of all Birds} | {hug the cheese close, then forget it when praised}
  - INSIDE: How she feels | {proud and greedy, then flattered, then foolish} | {chest puffed out, then a shocked face when the cheese falls}

## Questions
choice: Which line shows what the Crow wants most when the Fox praises her?
  - "Mine," she thought. "All mine."
  * The Queen of all Birds! She wanted everyone to hear her.
  - Down fell the cheese.
choice: Which is an OUTSIDE detail an audience can see?
  * She hopped from one foot to the other.
  - She wanted to be admired.
  - She believed the Fox.

## Stretch
write: The Crow's want changes in the middle of the story. What does she want at the beginning, and what does she want after the Fox speaks? How would you show the change onstage?
  lines: 4
  key: At first she wants to keep the cheese all to herself; after the Fox's praise she wants to be admired and heard. Onstage: hold the cheese tight and look around suspiciously at first, then slowly loosen my grip and turn toward the Fox, puffing up, until I forget the cheese and open my beak wide.

# 20. Toy Theater Set: The Park
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, crayons or colored pencils
ican: I can design a set that shows where a scene happens and how actors get onstage.
directions: Plan a paper toy theater set for the park scene of our class play. Learn the parts of the set, draw the backdrop and 2 set pieces inside the stage frame, and add arrows where actors enter. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often draw tiny pictures in the middle with an empty stage, or crowd the stage so actors have nowhere to stand. Remind them: the backdrop fills the back, set pieces sit on the stage floor, and the middle stays open for actors. Watch for entrance arrows drawn through the backdrop; actors enter from the wings at the sides (or from the front).
notes: Toy theaters (paper theaters with cut-out scenery and characters on sliders) were popular in Britain and Europe in the 1800s; families bought printed sheets, colored them and put on plays at home. For this sheet, students plan on paper; building it is optional (a shoebox on its side makes a stage). The park scene comes from the class play about a littered park (sheets 16 to 19). Accept any set that shows a park, keeps acting space open and has entrances at the wings.

## Warm up: Parts of a set
match: Match each part of a set to what it is.
  - Backdrop = A large painted picture across the back of the stage
  - Set piece = An object onstage, like a bench or a tree, that actors can use
  - Wings = The hidden spaces at the sides where actors wait to enter
  - Proscenium = The frame around the front of the stage, like a picture frame
  - Stage floor = Where the actors stand and move

## Main: Draw the set
draw: Inside the frame: the backdrop at the back, 2 set pieces on the floor, an open space in the middle, and arrows where actors enter from the wings. | 3.4
table: Part | What I drew | Why it helps the park scene
  height: 0.55
  - Backdrop | {trees, a path and a blue sky} | {shows the audience the scene is outdoors in a park}
  - Set piece 1 | {a park bench} | {a place for characters to sit and talk}
  - Set piece 2 | {an overflowing trash can} | {shows the problem: the park is littered}
  - Entrances | {one arrow from each wing} | {characters can arrive from different sides of the park}

## Questions
choice: Why should the middle of the stage stay mostly empty?
  - So the backdrop is easier to paint
  * So actors have room to stand and move where the audience can see them
  - So there is space for the audience
choice: The park worker enters from stage right. Stage right is ...
  - the audience's right
  * the actor's right as the actor faces the audience
  - the back of the stage

## Stretch
write: Choose one set piece that tells the audience about the park's problem. Explain what the audience will understand when they see it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: An overflowing trash can with cups and wrappers on the ground around it shows right away that the park is littered and that the trash is not being picked up, before any actor speaks.

# 21. One-Piece Costume Sheet
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, crayons or colored pencils
ican: I can design a costume piece that shows who a character is.
directions: Our class play, The Day the Bakery Oven Broke, has 4 characters from one neighborhood. Design one costume piece for each character. Then explain how it shows the character's job or personality.
mistakes: Students often draw a whole outfit; one piece (a hat, an apron, a vest, a bag) is the goal, and it must be something an audience can see from the back row. Watch for pieces that show nothing about the character ("a blue shirt"): ask "How would the audience know her job from that?"
notes: The play is an original premise for this sheet: early one morning the bakery oven breaks before the neighborhood breakfast, and the baker, the mail carrier, the crossing guard and the librarian work together to bake bread in other ovens up and down the street. One-piece costumes are cheap, quick to change and easy to make from things in a classroom: paper, cardboard, old shirts, tape. Accept any piece that clearly shows the job or personality and has a reason.

## Warm up
choice: Which one costume piece best shows the audience that a character is a crossing guard?
  - A pair of plain sneakers
  * A bright safety vest
  - A blue sweater
choice: The baker is cheerful and messy. Which costume piece shows both her job and her personality?
  - A clean black jacket
  * An apron with flour handprints all over it
  - A baseball cap

## Main: One piece each
draw: The baker: cheerful and a little messy | 1.4
write: How does your piece show the baker?
  lines: 1
  key: A floury apron or a tall puffy chef's hat shows the job; flour smudges or a bright color shows she is cheerful and messy.
draw: The mail carrier: knows everyone on the street | 1.4
write: How does your piece show the mail carrier?
  lines: 1
  key: A shoulder bag full of letters shows the job; letters with many names on them, or a friendly badge, show she knows everyone.
draw: The crossing guard: careful and loud | 1.4
write: How does your piece show the crossing guard?
  lines: 1
  key: A bright vest or a big stop sign shows the job; a whistle on a cord shows he is careful and loud.
draw: The librarian: quiet but full of ideas | 1.4
write: How does your piece show the librarian?
  lines: 1
  key: A cardigan with a pocket full of bookmarks or a book bag shows the job; a pin shaped like a light bulb, or glasses on a chain, shows she is full of ideas.

## Stretch
write: Pick one of your pieces. How would you make it from things in the classroom?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary: an apron from a paper grocery bag with flour drawn in chalk; a vest from an old T-shirt and yellow paper tape; a mail bag from a cardboard folder and yarn.

# 33. Star Ratings With Reasons
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can rate a performance on criteria and give a reason with "because."
directions: Read about 2 versions of the same scene from The Fox and the Grapes. Rate each version on the 3 criteria by coloring 1 to 5 stars. Then write a reason for each rating using "because" and a detail from the description.
mistakes: Students give a version 5 stars on everything because they liked it, or write reasons with no evidence ("because it was good"). Every reason needs a detail an audience could see or hear. Watch for ratings that do not match the reason (2 stars, "because the voice was clear").
notes: The Fox and the Grapes is an Aesop fable: a fox cannot reach some grapes and walks away saying they were probably sour anyway. The two versions are described here so the whole class rates the same performances; if your class performed its own versions (sheets 30 and 31), rate those instead. The key ratings are a model; accept other ratings when the reason cites a detail that supports it.

## The two versions
text: **Version A (slow and sad):** The Fox enters slowly, looking up at the grapes. Each jump is smaller and more tired. She speaks clearly and loudly enough to hear in the back row. After the last jump, she sits with her head down for a long, quiet moment, then whispers, "They were probably sour anyway." Some of the audience looked restless during the long pause.
text: **Version B (fast and silly):** The Fox races in, bouncing and spinning. She tries silly jumps: a hop on one foot, a cartwheel, a jump off a chair. The audience laughs a lot. She talks very fast, and during the laughter some lines get lost. At the end, she crosses her arms, sticks her nose in the air, and shouts, "Sour grapes! Who wants them?"

## Main: Rate and give reasons
table: Criterion | Version A | Version B
  height: 0.5
  - Clear voice | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆
  - Believable character | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆
  - Good pace | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ | ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆
write: Version A, clear voice: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 5 stars because she spoke clearly and loudly enough to be heard in the back row.
write: Version B, clear voice: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 2 stars because she talked very fast and some lines were lost under the laughter.
write: Version A, believable character: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 4 stars because her jumps got smaller and more tired, like a real hungry fox giving up.
write: Version B, believable character: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 3 stars because the cartwheel was funny but not something a fox would do; the crossed arms and nose in the air did show a proud fox.
write: Version A, good pace: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 3 stars because the long pause showed sadness, but some of the audience got restless.
write: Version B, good pace: I gave ... stars because ...
  lines: 1
  key: Model: 3 stars because it moved quickly and kept the audience laughing, but it rushed past lines we needed to hear.

## Stretch
write: Give one tip to the actor in the version you rated lower overall. Start with "Next time, try ..."
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Model for Version B: Next time, try waiting for the laughter to stop before saying your next line, so the audience hears every word.
