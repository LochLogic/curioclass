# 1. Life Transitions and Stress
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain why big life transitions cause stress and what helps people move through them, using quoted evidence.
directions: Read the passage; its paragraphs are numbered. Answer each question with a short quotation from the passage in quotation marks, followed by the paragraph number, then explain it in your own words.
mistakes: Students quote a whole paragraph or quote without explaining. A good quotation is a phrase or a sentence, followed by a sentence of their own. Watch for the idea that only bad events are stressful: paragraph 2 makes the point that happy changes count too.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 600 words). Sources behind it: Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe's Social Readjustment Rating Scale (1967) listed 43 life events, with death of a spouse at 100 points and marriage at 50; William Bridges' book Transitions (1980) describes endings, a neutral zone and new beginnings; Nancy Schlossberg's transition model names four S's: situation, self, support and strategies. The examples are fictional young adults. No question asks students about their own plans or history; if a student brings up a hard personal transition, follow up privately and involve the counselor as needed.

## Read
passage: Between One Life and the Next
  (1) In the year after high school, Aisha moved three hundred miles away to start college. Her friend Marcus stayed in his hometown and began a full-time job at a hospital. Both had looked forward to this moment for years. Both were surprised, a few months in, by how tired, unsettled and homesick they felt. Neither had done anything wrong. They were living through a transition, and transitions are among the most predictable sources of stress in a person's life.
  (2) In 1967, the psychiatrists Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe published a list of 43 life events and gave each one a score for how much readjustment it demands. The death of a spouse topped the list at 100 points. Marriage, usually a happy event, was given 50. Other items included changing schools, changing to a new line of work, retiring, moving and even an outstanding personal achievement. The scale has been criticized and revised over the years, but its central insight has held up: change itself is stressful, whether it is welcome or not. Every new situation asks people to give up old routines and build new ones, and that work takes energy.
  (3) The writer William Bridges drew a useful line between change and transition. A change is the outside event: the move, the graduation, the first day of work. A transition is the inner process of adjusting to it, and it often takes far longer. Bridges described three phases. First comes an ending, letting go of an old identity or way of life, even one that was ready to end. Next comes what he called the neutral zone, an uncomfortable in-between time when the old life is gone but the new one does not yet feel real. Only then comes a new beginning, when the new role starts to feel like one's own.
  (4) Much of the stress of a transition lives in that neutral zone. Aisha has a dorm room and a class schedule, but she does not yet have a person to sit with at dinner. Marcus has a badge and a paycheck, but he is no longer a student and does not yet feel like a professional. People in this phase may feel restless, lonely or unsure of themselves. These feelings are a normal part of the process, not proof that the decision was a mistake.
  (5) Why do some people move through transitions more easily than others? The counseling researcher Nancy Schlossberg pointed to four factors, often called the four S's. The situation matters: Was the change chosen? Is it happening alongside other changes? The self matters: a person's past experience with change and their outlook. Support matters: friends, family, mentors and communities who can listen and help. And strategies matter: the specific actions people take to cope.
  (6) Strategies can be simple. Building small routines, such as a regular wake-up time or a weekly call to an old friend, restores a sense of control. Joining one group, whether a club, a team or a faith community, speeds up the search for new support. Naming feelings, in a journal or to a trusted person, makes them easier to manage. Setting a few short-term goals turns a vague new life into concrete steps. Many colleges and workplaces offer counseling or mentoring precisely because they expect newcomers to struggle at first.
  (7) By spring, Aisha had joined a study group that met every Tuesday, and Marcus had found a coworker who remembered his own rough first year. Neither of their transitions was finished. But both had moved from the neutral zone toward a new beginning, carried less by luck than by the slow, ordinary work of building a new life.

## Questions
write: Why does the author begin with Aisha and Marcus being "surprised" by how they felt? Quote and explain.
  lines: 2
  key: "Neither had done anything wrong. They were living through a transition" (paragraph 1). The author shows that stress after a big change is normal and expected, even when the change was wanted.
write: What is the central insight of the Holmes and Rahe scale? Quote the sentence that states it.
  lines: 2
  key: "Change itself is stressful, whether it is welcome or not" (paragraph 2). Even happy events like marriage or an achievement require readjustment, which takes energy.
write: How does Bridges separate change from transition? Quote and explain.
  lines: 2
  key: "A change is the outside event... A transition is the inner process of adjusting to it, and it often takes far longer" (paragraph 3). The event happens on one day, but adjusting inside can take months.
write: Name Bridges' three phases in order, and quote the description of the phase where the author says most stress lives.
  lines: 3
  key: Ending, neutral zone, new beginning. The neutral zone is "an uncomfortable in-between time when the old life is gone but the new one does not yet feel real" (paragraph 3); paragraph 4 says "Much of the stress of a transition lives in that neutral zone."
write: Choose two of Schlossberg's four S's and show how each one helped Aisha or Marcus by the end of the passage. Quote the passage.
  lines: 4
  key: Support: Marcus "found a coworker who remembered his own rough first year" (paragraph 7). Strategies: Aisha "joined a study group that met every Tuesday" (paragraph 7), which matches "Joining one group... speeds up the search for new support" (paragraph 6).
write: The author ends by saying Aisha and Marcus were "carried less by luck than by the slow, ordinary work of building a new life." What is the author's message? Support it with one more quotation.
  lines: 3
  key: Getting through a transition is a process people can work at, not a matter of luck. Support: "Building small routines... restores a sense of control" (paragraph 6), or "These feelings are a normal part of the process, not proof that the decision was a mistake" (paragraph 4).

## Stretch
write: A fictional friend, Leo, is three weeks into a new job in a new city and says, "I think I made a huge mistake." Using two ideas from the passage, write what you could say to him.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: "What you're feeling sounds like the neutral zone. The passage says these feelings are normal and not proof the decision was a mistake. Could you try one small routine, like a weekly call home, or join one group at work?"

# 21. Running for Local Office
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can map the steps, requirements and supporters a candidate needs to run for local office.
directions: Read about Daniela and the Pine Valley School Board rules. Place each campaign step on the timeline, check whether Daniela meets each requirement, match supporters to their roles, and then write lines she could use at a candidate forum.
mistakes: Students put the petition after the filing deadline or forget that some signatures get thrown out. Remind them to work backward from the deadline. In the requirements chart, read the profile closely: Daniela's part-time job is not with the school district.
notes: Pine Valley, its school board rules and Daniela are fictional. Real rules differ by state and district: age, residency, signature counts, filing fees and finance reporting all vary, so a student interested in running should check with their own county or state election office. Months on the timeline are numbered 1 (January) to 11 (November); each step's month comes from the rules or Daniela's calendar. Filing the petition goes at August, the deadline month; accept July if a student explains she filed early.

## Read
text: **The candidate:** Daniela Ortiz is 19 and a community college student. She graduated from Pine Valley High two years ago and has lived in the district since she was six. She is registered to vote at her address in Pine Valley and works part time at a grocery store. She wants the board to add more career and technical classes.
text: **Pine Valley School Board rules:** A candidate must be at least 18, a registered voter living in the district for at least one year, and not employed by the school district. To get on the ballot, a candidate files a petition with at least 100 valid signatures from registered voters in the district by August 15. Petition forms are available June 1. Any campaign that raises more than 1,000 dollars must name a treasurer and file a finance report by October 15. Election Day is in early November.
text: **Daniela's calendar:** She starts going to school board meetings in February. In April she asks a friend to be her treasurer, in case she raises over 1,000 dollars. The Pine Valley Civic Club holds its candidate forum in September.

## Warm up
choice: Why should Daniela collect about 150 signatures instead of exactly 100?

  * Some signatures may be ruled invalid, for example from people not registered in the district.
  - She needs 150 to win the election.

## Main: Steps on a timeline
timeline: 1 11 1
  - 2 | Attend school board meetings to learn the issues.
  - 4 | Ask a trusted, organized friend to serve as campaign treasurer.
  - 6 | Pick up petition forms and start collecting signatures.
  - 8 | File the petition before the deadline.
  - 9 | Speak at a candidate forum.
  - 10 | File the campaign finance report (needed once she raises over 1,000 dollars).
  - 11 | Election Day.

## Supporters
match: Match each supporter to the job they do for the campaign.
  - Treasurer = Tracks donations and spending and files the finance report.
  - Campaign manager = Plans the schedule and keeps every task on track.
  - Volunteers = Knock on doors, hand out flyers and collect signatures.
  - Endorsers = Well-known people or groups who publicly say they support her.
  - Petition signers = Registered district voters who help her get on the ballot.

## Stretch
write: Daniela is young and has never held office. Write two sentences she could use at the candidate forum to turn that into a strength.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: "I graduated from Pine Valley High just two years ago, so I know what today's students need. I've already gone to every board meeting since February, and I'll bring a fresh voice for career and technical classes."
