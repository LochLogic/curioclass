# 1. Carnegie's Gospel of Wealth
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, two highlighters (two colors)
ican: I can compare two primary sources' views on the duties of the rich in the Gilded Age.
directions: Read the background and both sources. Highlight what each writer says rich people owe to others in one color, and what each says about workers in the other. Then answer the questions, citing source letters and excerpt numbers.
mistakes: Students often read Carnegie as simply generous or simply greedy. His argument has two halves: competition and great inequality are good for progress ("survival of the fittest"), and the rich must give their surplus away during life, on their own judgment. Debs attacks the second half by pointing to the first: the wealth came from workers' wages and labor, so giving it back as libraries is not a gift to them. Also watch for students who think Carnegie supported higher wages; he does not mention them.
notes: Source A follows the text of "Wealth," North American Review 148, no. 391 (June 1889), pages 653 to 664; cuts are marked [...]. Source B follows a transcription of Eugene V. Debs, "The Pinkertons at Homestead," Locomotive Firemen's Magazine 16, no. 8 (August 1892), the journal of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen, a railroad union (a labor magazine rather than a newspaper); check both against scans before printing. Debs uses harsh words elsewhere in the essay; the excerpts here keep his argument without the insults. Carnegie gave away about 90 percent of his fortune before his death in 1919, including money for more than 2,500 public libraries.

## Background
text: Andrew Carnegie came to the United States from Scotland as a poor boy and became one of the richest men in the world through steel. In 1889 he published an essay called "Wealth"; readers soon called it "The Gospel of Wealth." In 1892, workers at Carnegie's steel works in Homestead, Pennsylvania, refused a wage cut. Henry Clay Frick, who ran the company, locked them out and hired armed Pinkerton guards; a battle on July 6 left both strikers and guards dead. Eugene V. Debs, a union leader and editor, wrote a reply.

## Read: Two sources
source: Source A: Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," North American Review, June 1889
  [1] "The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship."
  [2] "[...] while the law may be sometimes hard for the individual, it is best for the race, because it insures the survival of the fittest in every department."
  [3] "This, then, is held to be the duty of the man of Wealth: First, to set an example of modest, unostentatious living, shunning display or extravagance; to provide moderately for the legitimate wants of those dependent upon him; and after doing so to consider all surplus revenues which come to him simply as trust funds, which he is called upon to administer [...] the man of wealth thus becoming the mere agent and trustee for his poorer brethren, bringing to their service his superior wisdom, experience, and ability to administer, doing for them better than they would or could do for themselves."
  [4] "[...] the best means of benefiting the community is to place within its reach the ladders upon which the aspiring can rise [...]"
  [5] "Of such as these the public verdict will then be: 'The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced.'"
source: Source B: Eugene V. Debs, "The Pinkertons at Homestead," Locomotive Firemen's Magazine, August 1892
  [1] "Suddenly Carnegie, to use a phrase, 'gets religion,' and begins to blubber about the duty of rich men to the poor."
  [2] "[...] concluded to bestow a portion of his plunder to build libraries bearing his name to perpetuate his fame."
  [3] "They themselves had built the steel works. From their toil had flowed a ceaseless stream of wealth into the coffers of Carnegie and his associates."

## Analyze
choice: In excerpt A2, "the law" Carnegie defends is the law of competition. What does he claim about it?
  - It is unfair and should be replaced by government control.
  * It can be hard on individuals but is best for society as a whole.
  - It only works in Scotland, not in America.
  - It makes everyone equally wealthy over time.
write: According to excerpt A3, what are the duties of a rich man? List at least three.
  lines: 3
  key: Live modestly without showy spending; provide moderately for his family and dependents; treat all surplus money as a trust fund to manage for the good of the community; use his own judgment ("superior wisdom") to do more good than the poor could do for themselves.
write: What does Carnegie mean in A5 that "the man who dies thus rich dies disgraced"?
  lines: 2
  key: Someone who hoards a great fortune until death, instead of giving it away for public good during life, should be judged shamefully by the public.
write: Debs calls Carnegie's gifts "plunder" (B2). Using B3, explain Debs's reasoning.
  lines: 3
  key: Debs argues that the workers created the wealth with their labor, so Carnegie's fortune was taken from them (through low wages and the wage cut at Homestead); giving part of it back as libraries with his own name on them is not charity but returning stolen money while seeking fame.
write: Compare: how would Carnegie and Debs each answer the question "What do the rich owe working people?"
  lines: 5
  key: Carnegie: the rich owe the community wise use of their surplus wealth, such as libraries, parks and schools, decided by the rich themselves, because they know best (A3, A4). Debs: the rich owe workers fair wages and a share of what their labor produces; charity cannot replace justice at work (B2, B3). Carnegie accepts inequality as good for progress (A2); Debs sees it as the problem.

## Stretch: Evaluate
write: Carnegie wrote that rich people could do more good "than they would or could do for themselves" (A3). Is that view generous, insulting to working people, or both? Defend your answer with evidence from both sources.
  lines: 5
  key: Strong answers take a side and use both sources. Generous: Carnegie pushes the rich to give nearly everything away and funded thousands of libraries. Insulting: he assumes workers cannot judge their own needs, and Debs shows workers wanted fair wages, not gifts. Both: the libraries helped many people, but the gifts kept control over money, and decisions, with Carnegie.

# 6. Who Is Missing From This Archive?
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can inventory an archive, identify whose voices are missing, and explain how gaps in the record limit what historians can know.
directions: The Millbrook Historical Society holds the papers of a factory town from 1885 to 1895. Millbrook is made up, but its archive is like many real ones. Fill in the inventory for each item, then list whose voices are missing and why, and suggest where a historian could look for them.
mistakes: Students say workers are "in" the archive because they appear in the payroll or the photographs. Being written about or pictured is not the same as speaking: the payroll records names and wages chosen by the company, and the photographer chose who stood where. Ask of every item: who made it, for whom, and whose words are actually in it. Students also blame the gaps on workers "not caring" about history. Long hours, little schooling in English, and archives that collected only from leading families explain most silences.
notes: The archive is invented for teaching but each item type is typical of real Gilded Age collections, which often began as donations from mill owners and leading families. Real places to recover missing voices include foreign-language newspapers, union and benefit society records, church registers of immigrant parishes, court and police records, census manuscripts, and later oral histories (the next sheet, Finding Silenced Voices, works on this). Accept any absent group the inventory supports with a reason.

## The archive
source: Millbrook Historical Society, Box 1: Millbrook Mills and Town, 1885 to 1895 (a made-up archive)
  [1] Payroll ledgers of Millbrook Mills, 1885 to 1895: names, jobs, hours and wages of about 1,200 workers, including children as young as 12.
  [2] Letters from mill owner Josiah Hale to his investors in Boston about profits, new machines and "labor troubles."
  [3] The Millbrook Courier, the town's English-language newspaper, owned by Hale's brother-in-law. Its 1892 strike coverage calls the strikers "outside agitators."
  [4] Twenty photographs of the mill floor, taken for the company's 1890 anniversary book. Workers stand beside their machines.
  [5] Records of First Congregational Church, attended by the mill's managers and owners: baptisms, marriages and sermons.
  [6] Diary of Mrs. Hale, the owner's wife, describing charity visits to "the poor families by the river."
  [7] Police court docket, 1892: names of 14 strikers fined for "disturbing the peace."
  [8] A printed list of mill rules posted in every workroom, with fines for lateness and talking.
  [9] A note in the catalog: "French and Polish newspapers published in Millbrook, 1888 to 1895: not collected."

## Fill the gaps
write: Choose one missing group. Name a kind of source, outside this box, that might hold their own words, and explain why it would.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: for the immigrant families, the French and Polish newspapers themselves (if a library elsewhere saved copies), because they were written by and for those families in their own languages and would report on the strike, church life and working conditions from their side. (Also accept: union records, immigrant church registers, letters home to family abroad, later oral history interviews.)

## Stretch: What the gap does to history
write: A historian writes the history of the 1892 strike using only Box 1. What story would the archive tell, and what could it get wrong?
  lines: 4
  key: The archive would tell the owner's story: outside agitators stirred up trouble (3), the law restored order (7) and the owners cared for the poor (6). It could get wrong why workers struck (wages, fines, child labor, hours), who led the strike, and whether it was local, because no striker's own words survive in the box.

# 17. Harlem Renaissance Voices
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, a projected or printed image of Jacob Lawrence's The Migration Series, Panel 1
ican: I can analyze a poem and a painting as primary sources and explain what they show about the Great Migration.
directions: Read the background and the excerpt from Langston Hughes's poem. Study Panel 1 of Jacob Lawrence's Migration Series (your teacher will show it). Answer the questions about each, then compare them.
mistakes: Students read art as a photograph of a single real event. Lawrence painted Panel 1 in 1940 to 1941, about 25 years after the migration's first wave, using research, family stories and the memories of his community; it is an interpretation. Students also read the poem's "North" as a promised land. Hughes calls it "cold-faced" and says only "they say" it is kinder: he is hopeful but uncertain, and the North had its own segregation and violence.
notes: The poem excerpt is the final 11 lines of "The South," quoted from The Weary Blues (1926), public domain (Project Gutenberg text). The original line "So now I seek the North" ends with a dash, left off here to match house style; earlier lines of the poem use language not suited to this sheet. Lawrence's Migration Series (60 panels, 1940 to 1941) is shared by The Phillips Collection (odd-numbered panels) and the Museum of Modern Art (even-numbered); show Panel 1 from the Phillips website. The image and Lawrence's captions are under copyright, so the sheet describes the panel rather than printing it. About 6 million Black Southerners moved north and west between the 1910s and 1970.

## Background
text: Starting around 1915, hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners moved to cities in the North, such as Chicago, New York and Detroit. They left behind Jim Crow laws, lynching, low pay and cotton fields ruined by the boll weevil. Factories in the North needed workers during World War I. In Harlem, a neighborhood of New York City, a flowering of Black art, music and writing grew in the 1920s: the Harlem Renaissance. Langston Hughes was one of its leading poets. Jacob Lawrence, born in 1917 to parents who had moved north, grew up in Harlem and was 23 when he finished his series of 60 paintings about the migration.

## Source A: A poem
source: Langston Hughes, from "The South," in The Weary Blues (1926)
  And I, who am black, would love her
  But she spits in my face.
  And I, who am black,
  Would give her many rare gifts
  But she turns her back upon me.
  So now I seek the North
  The cold-faced North,
  For she, they say,
  Is a kinder mistress,
  And in her house my children
  May escape the spell of the South.
choice: In the poem, Hughes describes the South as a woman. How does she treat the speaker?
  - She welcomes him with gifts.
  * She rejects him though he would love her.
  - She follows him to the North.
  - She teaches his children.
write: Why does the speaker go north? Quote two words or phrases.
  lines: 3
  key: He is rejected in the South ("she spits in my face," "turns her back upon me") and hopes the North will be "a kinder mistress" where "my children / May escape the spell of the South." He goes mostly for his children's future.
write: The North is "cold-faced," and it is kinder only "they say." What does that tell you about the speaker's hopes?
  lines: 3
  key: He is hopeful but not sure. "Cold-faced" suggests the North may be unwelcoming or indifferent, and "they say" shows he is trusting rumors, not experience. Migrants did find better pay and schools, but also segregation and hostility in Northern cities.

## Source B: A painting
text: **Describe what you see in Panel 1.** In Panel 1, a dense crowd of travelers carrying bags and bundles presses toward three gates at a train station. Each gate has a sign for a city: Chicago, New York and St. Louis.
table: Look closely | What I notice
  height: 0.55
  - People: how many, how they are drawn | {A huge crowd, drawn as simple shapes with few faces; they look like one moving mass}
  - Colors and shapes | {Flat, bold colors and repeated shapes; the crowd leans toward the gates}
  - Words in the painting | {The city names Chicago, New York and St. Louis on the gates}
write: What does Lawrence want viewers to understand about the migration from this panel?
  lines: 3
  key: That the migration was a mass movement of ordinary people, not a few individuals: so many people left that they become one surging crowd, all heading to Northern cities at once. The bags show they took what they could carry and were leaving home for good.

## Compare
write: Both works show leaving the South. How does the poem's view differ from the painting's? Use one detail from each.
  lines: 4
  key: The poem is one person's private feeling: love and hurt toward the South ("she spits in my face") and uncertain hope for the North ("they say"). The painting shows the whole people moving together; its crowd pushing toward the city gates feels urgent and collective. The poem explains why one person leaves; the painting shows how many left.

## Stretch: Art as evidence
write: A historian wants to know what the Great Migration felt like to those who lived it. Are a poem and a painting good evidence? Give one strength and one limit.
  lines: 3
  key: Strength: they show feelings, hopes and how Black artists of the time understood the migration, which numbers and laws cannot. Limit: they are artistic interpretations, created years later (the painting) or as one voice (the poem), so they must be checked against letters, newspapers such as the Chicago Defender, and census data.

# 24. Mobilizing the Home Front
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator, colored pencil
ican: I can use home front data to make and support claims about how World War II changed American life.
directions: Study the three data sets on women at work, aircraft production and rationing. Answer the questions, graph aircraft production, then write 3 claims, each supported by a number from the data.
mistakes: Students often claim that women entered the workforce for the first time during the war. # 29. Brown v. Board and Mendez
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, two highlighters (two colors)
ican: I can compare the reasoning of two court decisions and explain how one case built toward another.
directions: Read the background and the excerpts from both cases. Highlight reasons about harm to children in one color and reasons about the law in the other. Then compare how each court reached its decision, citing excerpt numbers.
mistakes: Students say Mendez struck down "separate but equal." The Ninth Circuit ruled more narrowly: California law allowed separate schools for some groups but not for children of Mexican descent, so the districts had acted without legal authority, and the court declined to rule on segregation as a whole. The district judge's opinion (M1, M2) went further, and those arguments anticipated Brown. Students also forget that the children in Mendez were segregated by ancestry and language, not under a law naming them.
notes: Excerpts are quoted exactly from the opinions: Mendez v. Westminster School District, 64 F. Supp. 544 (S.D. Cal., February 18, 1946, Judge Paul McCormick), affirmed in Westminster School District v. Mendez, 161 F.2d 774 (9th Cir., April 14, 1947); Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (May 17, 1954). Inner quotation marks are changed to single marks. Links between the cases: the NAACP, with Thurgood Marshall, filed a brief supporting the Mendez families; Governor Earl Warren signed the 1947 law ending California's school segregation laws, and as Chief Justice he wrote the unanimous Brown opinion.

## Background
text: In 1944 Gonzalo and Felícitas Méndez tried to enroll their children at a school in Westminster, California, and were sent to a separate "Mexican school." With other families they sued four Orange County school districts. In 1954 the Supreme Court decided Brown v. Board of Education, a group of cases challenging laws that required separate schools for Black and white children. Since Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), courts had allowed "separate but equal" public facilities.

## Read: Two cases
source: Mendez v. Westminster: the federal district court, 1946 (M1, M2) and the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 1947 (M3)
  [1] "'The equal protection of the laws' pertaining to the public school system in California is not provided by furnishing in separate schools the same technical facilities, text books and courses of instruction to children of Mexican ancestry that are available to the other public school children regardless of their ancestry. A paramount requisite in the American system of public education is social equality. It must be open to all children by unified school association regardless of lineage."
  [2] "It is also established by the record that the methods of segregation prevalent in the defendant school districts foster antagonisms in the children and suggest inferiority among them where none exists."
  [3] "By enforcing the segregation of school children of Mexican descent against their will and contrary to the laws of California, respondents have violated the federal law as provided in the Fourteenth Amendment to the Federal Constitution by depriving them of liberty and property without due process of law and by denying to them the equal protection of the laws."
source: Brown v. Board of Education, United States Supreme Court, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren for a unanimous Court
  [1] "Does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even though the physical facilities and other 'tangible' factors may be equal, deprive the children of the minority group of equal educational opportunities? We believe that it does."
  [2] "To separate them from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone."
  [3] "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

## Compare the reasoning
table: Question | Mendez | Brown
  height: 0.75
  - Are equal buildings and books enough? | {No (M1): the same facilities in separate schools do not give equal protection} | {No (B1): even if tangible factors are equal, segregation denies equal opportunity}
  - What harm does segregation do to children? | {It fosters antagonisms and suggests inferiority "where none exists" (M2)} | {It generates "a feeling of inferiority" that may never be undone (B2)}
  - What part of the Constitution? | {The 14th Amendment: due process and equal protection (M3)} | {The 14th Amendment's equal protection clause}
  - How far did the final ruling reach? | {Narrow: California law did not allow segregating children of Mexican descent, so the districts acted illegally (M3)} | {Broad: "separate but equal" has no place in public education anywhere (B3)}
choice: Which phrase in M3 shows that the Ninth Circuit's ruling depended on California's own laws?
  - "school children of Mexican descent"
  - "against their will"
  * "contrary to the laws of California"
  - "the equal protection of the laws"
choice: Which excerpt from Brown overturned the reasoning of Plessy v. Ferguson for schools?
  - B1
  - B2
  - None of them
  * B3

## Explain
write: Both cases say segregation harms children's sense of themselves. Compare M2 and B2: what idea do they share, and how is the wording different?
  lines: 4
  key: Both say segregation teaches children they are inferior: M2 says it suggests "inferiority among them where none exists" and fosters antagonisms; B2 says it generates "a feeling of inferiority" that may affect "hearts and minds" forever. Mendez stresses the false message and the hostility it creates between groups; Brown stresses the lasting damage inside the child.
write: Why might the Ninth Circuit have chosen a narrower ruling than the district judge, and why does the difference matter?
  lines: 4
  key: Supreme Court precedent (Plessy and later school cases) still allowed "separate but equal," and an appeals court is bound by that precedent, so it decided on the narrow ground that California law did not authorize this segregation. That ended segregation for Mexican American children in those districts but left other segregation laws standing; only Brown, decided by the Supreme Court itself, could overturn the precedent nationwide.
