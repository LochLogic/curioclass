# 1. Tint, Hue, Shade Scale
minutes: 35
materials: paint (one color plus white and black), brush, water, paper towel; or colored pencils with a white and a black pencil
pairs: paint-mixer
ican: I can make tints and shades of a hue by adding white or black.
directions: Read the example. Choose one hue. Paint or color a 7-step scale: three tints on the left, the pure hue in the middle, three shades on the right. Label every step.
mistakes: Black is very strong: students who add a big spoonful get black, not a shade. Add black a tiny dot at a time. For tints, start with white and add the color to it, not the other way around, or you will use up a lot of white. Steps should change evenly; if two neighbors look the same, add a little more.
notes: Bright hues like red, blue and green show the scale best; yellow turns greenish when black is added, which is a fun thing to notice but can frustrate. A **tone** (adding gray) is the stretch idea. The Paint Mixer tool makes tints and shades on screen if you want to demonstrate.

## Warm up: Words to know
example: **Hue** is the pure color, like red. A **tint** is a hue plus white (pink is a tint of red). A **shade** is a hue plus black (maroon is a shade of red).
fill:
  - hue + white = a {tint}
  - hue + black = a {shade}
  - Pink is a {tint} of red.
  - Navy is a {shade} of blue.

## Main: My scale
text: My hue: ____________________
chart: 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7
  rows: 1
table: Step | What I added | Label
  - 1 | {the most white} | {tint}
  - 2 | {some white} | {tint}
  - 3 | {a little white} | {tint}
  - 4 | {nothing} | {pure hue}
  - 5 | {a little black} | {shade}
  - 6 | {some black} | {shade}
  - 7 | {the most black} | {shade}

## Check
choice: Which step is the lightest?
  * step 1
  - step 4
  - step 7
choice: To make a shade, how should you add black?
  - all at once
  * a tiny bit at a time
  - only after adding white
choice: Value means how light or dark a color is. What changes across your scale?
  - the hue
  * the value
  - the texture

## Stretch
write: Adding gray to a hue makes a **tone**. Where would you use tints, and where would you use shades, in a painting of a sunny tree?
  lines: 3
  key: Tints where light hits: the sunny side of the leaves and trunk. Shades in the shadows: under the leaves, the shady side of the trunk, the shadow on the ground.

# 5. Complements in The Night Café
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, red, green and yellow colored pencils; the teacher shows Van Gogh's The Night Café
ican: I can find complementary colors in a painting and explain the mood they create.
directions: Review complements on the color wheel. Then look closely at The Night Café. Find where Van Gogh used red and where he used green, and answer the questions with what you see.
mistakes: Students may say a painting with red and green "looks like a holiday." Ask them to look at how the colors sit side by side: complements next to each other make both look stronger, so the room feels loud and tense, not cozy. Also watch for "the walls are orange": they are a deep red, and the floor is yellow.
notes: Show Vincent van Gogh's The Night Café (1888, Yale University Art Gallery), public domain; Yale's website has a large open-access image. It shows an all-night café in Arles, France, late at night: red walls, a green ceiling, a yellow floor, four hanging gas lamps with glowing halos, a green billiard table in the middle and a few customers slumped at tables. The man in white beside the table is usually identified as the owner. In a letter to his brother Theo, Van Gogh wrote (in one common English translation): "I have tried to express the terrible passions of humanity by means of red and green." He also described the café as a place where a person could ruin themselves. Accept any mood word students can back up with color evidence.

## Warm up: Complements
example: **Complementary colors** sit across from each other on the color wheel. Red and green are complements. When complements touch, each one looks brighter and stronger.
fill:
  - The complement of red is {green}.
  - The complement of blue is {orange}.
  - The complement of yellow is {violet}.
choice: What happens when you put two complements side by side?
  - They blend into gray.
  * Each color looks brighter and stronger.
  - They both look darker.

## Main: Look closely
text: Color a small patch of each color you find, then write where you see it in the painting.
table: Color | My patch | Where I see it in the painting
  - red |  | {the walls}
  - green |  | {the ceiling and the billiard table}
  - yellow |  | {the floor and the lamps}
choice: Where in the painting do red and green touch each other the most?
  - only in the clothes of the people
  * where the red walls meet the green ceiling, and around the green billiard table
  - nowhere; they are kept far apart
  - only in the bottles on the bar
choice: Which word best fits the mood of this café?
  - calm and sleepy
  - cheerful and cozy
  * uneasy and tense
  - cold and icy
write: What makes the café feel this way? Use at least two color details as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: The deep red walls press against the bright green ceiling and billiard table, and the complements fight each other so the room feels loud and uneasy. The yellow lamps glow with rings around them like they are buzzing, and the few people are slumped alone at tables in all that harsh color.
text: Van Gogh wrote to his brother that he tried to show the strong, "terrible" feelings of people by using red and green.
write: Do you think he succeeded? Explain using what you see.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Yes: the clashing red and green make the room feel restless and tense, matching sad, lonely or angry feelings, even though nothing dramatic is happening. A student who says no should give a color reason.

## Stretch
write: Imagine the same café painted only in soft blues and greens (colors next to each other on the wheel). How would the mood change?
  lines: 3
  key: The room would feel calmer, quieter and cooler, maybe sleepy or peaceful, because neighbor colors blend instead of clashing; the tense feeling would mostly disappear.

# 15. Deep Landscape Plan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, eraser, colored pencils, drawing paper for the final landscape
ican: I can plan a landscape with a foreground, middle ground and background and use four tricks to show depth.
directions: Match each depth trick to what it does. Then plan your landscape: decide what goes in each ground, sketch a small plan and check every item on the list before you draw your final picture.
mistakes: Students often put the background things (mountains, far trees) near the bottom of the page. Things that are far away sit higher on the page, closer to the horizon. Also watch for far things drawn with as much detail and bright color as near things: far things get smaller, simpler and paler.
notes: This builds on the grade 3 Landscape with Depth Plan (horizon, overlapping, big to small) by adding the three grounds, placement and atmospheric perspective. Atmospheric perspective: air and dust between us and faraway things make them look lighter, bluer and less detailed. Show any public-domain landscape with clear grounds, such as a Hudson River School painting.

## Warm up: Four depth tricks
match: Match each trick to what it does.
  - overlapping = a near object covers part of an object behind it
  - size = near things are drawn bigger, far things smaller
  - placement = far things sit higher on the page, near the horizon
  - detail and color = far things are paler, bluer and less detailed
fill:
  - The part of a landscape closest to the viewer is the {foreground}.
  - The part farthest away is the {background}.
  - The part in between is the {middle ground}.

## Main: My plan
table: Ground | What I will draw there | How big? | How much detail? | Colors
  - Foreground |  | {biggest} | {the most} | {brightest, strongest}
  - Middle ground |  | {medium} | {some} | {a little softer}
  - Background |  | {smallest} | {the least} | {palest, bluest}
  height: 0.6
draw: My plan sketch: draw the horizon line first, then quick shapes for each ground | 2.6
text: **Check before you draw.** Mark yes or no. Fix any "no" in your sketch first.
table: Checklist | Yes or no
  - My horizon line is drawn. |
  - I have something in all three grounds. |
  - At least one object overlaps another. |
  - The same kind of object is bigger in front and smaller in back. |
  - Far objects sit higher on the page. |
  - Far objects are paler and have less detail. |

## Stretch
write: Which depth trick will be the hardest for you to show? What will you do to make it work?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: Atmospheric perspective is hardest because I like bright colors; I will press lightly and add a little blue to my far hills so they fade back.

# 21. Monet or Van Gogh?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil; the teacher shows two paintings on a screen or as printouts
ican: I can compare Impressionism and Post-Impressionism by looking at brushwork, color, subject and feeling.
directions: Look closely at the two paintings your teacher shows. Read the facts. Fill in the T-chart with what you see in each painting, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often write "pretty" or "messy." Push for what they see: short dabs or long swirls, soft or bold color. Both artists painted outdoors and both used visible brushstrokes; the difference is that Monet painted what light looked like in a moment, and Van Gogh painted how he felt.
notes: Show Claude Monet's Impression, Sunrise (1872) and Vincent van Gogh's The Starry Night (1889), both public domain, with good images from museum open-access sites. Monet's painting gave Impressionism its name: a critic, Louis Leroy, mocked the title in a review of the 1874 exhibition. "Post" means after; Post-Impressionists learned from the Impressionists and then went their own ways. The T-chart key gives model answers; accept any accurate observation.

## Warm up: The facts
text: **Claude Monet** (1840-1926) was a French painter and a leader of **Impressionism**. He tried to catch the light of one moment outdoors. **Vincent van Gogh** (1853-1890) was a Dutch painter who worked in France. He is called a **Post-Impressionist** because he came after the Impressionists and used color and brushstrokes to show feelings.
fill:
  - The word Impressionism came from Monet's painting {Impression}, Sunrise.
  - Post means {after}.

## Main: T-chart
table: Look at | Monet, Impression, Sunrise (Impressionism) | Van Gogh, The Starry Night (Post-Impressionism)
  - Brushwork | {short, quick dabs; loose and sketchy} | {thick paint in long swirls and curving strokes}
  - Color | {soft blues and grays with an orange sun} | {bold blues and bright yellows side by side}
  - Subject | {a harbor at sunrise, boats in the mist} | {a night sky over a village, a tall cypress tree}
  - Feeling | {calm, quiet, a misty moment} | {energetic, swirling, full of emotion}

## Think about it
choice: Which painter was trying to show how light looked at one moment?
  * Monet
  - Van Gogh
choice: Which painting has brushstrokes that swirl and curl across the sky?
  - Impression, Sunrise
  * The Starry Night
write: How are the two painters alike? Give two ways.
  lines: 2
  key: Both show their brushstrokes instead of hiding them; both painted landscapes and skies; both used color in a fresh way; Van Gogh learned from Impressionists like Monet.

## Stretch
write: Which painting would you rather step into? Use two details from your chart to explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the painting and uses chart details, such as "The Starry Night, because the swirling strokes and bright yellow stars make the sky feel alive."

# 25. Venue Scout Report
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, clipboard; a visit to the venue with a tape measure, or photos and measurements from the teacher
ican: I can judge whether a place is a good, safe venue to show artwork.
directions: Answer the warm up questions. Then visit a real place in your community (the library, town hall, a hallway) or study the photos your teacher shares. Record the wall space, the light and the visitors, then rate the venue and explain your score.
mistakes: Students often rate a sunny wall highest because it looks bright and nice. Direct sunlight fades paper and paint, so a bright window wall is a risk. Also watch for "more visitors is always better": a busy hallway gets the art seen but can also get it bumped, so look at where people walk.
notes: If a visit is not possible, take 4 to 6 photos of one or two spaces (the library, the front office, a hallway) and give the measurements. Count visitors for 5 minutes at the same time of day if you compare two venues. Museums keep light on paper art low and away from direct sun; heat and damp are risks too (radiators, vents, sinks). The math item: 6 artworks use 6 x 2 = 12 feet, plus 7 spaces of 1 foot = 19 feet, which fits; 7 artworks would need 22 feet.

## Warm up: What makes a good venue?
choice: Why is a wall in direct sunlight a risky place to hang art?
  - Visitors cannot see it.
  * Sunlight can fade the colors over time.
  - Sunlight makes the frames heavier.
choice: A wall is right next to a busy door that swings open. What is the biggest risk?
  * Art could get bumped or knocked down.
  - The art will be too quiet.
  - Nobody will walk by.
choice: Which spot is best for art made of paper?
  - next to a heating vent
  - above a sink
  * on a dry wall with steady, indirect light

## Main: My scout report
text: Venue name: ____________________ Date I visited: ______________
chart: What I found
  labels: Wall space: how long is the open wall?; Wall height: how high can art safely hang?; Light: windows, lamps, direct sun?; Visitors: how many passed in 5 minutes?; Where do people walk?; Dangers: vents, heat, water, doors?
  height: 0.55
fill: A wall is 20 feet long. Each framed artwork is 2 feet wide, and you need 1 foot of space between artworks and at each end. How many artworks fit? {6}
text: **Rate it:** 1 = poor, 2 = okay, 3 = great.
chart: Score (1, 2 or 3) | Why I gave this score | How it could be better
  labels: Wall space; Light; Visitor traffic; Safety for the art
  height: 0.45
text: Total score: ______ out of 12

## Stretch
write: Would you choose this venue for your class art show? Give your reasons and one thing you would change to make it better.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers use their data, for example: "Yes, the library wall is 18 feet long and about 30 people passed in 5 minutes, but one end gets afternoon sun, so I would leave that part empty or hang only the copies there."

# 35. Morning, Noon, Night
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, eraser, colored pencils (include a dark blue or violet and a yellow)
ican: I can use value and color to show different times of day and the moods they create.
directions: Draw the same simple building three times (trace your first drawing if you like). Plan the light for each time of day in the chart. Then shade each building with value and color so a viewer can tell the time and feel the mood.
mistakes: Students often color all three the same and only add a sun or moon. The light itself must change: long shadows in the morning, short shadows at noon, and dark values with glowing windows at night. Remind them that value means light and dark, so the night building needs the darkest darks on the page.
notes: Claude Monet painted the front of Rouen Cathedral more than 30 times in the 1890s to show how light and color change through the day; several are public domain on museum websites and make a strong opener. Keep the building simple (a house, a shop, the school front) so the time spent goes into shading. Morning sun is low in the east, so shadows are long; at noon the sun is high, so shadows are short and fall close under the building.

## Warm up: Light through the day
choice: When are shadows the shortest?
  - early morning
  * noon
  - sunset
choice: **Value** means how light or dark a color is. Which scene needs the darkest values?
  - a sunny noon
  - a misty morning
  * a night street
fill:
  - Long shadows stretch across the ground when the sun is {low} in the sky.
  - At night, the brightest spots are usually {lights in the windows or streetlights}.

note: Model plan. Morning: low sun from one side, long soft shadows, pale yellows, pinks and light blue sky, mood fresh and calm. Noon: sun high overhead, short dark shadows under the roof edges, bright full colors, mood busy, hot or cheerful. Night: light from windows, a moon or a streetlight, very dark blues and violets with yellow glowing windows, mood quiet, lonely, cozy or mysterious.

## Stretch
write: Which of your three buildings has the strongest mood? Which value or color choice made the biggest difference?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name a specific choice, such as "The night building feels lonely because almost everything is dark blue and only one window glows yellow."
