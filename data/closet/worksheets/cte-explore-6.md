# 1. Fourteen Clusters Cut and Sort
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can sort jobs into the 14 career clusters.
directions: Cut apart the 28 job cards at the bottom of the sheet. Glue 2 cards next to each career cluster. Then star 2 clusters you want to learn more about, and explain one choice.
pairs: career-cluster-explorer
mistakes: Some jobs could fit more than one cluster in real life (a chef in a hospital kitchen, a teacher of welding). Sort each job by what the work mostly is, and accept a different placement when a student can defend it. Students often put the accountant in Management and Entrepreneurship; accounting belongs to Financial Services.
notes: The 14 clusters are the Advance CTE Career Clusters Framework (2024), the same names Career Cluster Explorer uses. Cards are in a cut-apart grid; each cluster gets exactly 2. To check quickly, have students read their pairs aloud by cluster. The cut grid is a table so its lines are solid: cut along every line.

  - Construction

# 7. Interest or Skill?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell an interest from a skill and connect skills to jobs.
directions: Read the example. Cut out the 12 statement cards and glue each one under Interest or Skill. Then match each skill to a job that uses it.
mistakes: The words "I like" and "I enjoy" point to an interest; "I can" points to a skill. Students sometimes sort by whether the activity sounds like work. Some statements could be both in real life (a person who enjoys cooking may also be good at it); sort each card by what the sentence says. Watch for matching a skill to a job only because of one word (bike chain does not mean truck driver).
notes: The 12 statements are invented. Interests: drawing, being outdoors, animals, how computers work, puzzles, music. Skills: fix a bike chain, speak two languages, cook from a recipe, measure and saw wood, calm a crying toddler, type fast and accurately. The glue table's key lists cards in one possible order; any order within the right column is correct. The cut grid is a table so its lines are solid. The skill-to-job matches in the key are the clearest fit; accept another job a student can defend.

## Example
example: **An interest** is something you like or are curious about: "I enjoy reading about space."
  **A skill** is something you can do, that you learned and practiced: "I can use a telescope to find the Moon."
  An interest can grow into a skill: someone who enjoys space might learn to use a telescope.

## Warm up
choice: "I like learning about how bridges stay up." This statement is...
  * an interest
  - a skill
choice: "I can sew a button back on a shirt." This statement is...
  - an interest
  * a skill

## Main: Sort the statements
text: Glue each card in the Interest column or the Skill column. Any order is fine.
table: Interest | Skill
  - {I enjoy drawing} | {I can fix a bike chain}
  - {I love being outdoors} | {I can speak two languages}
  - {I like taking care of animals} | {I can cook a meal from a recipe}
  - {I am curious about how computers work} | {I can measure and saw wood}
  - {I like solving puzzles} | {I can calm a crying toddler}
  - {I enjoy listening to music} | {I can type fast and accurately}
  height: 0.42

## Cut out the statement cards

  - I can type fast and accurately | I like solving puzzles | I can calm a crying toddler

  - I can speak two languages | I am curious about how computers work | I can fix a bike chain
  - I like taking care of animals | I can cook a meal from a recipe | I enjoy listening to music

## Main: Match each skill to a job
match: Write the letter of the job that uses each skill.
  - I can fix a bike chain = Bicycle mechanic
  - I can speak two languages = Interpreter
  - I can cook a meal from a recipe = Line cook
  - I can measure and saw wood = Carpenter
  - I can calm a crying toddler = Child care worker

## Stretch
write: Pick one interest card. What skill could someone with that interest learn, and what job could it lead to?
  lines: 3
  starter: Someone who ___ could learn to ___, which could lead to a job as ___.
  key: Answers vary. Example: Someone who likes taking care of animals could learn to groom dogs or give animals medicine, which could lead to a job as a veterinary technician or pet groomer.

# 20. Snow Season Prices Review
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can read a demand table, predict how events change a market, and explain how a wage gets set.
directions: Read the invented demand table for snowboard rentals at a ski shop. Answer the questions, graph the demand, predict two market changes, and explain how wages for shop workers are set.
mistakes: Students often think a higher price always brings in more money. At $35 the shop earns less than at $25 because far fewer people rent. In the predictions, watch for students who say a new rival shop raises prices; more sellers competing for the same customers usually pushes prices down. Wages are prices too: when workers are scarce, shops must pay more to attract them.
notes: The shop and its numbers are invented. Money taken in = price x rentals: $15 x 90 = $1,350; $20 x 75 = $1,500; $25 x 60 = $1,500; $30 x 45 = $1,350; $35 x 30 = $1,050; $40 x 15 = $600. Law of demand: as the price rises, the quantity people want to buy falls. A big snowfall raises demand; a new rival raises supply and competition.

  - $25 | 60 | {$1,500}
  - $30 | 45 | {$1,350}

## Main: Predict two market changes
choice: A huge snowstorm is coming, and every hotel in town is full for the weekend. What happens to the demand for rentals?
  - It goes down.
  - It stays the same.
  * It goes up.
write: Because of the storm, what will probably happen to the rental price? Explain why.
  lines: 2
  key: The price will probably go up, because many more people want to rent the same number of snowboards, so the shop can charge more and still rent them all.
choice: A new rental shop opens right next door with 100 snowboards. What will probably happen to prices?
  * They go down, because the shops compete for customers.
  - They go up, because there are more snowboards.
  - They stay the same, because shops never change prices.
write: Explain your answer about the new shop.
  lines: 2
  key: With two shops, customers can choose. Each shop may lower its price to win customers, so prices probably fall.

## Main: How a wage gets set
text: Ski shops in town want to hire 40 workers at $14 an hour, but only 25 people apply.
choice: What will the shops most likely do?
  - Lower the wage to $12 an hour
  - Keep the wage at $14 and wait
  * Raise the wage until more people apply
write: Explain how a wage gets set. Use the words workers, employers, supply and demand.
  lines: 3
  key: A wage is the price of work. Employers demand workers and workers supply their time. If employers want more workers than are available, they raise the wage to attract them; if many people want the jobs, the wage can stay lower. The wage settles where the number of workers wanted matches the number willing to work.

# 21. Working, Looking or Neither?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can classify people as employed, unemployed or not in the labor force.
directions: Read the passage. Then classify each invented person as employed (E), unemployed (U) or not in the labor force (N), and give the reason.
mistakes: The biggest mix-up: thinking anyone without a job is unemployed. To be unemployed you must be available and actively looking (or on a temporary layoff). Students also often count part-time workers and people on vacation as unemployed; any paid work, or a job you are away from for a short time, counts as employed. Volunteering is not paid work.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 250 words) and follows the definitions the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics uses for its monthly Current Population Survey. The people are invented. Unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force x 100.

## Read
passage: Who Counts as Unemployed?
  Every month, the United States government asks thousands of households a set of questions about work. The answers are used to count how many people are working and how many are looking for work. To do this, the survey sorts every person age 16 and older into one of three groups.
  Employed people did any work for pay or profit during the survey week. It does not matter whether they worked full time or just a few hours, or whether they work for a company or for themselves. People who have a job but were away from it that week, because they were sick or on vacation, also count as employed.
  Unemployed people do not have a job, but they are available to work and have actively looked for one in the past four weeks. Actively looking means doing something, like sending out applications or going to job interviews. People who were laid off for a short time and expect to be called back to their job also count as unemployed.
  Everyone else is not in the labor force. This group includes most full-time students who are not working, retired people, people who stay home to care for their families, and people who want a job but have stopped looking.
  The labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. The unemployment rate is the share of the labor force that is unemployed. It tells us how hard it is for people who want a job to find one.
## Warm up
fill: The labor force = employed + {unemployed}.
fill: The survey counts people age {16} and older.

## Main: Classify each person
table: Person | E, U or N | Reason
  - Marcus, 34, works 10 hours a week at a bike shop | {E} | {any paid work counts, even part time}
  - Ruth, 70, retired; volunteers 20 hours a week at the library and is not looking for a job | {N} | {retired and not looking; volunteering is not paid}
  - Jae, 16, full-time high school student, no job, not looking | {N} | {student, not looking}
  - Tomas, 29, lost his job; sent applications and had an interview last week | {U} | {no job, available, actively looking}
  - Dana, 41, a nurse who was on vacation the whole survey week | {E} | {has a job, away for a short time}
  - Kofi, 50, wants a job but stopped looking 3 months ago because he thinks there are none | {N} | {not looking in the past 4 weeks}
  - Lena, 25, laid off for 2 weeks while the factory fixes its machines; expects to be called back | {U} | {temporary layoff, expects recall}
  - Sofia, 37, runs her own dog-walking business | {E} | {works for profit for herself}

## Stretch: Compute
fill: Among the 8 people, how many are in the labor force? {5}
fill: The unemployment rate for these 8 people = unemployed / labor force = {2} / {5} = {40}%
fill: A town has 1,140 employed, 60 unemployed and 800 not in the labor force. Its labor force is {1,200} and its unemployment rate is {5}%.

# 33. One Company or the Whole Basket?
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencils if you have them
ican: I can compare the risk of owning one company with owning a basket of companies.
directions: Five made-up companies each started with $100 of investment. The table shows what each $100 was worth every 2 years. The basket is $100 split into $20 in each company. Find the basket's value, graph three lines, and compare the risk.
mistakes: Students may add the five values to find the basket. Since the basket holds $20 (one fifth) of each, its value is the sum divided by 5 (the average). Watch for "the basket always wins": it ended below Companies A and E, but it never fell below its starting $100 in these numbers and its drops were small. Risk means how much and how suddenly a value can go up or down, not just the ending value.
notes: All companies and numbers are invented; real investments can lose money, and past results do not predict future ones. Basket = (A + B + C + D + E) / 5: year 0, 500/5 = 100; year 2, 560/5 = 112; year 4, 600/5 = 120; year 6, 630/5 = 126; year 8, 600/5 = 120; year 10, 700/5 = 140. Key lines: solid = basket, dashed = Company B, dotted = Company C.

## The data
table: Value of $100 | Year 0 | Year 2 | Year 4 | Year 6 | Year 8 | Year 10
  - Company A (bakery) | $100 | $110 | $120 | $130 | $140 | $150
  - Company B (scooters) | $100 | $150 | $200 | $120 | $40 | $0
  - Company C (robot toys) | $100 | $90 | $120 | $180 | $260 | $350
  - Company D (DVD rental) | $100 | $80 | $70 | $60 | $50 | $40
  - Company E (farm) | $100 | $130 | $90 | $140 | $110 | $160
  - Basket ($20 in each) | $100 | {$112} | {$120} | {$126} | {$120} | {$140}

## Warm up
example: Year 2 basket: add the five companies: 110 + 150 + 90 + 80 + 130 = 560. Divide by 5: 560 / 5 = **$112**.
fill:
  - The company that ended with the most money is {Company C}, with {$350}.
  - The company that went out of business (fell to $0) is {Company B}.

## Main: Graph three lines

graph: Year | 0 10 1 | Value ($) | 0 360 40

  key2: 0,100 2,150 4,200 6,120 8,40 10,0
  key3: 0,100 2,90 4,120 6,180 8,260 10,350

## Main: Compare the risk
table: | Lowest value | Highest value | Biggest drop between two checks
  - Company B | {$0} | {$200} | {$80 (year 4 to 6, and 6 to 8)}
  - Company C | {$90} | {$350} | {$10 (year 0 to 2)}
  - Basket | {$100} | {$140} | {$6 (year 6 to 8)}
choice: Which line moved up and down the least?
  - Company B
  - Company C
  * The basket
write: An investor in year 0 could not know which company would win. Would you rather own one company or the basket? Use the data to explain the risk and reward.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer: the basket is safer. One company could become C ($350) or B ($0), and there was no way to know which in year 0. The basket grew more slowly (to $140) but never dropped below $100 and its biggest drop was $6, because when one company fell, others rose. The trade-off is less risk for less chance of the biggest reward.

## Stretch
fill: If you had put $100 into Company B alone, you would have lost ${100}. In the basket, the $20 in Company B was lost, but the basket still gained ${40} overall.
