# 1. Fourteen Clusters Cut and Sort
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can sort jobs into the 14 career clusters.
directions: Cut apart the 28 job cards at the bottom of the sheet. Glue 2 cards next to each career cluster. Then star 2 clusters you want to learn more about, and explain one choice.
pairs: career-cluster-explorer
mistakes: Some jobs could fit more than one cluster in real life (a chef in a hospital kitchen, a teacher of welding). Sort each job by what the work mostly is, and accept a different placement when a student can defend it. Students often put the accountant in Management and Entrepreneurship; accounting belongs to Financial Services.
notes: The 14 clusters are the Advance CTE Career Clusters Framework (2024), the same names Career Cluster Explorer uses. Cards are in a cut-apart grid; each cluster gets exactly 2. To check quickly, have students read their pairs aloud by cluster. The cut grid is a table so its lines are solid: cut along every line.

# 21. Working, Looking or Neither?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can classify people as employed, unemployed or not in the labor force.
directions: Read the passage. Then classify each invented person as employed (E), unemployed (U) or not in the labor force (N), and give the reason.
mistakes: The biggest mix-up: thinking anyone without a job is unemployed. To be unemployed you must be available and actively looking (or on a temporary layoff). Students also often count part-time workers and people on vacation as unemployed; any paid work, or a job you are away from for a short time, counts as employed. Volunteering is not paid work.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 250 words) and follows the definitions the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics uses for its monthly Current Population Survey. The people are invented. Unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force x 100.

## Read
passage: Who Counts as Unemployed?
  Every month, the United States government asks thousands of households a set of questions about work. The answers are used to count how many people are working and how many are looking for work. To do this, the survey sorts every person age 16 and older into one of three groups.
  Employed people did any work for pay or profit during the survey week. It does not matter whether they worked full time or just a few hours, or whether they work for a company or for themselves. People who have a job but were away from it that week, because they were sick or on vacation, also count as employed.
  Unemployed people do not have a job, but they are available to work and have actively looked for one in the past four weeks. Actively looking means doing something, like sending out applications or going to job interviews. People who were laid off for a short time and expect to be called back to their job also count as unemployed.
  Everyone else is not in the labor force. This group includes most full-time students who are not working, retired people, people who stay home to care for their families, and people who want a job but have stopped looking.
  The labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. The unemployment rate is the share of the labor force that is unemployed. It tells us how hard it is for people who want a job to find one.
