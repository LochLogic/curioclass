# 1. Church Modes in Folk Songs
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; a keyboard or barred instrument (optional)
ican: I can build the Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian and Mixolydian modes and identify the mode of a folk melody.
directions: Build each mode two ways: on the white keys and by altering a major or minor scale. Then identify the mode of four folk melodies from their home note and their characteristic tone.
mistakes: Students often decide the mode by the first note instead of the home note (final). Have them find the note the melody comes to rest on, then compare the other notes to a major or natural minor scale on that note. Watch for Dorian and Aeolian (natural minor) confused: Dorian's raised 6th is the only difference.
notes: White-key modes: Dorian D to D, Phrygian E to E, Lydian F to F, Mixolydian G to G. Formulas: Dorian = natural minor with a raised 6th; Phrygian = natural minor with a lowered 2nd; Lydian = major with a raised 4th; Mixolydian = major with a lowered 7th. Scarborough Fair, a traditional English ballad, is most often sung in Dorian; Old Joe Clark, a traditional Appalachian fiddle tune, is a standard Mixolydian example. Melodies 3 and 4 are short originals written for this sheet. Versions of folk songs vary; if a class songbook prints Scarborough Fair with a lowered 6th, it is in Aeolian there, which makes a good discussion.

## Warm up: White-key modes
fill:
  - Dorian runs on the white keys from {D} to {D}.
  - Phrygian runs from {E} to {E}.
  - Lydian runs from {F} to {F}.
  - Mixolydian runs from {G} to {G}.

table: Mode | Change from | Starting note | Notes of the mode

  - Lydian | major, raise the 4th | C | {C D E F# G A B C}
  - Mixolydian | major, lower the 7th | D | {D E F# G A B C D}

table: Melody | What you see | Home note | Major or minor sound? | Mode
  - 1. Scarborough Fair (traditional) | uses D E F G A B C; rests on D | {D} | {minor} | {Dorian}
  - 2. Old Joe Clark (traditional) | uses A B C# D E F# G; rests on A | {A} | {major} | {Mixolydian}
  - 3. Original: E F G A B C B A G F E | ends on E with F natural | {E} | {minor} | {Phrygian}
  - 4. Original: F G A B C B A G A F | ends on F with B natural | {F} | {major} | {Lydian}
write: For melody 1, which single note makes it Dorian instead of natural minor? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: B natural. D natural minor would have Bb as its 6th; raising it to B natural gives the Dorian raised 6th.

## Stretch: Compose in a mode
write: Write a 4-measure melody in G Mixolydian (letter names). Circle the note that gives it its Mixolydian sound and end on the home note.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. The melody uses G A B C D E F (F natural, no F#), ends on G, and circles at least one F natural, the lowered 7th that sets Mixolydian apart from G major.

# 7. Full Movement Form Chart
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; a score of Mozart's Piano Sonata in C major, K. 545, first movement, with measure numbers (public domain); a recording
ican: I can chart the form of a full sonata-form movement with measure numbers, keys, themes and cadences.
directions: Listen to the whole movement with the score. Then complete the form chart: where each section and theme begins, its key, and how it ends. Some cells are done for you. Last, answer the questions about what makes this movement unusual.
mistakes: Students mark the second theme where the scales begin; the scales are the transition, and the second theme starts after the arrival on the new key (m. 14). Watch for the recapitulation placed at m. 59, where C major returns: the recapitulation begins at m. 42 with the first theme in F major. Some students forget that the second theme comes back in the tonic.
notes: Measure numbers follow the standard 73-measure edition (the exposition is 28 measures, repeated; the development and recapitulation, mm. 29 to 73, are also repeated). Check your edition before class. Exposition: first theme mm. 1 to 4 in C (Alberti bass), closing with an authentic cadence in m. 4; transition mm. 5 to 13, scales that modulate, arriving on G at m. 12, with a quiet link in m. 13; second theme from m. 14 in G major; closing section from m. 22, ending with a perfect authentic cadence in G at m. 28. Development mm. 29 to 41: starts in G minor, moves through D minor and A minor, ends on C7, the dominant of F. Recapitulation from m. 42: first theme in F major (the subdominant, unusual for the time), transition (mm. 46 to 58) adjusted to lead to G, the dominant of C, second theme from m. 59 in C, closing section from m. 67, final cadence in C at m. 73. Accept measure numbers within one bar where a section's start is debatable.

## Warm up: Sonata form map
fill:
  - The three big sections of sonata form are the exposition, the {development} and the {recapitulation}.
  - In a major-key exposition, the second theme is usually in the {dominant} key.
  - In the recapitulation, the second theme returns in the {tonic} key.
  - The section that bridges the first and second themes is the {transition}.

## Main: The form chart
table: Section | Measures | Key or keys | Theme or material | How it ends

  - Exposition: transition | {5 to 13} | {C major, moving to G} | {rising and falling scales} | Arrives on G in m. 12
  - Exposition: second theme | {14 to 21} | {G major} | {new melody over a broken-chord left hand} | {continues into the closing}
  - Exposition: closing section | {22 to 28} | {G major} | {chromatic turns, then a long trill} | {perfect authentic cadence in G, m. 28}
  - Development | {29 to 41} | {G minor, D minor, A minor} | {closing material in minor keys} | {on C7, the dominant of F}
  - Recapitulation: first theme | {42 to 45} | {F major} | {first theme} | {continues into transition}
  - Recapitulation: second theme | {59 to 66} | {C major} | {second theme} | {continues into the closing}
  - Recapitulation: closing section | {67 to 73} | {C major} | {chromatic turns, then a long trill} | {perfect authentic cadence in C, m. 73}

## Questions
choice: What is unusual about the start of this recapitulation?
  - It begins with the second theme.
  * The first theme returns in F major, the subdominant, instead of C major.
  - It is in a minor key.
choice: The development begins in G minor. How is G minor related to the exposition's ending key?
  - It is the relative minor of G major.
  - It is the dominant of C major.
  * It is the parallel minor of G major.
write: Mozart starts the recapitulation in F major. Why does that let him repeat the transition with few changes and still land in C for the second theme?
  lines: 4
  key: In the exposition the transition moves up a fifth, from C to G. Starting the same journey a fifth lower, in F, makes the same kind of move up a fifth land in C, the tonic. So the second theme can return in C without rewriting the transition much.

## Stretch
write: Draw an arrow timeline of the keys in this movement, from m. 1 to m. 73. Mark the moment you think is most dramatic and explain why.
  lines: 4
  key: C (m. 1) to G (m. 14 to 28), then G minor, D minor, A minor (mm. 29 to 41), F (m. 42) and back to C (m. 59 to 73). Answers vary on the dramatic moment; a good one cites a measure, such as the turn to G minor at m. 29, where the bright closing music suddenly darkens.

# 20. Research Paper Outline
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; your research notes
ican: I can outline a music history research paper with a thesis, three credible sources and evidence from scores or recordings.
directions: Read the model outline on recording and early jazz. Then outline your own paper: topic, thesis, three sources with what each proves, evidence from scores or recordings, and a counterargument.
mistakes: Students write a topic as a thesis ("This paper is about jazz"). A thesis makes a claim someone could argue with. Watch for sources that are all websites of unknown authors; aim for at least one book or article by a scholar and one primary source (a score, a recording, a letter, a review). Evidence needs a location: a measure number, a timestamp or a page.
notes: Model facts: the first jazz records were made in 1917 by the Original Dixieland Jass Band (Livery Stable Blues, released by Victor). Louis Armstrong recorded with his Hot Five and Hot Seven from 1925 to 1928; West End Blues (1928) opens with his famous unaccompanied trumpet cadenza. Books: Gunther Schuller, Early Jazz: Its Roots and Musical Development (Oxford University Press, 1968); Ted Gioia, The History of Jazz (Oxford University Press, 1997). Students should use the citation style your school uses; the key shows a simple format.

## Model outline
example: **Topic:** How records spread early jazz.
  **Thesis:** In the 1920s, records, not sheet music, carried jazz beyond New Orleans, because the improvised solos that defined the style could be copied by ear from a disc but not fully written down.
  **Source 1:** Gunther Schuller, Early Jazz (1968), a scholarly book: analysis of 1920s jazz recordings.
  **Source 2:** Louis Armstrong and His Hot Five, West End Blues (1928), a recording: a primary source.
  **Source 3:** Ted Gioia, The History of Jazz (1997), a history: how musicians learned from records.
  **Evidence:** The opening cadenza of West End Blues (0:00 to about 0:15): a free solo that no printed part of the time captured.
  **Counterargument:** Bands still sold sheet music; answer: the printed arrangements left out the improvised solos.

## Warm up
choice: Which is a thesis, not just a topic?
  - My paper is about the history of the piano.
  * The invention of the piano changed how composers wrote, because players could now shape loud and soft within a phrase.
  - Pianos have 88 keys.
choice: Which source is a primary source for a paper on Beethoven's deafness?
  - A 2015 website summary of Beethoven's life
  * The Heiligenstadt Testament, a letter Beethoven wrote in 1802
  - A textbook chapter on the Classical era

  - Thesis (a claim with a "because") | {Answers vary: a claim someone could argue with, plus a reason}

write: Topic sentences for body paragraphs 1, 2 and 3: one line each.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model for the jazz paper: 1. Before records, jazz lived in New Orleans clubs and parades. 2. Recordings like West End Blues let young players copy solos note for note. 3. Printed arrangements spread the tunes but not the improvisation that made them jazz.

## Stretch
write: Which of your three sources is weakest? How will you check or replace it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the source, the weakness (unknown author, no citations, too general, much later than the events) and a fix (find the original document, a scholarly book or a primary source it cites).

# 21. History Capstone Checkpoint
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can identify post-1945 styles, technologies and influences and explain how a historical change shaped a genre.
directions: Place the events on the timeline, match technologies to what they changed, identify styles from descriptions, and write an explanation of how one historical change shaped a genre.
mistakes: Students tend to explain a genre by naming a single famous artist. Push for the change itself (a technology, a migration, an economic shift) and the musical result (new sounds, forms or ways of making music). Watch for hip hop described only as rapping: DJing with two turntables to extend the break came first.
notes: Dates: Pierre Schaeffer made his first musique concrète studies from recorded sounds in 1948, the same year the long-playing record went on sale; John Cage's 4'33" was first performed in 1952; Terry Riley's In C premiered in 1964; DJ Kool Herc's back-to-school party in the Bronx in August 1973 is often named as the birth of hip hop; compact discs went on sale in 1982; the MIDI standard was published in 1983. Accept timeline placements within a few years. A strong capstone answer names the change, the musical result and evidence, and may cover a genre beyond this sheet.

## Warm up: Place the events
timeline: 1945 1990 5
  - 1948 | Schaeffer's first musique concrète from recorded sounds
  - 1952 | Cage's 4'33" first performed
  - 1964 | Riley's In C premiered
  - 1973 | DJ Kool Herc's Bronx party, an origin of hip hop
  - 1983 | the MIDI standard published

## Main: Technologies and styles
match: Match each technology to what it changed.
  - Magnetic tape = sounds could be cut, spliced and looped in the studio
  - Long-playing record = an album could hold about 20 minutes per side
  - Multitrack recording = musicians could record parts separately and layer them
  - Synthesizer = new sounds built from electronic oscillators
  - Turntables and mixer = DJs could repeat and extend a record's drum break
  - Digital sampling = any recorded sound could be stored and played back as an instrument
  - MIDI = electronic instruments and computers from different makers could talk to each other
choice: A short pattern repeats again and again while it changes very slowly over many minutes. Which style?
  - bebop
  * minimalism
  - musique concrète
choice: Fast tempos, complex chord changes and virtuosic improvised solos for small combos in the 1940s. Which style?
  * bebop
  - serialism
  - hip hop
choice: Every note is organized from a fixed ordering of all 12 pitches, often extended to rhythm and dynamics. Which style?
  - minimalism
  * serialism
  - rock and roll
choice: A backbeat on 2 and 4, electric guitar and a 12-bar blues form, popular with teenagers in the mid 1950s. Which style?
  - cool jazz
  - Impressionism
  * rock and roll
choice: Which influence most directly shaped early hip hop DJing?
  * Jamaican sound-system culture brought to the Bronx
  - Medieval chant
  - Classical sonata form

## Stretch: Capstone explanation
write: Choose one historical change after 1945 (a technology, a migration, a social movement or an economic shift). Explain how it shaped one genre. Name the change, the musical result, and specific evidence (a date, a work or a practice).
  lines: 10
  starter: After ..., the genre of ... changed because ...
  key: Answers vary. For example: "Turntable and sampling technology shaped hip hop. In the Bronx in 1973, DJ Kool Herc used two copies of a record to repeat its drum break, so dancers could keep moving; this turned existing records into new music. When digital samplers became affordable in the 1980s, producers could build whole tracks from short recorded sounds, and MIDI (1983) let drum machines, samplers and computers play in sync. The result was a genre built on loops and layers rather than on a band playing live."

# 33. Venue and Tech Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler; a visit to the recital venue or its floor plan
ican: I can map a recital venue and plan where the stage, seating, piano, lights, sound and audience route go.
directions: Learn the stage terms in the warm up. Then visit your venue (or use its floor plan) and draw a map from above. Show the stage, seating, piano, lighting, sound equipment and the audience's entry route. Use the legend symbols. Then complete the tech checklist.
mistakes: Students mix up stage left and stage right; they are the performer's left and right facing the audience. Watch for maps that block an exit or a wheelchair space with equipment, or run a cable across the entry route. Some forget where performers wait and enter.
notes: A grand piano is usually set so its open lid reflects sound toward the audience: the long curved side faces the house, the lid propped open on that side, and the pianist sits with the audience on their right. Fire codes require exits to stay clear and marked; check your venue's rules on seating and aisle widths with the building manager. Model answers in the checklist describe a typical school recital hall.

## Warm up: Stage terms

  - The side areas offstage where performers wait are the {wings}.
