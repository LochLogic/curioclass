# 1. Dewey Neighborhoods Sort
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can use the Dewey numbers to find where a nonfiction book lives.
directions: (Read aloud.) Nonfiction books live in 10 neighborhoods, each with a number. Use the picture key at the end of the sheet. Cut out each topic card. Glue it under the number of its neighborhood.
mistakes: Children often put Soccer in 600 or 300 because a sport feels like "doing" or "people." Sports and games live with the arts in the 700s. Poems go in the 800s, not with storybooks. Money is about how people live together, so it lives in the 300s.
notes: The Dewey Decimal Classification has 10 main classes: 000 computer science, information and general works; 100 philosophy and psychology; 200 religion; 300 social sciences; 400 language; 500 science; 600 technology; 700 arts and recreation; 800 literature; 900 history and geography. The key uses kid-friendly names for them. Each card fits one neighborhood: Encyclopedias 030, Feelings 152, Religions of the world 200, Money 332, Learning Spanish 468, Sharks 597, Cars and trucks 629, Soccer 796, Poems 811 (American poetry), Ancient Egypt 932. The cards for 000 to 400 and for 500 to 900 come on separate strips. After the sheet, walk the class to one shelf from each neighborhood.

## Warm up: How it works
example: A book about sharks is about science and nature. Science lives in the **500** neighborhood, so a shark book has a call number in the 500s, like 597.

## Main: Sort the cards
sort: Neighborhoods 000 to 400
  - 000: Encyclopedias
  - 100: Feelings
  - 200: Religions of the world
  - 300: Money
  - 400: Learning Spanish
sort: Neighborhoods 500 to 900
  - 500: Sharks
  - 600: Cars and trucks
  - 700: Soccer
  - 800: Poems
  - 900: Ancient Egypt

## Picture key
picword:
  - computer | 000 facts
  - think | 100 thinking
  - candle | 200 religions
  - people | 300 people
  - abc-blocks | 400 languages
  - planet | 500 science
  - wrench | 600 machines
  - paintbrush | 700 arts, sports
  - pencil | 800 poems, plays
  - globe | 900 history, places

## Stretch: Explain one
write: Pick one card. Tell why it lives in its neighborhood.
  lines: 2
  starter: ... lives in the ... because ...
  key: Answers vary. Example: Ancient Egypt lives in the 900s because it is about history and a place.

# 7. Find It in the Catalog
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can choose search words and pick the catalog record that matches what a reader wants.
directions: (Read aloud.) Five readers ask for a book. For each one, write two or three search words. Then look at the catalog results and write the letter of the record that fits best. Check the call number and the subjects too.
mistakes: Children pick the first record with the right word in the title, like G for the shark report; The Great Shark Race is a story (call number E), not a fact book. Others write the whole request as search words; show how "I need facts about sharks for my report" shrinks to "sharks." Some ignore the word "mystery" in request 4 and pick D, a fact book about volcanoes.
notes: The records are made up for Curio Class, but the call numbers follow real Dewey numbers: 597.3 sharks, 636.7 dogs, 551.21 volcanoes, 641.8 cooking batters and breads, 796.334 soccer, 523.4 planets, 743.6 drawing animals. E marks an easy picture book and FIC a chapter book (fiction); your library may use different labels. Key: 1 A, 2 C, 3 B, 4 J, 5 D. Search words vary; the key shows good ones. Stretch: Let's Make Pancakes, E, 641.8.

## Warm up: Good search words
example: Request: "Do you have a book that tells me how to draw a horse?"
  Search words: **drawing horses** (or **drawing animals**). Leave out little words like "do," "you" and "a."
choice: Which search words fit best for "I want to learn about the planets"?
  - I want to learn
  * planets
  - about the

# 20. Do the Two Sources Agree?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can find the facts that two sources agree on.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read both passages about penguins. Then read each fact. Put a check mark if both passages tell that fact. Put an X if only one passage tells it.
mistakes: Children check a fact that is true but appears in only one passage, such as the black and white colors. The question is not "Is it true?" but "Do both passages say it?" Have them find the fact in Passage 1 and then in Passage 2 with a finger. Some miss that "big bird" in Passage 2 tells that penguins are birds.
notes: Both passages are original to Curio Class. Facts are checked: penguins are birds that cannot fly and use their wings as flippers; they eat fish, krill and squid; most species live in the Southern Hemisphere (the Galapagos penguin lives near the equator); the dark back and white belly are countershading, which helps hide them in the water; emperor penguins are the largest penguins, up to about 4 feet (1.2 m) tall, and the father keeps the egg on his feet for about two months. Key: checks for 1, 2, 3 and 7; X for 4, 5, 6 and 8.

## Read
passage: Passage 1: Penguins
  Penguins are birds, but they cannot fly. Their wings work like flippers, so penguins are fast swimmers. They dive into the sea to catch fish and tiny animals called krill.
  Most penguins live in the southern half of the world. A penguin's black back and white belly help it hide in the water. From above, its back looks like the dark sea. From below, its belly looks like the bright sky.
passage: Passage 2: The Emperor Penguin
  The emperor penguin is the biggest penguin. This big bird can grow almost four feet tall. Emperor penguins live in Antarctica, the icy land at the bottom of the world.
  Like all penguins, they cannot fly. They use their wings as flippers to swim and dive deep for fish and krill. In the cold, dark winter, the mother lays one egg. The father keeps the egg warm on his feet for about two months.

## Warm up: Find it
fill:
  - Passage {2} tells how tall the emperor penguin can grow.
  - Passage {1} tells why penguins are black and white.

## Main: Check or X?
table: Fact | Check if both tell it, X if only one
  - 1. Penguins are birds. | {✓}
  - 2. Penguins cannot fly. | {✓}
  - 3. Penguins eat fish and krill. | {✓}
  - 4. The father keeps the egg warm on his feet. | {X}
  - 5. A black back and white belly help penguins hide. | {X}
  - 6. Emperor penguins can grow almost four feet tall. | {X}
  - 7. Penguins use their wings like flippers to swim. | {✓}
  - 8. Most penguins live in the southern half of the world. | {X}

## Stretch
write: Why is it good when two sources agree on a fact?
  lines: 2
  key: If two different sources say the same thing, we can be more sure the fact is true.

# 21. Keyword Note Catcher
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter or crayon
ican: I can take notes with keywords instead of whole sentences.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read the passage with me. Highlight the most important words. Then write keyword notes, just a few words each, in the four boxes.
mistakes: Many children copy whole sentences into the boxes. Show how "Magma is melted rock under the ground" shrinks to "magma = melted rock." If a note has more than about 5 words, ask, "Which words could we cross out and still remember it?"
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 100 words). Facts are checked: magma is melted rock below the surface and is called lava once it reaches the surface; gas helps push magma up in an eruption; the Hawaiian Islands formed from volcanoes; many volcanoes are on the ocean floor. The key shows sample notes; accept any short notes that hold the same ideas.

## Read
passage: Volcanoes
  A volcano is an opening in the crust of the Earth. Deep under the ground, it is so hot that rock melts. Melted rock under the ground is called magma.
  Gas pushes the magma up. When it bursts out, the volcano erupts. Melted rock that comes out of a volcano is called lava. Lava is much hotter than a kitchen oven.
  When lava cools, it turns into hard rock. Over a long time, this rock can build new land. The islands of Hawaii were made by volcanoes. Some volcanoes are hidden under the ocean.

## Warm up: Keywords or a sentence?
example: Sentence: Melted rock under the ground is called magma.
  Keyword note: **magma = melted rock under ground**
choice: Which one is a keyword note?
  - When lava cools, it turns into hard rock.
  - I read that lava turns into rock when it gets cool.
  * lava cools = hard rock

## Main: Fill the four boxes
table: What is a volcano? | How does it erupt?
  - {opening in Earth's crust} | {gas pushes magma up}
  - {magma = melted rock} | {lava comes out}
table: What happens to lava? | Volcanoes make land
  - {cools into hard rock} | {builds new land}
  - {hotter than an oven} | {Hawaii made by volcanoes}

## Stretch: Notes to a sentence
write: Use your notes to write one sentence about volcanoes in your own words.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: Gas pushes melted rock up, and when the lava cools it can make new land like Hawaii.

# 33. Write a Classmate's Page
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, crayons, interview notes about a classmate
ican: I can turn interview notes into a page about a classmate.
directions: (Read aloud.) First, see how notes about Jada became a page. Next, ask your partner the four questions and jot short notes. Then use the frame to write four sentences about your partner for our class book. Last, draw your partner doing something they like.
mistakes: Children copy the notes word for word ("soccer, drawing") instead of making sentences. Point to the frame: each sentence starts with a capital, uses the partner's name or he, she or they, and ends with a period. Others write about themselves; remind them the page is about the partner. Watch for the possessive: Jada's, not Jadas.
notes: Jada is a made-up classmate. Keep the interview to school-friendly topics (a favorite book, something they like to do, something they are good at, something they want to learn); do not ask about family, home or anything private, and let partners skip a question. Pages go into the class book "Many Voices." Answers vary; a strong page has four complete sentences built from the notes, the partner's name, capitals and end marks.

## Warm up: From notes to a page
table: Question | Notes about Jada
  - What is your favorite book about? | whales
  - What do you like to do? | soccer, drawing
  - What are you good at? | saying hello in 3 languages
  - What do you want to learn? | how volcanoes work
example: **Jada's page:** This is Jada. Jada's favorite book is about whales. She likes to play soccer and draw. Jada can say hello in three languages, and she wants to learn how volcanoes work.
fill:
  - The notes said "soccer, drawing." The page says: She likes to {play soccer and draw}.

## Main: Interview your partner
chart: My notes about my partner
  labels: What is your favorite book about?; What do you like to do?; What are you good at?; What do you want to learn?
  key: Short notes, a few words each, such as "dinosaurs," "riding bikes," "building with blocks," "how to swim."
write: Write your partner's page. Use the frame.
  lines: 5
  starter: This is ____. ____'s favorite book is about ____. ____ likes to ____. ____ is good at ____ and wants to learn ____.
  key: Model: This is Leo. Leo's favorite book is about dinosaurs. He likes to ride bikes. Leo is good at building with blocks and wants to learn how to swim.

## Stretch: Draw it
draw: Draw your partner doing something they like. | 2.0
