# 1. Genre Conventions
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can identify the shot, sound and lighting conventions that signal a film genre.
directions: Answer the warm up questions about lighting and sound terms. Then complete the chart: write one genre in each row (comedy, horror, western, science fiction) and the shots, sound and lighting it usually uses. Finish by planning a scene that bends one convention.
mistakes: Students often list plot elements (a monster, a cowboy) instead of film technique. The chart asks how the film is shot, heard and lit. Conventions are tendencies, not rules: plenty of films mix or break them on purpose, and that is worth discussing.
notes: Students can draw on films they have seen, but the chart works from general knowledge. Avoid assigning specific horror films; discuss technique. High-key lighting is bright and even with few shadows; low-key lighting has strong contrast and deep shadows. Diegetic sound comes from the story world (footsteps, a radio); non-diegetic sound is added for the audience (the score, a narrator).

## Warm up
choice: High-key lighting means:
  * Bright, even light with few shadows
  - Very dark scenes with deep shadows
  - Colored lights from below
  - Light only from candles
choice: A light placed below an actor's face, pointing up, is a classic convention of which genre?
  - Comedy
  * Horror
  - Western
  - Musical
choice: The background music score that the characters cannot hear is:
  - Diegetic sound
  * Non-diegetic sound
  - Foley
  - Room tone
choice: Wide shots of open desert and a lone rider on the horizon most strongly signal:
  - Science fiction
  - Comedy
  * Western
  - Horror

## Main: Conventions chart
chart: Genre | Shots | Sound | Lighting
  rows: 4
  key: Comedy: wide and medium shots that show physical comedy and reactions, reaction close-ups, steady framing; upbeat music, timed pauses, comic sound effects; high-key, bright and even. Horror: tight close-ups, point-of-view shots, framing that hides what is off screen, tilted angles; silence broken by sudden loud stings, low drones, creaks; low-key, hard shadows, underlighting. Western: extreme wide shots of landscape, medium wide shots at hip level, extreme close-ups of eyes in standoffs; wind, spurs, hoofbeats, sparse guitar or harmonica score; harsh sunlight, high contrast, silhouettes at sunset. Science fiction: wide establishing shots of worlds and ships, low angles to show scale; electronic score, invented machine hums and beeps; cool blue or neon tones, light from screens and panels, strong contrast.
write: Pick one convention from your chart. Explain why it works on an audience.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: horror's silence before a sudden sting works because the quiet makes viewers lean in and listen, so the loud sound startles them.

## Stretch
write: Plan a short scene that breaks one convention on purpose, such as a horror scene in bright, high-key light. What effect would it have on the audience?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the convention, the change and the effect: a horror scene in cheerful daylight can feel more unsettling because danger appears where the audience feels safe.

# 7. Méliès's Tricks
minutes: 50
materials: pencil; projector to show A Trip to the Moon (1902), which is in the public domain
ican: I can explain how early filmmakers created in-camera effects and judge why they work.
directions: Read about Georges Méliès. Watch A Trip to the Moon as your teacher shows it. Match each still described below to the trick that made it. Then answer the critique questions and plan a trick of your own.
mistakes: Students assume the effects were added later on a computer or by drawing on the film. Everything here happened in the camera, on the set or in the splicing room. Students also mix up the substitution splice (stop, change something, start again) with the dissolve (one shot fades into the next). The moon's face grows because the actor was pulled toward the camera, not because the camera moved.
notes: A Trip to the Moon (Le Voyage dans la Lune) was made in 1902 by Georges Méliès, a stage magician who owned the Théâtre Robert-Houdin in Paris, at his glass-walled studio in Montreuil. It is about 260 meters (845 feet) of film, around 17 minutes at the 12 to 14 frames per second Méliès preferred, though copies online run at different speeds. Facts about the effects follow the film's documented production history: many tricks used the substitution splice; the approaching Moon was an actor covered to the neck in black velvet, on a chair pulled up a ramp toward the camera; the plunge into the sea was a multiple exposure over real ocean footage; the underwater glimpse combined a moving cardboard cutout with an aquarium holding tadpoles and air jets; scenes are joined by dissolves. Some prints were hand-colored frame by frame, and a restored color print premiered in 2011.

## Warm up
text: Georges Méliès was a stage magician before he made films. When he saw the first projected movies in the 1890s, he built a glass-walled studio and used the camera to perform impossible tricks. A Trip to the Moon (1902) follows six astronomers who are shot to the Moon in a capsule, meet the Moon's insect-like Selenites, and escape back to Earth.
choice: Why did a magician make such a good early filmmaker?
  - Magicians owned all the cameras
  * He already knew how to fool an audience's eyes, and film gave him new ways to do it
  - Films had to be about magic in 1902
  - Magicians did not need actors
choice: In a substitution splice, what does the filmmaker do?
  - Paints each frame by hand
  - Moves the camera on a track
  * Stops the camera, changes something in the scene, then starts filming again
  - Plays two films at once

## Main: Match the still to the trick
match: Write the letter of the trick that made each still.
  - The Man in the Moon's face grows larger as the capsule approaches = An actor in a moon face, covered to the neck in black, is pulled on a chair up a ramp toward the camera
  - The capsule is suddenly stuck in the Moon's eye = Stop the camera, put the model capsule in the eye, start again
  - A Selenite struck by an astronomer vanishes in a puff of smoke = Stop the camera, remove the actor, set off a puff of smoke, start again
  - An astronomer's umbrella, stuck in the ground, becomes a giant mushroom = Stop the camera, swap the umbrella for a mushroom prop, start again
  - The capsule plunges into real ocean waves = A shot of the capsule falling against black, laid over film of the real sea (multiple exposure)
  - Underwater, the capsule floats up past bubbles and tiny creatures = A moving cardboard cutout combined with an aquarium of tadpoles and air jets
  - One scene melts gradually into the next = A dissolve

## Main: Critique
choice: Why did Méliès move the actor toward the camera instead of moving the camera?
  - Cameras could not record moving actors
  - The actor asked to be moved
  - It was the only way to make the film in color
  * His camera was heavy, and moving the actor let him place the face exactly where he wanted in the frame
write: Choose the trick you think works best. Explain why it fools or delights the audience, using what the viewer sees and what the viewer does not see.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a trick, describes the effect on screen, and explains the hidden method, such as "The Selenite vanishing works because the cut is hidden in the smoke: the viewer sees a puff and assumes the creature exploded, never seeing the actor walk off between shots."
write: Name a modern effect that does the same job as one of Méliès's tricks, and explain the connection.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: a jump cut or cut-on-action in a magic video works like the substitution splice; green screen and digital compositing do what his multiple exposure did by layering separate shots; a dolly or zoom does what his moving chair imitated.

## Stretch
write: Plan an in-camera trick you could film with a phone on a tripod, using a substitution splice. Describe what the viewer sees and the exact steps to film it.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Example: a backpack turns into a pile of books. Steps: lock the phone on a tripod, film the backpack on a desk, stop recording without moving anything, swap in the books in the same spot, record again, and trim the clips so the change happens in one frame. A strong plan keeps the camera and everything else still.

# 20. Contrast Check
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can test text and background colors against contrast guidelines and fix the pairs that fail.
directions: Read how contrast ratio works. For each of the 8 color pairs, calculate the contrast ratio from the luminance values, decide whether it passes, and then fix every pair that fails using the swatches.
mistakes: Students put the darker color on top of the fraction; the lighter color always goes on top, so the ratio is at least 1. Students also forget the 0.05 in both the top and the bottom. Large text has a lower minimum (3:1) than body text (4.5:1); a pair can pass as a headline and fail as body text.
notes: The rule is from the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2), level AA: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (about 18 point, or 14 point bold). Level AAA asks for 7:1 and 4.5:1. Relative luminance runs from 0 (black) to 1 (white); the values on the sheet were computed from each color's hex code with the WCAG formula and rounded to 4 places, and the key's ratios are rounded to one decimal. The same guideline is a good rule of thumb for posters and slides, which are often viewed from a distance or in poor light. Contrast is one check: size, font weight and not using color alone matter too.

## Warm up
example: **Contrast ratio** = (L of the lighter color + 0.05) / (L of the darker color + 0.05), where L is the color's relative luminance. **Example:** gray #767676 (L = 0.1812) on white #FFFFFF (L = 1). Ratio = 1.05 / 0.2312 = 4.54, written 4.5:1. That passes for body text, just barely.
fill:
  - Black (L = 0) on white (L = 1): ratio = {21}:1
  - Body text needs a ratio of at least {4.5}:1.
  - Large text needs a ratio of at least {3}:1.

## Main: Test the 8 pairs
table: # | Text color (L) | Background (L) | Used for | Ratio | Pass?
  - 1 | gray #999999 (0.3185) | white #FFFFFF (1) | body text | {2.8} | {no}
  - 2 | navy #003366 (0.0333) | white #FFFFFF (1) | body text | {12.6} | {yes}
  - 3 | white #FFFFFF (1) | orange #FFA500 (0.4817) | large heading | {2.0} | {no}
  - 4 | blue #0066CC (0.1386) | white #FFFFFF (1) | body text | {5.6} | {yes}
  - 5 | gray #666666 (0.1329) | light gray #E0E0E0 (0.7454) | body text | {4.3} | {no}
  - 6 | white #FFFFFF (1) | green #008000 (0.1544) | large heading | {5.1} | {yes}
  - 7 | blue #0000FF (0.0722) | black #000000 (0) | body text | {2.4} | {no}
  - 8 | dark gray #444444 (0.0578) | gray #D4D4D4 (0.6584) | body text | {6.6} | {yes}
choice: Pair 5 fails as body text. What if the same colors were used for a large heading instead?
  - It would still fail
  * It would pass, because large text needs only 3:1
  - It would pass only in color printing
  - Contrast does not apply to headings

## Main: Fix the failing pairs
text: **Swatches:** dark gray #595959 (L = 0.0999), near-black #1A1A1A (0.0103), charcoal #333333 (0.0331), yellow #FFFF00 (0.9278). Change only one color in each failing pair. Show the new ratio.
table: Failing pair | Color you change | New color | New ratio | Passes now?
  - {1} | {text: #999999} | {#595959} | {7.0} | yes
  - {3} | {text: white} | {#1A1A1A} | {8.8} | yes
  - {5} | {text: #666666} | {#333333} | {9.6} | yes
  - {7} | {text: #0000FF} | {#FFFF00} | {19.6} | yes
note: Other fixes can work: in pair 5, #595959 on #E0E0E0 gives (0.7454 + 0.05) / (0.0999 + 0.05) = 5.3, which passes. Accept any swatch whose correctly calculated ratio meets the minimum for its use.

## Stretch
write: A designer says, "My orange and white logo looks great, so I will use it for the small print too." Using pair 3, explain why that is a problem and suggest a fix that keeps the brand's orange.
  lines: 3
  key: White on orange is only 2.0:1, which fails even for large text, so small print would be very hard to read, especially for people with low vision or in bright light. Keep the orange as a background with near-black text (8.8:1), or use the orange for shapes and accents while body text stays dark on white.

# 21. Festival Poster Series
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler, colored pencils or markers
ican: I can design a poster series with a consistent visual identity.
directions: Read the festival brief. Answer the questions about visual identity. Plan what stays the same and what changes across the series. Then sketch three posters that look like one family.
mistakes: Students often design three good but unrelated posters. Check: would someone know at a glance they come from the same festival? The logo, grid and type should repeat; one or two things (an image, an accent color) should change. Students also crowd the posters: leave room so the event name and date read from across a hallway.
notes: The festival is fictional. A quick test for consistency: hold up all three thumbnails from across the room and ask the class what they have in common. Thumbnails can be small and rough; the plan matters more than finished art.

## Read the brief
example: **Riverbend Student Film Festival**, three nights in the school auditorium. **Night 1:** Animation. **Night 2:** Documentary. **Night 3:** Comedy Shorts. All shows start at 7:00 p.m. Admission is free. The festival wants a poster for each night that clearly belongs to one festival.

## Warm up
choice: What most makes a poster series feel consistent?
  - Every poster uses a different typeface
  * The same logo, grid, type and placement repeat on every poster
  - Every poster uses a different layout
  - The posters are different sizes
choice: In a series, what is a good thing to change from poster to poster?
  - The festival name
  - The logo
  * The main image or accent color for each night
  - The typeface for the date
choice: What should a viewer read first on each poster?
  * The festival and night name
  - The small print about seating
  - The printer's name
  - The page number
choice: A grid in poster design is:
  - A pattern of lines printed on the poster
  * An invisible set of columns and margins that keeps placement consistent
  - A type of font
  - A frame around the edge

# 33. Structure Options
minutes: 50
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare chronological, thematic and character-driven structures and choose one for my documentary.
directions: Identify the structure in each warm-up description. Use the footage list for the sample film to plan it three ways. Then plan your own topic three ways and choose a structure with reasons.
mistakes: Students confuse thematic with chronological because both can move forward in time. A thematic film is organized by ideas (each section answers a question or explores a theme); a chronological film is organized by when things happened. Character-driven does not mean "has interviews"; it means the film follows a person's goal and change, and the audience's question is "What happens to them?"
notes: The Night Market and its people are made up. Most real documentaries mix structures (for example, a character-driven spine with thematic sections), so the final choice can be a blend if the student names which structure leads. Students who have not picked a topic can plan a documentary about a place in their school or neighborhood.

## Warm up
choice: A film moves from 1950 to today, showing how a town's river was polluted and then cleaned up, year by year. Which structure?
  * Chronological
  - Thematic
  - Character-driven
choice: A film about school lunch has three sections: "Cost," "Nutrition," and "Waste." Which structure?
  - Chronological
  * Thematic
  - Character-driven
choice: A film follows one teenager through the season as she tries to make the state chess finals. Which structure?
  - Chronological
  - Thematic
  * Character-driven

## Main: Plan the sample film three ways
example: **The Night Market (sample footage list).** Old photos of the market's first night in 2015. Ana, who has sold tamales there since the first night, and her daughter Rosa, who wants to take over the stand. A teen musician who plays every Friday. The city council vote to move the market to a new parking lot. A rainy night with almost no customers. The first night at the new location. Interviews with shoppers about why they come.
chart: How it is organized | Opening scene | Ending scene | Strength | Risk
  labels: Chronological; Thematic; Character-driven
  height: 1
  key: Chronological: from 2015 to the move, in order; opens on old photos of the first night; ends on the first night at the new location; strength: easy to follow, shows change over time; risk: can feel like a list of events with no one to care about. Thematic: sections such as "Food," "Music," "Belonging" and "Change"; opens on shoppers saying why they come; ends on the strongest idea (belonging survives the move); strength: explores what the market means; risk: can lose story tension and repeat itself. Character-driven: follows Ana and Rosa through the year of the move; opens on Ana setting up her stand; ends on Rosa running the stand at the new location; strength: emotional and suspenseful (will the stand survive?); risk: leaves out other vendors and the bigger picture unless woven in.

## Main: Your topic
write: My documentary topic, in one sentence:
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a specific, filmable topic with real people or places the student can reach.
chart: Opening scene | Middle (two or three sequences) | Ending scene
  labels: Chronological; Thematic; Character-driven
  height: 0.9
  key: Answers vary. Each row should be truly organized by its structure: by time, by idea, or by a person's goal and change.
write: Which structure will you use? Give two reasons based on your footage and your audience, and one risk you will watch for.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer picks one structure (or names the lead structure in a blend), ties it to what the student can actually film and what the audience should feel or learn, and names the matching risk from the chart with a plan to avoid it.
