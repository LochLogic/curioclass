# 1. One Question, Many Experts
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how historians, geographers, economists and political scientists study the same question in different ways.
directions: Our compelling question is "Why did Rome fall?" Read the fact file. Match each expert to what they study. Then fill each expert's panel with one question they would ask and one piece of evidence from the fact file they would use.
mistakes: Students often give every expert the same question with new labels. Push each panel toward its own lens: the historian asks about change over time and sources, the geographer about place, borders and movement, the economist about money, trade and resources, and the political scientist about power, leaders and government. Students also say "Rome fell in a day"; the fact file shows a long process, and the eastern half lasted until 1453.
notes: All facts in the fact file are standard textbook facts about the Western Roman Empire. Historians still debate how much each cause mattered, which is the point of the sheet: different experts weigh different evidence. A quick follow-up: have panels of four students each take one expert and argue for their cause.

## Fact file: The Western Roman Empire
fill:
  - From 235 to 284 CE, Rome had more than 20 emperors, and most were killed or died in battle.
  - Emperors put less and less silver in Roman coins, so prices rose sharply.
  - The empire's borders along the Rhine and Danube rivers stretched thousands of miles and needed huge armies.
  - In 395 CE the empire was split for good into a western half (capital Rome, later Ravenna) and an eastern half (capital Constantinople).
  - Goths and other peoples moved into the empire, some pushed west by the Huns. Visigoths captured and looted Rome in 410 CE.
  - Heavy taxes pushed some farmers to give their land to rich landowners for protection.
  - In 476 CE, Odoacer, a Germanic general, removed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus.
  - The eastern half, later called the Byzantine Empire, lasted until 1453.

## Warm up: Who studies what?
match: Match each expert to their focus.
  - Historian = How things changed over time, using sources from the past
  - Geographer = Places, land, borders and the movement of people
  - Economist = Money, trade, taxes and how people use resources
  - Political scientist = Power, leaders, laws and how governments work

## Main: Four panels
write: **Historian.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: Did Rome fall suddenly in 476, or decline over centuries? Evidence: the crisis of 235 to 284, the split in 395, the sack of 410 and the end in 476 show a long process.
write: **Geographer.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did the size and borders of the empire make it hard to defend? Evidence: the Rhine and Danube frontiers stretched thousands of miles; peoples migrating west crossed them.
write: **Economist.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did money problems weaken the empire? Evidence: less silver in coins caused rising prices, and heavy taxes pushed farmers to give up their land.
write: **Political scientist.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did unstable leadership weaken Rome's government? Evidence: more than 20 emperors in about 50 years, most killed, and a general removing the last emperor in 476.

## Stretch: Did Rome fall?
write: The eastern half of the empire lasted almost 1,000 more years. Which expert would be most interested in that fact, and how might it change the compelling question?
  lines: 3
  key: A historian (or political scientist): it suggests asking "Why did the western half fall while the eastern half survived?" Answers might point to the East's richer cities, stronger defenses at Constantinople and steadier government.

# 6. Procopius on Justinian and Theodora
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, two highlighters (two colors)
ican: I can compare two sources by the same author and explain how purpose and audience shape what a source says.
directions: Read the background and both sources. Highlight praise in one color and attacks in the other. Then answer the questions, citing the source letter and excerpt number (for example, B2).
mistakes: Students decide that one source is "true" and the other is "lies." Both are evidence: Buildings shows what Procopius could say in public under the emperor's eye; the Secret History shows what he said in private, full of anger and gossip. Neither is a neutral report. Students also miss that some claims can be checked: Hagia Sophia still stands in Istanbul, so A2 can be tested, while B3 (rulers as demons) cannot be checked at all.
notes: Procopius of Caesarea (about 500 to 565 CE) served as secretary to the general Belisarius and wrote the Wars, the official history of Justinian's wars. The Secret History was written about 550 but kept hidden; it was found in the Vatican Library and first printed in 1623. Buildings dates to the 550s. All excerpts are adapted (shortened, modern wording) except B2, which quotes the 1896 English translation of the Secret History (Project Gutenberg). A3 follows Buildings, Book 1, on a statue of Theodora; check the wording against a full translation before quoting it as exact. Keep the discussion on purpose and evidence; the Secret History's attacks on Theodora's early life are not part of this sheet.

## Background
text: Justinian ruled the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire from 527 to 565 CE, with his wife, Empress Theodora, as a powerful partner until her death in 548. He rebuilt Constantinople's great church, Hagia Sophia, reconquered lands in Italy and North Africa, and organized Roman law into the Justinian Code. The historian Procopius wrote about him in two very different books.

## Read: One author, two books
source: Source A: adapted from Procopius, Buildings, Book 1 (written in the 550s, a public book praising the emperor's building projects)
  [1] In our own time the Emperor Justinian took over a state that was shaken and weak. He made it greater in size and far more splendid.
  [2] The church of Hagia Sophia is a sight of wonderful beauty. Its huge dome seems not to rest on stone at all, but to hang from heaven on a golden chain.
  [3] A statue of the Empress Theodora stands here. The statue is lovely, yet it still falls short of the beauty of the Empress herself.
source: Source B: Procopius, the Secret History (written about 550, hidden during his life). B2 is quoted from an 1896 translation; the others are adapted.
  [1] In my earlier books I could not tell the whole truth about these rulers while they were alive. I feared their spies, and I feared a cruel death. Now I will reveal what was hidden.
  [2] "Justinian exhausted all this wealth in a very short time, partly by senseless buildings on the coast, partly by presents to the barbarians [...]"
  [3] Justinian and Theodora were not human beings but demons in human form, who set out to bring the greatest possible harm to all people.
  [4] The Empress ruled by fear. Anyone who angered her was punished, and officials obeyed her more than they obeyed the laws.

## Analyze
choice: Who was the most likely audience for Source A?
  - Only Procopius himself, as a private diary
  * The emperor's court and the public, during Justinian's reign
  - Enemies of the empire in Persia
  - Readers a thousand years later
choice: According to B1, why did Procopius write so differently in his earlier books?
  - He had not yet met Justinian.
  - He was paid more for the Secret History.
  - He changed his mind after the emperor died.
  * He feared spies and punishment if he criticized the rulers.
table: Topic | What Source A says | What Source B says
  height: 0.6
  - Justinian's rule | {A1: he made a weak state greater and more splendid} | {B3: he was a demon who set out to harm everyone}
  - Justinian's buildings | {A2: Hagia Sophia is wonderfully beautiful, its dome seems to hang from heaven} | {B2: he wasted the treasury on "senseless buildings"}
  - Theodora | {A3: more beautiful than even her lovely statue} | {B4: she ruled by fear and punished anyone who angered her}
write: Which claim in these sources can a historian check today, and which cannot be checked at all? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: A2 can be checked: Hagia Sophia still stands in Istanbul, and its dome does look as if it floats on a ring of windows. B3 cannot be checked, because no evidence could prove someone was a demon; it is an insult, not a fact. (Also accept: B2 can be partly checked against records of building costs and taxes.)
write: What was Procopius's purpose in each book? Use one quotation or excerpt number from each.
  lines: 4
  key: In Buildings his purpose was to praise the emperor in public and keep his favor, so he flatters even Theodora's statue (A3). In the Secret History his purpose was to expose and attack rulers he hated, writing in secret where he could not be punished (B1), so he calls their building "senseless" (B2) and calls them demons (B3).

## Stretch: Is either one reliable?
write: A classmate says, "Procopius lied in one book, so we can't trust him at all." Do you agree? Explain how a historian could still use both books.
  lines: 4
  key: Disagree: both books are biased in opposite directions, but each is still useful. Buildings shows what the court wanted people to believe and describes buildings we can check. The Secret History shows real anger among some officials and may hold facts that could not be printed, but its claims must be checked against other sources such as laws, other writers and surviving buildings. Comparing them shows how fear and purpose shape history.

# 18. Tang and Song Invention Trading Cards
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, textbook chapter on Tang and Song China, scissors (optional, to cut the cards apart)
ican: I can explain how Tang and Song inventions worked, how they spread and how they changed the world.
directions: Each row of the big table is one trading card. Use your textbook to fill in each card: when it was invented, what it was used for, how it spread beyond China, and its impact. Then rank the inventions by how much they changed the world.
mistakes: Students put every invention in the Tang dynasty. Several are Song inventions (movable type, the compass for sailing, paper money, the pound lock); the Tang dynasty ended in 907 and the Song began in 960. Students also say gunpowder was invented for war. Tang alchemists found it while searching for a medicine to make people live forever; it was used for fireworks and signals before weapons. Watch for "Europeans copied it" for printing: Gutenberg's press (about 1450) may have been invented separately.
notes: Dates are the earliest well-documented evidence. The Diamond Sutra (868) is the oldest dated printed book that survives. Bi Sheng's movable type (1040s) is known from Shen Kuo's Dream Pool Essays, which also describes the magnetic needle (1088). The first written gunpowder formula is in the Wujing Zongyao, a military book of 1044. The Song government issued official paper money (jiaozi) in the 1020s, after merchants in Sichuan used private notes. Qiao Weiyue built a pound lock on the Grand Canal in 984. Accept reasonable textbook dates within a few decades and any accurate impact.

## Warm up: Two dynasties
fill:
  - The Tang dynasty ruled China from 618 to {907} CE.
  - The Song dynasty ruled China from {960} to 1279 CE.
  - The Song dynasty lasted {319} years.
  - The trade routes that carried goods and ideas across Asia by land were called the {Silk Road}.

## Main: Fill the six cards
table: Card | Date and dynasty | What it was used for | How it spread | Impact
  height: 1.0
  - **Printing** | {Woodblock printing in the Tang (Diamond Sutra, 868); movable type by Bi Sheng in the Song (1040s)} | {Copying Buddhist texts, books, calendars and study books for exams} | {To Korea and Japan; Korea later made metal type} | {Cheaper books; more people could study for the civil service exams}
  - **Gunpowder** | {Found by Tang alchemists in the 800s; first written formula in the Song, 1044} | {Fireworks and signals, then fire arrows, bombs and early guns} | {Through the Mongols and traders to the Middle East and Europe in the 1200s} | {Changed warfare: cannons could break castle walls}
  - **Magnetic compass** | {Song; described by Shen Kuo in 1088; used at sea by about 1100} | {Finding direction at sea when clouds hid the sun and stars} | {By sea traders to the Arab world and Europe by about 1200} | {Made long ocean voyages safer; helped the Age of Exploration}
  - **Paper money** | {Song; government notes in the 1020s} | {Replacing heavy strings of iron coins for buying and trading} | {Used by the Mongol Yuan dynasty; described by Marco Polo; much later adopted in Europe} | {Trade grew easier, but printing too much caused prices to rise}
  - **Porcelain** | {Developed by the Tang, perfected by the Song} | {Fine, strong dishes, cups and vases} | {By the Silk Road and sea routes to Southeast Asia, the Middle East, East Africa and later Europe} | {A treasured export; Europeans called it "china" and tried for centuries to copy it}
  - **Canal pound lock** | {Song; built on the Grand Canal in 984} | {Raising and lowering boats safely between water levels} | {Locks appeared in Europe centuries later, perhaps invented again there} | {More grain and goods moved on the Grand Canal between south and north}

## Rank them
text: Number the inventions from 1 (changed the world the most) to 6 (the least): ___ printing ___ gunpowder ___ compass ___ paper money ___ porcelain ___ canal locks
write: Explain your number 1 choice. Use evidence from its card.
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: printing, because cheap books spread knowledge to far more people. In China it helped more people prepare for the civil service exams, and printed books later carried religion, science and news around the world. (Accept any choice backed by evidence from its card.)

## Stretch: A chain of effects
write: Choose two inventions that worked together and explain how. (Example: how could the compass and porcelain help each other?)
  lines: 3
  key: Sample: the compass made long sea voyages safer, so Song ships could carry heavy porcelain to Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean in large amounts, and porcelain became one of China's most valuable exports. (Also accept: paper money and the canal lock both made trade easier; printing spread knowledge of gunpowder.)

# 25. Black Death Spread Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils (4 colors)
ican: I can map how the Black Death spread and connect its path to trade routes.
directions: Plot each city on the grid and label it with the year plague arrived. Color the dots by year using the key you make. Draw the sea route the plague followed from Caffa to Genoa. Then explain how trade carried the disease.
mistakes: For the 1347 question, Alexandria is also correct. Students often draw one neat line through every city, as if plague marched in single file. It spread in many directions at once, along roads, rivers and especially sea lanes. Watch for longitude errors: London is just west of 0, so its dot sits slightly left of the 0 line.
notes: Dates are the years the plague reached each city in the 1346 to 1353 outbreak (Caffa during the siege of 1346; Constantinople summer 1347; Messina, Sicily, October 1347; Genoa and Venice January 1348; Bergen 1349; Moscow 1353). Historians estimate it killed between about a third and a half of Europe's people. As plague spread, Jewish communities in many cities were falsely blamed and attacked; if students raise it, address it factually. The grid has no coastlines; compare with a printed map of medieval trade routes afterward.

## Explain: Trade and disease
choice: Most of the first cities hit were alike in what way?
  - They were in the mountains, far from the sea.
  * They were busy seaports on trade routes.
  - They were the largest capitals in Europe.
  - They were all in the far north.
write: Explain how trade ships could carry plague from city to city. Use at least two cities from the table.
  lines: 4
  key: Ships carried goods, people, and rats with infected fleas. Genoese galleys fleeing Caffa reached Constantinople, then Messina in October 1347, then Genoa in January 1348; each port then passed the disease along its own trade routes inland and by sea.
write: Moscow and Bergen were reached last. Use location to explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: They were far from the Mediterranean ports where plague arrived first, so the disease needed more time to travel overland and along northern trade routes; Bergen was reached by a ship from England in 1349, and Moscow is far inland.

## Stretch: Then and now
write: Medieval cities sometimes made arriving ships wait offshore before anyone came ashore. Why might that work, and what does it show people understood even without knowing about germs?
  lines: 3
  key: Waiting kept sick people, rats and fleas off land until any illness showed itself; it shows people noticed the disease came with ships and travelers. Venice's ports later set a 40-day wait, which gave us the word quarantine (from the Italian for forty).

# 31. Maya, Aztec, Inca Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils, a physical map of the Americas (textbook or atlas)
ican: I can locate the Maya, Aztec and Inca civilizations and explain how each adapted to its landform.
directions: Plot and label each city on the grid from its latitude and longitude. Shade and label the land of each civilization using your atlas. Then complete the chart comparing their landforms and how people farmed them.
mistakes: Students often call Tikal "the Maya capital." The Maya were many city-states, each with its own king, never one empire with one capital; Tikal was one of the largest. Students also mix up the timelines: the Classic Maya cities were at their height about 250 to 900 CE, centuries before the Aztec (1325 to 1521) and Inca (about 1438 to 1533) empires, which both fell to Spanish invaders.
notes: Coordinates are modern locations of the sites, rounded (Tenochtitlan lies under Mexico City). Elevations are approximate: Tikal about 250 meters, Mexico City about 2,240 meters, Cusco about 3,400 meters. The grid has no coastlines: after plotting, have students compare with an atlas map and sketch the coast of Mexico, Central America and western South America. The key draws only the dots.

  - Aztec = Empire ruled from an island city in a lake, high in a mountain valley
  - Inca = Empire stretching along the Andes, linked by thousands of miles of roads

  lines: 3

Roads with bridges and rest houses held the empire together. The Maya were separate city-states, each ruling a smaller area, so no single ruler needed roads across the whole region.

# 43. Zheng He and Columbus Compare
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator (optional), a world map
ican: I can compare the voyages of Zheng He and Christopher Columbus using data and explain why their results were so different.
directions: Read the data table. Do the calculations, trace each route on your world map, then compare goals and results and explain why one set of voyages changed the world and the other ended.
mistakes: Students conclude that China was "less advanced" because its voyages stopped. Ming China had the larger fleet and the better ships; its rulers chose to stop because the voyages were costly and the empire's main threats were on its northern land border. Students also forget the people already living where Columbus landed. His voyages began the Spanish conquest; the Taíno people of the Caribbean were enslaved and died in huge numbers from violence and new diseases.
notes: Fleet numbers come from Ming records and are estimates; the size of Zheng He's largest "treasure ships" is debated (Ming records suggest more than 120 meters; many historians think 60 to 75 meters is more likely), so the sheet does not ask students to compute with it. Columbus's crew on the first voyage was about 90. Zheng He was a Muslim from Yunnan who served the Yongle Emperor as a court official. His seventh and last voyage ended in 1433.

## The data
table: | Zheng He (Ming China) | Christopher Columbus (for Spain)
  - Number of voyages | 7, from 1405 to 1433 | 4, from 1492 to 1504
  - Ships on the first voyage | about 300 | 3
  - Crew on the first voyage | about 27,800 | about 90
  - Farthest places reached | India, the Persian Gulf, Arabia and the coast of East Africa | The Caribbean islands and the coast of Central and South America
  - Who paid | The Ming emperor | The king and queen of Spain

## Warm up: Calculate
fill:
  - Zheng He's first voyage set out {87} years before Columbus's first voyage.
  - Zheng He's fleet had about {100} times as many ships as Columbus's.
  - Zheng He's first crew was about {300} times as large as Columbus's (round to the nearest hundred).
  - Zheng He's last voyage ended {59} years before Columbus sailed.

## Main: Goals, routes and results
table: | Zheng He | Columbus
  height: 0.75
  - Goals | {Show Ming power, bring rulers into China's tribute system, trade and diplomacy} | {Find a western sea route to Asia's spices and gold; win wealth and land for Spain; spread Christianity}
  - Route | {From China through Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean to India, the Persian Gulf and East Africa} | {From Spain to the Canary Islands, then west across the Atlantic to the Bahamas, Cuba and Hispaniola}
  - Results | {Gifts and envoys came to China (even a giraffe); the voyages stopped after 1433 and China did not build an overseas empire} | {Lasting contact between the hemispheres, the Columbian Exchange, Spanish conquest and the enslavement and deaths of the Taíno}
choice: Why did the Ming court stop Zheng He's voyages?
  - Zheng He's ships were too weak to cross oceans.
  - The voyages discovered nothing new.
  * The voyages were very expensive, and officials wanted to spend on defending the northern border instead.
  - Spain defeated the Ming fleet.
choice: How was Columbus's voyage different in its results?
  * It led to permanent European colonies and changed life in two hemispheres.
  - It brought tribute and gifts to Spain from Asian rulers.
  - It ended after one voyage.
  - It found the western sea route to Asia that Columbus was looking for.

## Stretch: What if?
write: The larger fleet did not change the world as much as the smaller one. Explain why, using the goals and results in your table.
  lines: 4
  key: Size did not decide the result; goals did. Zheng He sailed to show Ming power and collect tribute, then went home, and the court ended the voyages, so no colonies or lasting trade empire followed. Columbus's backers wanted land and wealth, and Spain kept sending ships, settlers and soldiers, so his voyages led to conquest and lasting change in both hemispheres.
