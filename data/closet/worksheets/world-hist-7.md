# 1. One Question, Many Experts
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how historians, geographers, economists and political scientists study the same question in different ways.
directions: Our compelling question is "Why did Rome fall?" Read the fact file. Match each expert to what they study. Then fill each expert's panel with one question they would ask and one piece of evidence from the fact file they would use.
mistakes: Students often give every expert the same question with new labels. Push each panel toward its own lens: the historian asks about change over time and sources, the geographer about place, borders and movement, the economist about money, trade and resources, and the political scientist about power, leaders and government. Students also say "Rome fell in a day"; the fact file shows a long process, and the eastern half lasted until 1453.
notes: All facts in the fact file are standard textbook facts about the Western Roman Empire. Historians still debate how much each cause mattered, which is the point of the sheet: different experts weigh different evidence. A quick follow-up: have panels of four students each take one expert and argue for their cause.

## Fact file: The Western Roman Empire
fill:
  - From 235 to 284 CE, Rome had more than 20 emperors, and most were killed or died in battle.
  - Emperors put less and less silver in Roman coins, so prices rose sharply.
  - The empire's borders along the Rhine and Danube rivers stretched thousands of miles and needed huge armies.
  - In 395 CE the empire was split for good into a western half (capital Rome, later Ravenna) and an eastern half (capital Constantinople).
  - Goths and other peoples moved into the empire, some pushed west by the Huns. Visigoths captured and looted Rome in 410 CE.
  - Heavy taxes pushed some farmers to give their land to rich landowners for protection.
  - In 476 CE, Odoacer, a Germanic general, removed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus.
  - The eastern half, later called the Byzantine Empire, lasted until 1453.

## Warm up: Who studies what?
match: Match each expert to their focus.
  - Historian = How things changed over time, using sources from the past
  - Geographer = Places, land, borders and the movement of people
  - Economist = Money, trade, taxes and how people use resources
  - Political scientist = Power, leaders, laws and how governments work

## Main: Four panels
write: **Historian.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: Did Rome fall suddenly in 476, or decline over centuries? Evidence: the crisis of 235 to 284, the split in 395, the sack of 410 and the end in 476 show a long process.
write: **Geographer.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did the size and borders of the empire make it hard to defend? Evidence: the Rhine and Danube frontiers stretched thousands of miles; peoples migrating west crossed them.
write: **Economist.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did money problems weaken the empire? Evidence: less silver in coins caused rising prices, and heavy taxes pushed farmers to give up their land.
write: **Political scientist.** A question this expert would ask about Rome's fall, and one fact they would use as evidence.
  lines: 3
  key: Question: How did unstable leadership weaken Rome's government? Evidence: more than 20 emperors in about 50 years, most killed, and a general removing the last emperor in 476.

## Stretch: Did Rome fall?
write: The eastern half of the empire lasted almost 1,000 more years. Which expert would be most interested in that fact, and how might it change the compelling question?
  lines: 3
  key: A historian (or political scientist): it suggests asking "Why did the western half fall while the eastern half survived?" Answers might point to the East's richer cities, stronger defenses at Constantinople and steadier government.

# 25. Black Death Spread Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils (4 colors)
ican: I can map how the Black Death spread and connect its path to trade routes.
directions: Plot each city on the grid and label it with the year plague arrived. Color the dots by year using the key you make. Draw the sea route the plague followed from Caffa to Genoa. Then explain how trade carried the disease.
mistakes: For the 1347 question, Alexandria is also correct. Students often draw one neat line through every city, as if plague marched in single file. It spread in many directions at once, along roads, rivers and especially sea lanes. Watch for longitude errors: London is just west of 0, so its dot sits slightly left of the 0 line.
notes: Dates are the years the plague reached each city in the 1346 to 1353 outbreak (Caffa during the siege of 1346; Constantinople summer 1347; Messina, Sicily, October 1347; Genoa and Venice January 1348; Bergen 1349; Moscow 1353). Historians estimate it killed between about a third and a half of Europe's people. As plague spread, Jewish communities in many cities were falsely blamed and attacked; if students raise it, address it factually. The grid has no coastlines; compare with a printed map of medieval trade routes afterward.

## Main: Map the spread
text: Make a color key: one color for 1346 and 1347, one for 1348, one for 1349 and one for 1350 to 1353. Plot and color each city.
graph: Longitude (°E; west is negative) | -10 40 5 | Latitude (°N) | 30 65 5
  key: 35,45 29,41 16,38 9,44
text: Draw the sea route Genoese galleys sailed: Caffa to Constantinople to Messina to Genoa. Straight lines may stand for sea lanes.
fill:
  - Where did plague first appear in this data? {Caffa}
  - Name one city plague reached in 1347 besides Constantinople. {Messina}
  - About how many years did it take to travel from Caffa to Moscow? {7}
  - In which year did plague reach the most cities in this table? {1348}

## Explain: Trade and disease
choice: Most of the first cities hit were alike in what way?
  - They were in the mountains, far from the sea.
  * They were busy seaports on trade routes.
  - They were the largest capitals in Europe.
  - They were all in the far north.
write: Explain how trade ships could carry plague from city to city. Use at least two cities from the table.
  lines: 4
  key: Ships carried goods, people, and rats with infected fleas. Genoese galleys fleeing Caffa reached Constantinople, then Messina in October 1347, then Genoa in January 1348; each port then passed the disease along its own trade routes inland and by sea.
write: Moscow and Bergen were reached last. Use location to explain why.
  lines: 3
  key: They were far from the Mediterranean ports where plague arrived first, so the disease needed more time to travel overland and along northern trade routes; Bergen was reached by a ship from England in 1349, and Moscow is far inland.

## Stretch: Then and now
write: Medieval cities sometimes made arriving ships wait offshore before anyone came ashore. Why might that work, and what does it show people understood even without knowing about germs?
  lines: 3
  key: Waiting kept sick people, rats and fleas off land until any illness showed itself; it shows people noticed the disease came with ships and travelers. Venice's ports later set a 40-day wait, which gave us the word quarantine (from the Italian for forty).
