# 1. Ad Tricks Detective
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can find the tricks ads use to sell food.
directions: Read the three pretend cereal ads. Use the trick checklist: write yes if the ad uses the trick and no if it does not. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students may call "packed with vitamins" or "made with real fruit" plain facts. They can be true and still be a health halo: a healthy-sounding claim that makes a sugary food seem healthy. Check the tiny print and the Nutrition Facts label. "Part of a complete breakfast" is tiny print: it means the cereal alone is not a complete breakfast.
notes: The three ads and their cereals are made up for Curio Class. Ads are not lies by themselves; the point is noticing what an ad wants you to feel and what it leaves out. Good follow-up: bring in a real cereal box (any brand) and run the checklist on its front panel, then compare with its Nutrition Facts label.

## Warm up: The trick checklist
text: **1. Mascot:** a cartoon character sells it. **2. Free prize:** a toy or prize inside or for box tops. **3. Health halo:** healthy-sounding words on a sugary food. **4. Everybody:** "all the kids love it." **5. Super power:** promises of energy, power or winning. **6. Tiny print:** small words that change the meaning.

## Read: Three pretend ads
source: Pretend ad 1: Rocket Rex Crunch (made up for this sheet)
  Rocket Rex the cartoon dinosaur blasts off! A glow-in-the-dark rocket in every box! Rocket Rex Crunch gives you super rocket power all morning long! (tiny print: part of a complete breakfast)
source: Pretend ad 2: Berry Bliss Bites (made up for this sheet)
  Made with real fruit! Now with whole grain! Kids everywhere are switching to Berry Bliss Bites. (tiny print: contains 2% or less of dried strawberries)
source: Pretend ad 3: Champ Choco Puffs (made up for this sheet)
  Champ the cartoon kangaroo says, "Every kid in school eats Champ Choco Puffs!" Packed with vitamins! Send in 5 box tops for a free soccer ball. You'll run faster than ever!

## Main: Check each ad
table: Trick | Ad 1: Rocket Rex | Ad 2: Berry Bliss | Ad 3: Champ
  - 1. Mascot | {yes} | {no} | {yes}
  - 2. Free prize | {yes} | {no} | {yes}
  - 3. Health halo | {no} | {yes} | {yes}
  - 4. Everybody | {no} | {yes} | {yes}
  - 5. Super power | {yes} | {no} | {yes}
  - 6. Tiny print | {yes} | {yes} | {no}

## Think like a detective
choice: Ad 2 says "Made with real fruit!" What does its tiny print tell you?
  - The cereal is mostly strawberries
  * There is only a very small amount of fruit in it
  - The cereal has no sugar
write: Which ad uses the most tricks? What would you check on its Nutrition Facts label before believing it?
  lines: 3
  key: Ad 3, Champ Choco Puffs (5 tricks). Check the Added Sugars line (% DV) and the fiber, and look at the serving size, to see whether "packed with vitamins" hides a sugary cereal.

## Stretch
write: Write an honest ad sentence for any of the three cereals. Use no tricks from the checklist.
  lines: 3
  key: For example: "Berry Bliss Bites are crunchy oat squares with a little dried strawberry. One cup has 12 grams of added sugar." Honest ads tell what the food is and give real facts.

# 5. Food Court Decision
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can use the steps of a decision model to choose a healthy meal.
directions: Leo needs lunch at the food court, and he has a basketball game right after school. Read the menu board. Fill in the meal chart, then use the 5 decision steps to choose his lunch.
mistakes: Students may pick the meal with the most food or the lowest price without checking the food groups. Watch for "the milkshake has dairy, so it is fine": it does, but it is also a sugary drink with lots of added sugar. In step 5, students often just repeat their choice; push them to say how Leo will know it worked (energy at the game, not too full, not hungry).
notes: Leo, the stalls and the prices are made up for Curio Class. The meal groups follow MyPlate (USDA). A meal 3 to 4 hours before a game works best with carbohydrates for energy, some protein, and not a lot of fat, which digests slowly. The 5 steps are a common elementary version of a decision model; your health program may use different words for the same steps. Keep the talk on Leo, not on what students themselves eat.

## Warm up: Leo's day
text: Leo is 10. His class is on a field trip to a science museum with a food court. Lunch is at 12:00, and his basketball game starts at 4:00, right after school. He has **$10** to spend.
example: **The 5 decision steps.** 1. Name the decision. 2. List the choices. 3. Think about each choice: Is it healthy? Will it help me reach my goal? Does it fit my family's rules and my budget? 4. Decide and act. 5. Look back: Did it work?

## Main: Use the steps
write: Step 1. What decision does Leo need to make?
  lines: 1
  key: Which lunch to buy at the food court so he has energy for his 4:00 game and stays within $10.
write: Step 2. Which choices can Leo really pick from? Cross out any he cannot pay for.
  lines: 1
  key: Taco Stop, Noodle Bowl and Snack Shack. Burger Barn costs $11.25, more than his $10.

  lines: 3
  key: Example: Taco Stop has all of fruit or vegetables, protein and grain and comes with water, so it gives lasting energy. Snack Shack has a grain but no fruit, vegetable or protein, and the slushie is mostly sugar, so Leo may feel tired or hungry by game time.

write: Step 5. At the game, how will Leo know if his choice worked?
  lines: 2
  key: He has energy and can focus during the game, he does not feel too full or too hungry, and he spent $10 or less.

## Stretch
choice: Leo likes the Noodle Bowl best. Which change makes it an even better choice for his game?
  - Add a cookie for quick energy

  lines: 2
  key: Sugar gives a quick burst of energy that wears off fast. A meal with whole grains, protein and fruit or vegetables gives energy that lasts until 4:00.

# 15. Communicable or Chronic Venn
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare communicable illnesses and chronic conditions and name ways to prevent or manage each.
directions: Read the 12 facts. Write each fact's number in the right part of the Venn diagram. Then fill in the prevention chart and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think a chronic condition can be caught from someone. Asthma and diabetes do not spread from person to person. Watch also for "you can prevent all chronic conditions": type 1 diabetes cannot be prevented, and asthma cannot always be prevented, but asthma attacks can often be prevented by avoiding triggers and using medicine as the doctor says.
notes: Facts follow the CDC. Communicable (infectious) illnesses are caused by germs and can spread; chronic conditions last a year or more and need ongoing care. Type 2 diabetes risk is lowered by being active and eating well; type 1 is an autoimmune condition and is not caused by anything a person did. Some students live with asthma, diabetes or other conditions: keep the talk on the characters and facts, never on a student's own health, and stress that people with chronic conditions play, learn and lead full lives.

## Warm up: Two kinds of illness
text: A **communicable** illness is caused by germs, like viruses or bacteria, and can spread from one person to another. A **chronic** condition lasts a long time, often for years, and does not spread from person to person.
fill:
  - The flu is a {communicable} illness.
  - Asthma is a {chronic} condition.

## Main: Twelve facts
text: **1.** Caused by germs such as viruses or bacteria. **2.** Can spread from person to person. **3.** Lasts a long time, often for years or for life. **4.** Examples: a cold, the flu, strep throat. **5.** Examples: asthma, diabetes. **6.** Can make it hard to play or go to school some days. **7.** A doctor or nurse can help. **8.** You cannot catch it from a friend. **9.** Often goes away in days or weeks. **10.** Managed every day, with things like an inhaler or checking blood sugar. **11.** Sleep, healthy food and exercise help the body. **12.** Some can be prevented with vaccines.
venn: Communicable illness | Chronic condition
  both: Both
  key: Communicable only: 1, 2, 4, 9, 12. Chronic only: 3, 5, 8, 10. Both: 6, 7, 11.

## Main: Prevention ideas
chart: Ways to prevent a communicable illness | Ways to prevent or manage a chronic condition
  rows: 3
  key: Communicable: wash hands with soap for 20 seconds, cough or sneeze into your elbow, get recommended vaccines, stay home when sick, do not share cups. Chronic: be active every day and eat healthy foods (lowers type 2 diabetes risk), stay away from smoke and other asthma triggers, take medicine as the doctor says, go to checkups.

## Check it
choice: Maya has asthma. Can you catch asthma by sitting next to her?
  - Yes, if she coughs on you
  - Yes, but only in winter
  * No, asthma does not spread from person to person
choice: Which habit helps prevent BOTH colds and some chronic conditions?
  * Getting enough sleep and eating healthy foods
  - Sharing water bottles with teammates
  - Skipping checkups when you feel fine
choice: Which one is a communicable illness?
  - Diabetes
  * Strep throat
  - Asthma

## Stretch
write: Jayden has diabetes. He checks his blood sugar before recess. How can his classmates be good friends to him?
  lines: 3
  key: Treat him like anyone else, do not tease or ask him to explain in front of everyone, include him in games, wait for him if he needs a snack or a check, and tell an adult if he says he feels shaky or unwell.

# 21. Hygiene Plan for a Sports Week
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan daily hygiene habits for a busy, active week.
directions: Marco has soccer camp every day this week. Read about his week. Fill in his plan for each day, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often plan laundry for the weekend only. Marco's camp clothes get sweaty every day, so they need washing (or a fresh set) before he wears them again. Watch for sleep plans under 9 hours; work out the hours on a clock if needed.
notes: Marco is a story character. Facts from mainstream guidance: children 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep (CDC, American Academy of Sleep Medicine); shower after hard exercise, dry feet well and wear clean socks to help prevent athlete's foot; wear sandals in shared showers; do not share water bottles, towels or razors. Deodorant is a personal and family choice; many tweens start using it when sweat begins to smell.

# 26. Why Bystanders Freeze
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain why bystanders stay quiet and name ways to help anyway.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions. Then write one way a bystander could get past each reason for staying quiet.
mistakes: Students may write "fight the bully" or "yell at them" as the way to help. Steer them to safe actions: stand with the target, invite them away, tell a trusted adult, check on them later. Watch for students who say telling an adult is "tattling": telling to keep someone safe is reporting, not tattling.
notes: The passage is written for Curio Class. Research on playground bullying (Hawkins, Pepler and Craig, 2001) found that when peers stepped in, bullying often stopped within seconds. The idea that people in a crowd each feel less responsible is called the bystander effect or diffusion of responsibility. Students should never be asked to share bullying they have seen at school in front of the class; offer a private way to report.

## Read
passage: Why Bystanders Freeze
  A bystander is someone who sees bullying happen. Most bystanders know that bullying is wrong. Still, many of them stay quiet. Here are four common reasons why.
  The first reason is thinking, "Someone else will do something." When a lot of people are watching, each person feels less responsible. Everyone waits for someone else, and nobody acts.
  The second reason is fear. A bystander may worry, "If I speak up, they will pick on me next."
  The third reason is not knowing what to do. Some kids are not sure if what they see is really bullying. Others think the only way to help is to stand up to the bully face to face, and that feels too hard.
  The fourth reason is wanting to fit in. If friends are laughing, it can feel easier to laugh along or to say nothing at all.
  Here is the good news. Bystanders have more power than they think. Helping does not have to mean a big speech. A bystander can walk over and stand with the person, invite them to leave together, tell a trusted adult, or check on them later. When even one person helps, others often join in, and the bullying often stops quickly.

## Questions
choice: What is a bystander?
  - The person who is doing the bullying
  * Someone who sees bullying happen
  - A teacher on recess duty
choice: Why does a big crowd make it LESS likely that someone helps?
  - People in crowds cannot see well
  - Bullying only happens in crowds
  * Each person feels less responsible and waits for someone else
choice: Which of these is a safe way for a bystander to help?
  * Invite the person to come play somewhere else
  - Push the bully away
  - Post about it online

## Main: Get past each reason
table: Reason bystanders stay quiet | One way to get past it
  height: 0.7
  - 1. "Someone else will do something." | {Decide that I will be the someone; I do not have to wait for others.}
  - 2. Fear of being picked on next | {Help in a safer way: tell an adult privately, or check on the person later.}
  - 3. Not knowing what to do | {Learn a few small moves ahead of time: stand with them, say "Come with me," tell an adult.}
  - 4. Wanting to fit in | {Do not laugh along; walk away, or ask a friend to help with me.}

## Stretch
write: Why might one person helping make it easier for others to help too?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows the others that someone cares and that helping is okay. People who wanted to help but were waiting now feel braver, and the person doing the bullying sees the crowd is not on their side.

# 36. Scam Message Spotter
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can spot red flags in online messages and choose a safe response.
directions: Study the red flag list and the example. Read each pretend message and underline the red flags. In the chart, write the red flags you found and a safe thing to do. One message may have no red flags.
mistakes: Students may think a message is safe because it uses the name of their game or says it is from "support." Scammers pretend to be companies and people you know. Watch for "reply and tell them to stop": the safest move is not to reply at all, and to tell a trusted adult. Message 5 is from a known teammate about a normal plan, so it has no red flags.
notes: All messages are made up for Curio Class. Common red flags follow the Federal Trade Commission's advice for kids and families: prizes you did not enter, a rush, requests for passwords, private information or money, links from strangers, and requests to keep a secret. Real companies never ask for your password. If a student has received a message like these, follow up privately.

## Warm up: Red flags
text: **P** A prize you did not enter. **R** A rush: "now," "in 10 minutes," "last chance." **I** Asks for a password or private information (address, school, photos). **L** A strange link to click. **S** From a stranger, or someone pretending to be a company or a friend. **K** Asks you to keep it a secret. **M** Asks for money or gift cards.
example: Message: "You have been chosen to win 5,000 free game coins! Click this link in the next hour!" Red flags: **P, R, L.** Safe response: Do not click. Delete it and tell a trusted adult.

## Main: Six pretend messages
text: **1.** "CONGRATULATIONS!!! You won a free tablet! Click here and type in your home address to get it."
text: **2.** "This is the Block Builders game team. Your account will be deleted in 10 minutes unless you send us your password."
text: **3.** From an unknown number: "Hi, it's your cousin! I got a new phone. Can you send me a grown-up's credit card number? Don't tell anyone."
text: **4.** "Free gems for your game! Just enter your username and password at this link."
text: **5.** In your team's group chat, from your teammate Rosa: "Practice is moved to 4:00 tomorrow."
text: **6.** "Your video got 1 million views!! You're going to be famous. To get paid, send us a photo of yourself and tell us your school."
table: Message | Red flags (letters) | Safe response
  height: 0.55
  - 1 | {P, I, L} | {Do not click or reply. Delete it and tell a trusted adult.}
  - 2 | {R, I, S} | {Never send a password. Do not reply; tell an adult and report it.}
  - 3 | {S, M, K} | {Do not send anything. Block the number and tell an adult.}
  - 4 | {P, I, L} | {Do not click. Delete it and tell an adult.}
  - 5 | {none} | {It is safe to read. Check the time with your coach if unsure.}
  - 6 | {P, I, S} | {Do not send a photo or your school. Block, report and tell an adult.}

## Check it
choice: A message says it is from your game's support team and asks for your password. What is true?
  - Support teams need your password to fix your account
  * Real companies never ask you for your password in a message
  - It is safe if the message spells the game's name right
choice: Which piece of information is safe to share with a stranger online?
  - Your school's name
  - Your home address
  - Your password
  * None of these

## Stretch
write: Write a rule for your class about scam messages, in 15 words or fewer.
  lines: 2
  key: Example: "If it rushes you, rewards you or wants private info, stop and tell an adult."
