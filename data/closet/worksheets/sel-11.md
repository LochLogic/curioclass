# 1. Acute and Chronic Stress
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how acute and chronic stress affect the body and support my explanation with evidence.
directions: Read the passage. Its paragraphs are numbered. Answer each question, and cite the paragraph number that supports your answer.
mistakes: Students read the passage as "stress is bad." Paragraphs 2 and 3 show that acute stress is a normal, useful response; the problem in paragraph 4 is a response that does not switch off. Watch for evidence that is a summary of the whole passage instead of a specific detail with a paragraph number.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 600 words) and keeps to well-established findings. "Allostatic load" is the term the neuroscientist Bruce McEwen used for the wear and tear of repeated or prolonged stress. The passage describes general patterns, not diagnoses; if a student seems worried about their own stress, connect them with the school counselor privately. No question asks students about their own experiences.

## Read
passage: Two Kinds of Stress
  (1) Imagine two students. Maya has a basketball final in ten minutes. Her heart is pounding, her palms are damp and her mind feels sharp. An hour after the game, she is calm and hungry. Jordan has been worried for months about a family member's job loss and the bills piling up at home. He sleeps badly, catches every cold going around and finds it hard to remember what he read the night before. Both are experiencing stress, but of two very different kinds.
  (2) The first kind, acute stress, is short-term. When the brain senses a challenge or a threat, a region called the hypothalamus sends an alarm. Within seconds, the sympathetic nervous system signals the adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys, to release adrenaline, also called epinephrine. The heart beats faster, breathing quickens, blood flows toward the large muscles and stored sugar is released for quick energy. Over the next several minutes, a second system, often called the HPA axis, raises the level of the hormone cortisol, which keeps energy available while the challenge lasts.
  (3) This response evolved to help animals escape predators, but it also helps people perform. A moderate burst of stress can sharpen attention and speed reactions, which is why Maya feels focused before her game. Just as important, the response is built to switch off. When the challenge passes, cortisol signals the brain to calm the alarm, and the parasympathetic nervous system, sometimes called the "rest and digest" system, slows the heart and restarts digestion. For most healthy people, acute stress leaves no lasting harm.
  (4) Chronic stress is different. It happens when stressors continue for weeks, months or longer, or when the alarm keeps firing even after a threat is gone. Caring for a sick relative, living with money problems, facing ongoing bullying or living in an unsafe neighborhood can all keep the stress response switched on. Instead of rising and falling, cortisol and other stress signals stay higher than normal for long stretches.
  (5) Over time, this constant activation can wear the body down. Scientists use the term allostatic load to describe this wear and tear. Long-term stress is linked to trouble sleeping, higher blood pressure and a greater risk of heart disease. It can weaken the immune system, so people may get sick more often and heal more slowly. It can upset digestion and is linked to headaches and muscle tension. Studies also connect chronic stress to problems with memory and concentration, and to a higher risk of anxiety and depression.
  (6) None of this means that stress can be avoided entirely, or should be. The goal is recovery: giving the body regular chances to switch off the alarm. Research points to a few reliable supports. Regular physical activity helps the body recover from stress and improves sleep. Sleep gives the brain time to reset. Slow, deep breathing can activate the rest and digest system within minutes. Strong relationships matter too: people with friends, family or mentors to lean on tend to handle long-term stress better. When stress comes from problems a person cannot fix alone, such as poverty or an unsafe home, support from communities, schools and health professionals becomes essential.
  (7) Maya and Jordan both have a stress response that is working the way it was designed to. The difference is time. Maya's alarm rang and then went quiet. Jordan's alarm has been ringing for months. Understanding that difference helps explain why the same system that helps us rise to a challenge can, when it never rests, begin to harm the body it was built to protect.

# 21. Testimony to the Town Council
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, a timer or clock with a second hand for practice
ican: I can write public testimony that makes a claim, supports it with evidence and makes a clear request.
directions: Read the fact sheet on the Elm Street lot. Decide what you want the council to do. Plan your testimony with the frame: claim, evidence, request. Then write the full testimony. It should take about two minutes to read aloud (about 260 to 300 words).
mistakes: Testimony often turns into a list of feelings with no evidence. Every claim needs at least two facts from the sheet. Also watch for requests that are not something a council can do ("Make people nicer"); a request should name a vote, a budget choice or a next step.
notes: The town of Riverbend, its council and every fact on the sheet are fictional. Students may argue either side; grade the frame and the evidence, not the position. Real public comment usually starts with a speaker's name and where they live; on this sheet students speak as themselves by first name only or as a made-up resident, and never write a real address. Most people read 130 to 150 words a minute aloud. Pair it with a mock hearing: each student gets two minutes, and the "council" must say which evidence was strongest.

## Read: The fact sheet
text: **Riverbend Town Council, public hearing, May 14.** The council will vote on what to build on the empty town-owned lot at 300 Elm Street.
text: **Option 1, parking lot:** 40 spaces. Cost: 180,000 dollars. Downtown shop owners say customers circle for 10 minutes or more on weekends. Parking fees would earn about 15,000 dollars a year.
text: **Option 2, skate park and green space:** Cost: 260,000 dollars; a state youth recreation grant would pay 100,000 dollars of it if the town applies by July 1. Riverbend has no skate park; the nearest is 25 miles away. Police logged 32 complaints last year about skateboarding on downtown sidewalks. In a town survey of 600 residents, 58 percent supported a skate park and 39 percent supported a parking lot.

## Main: Plan with the frame
write: Claim: state your position in one sentence.
  lines: 2
  key: Riverbend should build a skate park and green space on the Elm Street lot. (Or: Riverbend should build a parking lot on the Elm Street lot.)
write: Evidence: give at least two facts from the sheet and explain why each matters.
  lines: 5
  key: Skate park example: With the grant, it costs the town 160,000 dollars, less than the 180,000 dollar parking lot. The nearest skate park is 25 miles away, and police logged 32 sidewalk skating complaints, so a park gives skaters a safe, legal place. 58 percent of surveyed residents support it. Parking lot example: shop owners report customers circling 10 minutes or more on weekends, which can cost businesses sales, and fees would earn about 15,000 dollars a year.
write: Request: exactly what should the council do, and when?
  lines: 2
  key: Please vote yes on Option 2 tonight and direct town staff to apply for the state youth recreation grant before July 1.

## Write the testimony
write: Write your full two-minute testimony. Begin by greeting the council and giving your first name or a made-up resident's name.
  lines: 16
  key: Model opening and outline: "Good evening, members of the Riverbend Town Council. My name is Sam, and I'm here to speak about the lot at 300 Elm Street." Claim; two or three facts with explanations (cost after the grant, the 25-mile distance, the 32 complaints, the 58 percent survey result, or the parking and business facts); a response to the other side (for example, the skate park still costs less to the town than parking, or downtown parking could be improved with signs pointing to existing lots); the request with the vote and the July 1 deadline; "Thank you for your time." About 260 to 300 words.

## Stretch
write: Write one sentence that answers the strongest point the other side would make.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. For a skate park: "Shop owners are right that parking is tight, but the skate park costs the town 20,000 dollars less, and the council could study other ways to ease weekend parking." For a parking lot: "A skate park is popular, but 15,000 dollars a year in fees could fund youth programs too."
