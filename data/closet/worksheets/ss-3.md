# 1. Grid Detective: Zoo Map
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can use letter and number grid squares to find places on a map.
directions: The zoo map is split into grid squares. Letters run across the top and numbers run down the side. To name a square, find its letter, then its number: the gate is in A1. Find each animal and write its grid square.
mistakes: Students often write the number first (1A) or mix up rows and columns. Teach "across the hall, then up (or down) the stairs": letter first, then number. Some students count squares instead of reading the labels; have them slide one finger down from the letter and one finger across from the number until they meet.
notes: This map is a grid of squares, the same way many city and road maps are drawn, so students can practice without art. For a picture version, have students draw a small symbol for each animal in its square and add a key. Extension: ask partners to hide a "lost backpack" in a square and give clues.

## Map: Maple Grove Zoo
table: | A | B | C | D | E | F
  - 1 | Gate | | Lions | | Giraffes |
  - 2 | | Snakes | | Pond | | Zebras
  - 3 | Penguins | | Monkeys | | Snack bar |
  - 4 | | Restrooms | | Hippos | | Elephants
  - 5 | Bears | | Gift shop | Turtles | Owls |

## Warm up: How grid squares work
example: Find the letter **A** at the top. Slide down to the number **1**. The square where they meet holds the **Gate**, so the gate is in **A1**.
fill:
  - What is in square C5? {Gift shop}
  - What is in square B4? {Restrooms}

## Main: Find 8 animals
fill:
  - Lions: {C1}
  - Giraffes: {E1}
  - Snakes: {B2}
  - Zebras: {F2}
  - Penguins: {A3}
  - Monkeys: {C3}
  - Hippos: {D4}
  - Elephants: {F4}

## Stretch: 3 more and a route
fill:
  - Bears: {A5}
  - Turtles: {D5}
  - Owls: {E5}
write: You are at the Gate (A1) and want a snack. Name the grid squares you could walk through to reach the Snack bar, one square at a time.
  lines: 2
  key: Many routes work, such as A1, B1, C1, D1, E1, E2, E3, or A1, A2, B2, C2, D2, D3, E3. Each step must move to a square that touches the last one.

# 6. What Makes a Region?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick, a world map or atlas
ican: I can group places into regions by a feature they share and name each region's rule.
directions: A region is an area whose places share a feature, such as the same climate, the same landform or the same language. Cut out the 12 place cards. Sort them into 3 regions of 4 places each, using a world map to help. Then write the rule that makes each group a region.
mistakes: Students sometimes sort by continent ("these are all in Asia") instead of finding a shared feature. Ask: "What is the same about all four?" Others mix rules, putting a desert city with mountains because "both are rocky." Each region needs one rule that fits every card in it.
notes: Key groups: Spanish is the main language (Mexico, Spain, Colombia, Argentina); hot, dry desert climate (Phoenix, Cairo, Las Vegas, Riyadh); mountain ranges, a landform (the Rocky Mountains, the Alps, the Himalayas, the Appalachian Mountains). The three regions may be in any of the three columns; accept any order. The stretch shows that regions can overlap: one place can belong to several regions, depending on which feature you use.

## Warm up: One shared feature
choice: Florida, Louisiana and Texas all touch the Gulf of Mexico. What kind of feature makes them a region?
  - a language
  * a body of water they share
  - a holiday
choice: Which rule could make a region?
  * Places where most people speak French
  - Places I would like to visit
  - Places with big names

## Main: Sort 12 places
sort: Glue 4 places in each region.
  - Region 1: Mexico; Spain; Colombia; Argentina
  - Region 2: Phoenix, Arizona; Cairo, Egypt; Las Vegas, Nevada; Riyadh, Saudi Arabia
  - Region 3: the Rocky Mountains; the Alps; the Himalayas; the Appalachian Mountains
table: Region | Its rule: what all 4 places share | Kind of feature (climate, landform or language)
  - 1 | {Spanish is the main language} | {language}
  - 2 | {a hot, dry desert climate} | {climate}
  - 3 | {they are mountain ranges} | {landform}

## Stretch: Two regions at once
write: Santiago, the capital of Chile, sits right next to the Andes mountains, and people there speak Spanish. Which of your regions could Santiago belong to? What does that show about regions?
  lines: 3
  key: It fits the Spanish-language region and also a mountain region, because the Andes are a mountain range. Regions can overlap: one place can be in many regions, depending on which feature you choose.

# 17. How a Vote Works
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can put the steps of a town election in order and explain why ballots are secret.
directions: The 7 steps of a town election are mixed up below. Write each step's letter above its number on the line, from step 1 (first) to step 7 (last). Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often put "learn about the candidates" after going to vote. Ask: when should a voter decide? Some think poll workers look at each ballot as it goes in; the ballot stays private, and counting happens after the polls close.
notes: Many towns also let people vote by mail or vote early, and the order is the same: register, learn, get a ballot, mark it in private, return it, count. Who may vote is set by law; in most US elections a voter must be a citizen, 18 or older, and registered (North Dakota has no voter registration). Keep the stretch about why the secret ballot protects every voter, not about how anyone's family votes.

## Warm up: Words to know
fill:
  - The paper or form where you mark your vote is a {ballot}.
  - The place where people go to vote is the {polling place}.
  - A person running to be chosen is a {candidate}.

## Main: Steps in order
timeline: 1 7 1
  - 1 | Register: sign up so your name is on the list of voters.
  - 2 | Learn about the candidates and what each one wants to do.
  - 3 | On Election Day, go to the polling place.
  - 4 | Check in: a poll worker finds your name on the voter list.
  - 5 | Mark your ballot alone, in a private booth.
  - 6 | Put your ballot in the ballot box or scanner.
  - 7 | After the polls close, workers count the ballots and share the results.
choice: Why does a voter check in before getting a ballot?
  - to tell the worker who they will vote for
  * so each voter on the list votes only once
  - to pick up a sticker

## Stretch: Why is the ballot secret?
write: Why do voters mark their ballots alone, where no one can see? Give 2 reasons.
  lines: 4
  key: So no one can pressure, bribe or bully a voter into a choice, and so no one can punish or tease a voter afterward. Each person can vote for who they truly think is best.

# 23. Meeting Roles and Ground Rules
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan a fair class meeting with clear roles and ground rules.
directions: Our class is holding a Fix-It Meeting to solve a school problem. Match each meeting role to its job. Then plan who will do each role and write 4 ground rules everyone can agree to.
mistakes: Students often write rules as "don'ts" that are hard to check ("Don't be mean"). Push for rules that say what to do and that you could see or hear: "One person talks at a time." Watch for a leader who also decides everything: the leader runs the meeting, but the group decides.
notes: Rotate roles so every student gets a turn across the year. The ground rules work best when the class votes on them; post the winners. For the names in the plan, students may write classmates' first names or leave the box for the class vote.

## Warm up: Who does what?
match: Draw a line or write the letter of each job.
  - Leader = Keeps the meeting on topic and calls on people to talk
  - Recorder = Writes down ideas and what the group decides
  - Timekeeper = Watches the clock and tells the group how much time is left
  - Reporter = Shares what the group decided with the rest of the school

## Main: Plan the meeting
table: Role | What this person does | Who will do it
  - Leader | {runs the meeting, calls on people} |
  - Recorder | {writes ideas and decisions} |
  - Timekeeper | {watches the clock} |
choice: Two people start talking at the same time. Which role should help?
  - Recorder
  * Leader
  - Timekeeper
choice: The group has 2 minutes left and still has not voted. Which role should speak up?
  - Recorder
  - Reporter
  * Timekeeper
write: Ground rule 1
  lines: 1
  key: One person talks at a time.
write: Ground rule 2
  lines: 1
  key: Listen to every idea before we decide.
write: Ground rule 3
  lines: 1
  key: Talk about the problem, not about people.
write: Ground rule 4
  lines: 1
  key: When we vote, everyone gets one vote and we follow the result.

## Stretch: When a rule is broken
write: Someone laughs at a classmate's idea. Which ground rule does that break, and what should the leader say?
  lines: 3
  key: It breaks a rule like "Talk about the problem, not about people" or "Listen to every idea." The leader can calmly remind the group of the rule and invite the classmate to finish their idea.

# 28. Speak at the Hearing
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a timer (for practice)
ican: I can write a short statement with a claim, reasons and a request to share at a public hearing.
directions: The town pool is cracked and needs a 200,000 dollar repair. At a public hearing, anyone can speak to the town council for 1 minute. Choose one character below. Write their statement with a claim, 2 reasons and a request.
mistakes: Students often write reasons that only repeat the claim ("We should fix it because it should be fixed"). Ask "Why would that matter to the council?" Watch for a request that is not something the council can do ("Make it summer"). A request asks the council for an action: vote yes, find a cheaper fix, use a fee instead of a tax.
notes: A 1-minute statement is about 100 to 130 words read aloud. Have partners time each other and trim. Characters may speak for or against the repair, or for a different way to pay; all are fair positions at a hearing. Speakers start by saying their name and why they care, as people do at real hearings. Pairs with the other Pool Repair Vote sheets in this module.

## Warm up: Meet the speakers
table: Speaker | Who they are | What they care about
  - Coach Ana | teaches swim lessons at the pool every summer | Children learning to swim safely
  - Mr. Bell | owns the bakery on Main Street | Taxes going up for small shops
  - Grandpa Lou | swims laps at the pool every morning | Exercise for older people
  - June, age 10 | is on the town swim team | A place for the team to practice
choice: Which part of a statement asks the council to do something?
  - the claim
  - a reason
  * the request

## Example: A statement for the pool
example: "My name is Kim, and I am a lifeguard. **Claim:** I believe the town should repair the pool this year. **Reason 1:** Last summer, 300 children took swim lessons there. **Reason 2:** The closest other pool is 20 miles away, and many families cannot drive that far. **Request:** Please vote yes on the repair, and ask the pool to sell a summer pass to help pay for it. Thank you."

## Main: Write your statement
write: **Introduce yourself:** your character's name and why they care.
  starter: My name is
  lines: 1
  key: For example: "My name is Mr. Bell, and I own the bakery on Main Street."
write: **Claim:** What do you think the council should do?
  starter: I believe the town should
  lines: 2
  key: For example (Mr. Bell): "I believe the town should fix the pool without raising the tax on small shops."
write: **Reason 1:**
  starter: First,
  lines: 2
  key: For example: "First, my bakery already pays a lot in taxes, and a raise could make me cut a worker's hours."
write: **Reason 2:**
  starter: Also,
  lines: 2
  key: For example: "Also, the people who swim the most can help pay through a small pool fee."
write: **Request:** What exact action do you ask the council to take?
  starter: Please
  lines: 2
  key: For example: "Please pay for the repair with pool passes and fees instead of a new tax."

## Stretch: Answer a question
write: A council member asks your character a hard question: "Where will the money come from?" Write your character's answer.
  lines: 3
  key: For example (Coach Ana): "We could charge a small fee for swim lessons, ask local businesses to sponsor the pool, and fix it in two steps over two years."

# 39. Ripple Street Choices
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can weigh the benefits and costs of a choice for different groups of people.
directions: The town wants to close Ripple Street to cars every Saturday and Sunday in summer for an outdoor market. For each group, write one benefit (a good result) and one cost (a bad result or what they give up). Then decide: should the town close the street?
mistakes: Students often list only benefits for the groups they like and only costs for drivers. Push for at least one benefit and one cost for every group, even if one side is small. Costs are not only money: lost time, noise, a longer drive and trash are costs too.
notes: The chart key gives one model per cell; accept any reasonable idea. Grade the decision on whether it weighs both sides for several groups, not on yes or no. Compromise answers are strong economics thinking: close only on Saturday, keep one lane for buses, or close only one block. Pairs with the other Ripple Street sheets in this module.

## Warm up: Benefit or cost?
choice: The market brings new shoppers to the street. For the shops, that is a
  * benefit
  - cost
choice: Neighbors must park on another street on market days. For the neighbors, that is a
  - benefit
  * cost

## Main: Weigh it for 4 groups
chart: Benefit (good result) | Cost (bad result or what they give up)
  labels: Shops on Ripple Street; People who live on Ripple Street; Farmers and crafters who sell at the market; Drivers and bus riders who use Ripple Street
  height: 0.85
  key: Shops: more shoppers walk by (benefit); delivery trucks cannot reach them on weekends (cost). Neighbors: fresh food close to home and a fun place to meet (benefit); noise, crowds and no parking (cost). Farmers and crafters: a new place to sell and earn money (benefit); a booth fee and a long day of work (cost). Drivers and bus riders: a safer street to walk on (benefit); a longer, slower trip around the closed street (cost).

## Decide
write: Should the town close Ripple Street for the market? Use at least 2 groups from your chart in your answer.
  lines: 4
  key: For example: "Yes, because the shops and the farmers earn more money, and neighbors get fresh food close by. Those benefits are bigger than the cost for drivers, who only have to go around two days a week."

## Stretch: A better plan
write: Plan a compromise that keeps most of the benefits and cuts one big cost.
  lines: 3
  key: For example: "Close the street only on Saturday mornings and keep one lane open for the bus," or "Close one block, and let shops get deliveries before 8 a.m."
