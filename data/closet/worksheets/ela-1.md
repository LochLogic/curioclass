# 1. Blend Builders: st, fl, gr
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can hear and write the blends st, fl, gr and cl.
directions: Say the name of each picture. Write the blend that starts the word. Then cut out the word cards and glue each one under its blend.
mistakes: Children who write one letter (s for st) are hearing the first sound only: have them stretch the word slowly, "sssttt-ar," and listen for both sounds. Watch for fl and fr mixed up in flower.
notes: Say each picture name together before students start so nobody gets stuck on a picture (flashlight, stamp and grill are the least familiar). The cards on the second part can be sorted before gluing so you can check them.

## Warm up: Listen for the blend
example: star starts with **st**: s + t. Say it slowly: st-ar.

## Main: Write the blend
picword:
  - star | {st}ar
  - flag | {fl}ag
  - grapes | {gr}apes
  - clock | {cl}ock
  - stairs | {st}airs
  - flower | {fl}ower
  - graph | {gr}aph
  - cloud | {cl}oud
  - stamp | {st}amp
  - flashlight | {fl}ashlight
  - grill | {gr}ill
  - clam | {cl}am

## Sort: Blend chart
sort: Glue each word under its blend.
  - st: star, stairs, stamp
  - fl: flag, flower, flashlight
  - gr: grapes, graph, grill
  - cl: clock, cloud, clam

# 6. Smooth Reading Phrase Strips
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, a partner
ican: I can read words in phrases so my reading sounds smooth.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read each phrase strip in one breath, like one scoop. Draw a scoop (a curved line) under the whole phrase. Then read the story three times and check a box each time.
mistakes: Children draw a scoop under each word, which keeps reading choppy. Model one long scoop from the first word to the last. Some race through the story by the third read: smooth reading is not fast reading. Listen for pauses at periods and a voice that sounds like talking.
notes: Read each phrase strip aloud first while sweeping your finger under it, then have the class echo you. Partners can listen during the three reads and tell the reader one thing that sounded smooth. The story is original to Curio Class (about 70 words). The story's phrases match the strips, so children meet them again in the story. Scoops for the first two sentences: On a windy day / Sam and I / go to the park. I hold / my red kite / with a long tail.

## Warm up: One scoop
example: Read the words together, like one scoop of ice cream: **on a windy day**. Not on... a... windy... day.

## Main: Phrase strips
cards: Read each strip in one breath. Draw a scoop under it.
  plain: yes
  - on a windy day
  - up in the sky
  - my red kite
  - over the tall tree
  - with a long tail
  - at the park
  - pulls on the string
  - down to the grass

## Read: The story
passage: My Red Kite
  On a windy day, Sam and I go to the park. I hold my red kite with a long tail. Sam runs fast.
  The wind lifts my kite up in the sky. It flies over the tall tree. The kite pulls on the string.
  Then the wind stops. My kite floats down to the grass. We laugh and run to get it. We will fly it again tomorrow.
text: Draw scoops under the phrases in the first two sentences.
chart: Check when done | How did it sound?
  labels: 1st read; 2nd read; 3rd read
  key: Each row checked. Answers to "How did it sound?" will vary, such as "choppy," "better," "smooth." Most children sound smoother by the third read.

## Check: Did you understand?
choice: (Read aloud.) What happens when the wind stops?
  - The kite flies over the tree.
  * The kite floats down to the grass.
  - Sam runs home.
write: (Read aloud.) Which read sounded the most smooth? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Most often the third read, because the words were easy by then and I read them in phrases.

# 18. Capital Names and Dates
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use capital letters for names, days and months, and put a comma in a date.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read each sentence. Find the words that need a capital letter. Write them the right way. Then fix the dates: add capitals and a comma between the day and the year.
mistakes: Children capitalize every noun (Dog, Fish). Only the first word, I, and names of a certain pet, day or month get a capital. Watch for a comma after the month (May, 5 2024); the comma goes between the day and the year.
notes: All pet names are made up. Remind children that the first word of a sentence always gets a capital, so many sentences have two or three words to fix. Day and month names: the days of the week and the twelve months always start with a capital. Accept the words written in any order; extra credit for rewriting the whole sentence.

## Warm up: Name it right
example: my cat ziggy naps on monday. → **M**y cat **Z**iggy naps on **M**onday.
fill:
  - A pet's name, like Ziggy, gets a {capital} letter.
  - Days of the week, like Monday, get a capital. So do {months}, like May.

## Main: Fix the capitals
fill:
  - my dog patch likes to run. Fix: {My, Patch}
  - we feed pickles the fish on saturday. Fix: {We, Pickles, Saturday}
  - nibbles the hamster was born in may. Fix: {Nibbles, May}
  - on friday, i will walk my dog rex. Fix: {On, Friday, I, Rex}
  - my bird sunny sings in june. Fix: {My, Sunny, June}
  - we got our cat tiger in october. Fix: {We, Tiger, October}
  - is your turtle named shelly? Fix: {Is, Shelly}
  - the vet saw muffin on tuesday in april. Fix: {The, Muffin, Tuesday, April}

## Main: Fix the dates
fill:
  - march 3 2024 → {March 3, 2024}
  - july 4 2026 → {July 4, 2026}
  - november 18 2025 → {November 18, 2025}

## Stretch: Your turn
write: Make up a pet. Write a sentence with its name and the day you would play with it.
  lines: 2
  key: Any sentence with capitals for the first word, the pet's name and the day, such as "My frog Hops plays with me on Sunday."

# 25. Pictures and Words: What Each Tells
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can tell what I learn from the words and what I learn from the picture.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the page about owls and look at the picture. Read each fact. Put an X under Words if the words told you. Put an X under Picture if you saw it in the picture.
mistakes: Children may mark a fact as "words" because it is true, not because the page says it. Point back: "Find it in the words. Can you put your finger on it?" If they cannot, check the picture. "Owls have soft feathers" is in the words; "feathers that stick up on its head" is only in the picture.
notes: Read the page aloud twice, pointing to each word. Then look at the picture together without naming its details, so children find them. The page is original to Curio Class (about 55 words). The owl in the picture has ear tufts (feathers, not real ears), big round eyes, a short beak, and sits on a branch.

## Read: Night Hunters
passage: Night Hunters
  Owls hunt at night and sleep in the day. An owl can turn its head far around to look behind it.
  Owls have soft feathers, so they fly with no sound. An owl can hear a mouse move in the grass. Then it swoops down to catch it. Owls eat mice and bugs.
picword:
  - owl | owl

## Main: Words or picture?
table: Fact | Words | Picture
  - Owls hunt at night. | {X} | {-}
  - This owl has two big round eyes. | {-} | {X}
  - Owls fly with no sound. | {X} | {-}
  - This owl sits on a branch. | {-} | {X}
  - Owls eat mice. | {X} | {-}
  - This owl has feathers that stick up on its head. | {-} | {X}

## Stretch: Add to the page
choice: (Read aloud.) Why did the author put a picture on the page?
  - To tell us that owls eat mice
  * To show us what an owl looks like
  - To tell us when owls sleep
draw: Draw a new picture that shows a fact from the words. | 1.6
write: (Read aloud.) Which fact from the words does your picture show?
  lines: 1
  key: Any fact from the words, matching the drawing: for example, an owl flying at night, an owl catching a mouse, or an owl turning its head.

# 31. Who Has Seen the Wind? Sense Words
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can find words in a poem that show how things move, and draw what the poem shows.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the poem. Read it with me. Circle the words that show something moving. Then answer the questions and draw what the poem shows.
mistakes: Children circle "wind" as a motion word. The wind is the thing that moves; the motion words tell what happens: trembling, passing, bow down. Some think the poem says no one can feel the wind; it says we cannot see it, but we can see what it does.
notes: The poem is by Christina Rossetti (1830 to 1894), from her book of rhymes for children, Sing-Song (1872), and is in the public domain. It is printed here as in the 1872 book except for one spelling: the first edition prints "thro'" for "through." Motion words to circle: trembling, passing (twice), bow down. Act it out: children "hang trembling" with their fingers like leaves, then bow their heads like trees.

## Read: The poem
source: Christina Rossetti, "Who Has Seen the Wind?", from Sing-Song (1872). Spelling of one word modernized.
  Who has seen the wind?
  Neither I nor you:
  But when the leaves hang trembling
  The wind is passing through.
  Who has seen the wind?
  Neither you nor I:
  But when the trees bow down their heads
  The wind is passing by.

## Main: Words that move
write: (Read aloud.) Circle the words in the poem that show something moving.
  lines: 0
  key: trembling, passing (both times), bow down
fill:
  - The leaves hang {trembling}.
  - The trees {bow} down their heads.
  - The wind is {passing} through and passing by.
choice: (Read aloud.) Can we see the wind?
  - Yes, it is white.
  * No, but we can see what it moves.
  - Yes, at night.
choice: (Read aloud.) What do the trees do when the wind passes by?
  - They grow tall.
  - They lose their roots.
  * They bend down, like a bow.

## Draw: What the poem shows
draw: Draw the wind moving leaves and trees. | 2.4
write: (Read aloud.) Write a sentence about your picture. Use a word that shows motion.
  lines: 2
  key: Any sentence that tells what moves, such as "The leaves shake in the wind." or "The tree bends down."

# 43. Describe It with Five Details
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can describe something with details about what I see, hear and smell.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at the fruit stall and the things on it. Imagine you are standing there. Fill in the chart. Then write 5 complete sentences: what you see, hear and smell.
mistakes: Children list words (apples, bananas) instead of sentences. Check each sentence for a capital, a naming part, an action and a period. Others write only "I see" sentences; point to the chart's Hear and Smell columns. Color and number words make details stronger: "I see five red apples."
notes: The stall is shown as one picture of the stall and pictures of the things on it. Before writing, take a "sense walk": children close their eyes while you describe a market (people talking, a bell, the smell of ripe fruit and flowers). Sounds and smells are imagined, so any that fit a market are right. A complete sentence needs a capital letter, a who or what, what it does or is, and an end mark.

## Look: The fruit stall
picword:
  - market-stall | fruit stall
  - apple | apples
  - banana | bananas
  - grapes | grapes
  - cherries | cherries
  - flower | flowers
  - basket | basket
  - bell | bell

## Plan: My senses
chart: I see | I hear | I smell
  rows: 2
  height: 0.6
  key: See: red apples, yellow bananas, a basket, flowers, a striped roof. Hear: people talking, a bell ringing, someone saying a price. Smell: sweet fruit, flowers, ripe bananas.

## Main: Write five sentences
write: Something you see.
  starter: I see ...
  lines: 1
  key: I see a pile of shiny red apples.
write: Something else you see.
  starter: There is ...
  lines: 1
  key: There is a striped roof over the stall.
write: Something you hear.
  starter: I hear ...
  lines: 1
  key: I hear a bell ring at the stall.
write: Something you smell.
  starter: I smell ...
  lines: 1
  key: I smell sweet cherries and flowers.
write: One more detail, with a color or a number word.
  lines: 1
  key: The grapes are purple and round.
