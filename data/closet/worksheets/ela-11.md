# 1. American Literary Movements Timeline
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, five colored pencils or highlighters
ican: I can place major American works in their literary movements and name a trait of each movement.
directions: Write each work's letter at its year on the timeline. Then shade or bracket the span of each movement in its own color, name the movement of each work, and cite one trait of each movement that the work shows.
mistakes: Movements overlap and their dates are approximate: Romanticism and Transcendentalism run side by side in the 1830s to 1850s, and Transcendentalism is often treated as a branch of Romanticism. Accept reasonable brackets. Frost is a common sticking point: he wrote in traditional meter and rhyme, but he belongs to the Modernist era and shares its interest in isolation and uncertainty. Students sometimes confuse Realism (ordinary life told plainly) with a work being "realistic."
notes: Dates are first publication: Bradstreet's The Tenth Muse (London, 1650); Wigglesworth's The Day of Doom (1662); Edwards's sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (preached 1741); Irving's "Rip Van Winkle" (1819); Cooper's The Last of the Mohicans (1826); Emerson's Nature (1836); Poe's "The Raven" (1845); Thoreau's Walden (1854); Twain's Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884 in Britain, 1885 in the United States); Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894); Eliot's "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915); Frost's "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" (1923). All are in the public domain in the United States.

# 5. Devil's Dictionary Remix
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can analyze how satire uses irony to criticize, and use it in my own writing.
directions: Read eight definitions from Ambrose Bierce's The Devil's Dictionary. For each one, note what a normal dictionary would say, the twist Bierce gives it, and what human habit he is mocking. Then write four satirical definitions of your own.
mistakes: Students often call a definition "funny" without naming its target. Each definition mocks a real human habit (self-importance, flattery, asking for advice we will not take). Watch also for definitions that are only insults: satire works because it is partly true. Remind students that satire aims at habits and institutions, not at classmates or real people they know.
notes: Bierce published these definitions in newspapers from 1881, collected them as The Cynic's Word Book (1906), and retitled the book The Devil's Dictionary in 1911. The text follows Project Gutenberg eBook #972. Note that Bierce wrote "disapproval" in CONSULT; it is often misquoted as "approval," which makes a weaker joke. Bierce's habit of writing "him" for everyone is of his time; students' definitions should not copy it.

## Read: Eight definitions
source: Ambrose Bierce, The Devil's Dictionary (1911; first collected as The Cynic's Word Book, 1906)
  [1] BORE, n. A person who talks when you wish him to listen.
  [2] EGOTIST, n. A person of low taste, more interested in himself than in me.
  [3] ADMIRATION, n. Our polite recognition of another's resemblance to ourselves.
  [4] CONSULT, v.i. To seek another's disapproval of a course already decided on.
  [5] EDUCATION, n. That which discloses to the wise and disguises from the foolish their lack of understanding.
  [6] LEARNING, n. The kind of ignorance distinguishing the studious.
  [7] PATIENCE, n. A minor form of despair, disguised as a virtue.
  [8] DICTIONARY, n. A malevolent literary device for cramping the growth of a language and making it hard and inelastic. This dictionary, however, is a most useful work.

  - People who listen too politely

  * The definer shows the very fault he accuses others of.
  - The ending is a surprise twist of plot.
  - The audience knows something a character does not.
  - A word is used to mean exactly what it says.

# 16. Synthesis Essay Planner
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can plan a synthesis essay that takes a position and uses several sources to support it.
directions: Read the four sources about whether public libraries should go fully digital. Fill in the source grid, take a position, and plan a line of reasoning that uses at least three sources.
mistakes: Students often summarize one source per paragraph instead of synthesizing: a strong paragraph brings two sources together around one idea. Watch also for students who ignore sources that cut against their position. A qualified position ("mostly digital, but keep...") is often the strongest, and it must still take a clear side.
notes: All four sources and the city of Fairhaven are made up by Curio Class for practice; their numbers are invented. Source D's point about e-book licenses reflects a real practice: many publishers license e-books to libraries for a limited time or number of loans instead of selling them outright. The key models a qualified position.

## Read: Four sources
source: Source A: Fairhaven Public Library annual report (made up)
  E-book and audiobook loans rose 40 percent in five years and now make up 46 percent of all loans. Print loans fell 15 percent. The branches had 610,000 visits last year; 38 percent of visitors used a public computer or the free wifi.
source: Source B: an opinion column by a branch librarian (made up)
  A library is not a warehouse of books. It is the only indoor public place where anyone can stay all day without buying anything. Our story times, tax help and job clinics fill rooms that would sit empty if the collection moved to the cloud.
source: Source C: a survey of 1,200 Fairhaven library users (made up)
  Asked how they prefer to read, 52 percent said print, 31 percent said digital, and 17 percent said either. Among users under 30, 49 percent preferred digital. Among users over 65, 71 percent preferred print. One in nine users said they had no internet access at home.
source: Source D: a city budget memo (made up)
  A print book costs the library about $25 and can be lent for years. A license for the same e-book costs about $55 and expires after two years. Going fully digital would let the city close two storage rooms but would raise yearly collection costs by about 30 percent.

# 22. Would You Rather: Usage Debates
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can take and support a position on a contested usage question using style guides as evidence.
directions: Read the style guide notes. For each "would you rather" question, choose a side, then support it with at least one style guide note and the situation where your choice works best.
mistakes: Students often treat a style guide as a law of the language. Guides are house rules for particular publications and fields, and they change between editions. The strongest answers name the guide, its edition or year, and the audience: a choice that fits a news story or a blog may not fit a lab report. Either side can earn full credit if it is supported.
notes: The guide notes are paraphrased summaries, not quotations; check them against the editions your school owns, since guides update their advice. Positions summarized: APA's 7th edition (2019) endorses singular they; AP added singular they in limited cases in 2017; Fowler's Modern English Usage (1926) called the ban on ending with a preposition a superstition; The Chicago Manual of Style accepts split infinitives and sentences that begin with and or but; AP accepted hopefully meaning "it is hoped" in 2012; APA treats data as plural.

## Style guide notes
example: **A.** APA Style (7th edition, 2019) endorses singular they for a person whose gender is unknown or who uses they. The AP Stylebook (2017) allows it in limited cases.
  **B.** H. W. Fowler's A Dictionary of Modern English Usage (1926) called the rule against ending a sentence with a preposition a superstition. Modern guides, including Chicago, agree it is not an error.
  **C.** The Chicago Manual of Style says a split infinitive is acceptable, especially when keeping the words together would sound awkward or change the meaning.
  **D.** The Chicago Manual of Style says beginning a sentence with and or but is not an error and is common in good writing.
  **E.** The AP Stylebook (2012) accepted hopefully used to mean "it is hoped," after decades of objecting to it.
  **F.** APA Style treats data as a plural noun: the data are, these data.

## Warm up: Which guide says what?
match: Match each usage question to its style guide note.
  - Singular they = Note A
  - Ending with a preposition = Note B
  - Split infinitives = Note C
  - Starting with and or but = Note D
  - Hopefully = Note E
  - Data is or data are = Note F

## Main: Take a side
write: Would you rather write "Each student should bring their notebook" or "Each student should bring his or her notebook"? Support your choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "their," because APA (7th edition) endorses singular they and it avoids an awkward pair; or "his or her" for an audience or older guide that still expects it, though it excludes people who use they. Credit any side supported by note A and an audience.
write: Would you rather write "Which team are you on?" or "On which team are you?"
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "Which team are you on?" because Fowler and modern guides reject the rule (note B) and the other version sounds stiff; "On which team" may suit a very formal speech. Credit either with support.
write: Would you rather write "to boldly go" or "to go boldly"?
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "to boldly go," since Chicago accepts split infinitives (note C) and moving "boldly" changes the rhythm; "to go boldly" is also correct and may suit a reader who objects. Credit either with support.
write: Would you rather begin a sentence in an essay with "But" or with "However,"?
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "But" for a quick, strong turn, since Chicago calls it acceptable (note D); "However," for a more formal tone some teachers expect. Credit either with support.
write: Would you rather write "Hopefully, the bus will be on time" or "I hope the bus will be on time"?
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "Hopefully" is accepted by AP since 2012 (note E) and fits news and casual writing; "I hope" is clearer about who is hoping and avoids readers who still object. Credit either with support.
write: Would you rather write "The data is clear" or "The data are clear"?
  lines: 3
  key: Model: "are" in a science or psychology paper, because APA treats data as plural (note F); "is" in general writing, where data is often treated as a mass noun like information. Credit either with support and audience.

## Stretch
write: Language changes. Choose one of the six questions and predict how a style guide might treat it in twenty years. Use what you learned about how guides have changed.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: Singular they will likely be standard in every major guide, since APA endorsed it in 2019 and AP began allowing it in 2017; guides tend to follow widespread use after a period of debate, as AP did with hopefully in 2012.

# 27. Fix the Mistakes: College Essay Edition
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, red pen
ican: I can edit writing for correct hyphens, spelling, commas and usage.
directions: This sample college essay has 15 errors: 4 hyphen errors, 4 spelling errors, 4 comma errors and 3 usage errors. Mark each one in the essay. Then list them in order in the table with the correction and the type.
mistakes: Watch for students who "fix" correct sentences: the essay's other commas, semicolons and hyphens are correct, and an optional comma after a short opening phrase ("On my first day") is not an error. Compound adjectives take a hyphen before a noun (a well-designed machine) but not after it (the machine is well designed). A comma splice counts as a comma error.
notes: The essay is original to Curio Class and runs about 500 words. The 15 errors by type: hyphen (ten-speed, well-designed, seven-year-old, how-to cards), spelling (definitely, sandwiches, separate, receive), comma (said, "Again."; the comma splice "glamorous, it is"; teacher, but; spring, we) and usage (its for it's, than for then, fewer for less). Accept a period or ", and" for the comma splice. Everything else in the essay is correct as written.

## Read: The sample essay
passage: Sample personal essay: With Them, Not for Them | numbered
  The first bike I ever fixed was a rusty ten speed that someone had abandoned behind the community center. It had two flat tires, a chain that screeched like a seagull, and a seat held on with tape. I was fourteen, and I had volunteered at the Saturday repair shop mostly because my friend Leo needed a ride there. I did not plan to stay. I definately did not plan to find the thing I am best at.
  The shop was run by a retired machinist named Ms. Alvarez, who kept every tool in it's own outlined spot on a pegboard. On my first day she handed me a tire lever and said, "Show me what you know." I knew nothing. I pried the tire off the rim, pinched the new tube, and popped it within a minute. She did not laugh. She handed me another tube and said "Again."
  Over the next two years, I learned that repair is mostly patience. A bike is a well designed machine, and almost every problem has a logical cause. A chain that skips is usually worn; a wheel that wobbles usually has a loose spoke. Repair is not glamorous, it is often greasy and slow. The hard part is not knowing the answer but slowing down enough to look. I began to notice that I was calmer with a broken derailleur in my hands then I was anywhere else.
  What I did not expect was the people. Every Saturday, a line formed before we opened: kids with bent wheels, a nurse who rode to the hospital at dawn, and a man who delivered sandwichs across town. Ms. Alvarez had one rule. We did not fix bikes for people; we fixed bikes with them. If a customer needed a new brake cable, the customer stood beside us and learned to thread it. At first I resented the rule because it made every repair slower. Then I watched a seven year old girl tighten her own brakes, squeeze the lever, and grin, and I understood.
  Teaching turned out to be harder than fixing. Explaining a repair forced me to understand it completely, and I noticed there were less mistakes when I talked through each step out loud. I started making how to cards with drawings and taped them above the work stands. The volunteers kept a seperate binder of the cards for new helpers. I had never thought of myself as a teacher but the cards proved me wrong.
  When the shop lost its space last spring we moved everything into a church basement two blocks away. I did not recieve a paycheck for any of those Saturdays, and I never wanted one. What I got instead was a way of working: slow down, find the cause, and leave the person beside you able to do it next time. I plan to study mechanical engineering, but I suspect the most important thing I will bring to college is not a skill with wrenches. It is the habit of fixing things with people, not for them.

# 38. Comic-Strip Pacing Frame
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can control the pacing of a narrative scene by planning its beats.
directions: Plan a short scene as an 8-panel comic strip: sketch each panel and write a caption. Mark each panel's beat as FAST, SLOW or PAUSE. Then rewrite the scene as prose that keeps the same beats: a fast panel gets a quick sentence, a slow panel gets a full, detailed paragraph.
mistakes: Students often draw eight panels of equal weight and then write eight equal paragraphs. Comics show pacing by how many panels a moment gets: if three panels show one second, that second is SLOW. Prose shows it by how many words a moment gets. Watch for prose that adds new events not in the strip; the point is to translate the beats, not change the story.
notes: Scene ideas if students are stuck: the moment before a diver leaves the board; finding a note in a library book; a phone ringing at 2 a.m.; a dog that will not come back inside before a storm. The key's model uses the diving board. A useful rule of thumb for the prose: the number of panels a moment gets should roughly match the share of words it gets.

## Warm up: Read the beats
example: Comic panels and prose, one beat at a time.
  Panel: Mia climbs the ladder. Caption: "Ten rungs." Beat: FAST. Prose: Mia climbed the ten rungs without looking down.
  Panel: Close-up of her toes curled over the edge. Caption: "Cold." Beat: SLOW. Prose: The board was rough and cold under her feet. She curled her toes over the edge and felt the whole thing sway, just slightly, as if it were breathing.
choice: Three panels in a row show the same second from different angles. What does that do to the pacing?
  - speeds it up
  * slows it down
  - skips over it
  - has no effect
choice: Which prose sentence matches a FAST panel?
  * We ran for the bus.
  - The bus doors folded open with a sigh, and warm air rolled out over the step, smelling of rain and old seats.
  - I stopped and wondered whether any of this had been a good idea.
  - The driver's eyes met mine in the long mirror, and for a moment neither of us moved.

## Main: Rewrite it as prose
write: Rewrite your strip as a scene of 200 to 300 words. Keep every beat in order. Give FAST panels a sentence and SLOW panels a paragraph.
  lines: 12
  key: Model opening: Mia climbed the ten rungs without looking down and walked to the end of the board. Then she stopped. The board was rough and cold under her feet. "Bend, then go," her coach always said. She bent her knees. She went. (Strong responses keep the eight beats in order, give the SLOW beats the most words, and end on the PAUSE.)

  lines: 2
