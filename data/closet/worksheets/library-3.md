# 1. Keyword Highlighter
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, highlighter (optional)
ican: I can pick out the keywords in a research question.
directions: Underline the keywords in each question: the words that tell what you want to know about. Cross out the small words a search does not need. Then write your search words in the box.
mistakes: Students often keep question words (how, what, why) and small words (the, do, a) in their search. Others drop a word that matters, like "fall" in question C or "children" in question G, which changes the topic. Ask: "If you took this word out, would you still find what you need?"
notes: Search tools match the important words, so a short search of 2 to 4 keywords usually works better than a whole question. The key shows one good search for each question; accept any search that keeps the topic and the specific thing asked (for example "cheetah top speed" or "cheetah running speed"). Word order does not matter.

## Warm up: Watch me do one
example: Question: What do **koalas** like to **eat**?
  Cross out: What, do, like, to
  Keywords (underline): **koalas**, **eat**
  Search words: **koalas eat** (or **koala diet**)

## Main: Find the keywords
table: Research question | My search words
  - A. How fast can a cheetah run? | {cheetah speed}
  - B. What do giant pandas eat? | {giant pandas eat}
  - C. Why do leaves change color in the fall? | {leaves change color fall}
  - D. Who invented the telephone? | {telephone inventor}
  - E. How many moons does Jupiter have? | {Jupiter moons}
  - F. Where do sea turtles lay their eggs? | {sea turtles lay eggs}
  - G. What was life like for children in ancient Rome? | {children ancient Rome}
  - H. How do bees make honey? | {bees make honey}
  - I. When did people first walk on the Moon? | {first Moon walk}
  - J. What are the rules of chess? | {chess rules}

## Check: Which search is best?
choice: You want to know how tall giraffes grow. Which search is best?
  - how tall do giraffes grow when they are all grown up
  - giraffes
  - tall animals
  * giraffe height

## Stretch: Your question
write: Write a question you wonder about. Underline its keywords, then write your search words.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: Why do cats purr? Search words: cats purr why (or cat purring).

# 7. Narrow It Down Funnel
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can narrow a big topic into a smaller topic and then into one question I can research.
directions: A funnel is wide at the top and small at the bottom. Put each big topic in at the top. Write a smaller topic in the middle and one question at the bottom. The question should be small enough to answer in a short report.
mistakes: Students often stop at the middle step ("bats") and call it done; a whole topic is still too big for one report. Others write a bottom question that jumps to a new topic ("Space" to "How do volcanoes erupt?"). Each step should stay inside the step above it. Check the bottom line: is it a question, and could a library book or kid website answer it?
notes: The key gives one good funnel for each topic; accept any middle topic that fits inside the big topic and any research question that fits inside the middle topic. The model facts are checked: most bats eat insects, and some bats eat fruit or drink the nectar of flowers. A strong bottom question names one thing to find out (what, how, why, where, when).

## Warm up: How a funnel works
picword:
  - funnel | big to small
example: Big topic: **animals**
  Smaller topic: **bats**
  One question: **What do bats eat?**
table: Words | Number them: 1 = biggest, 3 = smallest

table: Words | Number them: 1 = biggest, 3 = smallest
  - Why do volcanoes erupt? | {3}

## Main: Six funnels
chart: Smaller topic (inside the big topic) | One question I could research

## Check: Which is narrowest?

## Stretch: Your own funnel
write: Pick your own big topic. Write your funnel: big topic, smaller topic, one question.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: dinosaurs, then T. rex, then How strong was a T. rex's bite?

# 20. Quote It Right
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can copy a speaker's exact words with quotation marks and tell who said them.
directions: Read the story about a class visitor. Then copy three things people said. Copy the words exactly, put quotation marks around them, and tell who said them.
mistakes: Students often change a word or two ("almost 20,000" for "more than 20,000") or drop the quotation marks. A quote must be the speaker's exact words. Others put the quotation marks around the speaker's name too; the name stays outside the marks.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 180 words). The beekeeper is a made-up character; the bee facts are checked: a strong honeybee hive in summer can hold tens of thousands of bees; a single worker bee makes only about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her life; honeybees do a "waggle dance" to show other bees which way to fly to find flowers. In this sheet the speaker's name can come before the quote (Mr. Brooks said, "...") or after it ("...," said Mr. Brooks); accept either with correct punctuation. A period or question mark at the end of the quote goes inside the closing quotation mark.

## Read
passage: A Visit From a Beekeeper
  On Friday, a beekeeper named Mr. Brooks visited our class. He brought a wooden frame from a hive, but no bees!
  "A honeybee hive can hold more than 20,000 bees," Mr. Brooks said. Everyone gasped.
  Lily raised her hand. "How much honey does one bee make?" she asked.
  Mr. Brooks smiled. "Each worker bee makes only about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life," he said. "That is why a jar of honey needs so many bees."
  Next, Omar wanted to know how bees find flowers. Mr. Brooks wiggled back and forth to show us. "Bees do a dance to tell each other which way to fly to the flowers," he explained.
  Before he left, our teacher, Ms. Diaz, thanked him. "Now we know why every bee matters," she said.

## Warm up: Spot the right quote
choice: Which sentence quotes Lily correctly?
  - Lily asked how much honey one bee makes.
  - "Lily asked, How much honey does one bee make?"
  * Lily asked, "How much honey does one bee make?"
choice: Which part goes inside the quotation marks?
  * Only the exact words the person said
  - The person's name
  - Everything in the sentence

## Main: Copy three quotes
example: What did Ms. Diaz say? **Ms. Diaz said, "Now we know why every bee matters."**
write: What did Mr. Brooks say about how many bees live in a hive?
  lines: 2
  key: Mr. Brooks said, "A honeybee hive can hold more than 20,000 bees."
write: What did Mr. Brooks say about how much honey one worker bee makes?
  lines: 2
  key: Mr. Brooks said, "Each worker bee makes only about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life."
write: What did Mr. Brooks say about why bees dance?
  lines: 2
  key: Mr. Brooks explained, "Bees do a dance to tell each other which way to fly to the flowers."

## Fix it
write: Sam wrote: Mr. Brooks said, "A beehive can have almost 20,000 bees." What did Sam do wrong? Write it the right way.
  lines: 3
  key: Sam changed the words; Mr. Brooks said "A honeybee hive can hold more than 20,000 bees." Right way: Mr. Brooks said, "A honeybee hive can hold more than 20,000 bees."

## Stretch
write: Why is it important to copy a speaker's exact words when you use quotation marks?
  lines: 2
  key: Quotation marks tell readers these are the person's own words, so changing them could change what the person meant and would not be fair to the speaker.

# 21. Picture Credit Lines
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can give credit to the person who made a picture and tell where I found it.
directions: Each picture card tells who made the picture and where it came from. Write a credit line for each picture using the pattern in the example.
mistakes: Students often write only where they found the picture ("from the internet") and leave out the person who made it. "The internet" is not a place: name the website, book or museum. Others copy the picture's title as the creator.
notes: All four pictures, people and sources on this sheet are made up for practice; the small line drawings stand in for the real photo, drawing and painting. A credit line is the short line printed under a picture; it is not yet a full citation (that comes in grade 5). The pattern on this sheet is kind of picture, "by" the creator, then "from" the source. Some sources also ask for the license, such as "CC BY," at the end: if a source gives one, it is good to add it.

## Warm up: The pattern
example: Picture card: a photo of a dog, taken by Lee Chen, found on the website Happy Paws Photos.
  Credit line: **Photo by Lee Chen, from Happy Paws Photos.**

  - Picture from the internet.
  - Photo by Lee Chen.

# 33. What Our Read-Alouds Show
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayon or colored pencil
ican: I can graph the settings of our read-alouds and use the graph to find what our collection is missing.
directions: Read the list of 15 read-alouds and where each one takes place. Make a tally for each kind of setting. Then color one box for each book to make a bar graph. Use your graph to answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often skip a book or count one twice; have them check off each book as they tally and make sure the totals add to 15. Others read "missing" as "a book I did not like." Ask: what kinds of places do our books almost never visit?
notes: The 15 titles are made up for practice. Swap in your own class read-aloud list if you like: keep the same five setting groups and the bar graph still works. The point of the last question is the Windows and Mirrors idea: a class collection should show readers many kinds of places and lives (windows) and also let readers see places like their own (mirrors). Counts: big city 5, small town or farm 4, make-believe world 3, nature 2, another country 1.
