# 1. Counting Intervals
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can find the size of an interval by counting letter names.
directions: An interval is the distance between two notes. Count every letter name from the first note to the second, including both. Name the notes on the staffs, then fill in the table.
mistakes: The classic error is counting only the steps between notes (C to E is "2") instead of counting both notes (C, D, E is a 3rd). Make students write every letter they count. For descending intervals, count down the alphabet (D, C, B, A, G). An octave is an 8th: the same letter name, eight letters apart.
notes: Each staff holds four intervals, read in pairs from left to right: notes 1 and 2 are interval 1, notes 3 and 4 are interval 2, and so on. Intervals 9, 11 and 12 go down. The line and space shortcut in Part C works for any staff: line to line or space to space gives an odd number (3rd, 5th, 7th), line to space gives an even number (2nd, 4th, 6th, octave).

## Warm up: Count the letters
example: C up to E: count C (1), D (2), E (3). It is a 3rd.
fill:
  - A up to C is a {3rd}.
  - G up to the next G is an {octave}.
  - E down to B is a {4th}.

## Stretch: Lines and spaces
write: Look at intervals 2, 4 and 6 on the staff. Are both notes on lines, both in spaces, or one of each? What do you notice about their sizes?
  lines: 3
  key: In interval 2 (E and G) and interval 4 (G and D) both notes are on lines; in interval 6, D sits in the space just below the staff and C is in space 3, so both are in spaces. All three are odd sizes (3rd, 5th, 7th): line to line or space to space always gives an odd number.

# 7. Chord Progression Puzzle
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; a keyboard, barred instrument or guitar for checking
ican: I can find the chords in a progression from clues about roots and quality.
directions: Build the chords of C major first. Then use each clue to find the missing chord in 4 progressions: write its Roman numeral, its name and its notes. Play each progression to check that it sounds right.
pairs: chord-explorer
mistakes: Students often count the root from the wrong place: the "4th note of the scale" in C major is F, counting C as 1. Watch for upper-case and lower-case Roman numerals: upper case (I, IV, V) means a major chord, lower case (ii, iii, vi) means minor. In G major, the chord on B has F sharp, not F.
notes: Chords built on each note of a major scale follow the same pattern of qualities in every key: I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, V major, vi minor, vii° diminished. Answers: progression A IV (F: F A C); progression B vi (A minor: A C E); progression C V (G: G B D); progression D, in G major, iii (B minor: B D F♯). Progression B (I V vi IV) is one of the most common progressions in popular music. The Chord Explorer tool plays each chord and progression if a keyboard is not handy.

## Warm up: The chords of C major
table: Roman numeral | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii°
  - Root | C | D | E | F | G | A | B
  - Chord notes | C E G | {D F A} | {E G B} | {F A C} | {G B D} | {A C E} | {B D F}
  - Quality | major | {minor} | {minor} | {major} | {major} | {minor} | {diminished}

## Main: Find the missing chord
text: **Progression A, in C major:** I, ?, V, I. Clue: the missing chord is major, and its root is the 4th note of the scale.
text: **Progression B, in C major:** I, V, ?, IV. Clue: the missing chord is minor, and its root is the 6th note of the scale.
text: **Progression C, in C major:** ii, ?, I. Clue: the missing chord is major, its root is the 5th note of the scale, and it pulls strongly home to I.
text: **Progression D, in G major** (one sharp, F♯): I, ?, IV, V. Clue: the missing chord is minor, and its root is the 3rd note of the G major scale.
table: Progression | Roman numeral | Chord name | Chord notes
  - A | {IV} | {F major} | {F A C}
  - B | {vi} | {A minor} | {A C E}
  - C | {V} | {G major} | {G B D}
  - D | {iii} | {B minor} | {B D F♯}
choice: Which chord in progression B is a minor chord besides the one you found?
  - I
  - V
  - IV
  * none: I, V and IV are all major

## Main: Check by playing
write: Play progression B two ways: with your answer, then with IV in the missing spot (I, V, IV, IV). How does the sound change?
  lines: 2
  key: With vi (A minor) the third chord turns darker or sadder for a moment before IV; with IV it sounds bright the whole way and less interesting because IV repeats.

## Stretch: Write a clue
write: Make a 4-chord progression in C major with one missing chord. Write a clue about the missing chord's root and quality, and give the answer.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: "I, IV, ?, I. Clue: a major chord on the 5th note of the scale. Answer: V, G major (G B D)."

# 20. Balance Pyramid
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a balance pyramid to explain how low parts support an ensemble's sound.
directions: Read about the balance pyramid. Place each band section and each choir part in its level of the pyramid. Then explain why the low parts sit at the base and solve two balance problems.
mistakes: Students often think the pyramid means the melody should be quietest, or that the tubas should simply blast. The pyramid is about fullness: lower parts give more sound so the upper parts can sit on top of them. Watch for horns placed at the top because they play high in some music; in the pyramid they belong with the middle (alto) voices.
notes: The balance pyramid is a widely taught band and choir idea (often credited to the band conductor and composer Francis McBeth): imagine the ensemble's sound as a pyramid with the bass voices as a broad base and the soprano voices as a narrow point. Reasons: our ears hear high sounds more easily than low ones, so low parts need more fullness to sound equal, and the low notes carry the roots of the chords that the upper parts tune to. Band levels used here (directors place a few instruments differently): soprano: flutes, clarinets, trumpets; alto: alto saxophones, horns; tenor: trombones, euphoniums; bass: tubas. Choir: sopranos, altos, tenors, basses from top to base. Problems: 1 B, 2 C.

## Warm up: Read about it
text: A **balance pyramid** shows how much sound each part of an ensemble gives. The lowest voices make a wide base, the middle voices sit above them, and the highest voices form the narrow top. A band or choir that sounds balanced like a pyramid sounds full and warm. One shaped like an upside-down pyramid, heavy on top, sounds thin and shrill.
fill:
  - In a balance pyramid, the {lowest} voices form the widest part.
  - A band with too much sound from the high instruments sounds thin and {shrill}.

## Main: Build the pyramid
text: **Band sections:** flutes, clarinets, trumpets, alto saxophones, horns, trombones, euphoniums, tubas. **Choir parts:** sopranos, altos, tenors, basses.
table: Pyramid level | Band sections | Choir part
  height: 0.45
  - Top (soprano voices) | {flutes, clarinets, trumpets} | {sopranos}
  - Upper middle (alto voices) | {alto saxophones, horns} | {altos}
  - Lower middle (tenor voices) | {trombones, euphoniums} | {tenors}
  - Base (bass voices) | {tubas} | {basses}
write: Explain why the low parts sit at the base of the pyramid. Use two reasons.
  lines: 3
  key: Our ears hear high sounds more easily than low ones, so the low parts need more fullness to be heard equally. The low notes are also usually the roots of the chords, so the other parts tune and fit their notes on top of them, like a building on its foundation.

## Main: Solve it
choice: The band sounds thin and piercing in a loud section. Which fix follows the pyramid?
  - Flutes and trumpets play louder.
  * Flutes and trumpets back off a little while tubas and trombones give fuller sound.
  - Everyone plays as loud as possible.
  - The tubas stop playing.
choice: A choir has 20 sopranos and only 4 basses, and the basses cannot be heard. What helps most?
  - The basses sing an octave higher.
  - The sopranos sing louder so the song stays strong.
  * The sopranos sing lighter and the basses sing with full, supported tone.
  - The altos stop singing.

## Stretch: Your ensemble
write: Draw or describe the balance pyramid for an ensemble you know, such as your class with recorders, barred instruments and voices. Which part is the base?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. For example: "Base: bass xylophones and the drum. Middle: alto xylophones and the alto singers. Top: recorders and soprano glockenspiels, which carry easily and should play lighter."

# 21. Weekly Rehearsal Goal Sheet
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, your music for this week
ican: I can set practice goals for my part and choose a strategy for each.
directions: Practice writing clear goals and matching problems to strategies. Then set 3 goals for your part this week, pick a strategy for each, and check off each day you work on it.
mistakes: Goals like "get better" or "practice more" cannot be checked off. Push for a measure number and a measurable target (a tempo, a number of clean repeats). Watch for strategies that do not match the problem, such as "play it faster" for a passage that is already rushed.
notes: A strong goal names where (measures), what (notes, rhythm, tone, dynamics) and how you will know (tempo marking, number of clean repeats). Revisit the sheet at the end of the week: students who did not reach a goal should name what got in the way, not just mark it "not yet." Practice can happen in class or at school; nothing on this sheet requires practice at home.

## Warm up: Goals and strategies
choice: Which goal is the clearest?
  - Get better at my part.
  - Practice the hard parts more.
  * Play measures 17 to 24 at 80 beats per minute with no stops, 3 times in a row.
choice: Which goal is the clearest?
  - Have better tone.
  * Hold the long note in measure 32 for all 4 beats with a steady sound.
  - Play louder sometimes.
match: Match each problem to a strategy that fixes it.
  - I rush in the fast run = Practice it slowly with a metronome, then raise the speed a little at a time
  - The same wrong note keeps coming back = Circle the note, then play just that measure slowly 5 times correctly
  - I run out of breath in a long phrase = Mark a breath spot with a check mark and practice breathing there
  - I lose my place during rests = Write the counts above the rests and count them silently
  - I play the dynamics the same everywhere = Highlight each dynamic marking and exaggerate it in practice

## Main: My goals this week
chart: Goal (where, what, how I will know) | Strategy | Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 | Day 4 | Day 5
  rows: 3
  key: Answers vary. Each goal names measures and a measurable target; each strategy fits its goal (slow practice with a metronome for tempo, isolating a measure for wrong notes, marking breaths for long phrases). Check marks show the days the student worked on it.

## Stretch: End of week
write: Which goal did you reach? For a goal you did not reach yet, what got in the way, and what will you try next week?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the goal reached with evidence ("I played measures 17 to 24 at 80 three times") and, for an unmet goal, a specific obstacle and a new strategy ("I kept rushing, so next week I will start at 60 instead of 72").

# 33. Two Ways to Play Greensleeves
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; two recordings of Greensleeves, one sung by a solo voice and one played by a string ensemble
ican: I can compare two performances of the same song using tempo, timbre and mood.
directions: Read about the song. Listen to each performance two times and fill the chart. Then decide which performance fits a scene, and support your choice with evidence from the chart.
mistakes: Students often describe the song instead of the performance ("it is sad"); both recordings play the same tune, so ask what this performer did differently. Watch for "timbre" answered with loud or soft: timbre is the color of the sound (breathy, warm, bright, rich). For tempo, have students tap the beat to compare rather than guess.
notes: Greensleeves is a traditional English song. It is often said that King Henry VIII wrote it, but there is no evidence for that, and historians point out that its style comes from after his time; it was first registered in London in 1580. The tune is in a minor mode, usually in a lilting 6/8 or 3/4. For the string version, Ralph Vaughan Williams's Fantasia on Greensleeves (1934, for strings, harp and flutes) is a good choice; for the solo voice, any unaccompanied or lightly accompanied folk recording works. Play each recording for about 90 seconds.

## Warm up: The words we need
fill:
  - The speed of the beat is the {tempo}.
  - The color or quality of a sound, like breathy or bright, is its {timbre}.
  - Greensleeves is a traditional song first registered in London in {1580}.
choice: Which word describes timbre, not tempo or dynamics?
  - slow
  * warm and rich
  - soft
choice: King Henry VIII wrote Greensleeves. Is this proven?
  - Yes, he wrote it for a queen.
  * No, there is no evidence, and its style comes from after his time.
  - Yes, the 1580 record names him.

## Main: Compare the performances
chart: Solo voice | String ensemble
  labels: Tempo (fast, medium, slow; steady or flexible?); Timbre (describe the sound color); Dynamics (loud, soft, changes?); Mood (how does it feel?)
  height: 0.6
  key: Answers vary. Example: Tempo: voice slow and flexible, stretching phrase ends; strings slow and steady. Timbre: voice clear and personal; strings warm, rich and full. Dynamics: voice mostly soft and even; strings swell louder and softer. Mood: voice lonely, like one person's story; strings dreamy and grand.

## Main: Choose for a scene
write: A filmmaker needs music for a scene where a traveler walks alone on a misty road at dawn. Which performance fits better? Support your choice with two pieces of evidence from your chart.
  lines: 4
  starter: The ___ performance fits better because ...
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a version and cites two chart details, for example: "The solo voice, because its slow, flexible tempo sounds like someone walking and thinking, and the single clear voice feels lonely, like the traveler."

## Stretch: Same notes, new feeling
write: Both performances use the same melody. Explain how a performer can change the mood without changing the notes.
  lines: 3
  key: By changing the tempo, dynamics, timbre and phrasing: playing slower or with more rubato, louder or softer, choosing instruments with a different color, or adding swells and pauses all change how the same notes feel.
