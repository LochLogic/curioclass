# 1. Four Stages, Four Audiences
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencil or highlighter for shading
ican: I can describe four kinds of stages and how each one changes the way a scene is staged.
directions: Draw each stage from above (a bird's-eye view). Shade where the audience sits and label the stage. Then write one staging challenge for each.
mistakes: Students often draw a thrust stage with the audience on one side; it has audience on three sides. Some think black box means a stage painted black; it is a flexible empty room whose seating can be set up in any shape for each show.
notes: Proscenium: the audience sits on one side, facing a stage framed by an arch (the proscenium arch). Thrust: the stage pushes out into the audience, who sit on three sides. Arena (theater in the round): audience on all sides. Black box: a plain, usually black room with movable seating, arranged as proscenium, thrust, alley or arena for each show. Challenges in the key are models; accept any accurate challenge.

## Warm up: Match
match: Match each stage to its description.
  - Proscenium = The audience faces the stage from one side, through a frame
  - Thrust = The stage pushes out with the audience on three sides
  - Arena = The audience surrounds the stage on all sides
  - Black box = An empty room where seats can be set up in any shape

## Main: Draw, shade and label
draw: Proscenium (bird's-eye view, shade the audience) | 1.7
write: One staging challenge:
  lines: 1
  key: Actors must stay open to one side (cheat out), and people in the back rows are far from the action.
draw: Thrust (bird's-eye view, shade the audience) | 1.7
write: One staging challenge:
  lines: 1
  key: Some of the audience always sees an actor's back, and tall scenery can only go at the back wall or it blocks the side seats.
draw: Arena (bird's-eye view, shade the audience) | 1.7
write: One staging challenge:
  lines: 1
  key: There is no back wall for scenery, so furniture must be low; actors must keep moving so no side sees only their backs; entrances come through the aisles.
draw: Black box (bird's-eye view, pick a seating shape and shade it) | 1.7
write: One staging challenge:
  lines: 1
  key: The room is small and usually has no fly space or wings, and the seating has to be planned and set up for each show.

## Stretch
write: A scene shows two friends whispering a secret at a lunch table. Which stage would make the audience feel closest to the secret? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Arena or a small black box puts the audience close on every side, so they feel they are leaning in to overhear; a thrust also works. A large proscenium puts more distance between the audience and the whisper.

# 7. Woods Ground Plan to Scale
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can draw a ground plan to scale with set pieces, entrances and clear sightlines.
directions: Our stage is 32 feet wide and 20 feet deep. On the grid, 1 square = 2 feet. Convert each set piece to squares, then draw the woods for A Midsummer Night's Dream, Act 3, Scene 1, from above. Label every piece and entrance.
mistakes: The most common mistake is forgetting to divide by 2: an 8-foot log drawn 8 squares long fills half the stage. Watch for tall pieces (the hawthorn bush, the tree) placed downstage center, where they block the audience's view of everything behind them. Titania's bower must be seen from every seat, since she sleeps on stage through the whole rehearsal.
notes: In Act 3, Scene 1, the Mechanicals rehearse their play in the woods while the fairy queen Titania sleeps nearby in her bower. Quince calls a green plot their stage and the hawthorn brake (a thicket of hawthorn) their tiring-house, the place to change, so a bush big enough for actors to hide behind fits the text. The key shows one model layout: the bower in solid red, upstage on the stage left side; the log in dashes, downstage; the hawthorn in dots, on the stage right side. Positions will vary. A sightline is a line from an audience seat to a point on stage; checking sightlines from the end seats of the front row finds pieces that block the view.

# 20. Improvisation Session Log
minutes: 15
materials: pencil
ican: I can record what happens in improvisation sessions and choose ideas to keep or cut.
directions: Fill in one row at the end of each improvisation session. Write short notes. A keeper is an idea that worked and could grow; a cut is an idea that did not help the scene.
mistakes: Students often write "it was fun" as the best moment. Push for a specific moment: who did what, and why it worked. Watch for empty "cut" boxes; cutting is part of devising, and a good reason to cut (it stopped the scene, it repeated an idea, nobody could follow it) is worth writing down.
notes: Plan about 5 minutes at the end of each of 4 sessions. The key shows a model row for each session from a sample group; logs will differ. Collect the log after session 2 to see whether groups are choosing keepers, not just listing everything they tried. Prompts can come from Scenario Task Cards (#21) or any improvisation game.

## Example
example: **Prompt:** Two campers find a map in an old tent. **Best moment:** Jo read the map upside down and walked off the wrong way with total confidence. **Keep:** the upside-down map as a running joke. **Cut:** the long argument about snacks; it stopped the story.

## Warm up
choice: Which note is the most useful "best moment"?
  - It was really fun.
  - Everyone did well.
  * When Sam froze mid-sentence, the whole group froze too, and the audience laughed.
  - We did a lot of scenes.
choice: Which is a good reason to cut an idea?
  * It stopped the scene, and nobody knew what to do next.
  - It was someone else's idea.
  - It made the audience laugh.
  - It was the first thing we tried.

## Main: Session log
chart: Prompt used | Best moment | Idea to keep | Idea to cut, and why
  labels: Session 1; Session 2; Session 3; Session 4
  height: 1.05
  key: Model rows: 1 Prompt: a lost dog at a bus stop. Best: the dog owner kept describing a tiny dog while a huge one waited. Keep: the size mix-up. Cut: the bus driver character; too many people. 2 Prompt: "This is not my suitcase." Best: the slow reveal of what was inside. Keep: the suitcase as the object of our scene. Cut: the chase; hard to stage. 3 Prompt: a sound, a thunderclap. Best: everyone ducked at once. Keep: the group freeze. Cut: the talking weather. 4 Prompt: a want, "I need you to forgive me." Best: the quiet apology at the end. Keep: ending on that line. Cut: the joke right before it; it broke the mood.

## Stretch
write: Look across all four sessions. Which keeper would you build a full scene around, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one keeper and gives a reason tied to the scene: it has a clear want or conflict, it surprised the audience, or it could grow into a beginning, middle and end.

# 21. Scenario Task Cards
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors
ican: I can write improvisation scenarios with a who, a where, a what and a want.
directions: Read the model card. Write 4 scenario cards for the next improvisation round. Each card needs a who (the characters), a where (the place), a what (what is happening) and a want (what one character is trying to get). Cut the cards apart when you finish.
mistakes: The want is where scenarios go flat. "Wants to have fun" gives actors nothing to play; "wants to get the last ticket before her cousin does" does. Watch for wheres that are only names ("Ohio"): a playable place has things in it to use (a crowded elevator, a campsite in the rain).
notes: The answers on the key are sample cards; every student card will differ. Check each card for all four parts and a want that one character can actively pursue, with something in the way. Keep the cards for warm-ups: pairs draw one and get 60 seconds to plan.

## Model
example: **Who:** two campers. **Where:** a small tent in a rainstorm. **What:** the tent has started to leak. **Want:** one camper wants to fix the leak without waking the camp counselor next door.

## Warm up
choice: Which want gives improvisers the most to play?
  - Wants to have a nice day
  * Wants to get the last concert ticket before her cousin does
  - Wants to be happy
choice: Which where is easiest to act out?
  - Somewhere in Ohio
  - A place
  * A crowded elevator that has just stopped between floors

## Main: Write 4 cards
cards: Write one scenario on each card. Then cut them apart.
  - Who: {a chef and a food critic} Where: {a busy restaurant kitchen} What: {the main dish just burned} Want: {the chef wants to serve something great before the critic notices}
  - Who: {two astronauts} Where: {a space station} What: {the radio to Earth has gone silent} Want: {one astronaut wants to fix it before the other panics}
  - Who: {a grandparent and a grandchild} Where: {a dock on a lake} What: {they are fishing and nothing is biting} Want: {the grandchild wants to catch a fish before sunset}
  - Who: {two museum guards} Where: {an art museum at night} What: {a statue has moved} Want: {one guard wants to prove the statue moved}

## Stretch
write: Choose your strongest card. What makes it good for improvisation?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer points to a clear, active want, a place with things to use, and an obstacle or time pressure that creates conflict.

# 33. Moved in Time
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how the time period a director chooses changes what a scene means to an audience.
directions: Read about two productions of the same scene from A Midsummer Night's Dream. Highlight the choices each director made. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often describe the costumes and stop there. Push them to the meaning: what the audience understands about Hermia and her father because of the era. Watch for "the modern one is better"; the question is how each era changes the scene, not which production is right.
notes: Both productions are invented for this sheet. The scene is real: in Act 1, Scene 1, Egeus asks Duke Theseus to make his daughter Hermia marry Demetrius, though she loves Lysander, and Theseus tells her the law of Athens is on her father's side. Directors often move Shakespeare's plays to other periods to make an idea clearer to today's audience. Passage about 330 words.

## Read
passage: One Scene, Two Eras | numbered
  In the first scene of A Midsummer Night's Dream, an angry father named Egeus storms into the court of Duke Theseus. He wants his daughter Hermia to marry Demetrius. Hermia loves Lysander. Egeus asks the Duke to enforce the old law of Athens, which says a daughter must obey her father's choice. The Duke tells Hermia to obey. By the end of the scene, Hermia and Lysander have decided to run away to the woods.
  Two theaters staged this scene in the same year, in two very different eras.
  The first director set the play in 1910. The court became a grand drawing room with dark wood and a portrait of the Duke over the fireplace. Egeus wore a stiff high collar and spoke slowly, as if he were reading a contract. Hermia wore a long white dress and stood with her hands folded. When she refused to marry Demetrius, the other guests gasped and turned away. In this world, a father having the final say over his daughter's marriage seemed normal, so Hermia's "no" felt brave and dangerous. The audience worried for her.
  The second director set the play in the present day. The Duke's court became a glass office, and the Duke sat at a desk with two phones. Egeus waved a folder of papers and shouted. Hermia wore jeans and a hoodie and rolled her eyes. When Egeus demanded that the law be enforced, a few people in the audience laughed, because in today's world the idea of a father legally choosing a husband sounds unbelievable. Here, Egeus seemed old-fashioned and a bit ridiculous, and Hermia seemed to be in the right from the start.
  Neither director changed Shakespeare's words. Only the world around the words changed. In 1910, the scene was about a young woman risking everything by breaking the rules of her time. In the present day, it became a scene about a father who has not noticed that the world has changed. The same lines, in two eras, asked two different questions.

## Main
choice: In the 1910 production, why did Hermia's refusal feel brave and dangerous?
  - Because she shouted at the Duke
  * Because in that world a father's final say over a marriage seemed normal, so saying no broke the rules
  - Because she wore a white dress
  - Because the Duke changed the law
choice: Why did some audience members laugh in the present-day production?
  - Shakespeare wrote the scene as a joke.
  - Hermia told a joke.
  * A father legally choosing a husband sounds unbelievable today.
  - The Duke dropped his phones.
write: Choose one design or acting choice from each production. Explain what each one told the audience. (Paragraphs 3 and 4)
  lines: 4
  key: Any pair, for example: 1910, the guests gasping and turning away showed that Hermia had broken a serious rule; present day, Hermia rolling her eyes in jeans and a hoodie showed she sees her father's demand as out of date.
write: Paragraph 5 says the two productions "asked two different questions." What question does each production ask?
  lines: 3
  key: 1910: Will Hermia dare to break the rules of her time, and what will it cost her? Present day: How can a father not see that the world has changed?
fill: Neither director changed Shakespeare's {words}; only the {world} around them changed.

## Stretch
write: Choose a third era for this scene (for example, ancient Athens, the 1960s, or the future). How would that era change what the scene means?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the era, one design or acting choice, and how the audience would see Egeus or Hermia differently, such as the 1960s, where Hermia joins a generation pushing back against their parents, making her escape part of a bigger change.
