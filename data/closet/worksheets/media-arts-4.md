# 1. Jumping Frog Zoetrope Strip
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler, a paper strip 24 inches long (tape two long strips together), markers
ican: I can plan an animation loop with evenly spaced frames.
directions: Read about the zoetrope. Use the number line to find where each frame starts. Finish the jump plan, sketch your 12 frames, then draw them on your paper strip with a ruler.
mistakes: Uneven frames are the biggest problem: a frame that is too wide or too narrow makes the frog jerk when it spins. Measure every 2 inches with a ruler; do not eyeball. Students also forget that the strip is a loop: frame 12 has to lead smoothly back into frame 1. Drawing the frog at a different size or a different spot across the frame makes it jump around instead of up and down.
notes: A zoetrope is a spinning drum with slits; looking through the slits at a strip of drawings inside makes them move. It was first made in the 1830s. If your class does not have a zoetrope, a paper plate or a cardboard tube version works, or students can flip the frames by cutting them into a flip book. The jump in the plan goes straight up and down in place so the loop repeats; the heights are in squares of an imaginary grid, so students can draw a light pencil ground line and measure up.

## Warm up: Loops and spacing
example: A zoetrope holds a strip of 12 drawings in a ring. As the drum spins, each slit shows one drawing for an instant. Each drawing is a little different, so your brain sees motion.
choice: Why must frame 12 look almost like frame 1?
  - So the strip is easier to cut
  * The strip is a loop, so the motion runs straight back into frame 1
  - So the frog can rest
  - Frame 12 is never seen
fill:
  - The strip is 24 inches long and has 12 frames. Each frame is {2} inches wide.
  - The line between frame 5 and frame 6 is {10} inches from the start.

## Main: Find the frame lines
text: Each tick is 2 inches. Write how far from the start each lettered line is.
numberline: 0 24 2
  show: 0 24
  mark: A=6 B=14 C=20
fill:
  - Line A is where frame {3} ends.
  - Line C is where frame {10} ends.

## Main: Plan the jump
text: The frog jumps straight up and lands in the same spot. Its height going up should match its height coming down.
table: Frame | Frog pose | Height above ground (squares)
  - 1 | sitting | 0
  - 2 | crouching low | 0
  - 3 | pushing off, legs stretching | 1
  - 4 | rising | {3}
  - 5 | rising | 5
  - 6 | top of the jump | 6
  - 7 | top of the jump | {6}
  - 8 | falling | 5
  - 9 | falling | {3}
  - 10 | legs reaching down | {1}
  - 11 | landing, crouched | 0
  - 12 | sitting | 0

## Main: Sketch your frames
draw: Frames 1 to 6 (draw 6 small, equal boxes first) | 1.5
draw: Frames 7 to 12 | 1.5

## Stretch
write: Your partner's frog seems to stutter and jump sideways when the zoetrope spins. Give two likely causes and how to fix them.
  lines: 3
  key: The frames are not evenly spaced (measure every 2 inches with a ruler), and the frog is drawn in a different spot or size in some frames (keep it centered over the same ground spot and the same size). A big jump in height between two frames can also cause a stutter.

# 7. Storyboard with Camera Notes
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can plan a stop-motion scene with a storyboard, shot sizes and frame counts.
directions: Answer the warm up. Read the story plan, choose the missing shot sizes and work out the frames for each panel. Then draw all 8 panels and write the shot size and frames under each one.
mistakes: Students multiply by the wrong number: at 12 frames per second, every second is 12 photos, so half a second is 6 frames. Some draw every panel as a medium shot; ask "What does the audience need to see here?" A close-up shows a face or a small detail; a wide shot shows the place and where everyone is.
notes: Many classroom stop-motion films are shot at 12 frames per second. The whole story is 144 frames, or 12 seconds, a realistic size for one or two class periods. The shot sizes in the key are the best fit, but accept another choice if the student can explain it. Panels can be quick stick-figure sketches: a storyboard is a plan, not finished art.

## Warm up
choice: Which shot size shows the whole place and where everyone is?
  * A wide shot
  - A close-up
  - A medium shot
  - An extreme close-up
choice: Which shot size is best for showing a character's surprised face?
  - A wide shot
  - A medium shot
  * A close-up
  - A shot from very far away
fill:
  - At 12 frames per second, 1 second of film needs {12} photos.
  - At 12 frames per second, 3 seconds needs {36} frames.
  - At 12 frames per second, half a second needs {6} frames.

## Main: The story plan
text: **The Snail and the Strawberry** is shot at 12 frames per second. Fill in the missing shot sizes (wide, medium or close-up) and the frames for each panel.
table: Panel | What happens | Shot size | Seconds | Frames
  - 1 | A garden. A strawberry sits on a rock. A snail is far away. | wide | 2 | {24}
  - 2 | The snail's eyes stretch toward the strawberry. | {close-up} | 1 | {12}
  - 3 | The snail starts to crawl. | medium | 1 and a half | {18}
  - 4 | A beetle walks in from the other side. | medium | 1 | {12}
  - 5 | The snail and the beetle race toward the rock. | {wide} | 2 | {24}
  - 6 | The beetle sees the snail is winning and looks shocked. | {close-up} | half | {6}
  - 7 | The snail reaches the strawberry first. | medium | 1 | {12}
  - 8 | The snail and the beetle share the strawberry. | wide | 3 | {36}
  - Total | | | 12 | {144}

## Main: Draw the storyboard
table: Panel 1 | Panel 2 | Panel 3 | Panel 4
  height: 1.25
  - | | |
table: Under 1 | Under 2 | Under 3 | Under 4
  - Shot: | Shot: | Shot: | Shot:
  - Frames: | Frames: | Frames: | Frames:
table: Panel 5 | Panel 6 | Panel 7 | Panel 8
  height: 1.25
  - | | |
table: Under 5 | Under 6 | Under 7 | Under 8
  - Shot: | Shot: | Shot: | Shot:
  - Frames: | Frames: | Frames: | Frames:
note: Each panel should match its row in the plan: the shot size and frame count written under it, and a drawing that fits the shot (panel 2 and panel 6 fill the box with eyes or a face; panel 5 shows both racers and the rock).

## Stretch
fill: Your class can take 24 photos in one class period. The whole film needs {6} class periods.
write: Panel 8 lasts 3 seconds. If nothing moves, the film will look frozen. How can you keep the shot alive?
  lines: 3
  key: Move something a tiny bit in every few frames: the snail's eyes blink or wiggle, the beetle's antennae twitch, bites disappear from the strawberry, or a leaf sways. Small moves keep a held shot from looking stuck.

# 20. Cue Sheet in Seconds
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan when music and sound effects start and stop in a video.
directions: Practice finding start times, end times and lengths. Read the plan for the video "Kite Day." Fill in the cue sheet, then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students mix up the end time and the length. Length is the end time minus the start time: a sound from 10 to 16 seconds lasts 6 seconds. Students also forget that sounds can overlap: the music keeps playing under the sound effects.
notes: A cue sheet tells the editor exactly when each sound starts and stops. Professional cue sheets use timecode with hours, minutes, seconds and frames; this one uses whole seconds. If your class has a video editor, students can build the soundtrack from their cue sheet, but the sheet works on paper alone.

## Warm up
example: A sound starts at 10 seconds and ends at 14 seconds. Its length is 14 - 10 = 4 seconds.
fill:
  - A sound starts at 12 seconds and ends at 17 seconds. It lasts {5} seconds.
  - A 4-second sound starts at 9 seconds. It ends at {13} seconds.
  - A sound ends at 30 seconds and lasts 8 seconds. It started at {22} seconds.

## Main: Read the plan
text: **Kite Day** (40 seconds). Cheerful music plays from the start until the kite string snaps. At 6 seconds a dog barks for 1 second. A gust of wind blows from 10 to 16 seconds. At 18 seconds the string snaps with a 1-second snap, and slow, sad music starts. Lucy's running footsteps go from 20 to 27 seconds. At 30 seconds the sad music stops and hopeful music plays to the end, while the kite lands in a tree with a leafy rustle that lasts 3 seconds. At 36 seconds Lucy's friend rings a bike bell for 1 second.

## Main: The cue sheet
table: Cue | Music or effect | Sound | Start (seconds) | End (seconds) | Length (seconds)
  - M1 | music | cheerful music | 0 | {18} | {18}
  - M2 | music | {slow, sad music} | {18} | {30} | {12}
  - M3 | music | {hopeful music} | {30} | {40} | {10}
  - SFX1 | effect | dog bark | {6} | {7} | {1}
  - SFX2 | effect | {wind gust} | {10} | {16} | {6}
  - SFX3 | effect | {string snap} | {18} | {19} | {1}
  - SFX4 | effect | {running footsteps} | {20} | {27} | {7}
  - SFX5 | effect | {leaf rustle} | {30} | {33} | {3}
  - SFX6 | effect | {bike bell} | {36} | {37} | {1}

## Main: Read your cue sheet
choice: At 25 seconds, which sounds are playing?
  - Cheerful music and the dog bark
  * Sad music and running footsteps
  - Hopeful music and the bike bell
  - Only the wind
choice: Which two cues start at the same moment?
  - M1 and SFX1
  - SFX2 and SFX4
  - M3 and SFX6
  * M2 and SFX3
choice: Which sound effect is the longest?
  * Running footsteps
  - Wind gust
  - Leaf rustle
  - Dog bark

## Stretch
write: Add a seventh sound effect to Kite Day. Give its start and end time, and explain how it helps the story.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer gives a sound that fits the plan with a start and end inside 0 to 40 seconds and a reason, such as "Lucy gasps from 18 to 19 seconds, so the audience feels her surprise when the string snaps."

# 21. Sound Effects Inventory
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; optional: dry rice, paper plate, poster board, scrap paper, plastic cups, an empty plastic bottle
ican: I can plan sound effects and choose objects to make them.
directions: Match each sound to a classroom object that can make it. Read the video plan and list every sound effect it needs, grouped by type, with the object you will use. Then plan a way to make the hardest sound.
mistakes: Students often list the thing in the picture (a horse) instead of the object that makes the sound (two cups). The inventory is about what you will hold in your hand. Some sounds are missed because they are quiet: footsteps, a zipper, a door closing.
notes: These classroom tricks are real foley methods. Let students test two or three if time allows; they quickly hear that the best sound often comes from something that looks nothing like the real thing. Keep the rice on a plate or tray for easy cleanup.

## Warm up: Match the sound to the object
match: Write the letter of the object next to each sound.
  - Rain on a tent = Dry rice poured slowly onto a paper plate
  - Thunder = A big sheet of poster board shaken hard
  - Footsteps in dry leaves = Scrap paper crumpled in a steady rhythm
  - Horse hooves = Two plastic cups clopped on a desk
  - Wind = Blowing gently across the top of an empty bottle
  - Knock on a door = Knuckles tapped on a desk

## Main: Our video plan
text: **Storm at the Campsite:** Two friends hike through the woods to their tent. Wind picks up, then rain starts. They run, unzip the tent and dive inside. Thunder booms. A friendly horse from the next farm clops past, and one friend laughs.
chart: Weather sounds (sound: object) | Movement sounds (sound: object) | Object sounds (sound: object)
  rows: 4
  key: Weather: wind (blow across a bottle), rain (rice on a plate), thunder (shake poster board). Movement: footsteps in leaves (crumpled paper), running (hands patting thighs), horse hooves (cups on a desk), diving in (a pillow or bag of clothes dropped). Objects: tent zipper (zip a pencil case or jacket), tent flap (shake a jacket or a sheet), laugh (a voice, recorded live).
choice: Which sound in the plan is easiest to forget because it is quiet?
  - The thunder
  * The tent zipper
  - The rain
  - The horse hooves

## Stretch
write: Which sound on your list will be hardest to make? Plan two different ways to try it.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a sound and two real tests, such as thunder: shake poster board, or roll a ball across a tabletop while a partner taps a box; then choose the one that sounds bigger.

# 33. Before and After Edits
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can find the changes made to photos in ads and explain why advertisers make them.
directions: Read about photo editing and answer the warm up. Read each before-and-after description, list the changes, and explain why the advertiser made them. Then share your opinion.
mistakes: Students list only one big change. Push for three or more: color, size, shine, background, light and things added or taken away. Some students think every edit is a lie; fixing a dust spot or a dark photo is normal. The question to ask is "Does this edit make the product seem better than it really is?"
notes: The products and ads on this sheet are made up. If you can, show a real before-and-after example from a trusted educational source, and preview it first. Food in ads is often styled, not just edited: stylists may use tricks like extra shine or propped-up buns before the photo is taken. Some countries now require a label on ads in which a person's body or skin has been retouched, including France (since 2017) and Norway (since 2022).

## Warm up
example: **Photo editing** means changing a photo after it is taken: brighter colors, a new background, a spot removed, or something made to look bigger or shinier.
choice: Why do advertisers edit photos of their products?
  - To make the products look worse
  * To make the products look as appealing as possible, so more people buy them
  - Because cameras cannot take color photos
  - Because the law says every ad must be edited
choice: Which edit only fixes the photo and does not change how the product looks?
  - Making a burger look twice as tall
  - Painting extra shine on a toy car
  * Removing a speck of dust that was on the camera lens
  - Adding cheese that is not on the real sandwich

## Main: Three ads
table: Ad | Before (the real photo) | After (the photo in the ad)
  - 1. Product A burger | A flat bun, droopy lettuce, cheese not melted, on a plain gray table | A tall, shiny bun, crisp bright green lettuce, melted cheese dripping, steam rising, on a wooden table in warm sunlight
  - 2. Product B running shoes | A small scuff on the toe, dull colors, shoes sitting on a messy closet floor | No scuff, bright bold colors, shoes floating over a mountain trail at sunrise
  - 3. Product C toothpaste | A smiling person with off-white teeth, a small scratch on the chin, a cloudy sky behind | The same person with very white teeth, no scratch, and a bright blue sky behind
write: **Ad 1:** List at least three changes. Why did the advertiser make them?
  lines: 3
  key: Taller, shinier bun; brighter green lettuce; melted, dripping cheese; steam added; wooden table and sunlight instead of a gray table. The advertiser wants the burger to look fresh, hot, big and delicious so people feel hungry and buy it.
write: **Ad 2:** List at least three changes. Why did the advertiser make them?
  lines: 3
  key: The scuff is removed, the colors are brighter, the messy closet is replaced with a mountain trail at sunrise, and the shoes float. The advertiser wants the shoes to look brand new and exciting, and wants buyers to connect them with adventure and the outdoors.
write: **Ad 3:** List at least three changes. Why did the advertiser make them?
  lines: 3
  key: The teeth are whitened, the scratch is removed, and the cloudy sky becomes bright blue. The advertiser wants people to believe the toothpaste makes teeth that white and to connect it with a happy, sunny feeling.

## Stretch
choice: You buy Product A after seeing the ad, and your burger is flatter than the one in the photo. What is the most likely reason?
  - The restaurant gave you the wrong burger
  - Burgers shrink when you look at them
  - The ad was a drawing, not a photo
  * The photo in the ad was styled and edited to look better than the real burger
write: Should ads have to say when a photo has been edited? Give your opinion and one reason.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer takes a side and gives a reason, such as "Yes, because people should know the product will not look exactly like the photo," or "Only for big changes, because fixing light or dust does not trick anyone."
