# 1. Six Styles Survey
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, video clips or live demonstrations chosen by the teacher
ican: I can compare dance styles by technique, music, costume and origins.
directions: Match each style to a fact about it. Then fill in the survey chart for all six styles, choosing one cultural or social dance form for the last row. Finish by comparing two styles.
mistakes: Students describe costumes or music but skip technique: technique is how the body is trained to move (turnout, isolations, contraction). Origins answers are often just "America": push for who created the form, where and roughly when. Watch for stereotyped descriptions of cultural forms; describe what dancers do, not what a group is "like."
notes: Show a short clip or demonstration of each style before students fill the chart. For the sixth row, a form with a strong local community connection works well (for example, Mexican folklórico, Irish step dance, West African dance, K-pop dance, Bhangra or a Native American social dance shown with the community's permission). Keys give the main facts; accept other accurate details.

## Warm up: Match the style
match: Match each style to a fact about its technique or origins.
  - ballet = turnout and pointe work; codified at the French court of Louis XIV in the 1600s
  - modern = bare feet, weight and the floor; a break from ballet in the early 1900s
  - jazz = isolations and syncopation; grew from African American vernacular dance with jazz music
  - tap = metal plates on the shoes make the dancer a percussionist
  - hip hop = breaking began at block parties in the Bronx, New York, in the 1970s

## Main: Survey chart
text: Write one style in each row, in this order: ballet, modern, jazz, tap, hip hop, then your choice.
chart: Style | Technique (how the body moves) | Music | Costume | Origins (who, where, when)
  rows: 6
write: Which fact from your chart surprised you most? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Chart key: Ballet: turnout, pointed feet, lifted posture, pointe work; orchestral or classical music; tutus, tights, pointe or ballet slippers; Italian Renaissance courts, developed and codified in France (Louis XIV founded the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661). Modern: contraction and release, fall and recovery, use of weight and the floor; varied music, new commissions or silence; simple costumes, bare feet; early 1900s United States and Germany (Isadora Duncan, Martha Graham, Doris Humphrey). Jazz: isolations, syncopation, low center, high energy; jazz then popular and show music; jazz shoes, varied stage costumes; African American vernacular dance, developed through Broadway and film. Tap: rhythms made by the feet, heel and toe sounds, improvisation; jazz music or no music; tap shoes; 1800s United States, blending African rhythmic traditions with Irish and British step dance. Hip hop: breaking, popping, locking, grooves, freestyle in cyphers; DJ breakbeats and rap; streetwear and sneakers; 1970s Black and Latino youth in the Bronx, with funk styles from California. Surprise answers vary.

## Compare
write: Choose two styles from your chart. Explain one way they are alike and one way they are different.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Tap and hip hop both grew from African American communities and value improvisation. They differ in how they make rhythm: tap dancers create sound with their shoes, while hip hop dancers move to a DJ's beat.

## Stretch
write: Many styles borrow from each other. Give one example of a style that mixes influences from at least two others.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Examples: contemporary dance mixes ballet and modern; jazz-funk mixes jazz and hip hop; tap grew from African and Irish traditions; lyrical mixes ballet and jazz.

# 7. Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Evaluate
minutes: 45
materials: pencil
ican: I can write a four-part critique of a dance using describe, analyze, interpret and evaluate.
directions: Sort the critique sentences into the four stages. Then read the account of the dance excerpt and write a four-part critique, one paragraph frame for each stage.
mistakes: Students judge in the describe stage ("a beautiful opening"); description stays neutral and factual. Analysis is often skipped: it looks at how the parts work together (relationships, repetition, contrast, structure), not at meaning yet. In evaluate, students give an opinion without criteria; the judgment must name a criterion and cite evidence.
notes: "Fault Lines" is an invented excerpt written for this sheet, so every student critiques the same material. After practice, use the frame with a live class performance or a video excerpt. The four stages follow the widely used Feldman-style model of art criticism, adapted for dance.

## Warm up: Sort the stages
sort: Which stage is each sentence?
  - Describe: Four dancers in gray stand in a tight square under a single overhead light; A cello replaces the percussion in the middle section
  - Analyze: The opening unison breaks into canon, and the canon returns at the end in reverse; The reaching phrase repeats three times, building tension
  - Interpret: The cracking square suggests a friendship group pulling apart under pressure; Facing upstage at the end may mean the group has closed itself off
  - Evaluate: The ending is effective because the slow return to unison resolves the tension; The middle drags because the reach is repeated without variation

## Main: Read
passage: Fault Lines (excerpt, about 3 minutes)
  The stage is dark except for one square of light. Four dancers in plain gray clothes stand inside it, shoulder to shoulder, facing the audience. A low rumble of percussion begins. In unison, they lift their arms slowly and lower them, breathing together, four times.
  On a loud crack in the music, the dancer on the far left jerks sideways, as if pushed. The others follow one by one in canon, each jerk a count later, until the square has split into four separate dancers. Each now repeats the same sharp sideways jerk alone, but at a different speed, and each travels along a straight line toward a different corner of the stage. The light widens to follow them.
  In the middle section the percussion drops away and a single cello plays. The dancers, far apart, reach toward the center at a low level. Two of them almost touch hands, then pull back sharply. Twice more they reach, and twice more they pull back.
  In the final minute the jerking motif returns in reverse: each dancer is pulled backward, one by one, into the center. They end shoulder to shoulder again, breathing in unison, but now facing upstage, away from the audience, as the light shrinks to a square and goes dark.
write: **Describe.** What did you see and hear? Stay factual: dancers, costumes, light, sound, movements, space.
  lines: 3
  key: Four dancers in gray begin in a square of light, breathing in unison to low percussion. On a crack in the music they jerk sideways in canon and travel to four corners. In a cello section they reach toward the center at a low level and pull back three times. The jerking motif returns in reverse, pulling them back into the square, and they end facing upstage as the light goes dark.
write: **Analyze.** How do the parts relate? Look at motifs, repetition, contrast, structure and use of space.
  lines: 3
  key: The sideways jerk is the main motif; it moves from canon to solo variations to a reversed return. The structure is ABA': unison, separation, unison again. Contrasts: sharp jerks against slow breathing and reaching; percussion against the cello; a tight square against the far corners. The repeated reach-and-pull-back builds tension in the middle.
write: **Interpret.** What do you think the dance means? Support your idea with details.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. For example: the dance is about a group, like friends or a community, being split apart by a shock; the crack and jerks are the break, the reaches show the wish to reconnect, and the return to the square facing away suggests they are together again but changed, or closed off from the world.
write: **Evaluate.** Judge the excerpt using 2 named criteria, with evidence for each.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names criteria (clarity of idea, use of a motif, contrast, design of light and sound, structure) and cites evidence, for example "The motif is used skillfully: the same jerk appears in canon, in solos and in reverse, so the dance feels unified. The ending is less effective for me, because facing upstage hides the dancers' faces just when the audience wants to see if they have reconciled."

## Stretch
write: Which stage of the critique was hardest to write, and why? What would help you next time?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Many students find analyze hardest because it asks for relationships between parts rather than a list or an opinion; watching twice, once for motifs and once for structure, helps.

# 20. Issue Dance Plan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, the music you are considering (optional)
ican: I can plan the structure, cast, music and devices of a dance about an issue, with an intent statement.
directions: Answer the warm up questions about planning choices. Then plan your issue dance: write an intent statement, map the structure, cast the dancers, choose and justify the music, and place 3 choreographic devices.
mistakes: Students pick an issue too big to show in movement ("world peace"). Narrow it to one situation or relationship an audience can see. Music is often chosen for being popular rather than for fitting the intent; lyrics can also say the message for the dance instead of letting movement show it. Devices need a purpose, not just a name.
notes: Good classroom issues: belonging, kindness online, the environment, sharing public space, screen time, fairness. Avoid issues that would ask students to share personal or family experiences or to portray real trauma. Check that music is classroom appropriate and that any instrumental track's use follows your school's rules.

## Warm up: Planning choices
choice: Which issue is narrow enough to show clearly in a 3-minute dance?
  - every problem in the world
  * one student being left out at lunch until a classmate makes room
  - the history of all of society
choice: A dance shows pollution slowly choking a river. Which music fits the intent best?
  * a slow, low instrumental track that grows thicker and darker
  - a cheerful pop song about summer
  - a march for a parade
choice: The choreographer wants the audience to feel a crowd turning against one person. Which device fits best?
  - retrograde of a solo
  - embellishment of a hand gesture
  * accumulation, as dancers join a pointing gesture one at a time

## Main: The plan
write: **Intent statement:** the issue, the audience, the mood and how movement will show it.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary, for example "Our dance is about plastic waste in the ocean, for middle school students at the spring showcase. The mood moves from calm to suffocating to hopeful. Flowing unison waves are slowly broken by stiff, tangled shapes, until dancers lift each other free."

# 21. Formations as Meaning
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils, open floor space
ican: I can choose stage formations that communicate ideas about an issue.
directions: Name the issue your dance is about. Answer the warm up questions. Then map 6 formations on the stage diagrams, using X for each dancer and an arrow for the way they face, and explain what each one says about your issue.
mistakes: Students draw the formation but explain only its shape ("it is a circle"). The explanation must connect the shape to the issue ("the closed circle shows a group that keeps others out"). Facing matters as much as placement: a circle facing in and a circle facing out mean different things.
notes: Good classroom issues include belonging, friendship, the environment, screen time or kindness. Avoid issues that would ask students to share personal experiences. Have groups test each formation on the floor and look at it from the audience side before drawing. Six dancers per formation is a manageable default.

## Warm up: What does it say?
choice: Which formation most clearly shows isolation?
  - all dancers in one straight line facing the audience
  * one dancer downstage right while the rest cluster upstage left, facing away
  - all dancers in a circle facing in
choice: Two lines of dancers face each other across the stage and step toward each other. This most likely shows
  - unity
  - rest
  * conflict or confrontation
choice: A scattered group slowly gathers into one tight circle facing in. This most likely shows
  * coming together, or unity
  - running away
  - isolation

## Main: Map 6 formations
text: My issue: ________________________________________
draw: 1. Isolation (back of stage at the top) | 1.3
write: What does formation 1 communicate about your issue?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; the explanation must connect the formation to the issue, for example "One dancer far from the group shows a student who feels left out."
draw: 2. Unity | 1.3
write: What does formation 2 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example "A tight circle facing in shows the group finally including everyone."
draw: 3. Conflict | 1.3
write: What does formation 3 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example "Two lines facing off show two sides of the argument."
draw: 4. My choice | 1.3
write: What does formation 4 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the formation (diagonal, V, cluster, scattered) and its meaning.
draw: 5. My choice | 1.3
write: What does formation 5 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the formation and its meaning.
draw: 6. Ending | 1.3
write: What does your ending formation communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; the ending should resolve or comment on the issue, for example a circle that opens to face the audience to invite them in.

## Stretch
write: Put your 6 formations in order. How does the sequence of formations tell a story about your issue?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer shows change over time, for example isolation, then conflict, then unity, so the audience sees a problem and how it is resolved.

# 33. Permissions and Safety Plan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, site photos or a site visit
ican: I can plan the permissions, safety checks, weather backup and supervision a site performance needs.
directions: Answer the warm up questions. Then complete the plan for your site performance: who must give permission, what to check for safety, the weather backup and who supervises. Finish with a final check.
mistakes: Students plan permission only from the school and forget the site's owner or manager (a park department, a library, a store owner). Safety checks are often vague ("make sure it is safe"); each check should name a hazard and what to do about it. A weather backup needs a real place and a decision time, not just "move inside."
notes: Use a site on or near school grounds that students can visit. School field trip and facilities rules come first; check with your administration about what permissions are needed. Public places can need a permit; students should draft requests, but adults submit them. Never plan a performance in traffic, on roofs, near water without lifeguards, or anywhere that blocks exits.

## Warm up: What could go wrong?
choice: Your group wants to perform on the front steps of the public library. Who must give permission besides your school?
  - the audience
  - the other dancers
  * the library's manager or the city office in charge of the building
choice: Which is a real safety check for an outdoor plaza?
  * Walk the surface for cracks, gravel and wet spots and mark any area to avoid.
  - Make sure everyone feels excited.
  - Choose costumes in matching colors.
choice: Which weather backup is best?
  - Hope it does not rain.
  - Dance anyway, even on a wet surface.
  * Decide by 9 a.m.; if rain is forecast, perform in the gym at the same time.

  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary; a strong backup names an indoor space, a decision time and who decides, for example "If the forecast at 8 a.m. shows rain, our teacher moves the show to the cafeteria at the same time, and we post a sign at the plaza."
write: **Supervision:** Which adults will be there, and what is each one responsible for?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary, for example "Ms. A, our teacher, leads and holds the first aid kit; a second teacher manages the audience area and passersby; a parent volunteer stays with dancers who are waiting."
note: The warm up answers are marked. For the plan, check that every hazard has a specific fix, that the site owner is included in permissions, and that supervision names at least two adults with clear jobs.

## Stretch
write: The day before, you learn the site will be busy with a farmers market. What two changes would you make to your plan?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary, for example "Move the dance to a corner away from the stalls and mark a clear audience area with cones; add an adult to manage the crowd and keep a path open for shoppers and emergency exits; ask the market manager for permission too."
