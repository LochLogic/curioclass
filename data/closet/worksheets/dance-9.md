# 1. Six Styles Survey
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, video clips or live demonstrations chosen by the teacher
ican: I can compare dance styles by technique, music, costume and origins.
directions: Match each style to a fact about it. Then fill in the survey chart for all six styles, choosing one cultural or social dance form for the last row. Finish by comparing two styles.
mistakes: Students describe costumes or music but skip technique: technique is how the body is trained to move (turnout, isolations, contraction). Origins answers are often just "America": push for who created the form, where and roughly when. Watch for stereotyped descriptions of cultural forms; describe what dancers do, not what a group is "like."
notes: Show a short clip or demonstration of each style before students fill the chart. For the sixth row, a form with a strong local community connection works well (for example, Mexican folklórico, Irish step dance, West African dance, K-pop dance, Bhangra or a Native American social dance shown with the community's permission). Keys give the main facts; accept other accurate details.

## Warm up: Match the style
match: Match each style to a fact about its technique or origins.
  - ballet = turnout and pointe work; codified at the French court of Louis XIV in the 1600s
  - modern = bare feet, weight and the floor; a break from ballet in the early 1900s
  - jazz = isolations and syncopation; grew from African American vernacular dance with jazz music
  - tap = metal plates on the shoes make the dancer a percussionist
  - hip hop = breaking began at block parties in the Bronx, New York, in the 1970s

## Main: Survey chart
text: Write one style in each row, in this order: ballet, modern, jazz, tap, hip hop, then your choice.
chart: Style | Technique (how the body moves) | Music | Costume | Origins (who, where, when)
  rows: 6
write: Which fact from your chart surprised you most? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Chart key: Ballet: turnout, pointed feet, lifted posture, pointe work; orchestral or classical music; tutus, tights, pointe or ballet slippers; Italian Renaissance courts, developed and codified in France (Louis XIV founded the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661). Modern: contraction and release, fall and recovery, use of weight and the floor; varied music, new commissions or silence; simple costumes, bare feet; early 1900s United States and Germany (Isadora Duncan, Martha Graham, Doris Humphrey). Jazz: isolations, syncopation, low center, high energy; jazz then popular and show music; jazz shoes, varied stage costumes; African American vernacular dance, developed through Broadway and film. Tap: rhythms made by the feet, heel and toe sounds, improvisation; jazz music or no music; tap shoes; 1800s United States, blending African rhythmic traditions with Irish and British step dance. Hip hop: breaking, popping, locking, grooves, freestyle in cyphers; DJ breakbeats and rap; streetwear and sneakers; 1970s Black and Latino youth in the Bronx, with funk styles from California. Surprise answers vary.

## Compare
write: Choose two styles from your chart. Explain one way they are alike and one way they are different.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Tap and hip hop both grew from African American communities and value improvisation. They differ in how they make rhythm: tap dancers create sound with their shoes, while hip hop dancers move to a DJ's beat.

## Stretch
write: Many styles borrow from each other. Give one example of a style that mixes influences from at least two others.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Examples: contemporary dance mixes ballet and modern; jazz-funk mixes jazz and hip hop; tap grew from African and Irish traditions; lyrical mixes ballet and jazz.

# 21. Formations as Meaning
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils, open floor space
ican: I can choose stage formations that communicate ideas about an issue.
directions: Name the issue your dance is about. Answer the warm up questions. Then map 6 formations on the stage diagrams, using X for each dancer and an arrow for the way they face, and explain what each one says about your issue.
mistakes: Students draw the formation but explain only its shape ("it is a circle"). The explanation must connect the shape to the issue ("the closed circle shows a group that keeps others out"). Facing matters as much as placement: a circle facing in and a circle facing out mean different things.
notes: Good classroom issues include belonging, friendship, the environment, screen time or kindness. Avoid issues that would ask students to share personal experiences. Have groups test each formation on the floor and look at it from the audience side before drawing. Six dancers per formation is a manageable default.

## Warm up: What does it say?
choice: Which formation most clearly shows isolation?
  - all dancers in one straight line facing the audience
  * one dancer downstage right while the rest cluster upstage left, facing away
  - all dancers in a circle facing in
choice: Two lines of dancers face each other across the stage and step toward each other. This most likely shows
  - unity
  - rest
  * conflict or confrontation
choice: A scattered group slowly gathers into one tight circle facing in. This most likely shows
  * coming together, or unity
  - running away
  - isolation

## Main: Map 6 formations
text: My issue: ________________________________________
draw: 1. Isolation (back of stage at the top) | 1.3
write: What does formation 1 communicate about your issue?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; the explanation must connect the formation to the issue, for example "One dancer far from the group shows a student who feels left out."
draw: 2. Unity | 1.3
write: What does formation 2 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example "A tight circle facing in shows the group finally including everyone."
draw: 3. Conflict | 1.3
write: What does formation 3 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary, for example "Two lines facing off show two sides of the argument."
draw: 4. My choice | 1.3
write: What does formation 4 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the formation (diagonal, V, cluster, scattered) and its meaning.
draw: 5. My choice | 1.3
write: What does formation 5 communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names the formation and its meaning.
draw: 6. Ending | 1.3
write: What does your ending formation communicate?
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary; the ending should resolve or comment on the issue, for example a circle that opens to face the audience to invite them in.

## Stretch
write: Put your 6 formations in order. How does the sequence of formations tell a story about your issue?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer shows change over time, for example isolation, then conflict, then unity, so the audience sees a problem and how it is resolved.
