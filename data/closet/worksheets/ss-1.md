# 1. Big Question, Small Questions Kite
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can ask small questions that help answer a big question.
directions: (Read aloud.) Our big question is on the kite: How do people get food? A big question needs small questions to help answer it. Draw or tell 3 small questions for the kite's tail.
mistakes: Children often ask questions that are about food but do not help answer the big question ("What is your favorite food?"). Ask: "If we found the answer, would it help us know how people get food?" Some children give an answer ("From the store!") instead of a question; turn it into one together: "Where do stores get food?"
notes: Do the warm up together, reading each question aloud and letting children vote with a thumb. Children who are not writing yet can draw each small question (a farm, a truck, a store) and dictate the words to you. Keep the class kite questions on chart paper; they guide the rest of the Wonder Kite module.

## Warm up: Does it help?
picword:
  - kite | How do people get food?
choice: (Read aloud.) Which small question helps answer the big question?
  - What color is your lunch box?
  * Where do farmers grow food?
choice: (Read aloud.) Which small question helps answer the big question?
  * How does food get to the store?
  - Do you like to fly a kite?

## Main: The kite's tail
draw: Tail bow 1: draw your small question. | 1.4
write: Tell or write tail question 1.
  lines: 1
  key: Any question that helps answer the big question, such as "Who grows our food?"
draw: Tail bow 2: draw your small question. | 1.4
write: Tell or write tail question 2.
  lines: 1
  key: For example, "How do trucks bring food to the store?"
draw: Tail bow 3: draw your small question. | 1.4
write: Tell or write tail question 3.
  lines: 1
  key: For example, "How do people pay for food?" or "How did people get food long ago?"

## Stretch: Who could help?
write: (Read aloud.) Who could we ask to answer one of our small questions?
  lines: 1
  key: A farmer, a grocery store worker, a truck driver, a cook, a librarian or a book about farms.

# 5. Many Kinds of Families
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can tell how people in different families help each other.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the stories about four storybook families. Every family is different. Then draw a line from each family to one way its members help each other.
mistakes: Children may match by the animal they like or by family size ("big family, big job"). Reread the one sentence that tells how each family helps. Some say a family "is not a real family" because it is small or has a grandpa in charge; restate the big idea: a family is the people who love and care for each other, and families come in many sizes and shapes.
notes: The four families are made-up animal families, so no child is asked about their own home. Read each story two times and pause after the helping sentence. The families show different shapes on purpose: one parent and one child, a grandparent raising grandchildren, two parents with two children, and a big family of aunts, uncles and cousins.

## Warm up: Meet the families
picword:
  - owl | Owl family
  - frog | Frog family
  - cat | Cat family
  - bird | Bird family
text: (Read aloud.) **The Owl family.** Mama Owl and her owlet, Hoot, live in a tall oak tree. It is just the two of them. Every night, Hoot helps Mama Owl tidy their nest.
text: (Read aloud.) **The Frog family.** Grandpa Frog takes care of three little froglets by the pond. Grandpa cooks bug soup, and the froglets set the table.
text: (Read aloud.) **The Cat family.** Papa Cat and Mama Cat live with big sister Mimi and baby Tam. At bedtime, Mimi reads a story to baby Tam.
text: (Read aloud.) **The Bird family.** Aunts, uncles and lots of cousins all live in one big tree. After a storm, everyone works together to fix the nests.

## Main: How do they help?
match: (Read aloud.) Draw a line from each family to how they help each other.
  - Owl family = tidy the nest
  - Frog family = set the table
  - Cat family = read a bedtime story
  - Bird family = fix the nests after a storm
choice: (Read aloud.) Which family is the smallest?
  * the Owl family
  - the Bird family
choice: (Read aloud.) Who takes care of the froglets?
  - Mama Frog
  * Grandpa Frog

## Stretch: One more way to help
draw: Draw one more way people in a family can help each other. | 1.8
note: Any helping act: feeding a pet, carrying groceries, helping a little one get dressed, watering plants, giving a hug.

# 16. Which Ocean Is My Home?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick, a globe or world map
ican: I can name the five oceans and find where they are on a map.
directions: (Read aloud.) Each animal card tells where it lives. Listen to each clue and find that ocean on our globe. Write the ocean's name on the clue card. Then cut out the 5 animal pictures and glue each one under its ocean.
mistakes: Children mix up the Arctic (top of the world) and the Southern Ocean (around Antarctica, at the bottom), because both are icy. Put one finger on the North Pole and one on the South Pole. The Indian and Pacific Oceans both touch Australia; the clue's other places (Africa, Hawaii) decide it.
notes: Keep a globe or world map in sight the whole time; the sort columns stand in for the map. Facts behind the clues: walruses live in the Arctic; emperor penguins live only around Antarctica; Atlantic puffins nest on North Atlantic coasts such as Iceland, Norway, Newfoundland and Maine; green sea turtles live around Hawaii in the Pacific; dugongs graze on sea grass in warm, shallow water from East Africa across the Indian Ocean to Australia (they also reach the western Pacific). Children who cannot write the names yet can point on the globe while you write.

## Warm up: Five oceans
choice: (Read aloud.) Which ocean is the biggest?
  - the Arctic Ocean
  * the Pacific Ocean
choice: (Read aloud.) Which ocean is at the top of the world, around the North Pole?
  * the Arctic Ocean
  - the Indian Ocean

## Main: Read the clue cards
picword:
  - walrus | walrus
  - penguin | penguin
  - puffin | puffin
  - sea-turtle | sea turtle
  - dugong | dugong
cards: (Read aloud.) Clue cards: write the ocean.
  - Walrus: I swim under thick ice at the top of the world, near the North Pole. {Arctic Ocean}
  - Penguin: I swim in the icy ocean all around Antarctica, at the bottom of the world. {Southern Ocean}
  - Puffin: I fly over the ocean between North America and Europe. {Atlantic Ocean}
  - Sea turtle: I swim near Hawaii, in the biggest ocean of all. {Pacific Ocean}
  - Dugong: I eat sea grass in the warm ocean between Africa, Asia and Australia. {Indian Ocean}

## Glue: Who lives where?
sort: Glue each animal under its ocean.
  - Arctic: icon:walrus
  - Atlantic: icon:puffin
  - Pacific: icon:sea-turtle
  - Indian: icon:dugong
  - Southern: icon:penguin

# 22. Hammy the Hamster's First Year
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can read a timeline and tell what happened before and after.
directions: (Read aloud.) This timeline shows Hammy the class hamster's first year. Read it from left to right. Then listen to each question and circle the answer.
mistakes: Children may read the timeline as a set of separate pictures. Run a finger along it left to right and say "first... next... then..." together. "Before" and "after" get mixed up: anchor with a known pair, "We eat lunch before we go home."
notes: Hammy is a made-up class pet, so the sheet needs no family or personal history. The answer words match the words under the pictures; children who are not reading yet can find the matching picture and circle the word next to it with your help. The timeline reads left to right, starting in June.

## Warm up: Read the timeline
text: (Read aloud.) Hammy's first year, from June to the next June. Put your finger on June. Slide to the right, then go down to the next row, just like reading a book.
picword:
  - hamster | June: Hammy is born
  - school | August: Hammy comes to our class
  - wheel | October: Hammy gets a wheel
  - book | January: we read a hamster book
  - cake | June: Hammy is 1!

## Main: Before or after?
choice: (Read aloud.) What happened first?
  * Hammy is born
  - Hammy gets a wheel
choice: (Read aloud.) Did Hammy come to our class before or after he was born?
  - before
  * after
choice: (Read aloud.) What happened right after Hammy came to our class?
  * Hammy gets a wheel
  - Hammy is 1!
choice: (Read aloud.) Did we read the hamster book before or after Hammy got his wheel?
  - before

  * Hammy is 1!

# 26. Class Museum Card
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons, the class collection of old objects
ican: I can use an old object as a clue about life long ago.
directions: (Read aloud.) Museums put a card next to each object. Choose one old object from our class collection. Draw it. Then fill in its museum card: its name, about how old it is, and what people used it for.
mistakes: Children often guess the age from how dirty or broken an object looks, or write "100 years" for everything. Model the clues that really help: a date stamped on it, the label, or what a grown-up who knows tells us. "Used for" answers often name what it looks like ("it is metal") instead of the job it did; ask "What did people do with it?"
notes: Build the collection ahead of time from school or donated items (an old key, a rotary phone, a film camera, a hand eggbeater, a typewriter, a record). Write each object's name and rough age on a sticky note on the table so children can copy them. The example card is a washboard, used for scrubbing clothes by hand; many homes used them about 100 years ago, before washing machines were common. No family heirlooms or personal history are needed.

## Warm up: Read a museum card
example: **Name:** washboard
  **About how old:** about 100 years old
  **Used for:** scrubbing clothes clean by hand, before most homes had washing machines
choice: (Read aloud.) What is the washboard on the card used for?
  - cooking soup
  * washing clothes
choice: (Read aloud.) Which clue best tells us how old an object is?
  * a date printed on it
  - its color

## Main: My museum card
draw: Draw your old object. | 2.4
write: **Name:** This object is called
  lines: 1
  key: For example, "a rotary phone."
write: **About how old:** It is about this old:
  lines: 1
  key: For example, "about 50 years old." Accept any age the label or a grown-up gave.
write: **Used for:** People used it to
  lines: 2
  key: For example, "call people on the telephone. You turned the dial for each number."

## Stretch: Then and now
write: What do people use today instead of your object?
  starter: Today people use
  lines: 1
  key: For example, "a cell phone" for a rotary phone, "a washing machine" for a washboard, "a digital camera or a phone" for a film camera.

# 37. Lights, Lanterns and Flowers
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can tell about traditions from cultures around the world.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to three short stories about holidays people celebrate around the world. Then match each holiday to its picture and to one of its traditions.
mistakes: Children may match by color or by which holiday they know. Reread the one sentence with the picture word ("lamps," "lanterns," "flowers"). Some think Dia de los Muertos is scary because of its name; it is a loving time to remember family members, with bright flowers and favorite foods.
notes: Each holiday is celebrated in many ways; the passages give common traditions, not the only ones. Diwali is the festival of lights for Hindus, Sikhs and Jains, in October or November. Lunar New Year begins with the new moon between late January and mid February and is celebrated in China, Korea, Vietnam and many other places. Dia de los Muertos is November 1 and 2. If children in the class celebrate one of these, invite (never require) them to share.

## Warm up: Listen
passage: Diwali
  Diwali is a festival of lights. Hindu, Sikh and Jain families around the world celebrate it in the fall. People light rows of small clay lamps called diyas. They make bright patterns on the floor with colored powder, called rangoli. Families share sweets and wear new clothes.
passage: Lunar New Year
  Lunar New Year starts with a new moon in January or February. Families in China, Korea, Vietnam and many other places celebrate. Many homes hang red lanterns and clean up for a fresh start. Children may get red envelopes with money inside. Families share a big dinner together.
passage: Dia de los Muertos
  Dia de los Muertos means Day of the Dead. Families in Mexico and many other places celebrate it on November 1 and 2. It is a happy time to remember family members who have died. Families make an ofrenda, a table with photos, favorite foods and orange marigold flowers.

## Main: Match
picword:
  - diya | clay lamp
  - lantern | red lantern
  - flower | marigold
match: (Read aloud.) Match each holiday to its picture.
  - Diwali = clay lamp
  - Lunar New Year = red lantern
  - Dia de los Muertos = marigold
match: (Read aloud.) Match each holiday to one tradition.
  - Diwali = patterns made with colored powder
  - Lunar New Year = red envelopes with money inside
  - Dia de los Muertos = a table with photos to remember family

## Stretch: Learn more
write: (Read aloud.) Which holiday would you like to learn more about? Tell or write one question you have.
  lines: 2
  key: Any real question, such as "Why are the envelopes red?" (red stands for good luck) or "What foods go on an ofrenda?"
