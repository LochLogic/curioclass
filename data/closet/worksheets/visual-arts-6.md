# 1. Three Kinds of Balance
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, compass or round lid, colored pencils
ican: I can create symmetrical, asymmetrical and radial balance and mark the axis or center of each.
directions: Warm up by finding lines of symmetry. Then draw one design for each kind of balance. Mark the axis with a dashed line on the symmetrical design, show what balances what on the asymmetrical one, and mark the center point on the radial one. Answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often make asymmetrical designs that are simply lopsided. Asymmetrical balance still feels even: one large, light or plain shape is balanced by several small, dark or busy ones. Radial designs need a clear center that everything spreads out from, not just a circle with random shapes inside.
notes: For asymmetrical balance, the seesaw comparison helps: a small child far from the middle can balance an adult close to it. Show these public-domain examples if you can: Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper (symmetrical, with Jesus at the center), the north rose window of Notre-Dame de Paris (radial), and Hokusai's The Great Wave (asymmetrical: the huge wave on the left is balanced by the open sky and small Mount Fuji on the right).

## Warm up: Find the axis
symmetry: Draw every line of symmetry. Write how many.
  - heart
  - kite
  - regular hexagon
  - scalene triangle

## Main: Make three designs
example: **Symmetrical:** both sides of an axis are mirror images. **Asymmetrical:** the sides are different but feel equal in visual weight. **Radial:** parts spread out from a center point, like spokes on a wheel.
draw: Symmetrical design: draw the axis as a dashed line | 2.3
draw: Asymmetrical design: label what balances what | 2.3
draw: Radial design: mark the center with a dot | 2.3

## Name the balance
text: Write symmetrical, asymmetrical or radial.
fill:
  - A bicycle wheel: {radial}
  - A big tree on the left and three small birds on the right: {asymmetrical}
  - A butterfly with matching wings: {symmetrical}
  - A sliced orange seen from the end: {radial}
choice: Leonardo da Vinci's The Last Supper places Jesus in the exact middle with apostles grouped on both sides. What kind of balance is this?
  * symmetrical
  - asymmetrical
  - radial
choice: A round rose window in a cathedral has patterns spreading out from its center. What kind of balance is this?
  - symmetrical
  - asymmetrical
  * radial

## Stretch
write: In your asymmetrical design, what made the two sides feel balanced? Name at least two tools (size, color, value, detail, placement).
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers name trade-offs, such as one large pale shape balanced by several small dark shapes, or a big empty area balanced by a small busy area of detail.

# 5. Rhythm Strips
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, fine black marker
ican: I can create regular, alternating and progressive rhythm with repeated shapes and lines.
directions: Read the example. Finish each pattern in the warm up. Then design three strips of your own, one for each kind of rhythm, using shapes and lines.
mistakes: Students often make a progressive rhythm that jumps around (small, big, small). In a progressive rhythm the change goes one way, one step at a time: each shape a little bigger, darker, wider or closer than the last. In an alternating rhythm, check that the turns really repeat: A B A B, or A B B A B B.
notes: Rhythm is a principle of design: repeated elements make the eye move through a work, the way beats move through music. Real-world examples to show: a picket fence or a row of columns (regular), a checkerboard or brick wall with two colors (alternating), a spiral shell or the Russian nesting dolls in a row (progressive). Strips can be cut out and joined to make a class border.

## Warm up: Finish the pattern
example: **Regular rhythm:** the same element with the same spacing, again and again. **Alternating rhythm:** two or more elements take turns. **Progressive rhythm:** the element changes a little each time, in one direction.

  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: Each triangle is a little taller than the one before, and the lines between them get thicker, so the strip seems to grow from left to right.

## Stretch
write: Which strip feels the calmest, and which feels the most like it is moving? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Usually the regular strip feels calmest because nothing changes, like a steady beat; the progressive strip feels most like it is moving or growing because each step pulls the eye to the next.

# 15. Hallway in One Point
pairs: vanishing-point-studio
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, ruler, eraser, drawing paper (or use the box below)
ican: I can use a horizon line and one vanishing point to draw a hallway with doors, lockers and floor tiles.
directions: Answer the warm up questions. Then follow the steps in order to draw a school hallway in one-point perspective. Use a ruler for every line, draw lightly, and check the list when you finish.
mistakes: The most common mistake is drawing the tops of doors and lockers flat (horizontal) on the side walls. On a side wall, the top and bottom edges of doors go toward the vanishing point; only the vertical edges stay straight up and down. On the floor, the tile lines going back aim at the vanishing point, and the lines going across stay horizontal and get closer together farther back.
notes: The Vanishing Point Studio tool's Find it mode has a hallway scene: trace its edges on the projector before students draw. Students who finish early can add a person (bigger near, smaller far, with eyes on the horizon line if the viewer is standing) or a trophy case. A real hallway photo works as reference too.

## Warm up: Rules of one point
choice: In one-point perspective, where do the lines of the floor that go back into the distance meet?
  * at the vanishing point
  - at the bottom corners of the page
  - they never meet
  - at the top of the nearest door
choice: On a side wall, the vertical edges of a door should be drawn...
  - slanted toward the vanishing point
  * straight up and down
  - curved
  - left out
choice: Floor tile lines that go straight across the hall (side to side) should be...
  - aimed at the vanishing point
  - slanted down to the right
  * horizontal, and closer together the farther back they are
  - all the same distance apart

## Main: Draw the hallway
example: **Steps** (draw lightly with a ruler)
  1. Draw a small rectangle in the middle of the box. This is the far end of the hallway.
  2. Put a dot inside the rectangle, a little below its center. This is the vanishing point. Draw the horizon line through it.
  3. Draw a line from the vanishing point through each corner of the rectangle out to the edge of the box. Now you have a floor, a ceiling and two walls.
  4. Doors: on one wall, draw a tall vertical line, then a shorter one farther back. Connect their tops, and their bottoms, with lines aimed at the vanishing point.
  5. Lockers: on the other wall, draw the top and bottom of the locker row as lines aimed at the vanishing point. Add vertical lines for each locker, closer together as they go back.
  6. Floor tiles: draw lines from the vanishing point down to the bottom edge. Then draw horizontal lines across the floor, closer together near the back wall.
  7. Ceiling lights: draw rectangles whose side edges aim at the vanishing point.
  8. Erase the guidelines you do not need. Darken your final lines and shade one wall.
draw: My hallway | 4.6

## Check your drawing
table: Check | Yes or not yet
  - All lines that go back into the distance meet at one vanishing point. |
  - Vertical edges (doors, lockers) are straight up and down. |
  - Doors and lockers get smaller as they go back. |
  - Floor tiles get thinner toward the back wall. |
  - Guidelines are erased. |

## Stretch
write: Where is the viewer's eye level in your drawing, and how can you tell?
  lines: 2
  key: The eye level is the horizon line, which runs through the vanishing point. Anything above it (the ceiling, the tops of doors) slopes down toward it, and anything below it (the floor) slopes up toward it.

# 21. Caves and Carvings
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; the teacher shows images of Lascaux, Chauvet and the Venus of Willendorf
ican: I can study prehistoric art and use evidence to explain how and why it may have been made.
directions: Read the fact cards. Look carefully at the images your teacher shows. Answer the questions using the facts and what you see. Where scientists are not sure, say so.
mistakes: Students often state one purpose as fact ("it was for hunting magic"). Nobody left writing about these works, so every purpose is an idea supported by evidence. Students also mix up the sites: Lascaux and Chauvet are painted caves in France; the Venus of Willendorf is a small carved figure found in Austria.
notes: Show cave images from both sites: Lascaux's Hall of the Bulls (horses, aurochs, deer) and Chauvet's lions and rhinoceroses or its horse panel. Photographs of the paintings are widely available from the French Ministry of Culture sites; for the Venus of Willendorf use an openly licensed photo (Wikimedia Commons has several; check the license). Dates are approximate and are revised as dating methods improve. The figure is shown without clothing; present it plainly as an ancient carving, as museums do.

## Read: Fact cards
table: Work | Where | About how old | Made with
  - Lascaux cave paintings | southwestern France, found in 1940 | about 17,000 years | mineral pigments (red and yellow ochre, black charcoal and manganese), brushes, pads, sprayed paint, stone lamps, scaffolds in high places
  - Chauvet cave paintings | southern France, found in 1994 | more than 30,000 years | charcoal and red ochre, fingers, scraped and smoothed walls, bumps of the rock used for shape
  - Venus of Willendorf | Austria, found in 1908 | about 25,000 to 30,000 years | carved from limestone, about 11 cm (4.4 inches) tall, once colored with red ochre

## Warm up
fill:
  - The oldest of the three works are the {Chauvet} cave paintings.
  - The Venus of Willendorf is a {sculpture} (carving), not a painting.
  - Red and yellow paint came from a colored earth called {ochre}.
choice: The caves were very dark. What evidence shows how artists could see?
  - Electric lights were found.
  * Stone lamps that burned animal fat were found.
  - The paintings glow in the dark.
  - The caves had windows.

## Main: Look and think
write: Look at the cave animals. Name three kinds of animals you see. What kind of thing is almost never painted?
  lines: 2
  key: Horses, aurochs (wild cattle), bison, deer, mammoths, lions, rhinoceroses, bears. People are rarely painted, and plants and landscapes almost never appear.
write: At Chauvet, artists used the bumps and curves of the rock wall. Why would that make an animal look more real?
  lines: 2
  key: The bulges give the animal a rounded, three-dimensional body, like a belly or shoulder, and in flickering lamplight the shapes seem to move.
write: Paintings were found high on walls and ceilings. What does that tell you about the work it took?
  lines: 2
  key: Artists needed planning and teamwork: they built scaffolds or climbed, carried lamps and mixed pigments, so the art mattered enough to take real effort.
write: Give two ideas scientists have about why people painted the caves. Which one do the facts support best, and why?
  lines: 4
  key: Ideas include: ceremonies or spiritual beliefs, hoping for a good hunt, teaching or storytelling about animals, marking a special place. Any choice supported by evidence works, for example the paintings are deep in dark caves where people did not live, which suggests a special or ceremonial use.

## Stretch
write: The Venus of Willendorf fits in your hand. Why might someone make a figure small enough to carry? What is one thing we do not know about it?
  lines: 3
  key: It could be carried or held: a good-luck charm, a symbol of health and new life, or a personal object. We do not know who made it, what it was called or exactly what it meant.

# 25. The Terracotta Army
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain the scale, production and purpose of an ancient artwork.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight numbers that show scale in one color or with a box, and steps of how the figures were made with underlining. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think every soldier was sculpted from one lump of clay by one artist. The passage explains a workshop system: parts from molds, assembled, then finished by hand. Also watch for "the army fought battles": it was buried to guard the emperor after death.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 320 words). Show public-domain or museum photos of the pits and of single warriors (faces, a kneeling archer, the bronze chariots). The figures date from the reign of Qin Shi Huang, who became king of Qin in 246 BCE, unified China in 221 BCE and died in 210 BCE. The figure of 700,000 workers comes from the historian Sima Qian, writing about a century later, and covers the whole tomb, not only the army. The emperor's burial mound itself has not been excavated.

## Read
passage: An Army Made of Clay | numbered
  In 1974, farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an, in China, struck pieces of baked clay. They had found the edge of one of the greatest discoveries in archaeology: an underground army of clay soldiers, more than 2,200 years old.
  The army was made for Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of a united China. He became king of the state of Qin at age 13, in 246 BCE, and work on his tomb began soon after. By 221 BCE he had conquered the other states and called himself the First Emperor. He died in 210 BCE.
  The scale is enormous. Archaeologists estimate that three pits hold more than 8,000 soldiers, plus about 130 chariots and hundreds of horses, and most are still underground. The soldiers are life-size, and the generals are the tallest. They stand in battle order: archers, foot soldiers, chariot drivers and officers. Many once held real bronze weapons.
  How could anyone make so many? Workshops used an assembly system. Legs, bodies, arms and heads were shaped separately, often pressed in molds, and then joined. Next, workers added clay by hand to shape faces, hair, armor and shoes, so no two faces are exactly alike. The figures were fired in kilns and then painted in bright colors. Many carry the stamped or scratched names of the workshops or foremen who made them, which let officials check the quality of each piece.
  Why build a clay army at all? Like many rulers, the emperor prepared for life after death. The army was buried about a mile east of his burial mound, facing east, to protect him forever. An ancient historian wrote that 700,000 workers labored on the whole tomb.
  Today, the original colors are mostly gone. When the figures are dug up, the paint can curl and flake within minutes in the dry air, so archaeologists dig slowly.

## Questions
choice: About how many soldiers do archaeologists estimate are in the three pits?
  - about 800
  - about 2,200
  * more than 8,000
  - exactly 700,000
fill:
  - The army was made for {Qin Shi Huang}, the first emperor of a united China.
  - Parts of the figures were often pressed in {molds} and then joined.
choice: Why are no two faces exactly alike?
  * Workers added and shaped clay by hand after the parts were joined.
  - Each soldier was a portrait made from a living person.
  - The kiln melted each face differently.
  - The faces were carved from different kinds of stone.
choice: What was the main purpose of the army?
  - to train real soldiers for battle
  - to scare away farmers
  * to protect the emperor in the afterlife
  - to show off in a public parade
write: Workshop and foreman names appear on many figures. What does this tell you about how the work was organized? Cite the paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: Paragraph 4: the work was organized like a big system of workshops, each responsible for its pieces; names let officials trace who made each figure and check quality, so workers were held accountable.
write: Scale is part of an artwork's meaning. What does the huge size of this army say about the emperor?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows his enormous power and wealth: he could command thousands of workers and huge amounts of material, and he expected to rule and be protected even after death.

## Stretch
write: Mass production with molds made the army possible. Name one thing that was gained and one thing that might have been lost by making art this way.
  lines: 3
  key: Gained: speed, huge numbers and a consistent size and quality. Possibly lost: the freedom of one artist to design a whole figure; but the hand-finished faces show the makers kept individuality anyway.

# 35. Mural Theme Web
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can brainstorm mural themes and choose one that fits a community.
directions: Answer the warm up questions. Build a theme web for a school mural, with at least 6 themes and pictures for each. Then score your 3 favorite themes and rank them.
mistakes: Students often pick a theme they personally love (a favorite game or team) that does not speak to the whole school. Push them to ask who walks past the wall every day. Also watch for themes that can only be shown with words; a mural theme needs images.
notes: Public murals are often made with the community they serve: artists hold meetings, listen and collect ideas before designing. A famous example is the Wall of Respect (1967) on Chicago's South Side, painted by artists of the Organization of Black American Culture to honor Black heroes chosen with the neighborhood. Keep themes positive and inclusive; avoid any theme that singles out a student or family.
