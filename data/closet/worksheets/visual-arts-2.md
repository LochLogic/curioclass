# 1. Texture Rubbing Collection
minutes: 35
materials: thin paper (copy paper works), peeled crayons, tape, pencil
ican: I can find textures, make rubbings of them and name each texture.
directions: (Read aloud.) First, name each drawn texture. Then find 6 textures in the room. Lay this paper on top, hold it still and rub the side of a peeled crayon over it. Write what you rubbed and choose a texture word for each one.
mistakes: Rubbing with the crayon tip makes scribbles: lay it flat on its side and rub in one direction. Smooth things make an almost blank rubbing, which is a real result, not a mistake. Students sometimes write the object ("table") in the texture column: the texture word describes how it feels.
notes: Good classroom spots: a sneaker sole (bumpy or ridged), a radiator or air vent (ridged), a coin (ridged edge, bumpy picture), a leaf (ridged veins), a brick or cement wall (rough), a plastic folder or tabletop (smooth), a basket or burlap (rough), a cork board (rough). Tape the paper down for students who struggle to hold it still. If rubbing on this page is hard, make rubbings on scrap paper and glue them in the boxes.

## Think about it
choice: Which texture makes the faintest rubbing?
  - ridged
  - bumpy
  * smooth
  - rough
write: Why does a bumpy or ridged thing show up best in a rubbing?
  lines: 2
  key: The crayon only touches the high parts, so the raised bumps and ridges get colored and the low parts stay white. A smooth thing has no high and low parts, so the color is even.

# 5. Mixing Chart
minutes: 40
materials: red, yellow and blue paint (tempera or watercolor), a brush, a water cup, paper towels, pencil
pairs: paint-mixer
ican: I can predict and mix the colors made by each pair of primary colors.
directions: (Read aloud.) First predict: write the color you think each pair will make. Then mix a tiny bit of each pair and paint it in the matching box of the paint chart. Rinse your brush between colors. Last, circle any box that surprised you.
mistakes: Dirty water and an unrinsed brush turn every mix brown: rinse and blot between each color. Children often mix half and half and get a dark result, because red and blue are stronger than yellow; start with the lighter color (yellow) and add a little of the darker one. The squares on the diagonal (red with red) do not make a new color, which surprises some children; it is a good thing to notice.
notes: Key: red + red = red; yellow + yellow = yellow; blue + blue = blue; red + yellow = orange; red + blue = violet (accept purple); yellow + blue = green. The chart is symmetric: red + yellow and yellow + red make the same orange, so students should notice that each new color appears twice. With classroom paint, red + blue often comes out dull or brownish; that is a real result, not a mistake, and a good thing to circle. The Paint Mixer tool shows the same mixes on the board if you want to compare predictions first.

## Warm up: The primaries
choice: (Read aloud.) Which three colors are the primary colors?
  - orange, green, violet
  - red, green, blue
  * red, yellow, blue
  - black, white, gray

## Main: Predict
text: (Read aloud.) Read across and down. Write the color you think each pair makes.
table: Mix | Red | Yellow | Blue
  - **Red** | {red} | {orange} | {violet}
  - **Yellow** | {orange} | {yellow} | {green}
  - **Blue** | {violet} | {green} | {blue}

## Main: Paint the chart
chart: Red | Yellow | Blue
  labels: Red; Yellow; Blue
  height: 0.9
note: Painted boxes vary. Check that the diagonal stays red, yellow and blue, and that each new color appears twice.

## Look and think
fill:
  - (Read aloud.) How many new colors did you make? {3}
  - (Read aloud.) Name them: {orange, green and violet}
write: (Read aloud.) Circle a box that surprised you. What happened, and why do you think so?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Common surprises: the violet came out dark or brownish, or the orange was more red than expected. A good answer tells what happened and a reason, such as "too much red" or "my brush was dirty."

# 15. Roman Mosaic Animal
minutes: 45
materials: colored paper squares (about 1/2 inch, pre-cut, or strips to cut), scissors, glue stick, pencil
ican: I can make a mosaic animal from small paper squares, like the ancient Romans made with tiny stones.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at the Roman floor mosaic your teacher shows. Answer the questions. Then sketch a simple animal outline in the big box. Glue paper squares along the outline first, then fill the inside and the background. Leave a tiny gap between squares.
mistakes: Children often glue squares edge to edge in rows like a checkerboard and lose the animal. Outline first, then fill: the squares follow the shape. Squares that overlap lose the mosaic look; a thin gap between pieces is like the grout lines in a real mosaic. A small, simple animal (a fish, a bird, a dog) works better than a big detailed one.
notes: Show a photo of a Roman floor mosaic. A good choice is the "Cave Canem" ("Beware of the dog") mosaic at the entrance of the House of the Tragic Poet in Pompeii: a black dog on a chain, made of tiny stone pieces. Pompeii was buried by the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 CE, which preserved many mosaics. Mosaic pieces are called tesserae (TESS-er-ee): small cubes of stone, glass or tile set in mortar. Romans put mosaics on floors because stone is hard-wearing and easy to wash, and they show patterns and pictures at the same time.

## Warm up: About mosaics
choice: (Read aloud.) What is a mosaic made of?
  - one big piece of paint
  * many small pieces put together to make a picture
  - a photograph
choice: (Read aloud.) Why did the Romans make mosaics from little stones on floors?
  - Stones are soft for sleeping on.
  - Stones melt in the sun.
  * Stone is strong, so people could walk on it for a long time.
fill:
  - (Read aloud.) The small pieces in a mosaic are called {tesserae}. (Word bank: tesserae, crayons)

## Main: Make the mosaic
text: (Read aloud.) Step 1: draw a big, simple animal outline. Step 2: glue squares along the outline. Step 3: fill the inside. Step 4: fill the background in a different color.
draw: My mosaic animal | 4.6

## Stretch: Be the museum guide
write: (Read aloud.) Tell how your mosaic is like a Roman mosaic and how it is different.
  lines: 2
  key: Alike: both make a picture from many small pieces with tiny gaps between them. Different: mine uses paper squares and glue; Roman mosaics use stone or glass pieces set in mortar on a floor, so they last for hundreds of years.

# 21. Moment Brainstorm
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can brainstorm everyday moments and choose the best one to tell as a story in art.
directions: (Read aloud.) Think of 4 everyday moments that would make a good picture. Sketch or write one in each box. Then answer the questions about each one. Put a big star on the box with the best story.
mistakes: Students often list things ("my dog," "pizza") instead of moments. A moment has an action and a place: "eating pizza at the table when the cheese stretched." Ask "What is happening?" Big events are fine, but small everyday moments often make the most interesting art.
notes: Moments can be real or made up, at school or anywhere. Nobody needs to share family details. Model your own four on the board: a game at recess, a windy walk, a messy lunch, a rainy day. The best story usually has something surprising, funny or exciting happen.

  * kicking the ball over the fence at recess
  - the weather
choice: Which one is a moment you could draw as a story?
  - a sandwich
  * my sandwich falling on the floor at lunch
  - a lunch box
  - a table

# 25. Four-Panel Comic Story
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, crayons or colored pencils
ican: I can tell a story in four comic panels with a beginning, middle and end.
directions: (Read aloud.) Plan a story about a fun moment, real or pretend. Write what happens and what someone says in each panel. Then draw the four panels. Put one speech bubble in each panel, with the words inside.
mistakes: Children often fill the bubble with too many words and run out of room: keep each bubble to a few words. Others draw the same picture four times; ask "What changes in this panel?" Draw the bubble first, then the people, so the bubble does not cover a face. The bubble's tail points to the person who is talking.
notes: The fun moment can be real or made up; nobody needs to share family details. Model a quick four-panel plan on the board: a kite gets stuck in a tree, a friend has an idea, they use a long stick, the kite flies again. Comic words: a panel is one box of the story, a speech bubble shows what a character says, a thought bubble (with little circles) shows what a character thinks, and a caption is a box with words that tell time or place.

## Warm up: Comic words
match: Match each comic word to what it does.
  - panel = one box of the story
  - speech bubble = shows what a character says
  - thought bubble = shows what a character thinks
  - caption = a box that tells when or where
choice: Which way does a speech bubble's tail point?
  - to the top of the page
  * to the character who is talking
  - to the next panel

## Plan
chart: What happens | What someone says
  labels: 1 Beginning; 2 Middle; 3 Middle; 4 End
  height: 0.5
  key: Answers vary. A strong plan has a change in every panel (a problem, trying something, the big moment, how it ends) and short speech, such as "Uh oh!" "I have an idea!" "Pull!" "It flies!"

## Stretch: A title
write: Give your comic a title that makes people want to read it.
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A good title hints at the action without telling the end: "The Kite That Got Away," "Snow Day Surprise."

# 35. Hallway Show Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, crayons
ican: I can plan where artworks, a welcome sign and labels go in an art show.
directions: (Read aloud.) Plan an art show for the hallway. List the 8 artworks. Then draw a map of the hallway from above. Mark where each artwork hangs with its number, add the welcome sign and draw a small label next to each artwork. Use the checklist to make sure your map has everything.
mistakes: A floor plan is drawn from above, like a bird looking down: children often draw the hallway from the side, like a picture. Show a quick map of the classroom on the board first. Watch for artworks crowded at one end; spacing them out lets visitors stop and look. Labels go beside the artwork, not on top of it.
notes: The 8 artworks can be the class's own or made-up ones. A map key helps: a rectangle with a number for an artwork, a small rectangle for a label, a star for the welcome sign. Put the welcome sign near where visitors come in. Talk about eye level: for a grade 2 show, hang art low enough that children can see it. After the plan, use it to hang the real show.

## Warm up: Reading a floor plan
