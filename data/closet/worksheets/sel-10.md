# 1. The Stress and Performance Curve
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can use the stress and performance curve to explain when stress helps and when it hurts.
directions: Plot the points from the table and connect them with a smooth curve. Label the three zones. Then place six fictional scenes on the curve as too little, just right or too much stress.
mistakes: Students think "no stress is best." The curve shows that too little stress can mean low effort and focus. Another mix-up: reading the y-axis as how stressed someone feels. The y-axis is performance; the x-axis is stress.
notes: The inverted-U idea traces back to a 1908 study by Robert Yerkes and John Dodson on learning in mice and is often called the Yerkes-Dodson law. It is a simplified model, not an exact law: research suggests the best level of arousal is lower for hard or unfamiliar tasks and higher for simple ones, and real data are messier than a neat curve. The table values are made up to show the shape. In the scenes table, any stress level inside the zone's range is correct. Scenes are fictional; students do not rate their own stress.

## Warm up: Plot the curve
table: Stress level (0 to 10) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 8 | 10
  - Performance (0 to 100) | 20 | 45 | 75 | 85 | 75 | 45 | 20
graph: Stress level | 0 10 1 | Performance | 0 100 10
  key: 0,20 2,45 4,75 5,85 6,75 8,45 10,20
text: Label the zones on your graph: **Too little** (stress 0 to 3), **Just right** (stress 4 to 6), **Too much** (stress 7 to 10).

## Read the curve
fill:
  - At what stress level is performance highest? {5}
  - Performance is 45 at stress level 2 and at stress level {8}.
  - From stress 8 to stress 10, performance goes {down} by {25} points.

## Main: Place the scenes
table: Scene | Zone | Stress level (0 to 10)
  - A. Dani has a math test tomorrow but feels so relaxed she never opens her notes. | {too little} | {0 to 3 }
  - B. Marco feels a little nervous before his debate, so he practices his opening twice and stays focused. | {just right} | {4 to 6 }
  - C. Priya's hands shake so much before her recital that she forgets the first line. | {too much  } | {7 to 10}
  - D. Jae has three projects due the same day, has slept badly all week and cannot focus on any of them. | {too much  } | {7 to 10}
  - E. The coach says the scrimmage does not count, so Sam jogs at half speed and misses easy passes. | {too little} | {0 to 3 }
  - F. Lila feels butterflies before tryouts and uses the energy to stay alert and quick. | {just right} | {4 to 6 }

## Stretch
write: Pick one "too much" scene. Name two things the character could do to move back toward "just right," and explain why each would help.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Example: Jae could break the projects into small steps and start with the one due soonest, which lowers the feeling of being overwhelmed, and get more sleep, which helps focus. Priya could take slow breaths before going on stage, which calms the stress response.

# 7. Stress in America Survey Table
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can analyze survey data about stress and write claims that are supported by the numbers.
directions: Study the two tables, adapted from the American Psychological Association's 2018 Stress in America survey. Answer the warm-up questions, then write three claims, each supported by at least two numbers from the tables.
mistakes: Students write claims the data cannot support, such as "Gen Z is the most stressed generation" (Millennials averaged higher) or "Social media causes stress" (not in these tables). Another miss: comparing percentages from different questions as if they measured the same thing. Watch the age labels: some rows compare Gen Z adults (18 to 21) with all adults.
notes: Source: American Psychological Association (2018), Stress in America: Generation Z, conducted online by The Harris Poll from July 27 to August 28, 2018, with 3,458 adults and 300 teens ages 15 to 17. In this survey Gen Z means ages 15 to 21, Millennials 22 to 39, Gen X 40 to 53, Boomers 54 to 72 and older adults 73 and up. The work figure for Gen Z adults is based on fewer than 100 people, so the report calls it directional. The survey used online panels, so it reports no margin of error. Numbers are copied from the report; the tables are rearranged. The report also asked about stressful news events (such as shootings); those rows are left out of this sheet on purpose. Students analyze national data, not their own stress.

  - Gen Z's average stress level is {0.4} points higher than the average for all adults.

  - Older generations were more likely to rate their mental health excellent or very good.

## Main: Three claims
write: Claim 1 (about stress levels by generation). Support it with at least two numbers.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Stress levels go down with age after the Millennial years. Millennials averaged 5.7 out of 10, while Boomers averaged 4.1 and older adults 3.3.
write: Claim 2 (about how stress shows up for Gen Z). Support it with at least two numbers.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Stress shows up in the body and in sleep for most Gen Z adults. 91 percent had at least one symptom of stress in the past month, compared with 74 percent of all adults, and 68 percent lay awake because of stress, compared with 45 percent.
write: Claim 3 (about coping or getting help). Support it with at least two numbers.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Gen Z wants more support than they are getting. Only 50 percent feel they do enough to manage stress (64 percent of all adults do), and 73 percent could have used more emotional support (56 percent of all adults). At the same time, Gen Z is the most likely to have received therapy (37 percent).

## Stretch: Read with care
write: Name one limit of this data that a careful reader should keep in mind before making a big claim about Gen Z.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: It is one survey from 2018, so it may not describe Gen Z today. It was taken online through panels, so there is no margin of error. The work number comes from fewer than 100 people. Some rows compare only 18- to 21-year-olds, not all of Gen Z. The data shows differences but not their causes.

# 20. Cesar Chavez the Organizer
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how a leader can lead by serving the people in a movement.
directions: Read the passage; its paragraphs are numbered. Highlight each action Chavez took to serve workers. Then answer the questions, citing paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students describe Chavez as a leader who "told people what to do" or "led the strike," missing the passage's point that he listened first and built services before he made demands. Ask "What did he do for workers before the strike?" Some students also think the strike began with Chavez; paragraph 5 says Filipino American workers led by Larry Itliong walked out first.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class. Facts follow standard histories of the farmworker movement (the United Farm Workers, the National Park Service's Cesar Chavez National Monument materials and biographies). Chavez's record is also debated: in the 1970s the union's opposition to undocumented strikebreakers and, later, internal conflicts drew criticism. Mention this if students research further. "Servant leadership" is a term from the writer Robert Greenleaf (1970); the passage does not claim Chavez used it. March 31, Chavez's birthday, is Cesar Chavez Day.

## Read
passage: Cesar Chavez: Leading by Serving | numbered
  Cesar Chavez knew farm work from the inside. He was born in 1927 near Yuma, Arizona, where his family had a small farm. During the Great Depression they lost their land and became migrant farmworkers in California, following the harvests from town to town. Chavez attended many different schools, and after eighth grade he left school to work in the fields full time.
  In 1952 he met Fred Ross, an organizer for the Community Service Organization (CSO), a group that helped Mexican American communities in California gain a stronger voice. Ross taught a simple method: go to people where they are, and listen before you ask anything of them. Chavez began holding house meetings, small gatherings in a family's living room where neighbors talked about their problems. He helped people register to vote, apply for citizenship and deal with government offices. Over the next several years he rose to become the CSO's national director.
  Chavez wanted the CSO to organize farmworkers, who earned low pay, had few legal protections and could be fired for complaining. When the group decided not to make farmworkers its focus, he resigned in 1962 and moved his family to Delano, a farm town in California's San Joaquin Valley. With Dolores Huerta and others, he started the National Farm Workers Association.
  He did not begin by calling a strike. Instead, he drove from camp to camp, asking workers what they needed and listening to their answers. The new association offered services before it made demands: a credit union where workers could borrow money at fair rates, and a death benefit to help families pay for funerals. Chavez and the volunteers who joined him lived on a few dollars a week plus room and board, not much more than the workers they served. Members paid dues, so the association belonged to them.
  In September 1965, Filipino American grape pickers led by Larry Itliong walked out of the Delano vineyards. Chavez's association voted to join them, and the strike lasted five years. Chavez insisted on nonviolence, even when strikers were threatened and attacked. In 1966 he led a march of nearly four weeks from Delano to the state capitol in Sacramento, and that year the two groups merged into the union that became the United Farm Workers. The strikers called for a boycott, and millions of Americans stopped buying table grapes. In 1968, when some members talked of answering violence with violence, Chavez fasted for 25 days to recommit the movement to nonviolence.
  In 1970, many of the Delano grape growers signed union contracts that raised pay and gave workers new protections on the job. Chavez died in 1993, and the next year he was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom. His way of leading, by listening first, sharing hardship and building power with people rather than for them, is still studied by organizers today.

## Questions
choice: What method did Fred Ross teach Chavez? (paragraph 2)
  - Start with a large protest to get attention.
  * Go to people where they are and listen first.
  - Ask the government to solve the problem.

  - To show the association was expensive
  - To show Chavez was paid well
  * To show the workers owned and ran the group, not an outside leader
write: Why do you think Chavez offered services **before** making demands? Use evidence from paragraph 3 or 4.
  lines: 3
  key: Farmworkers "could be fired for complaining" (paragraph 3), so asking them to strike right away was risky. By first asking what they needed and offering a credit union and death benefit (paragraph 4), Chavez showed the association would help them and built trust before asking them to take risks.
write: The author says Chavez built power "with people rather than for them." Explain what this means, using two pieces of evidence.
  lines: 4
  key: He did not act as a hero who fixed things for workers; he helped workers act together. Evidence: he listened at house meetings and camps to learn what workers needed (paragraphs 2 and 4), and members paid dues so the association belonged to them (paragraph 4). ## Stretch
write: A student is starting a club to improve the school cafeteria. Name one lesson from Chavez's approach the student could use, and explain how.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Example: Listen first. Before making demands, the student could survey or talk to students and cafeteria workers about what they need, then start with one small service, like a suggestion box that gets real answers, to build trust.

# 21. One-Minute Volunteer Pitch
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, a timer or clock with a second hand for practice
ican: I can write a short, persuasive pitch that recruits people to help my community.
directions: Read the facts about the Maple Street Community Garden. Plan each part of your pitch with the frame: hook, need, ask. Then write the full pitch. It should take about one minute to say out loud (about 130 to 160 words).
mistakes: Pitches that list every fact run long. Pick the two or three facts that matter most to the listener. The ask is often vague ("Please help out"). A strong ask says exactly what, when and how to sign up.
notes: The garden and its facts are fictional. Most people speak about 130 to 160 words a minute, so have students read their pitch aloud to a partner with a timer. A rubric: hook grabs attention in one or two sentences; need uses at least two facts; ask is specific; the whole pitch fits one minute.

## Read: The facts
text: **Maple Street Community Garden** has 24 raised beds on a lot that used to be empty. Last year it grew about 1,200 pounds of vegetables, and half went to the Eastside Food Pantry. This spring, 8 beds have rotted and need to be rebuilt. The garden needs **12 volunteers** for 3 Saturdays in April, from 9 to 11 a.m. No experience is needed; tools, gloves and snacks are provided. Sign up on the sheet at the library front desk by March 31.

## Warm up
example: Hook types: a question ("Have you ever eaten a tomato still warm from the sun?"), a surprising fact, or a quick picture ("Picture an empty lot full of weeds...").

  * Sign up at the library front desk by March 31 to build beds with us on a Saturday morning in April.

  lines: 2
  key: Last year, an empty lot on Maple Street grew 1,200 pounds of vegetables. This year, it needs you.

  lines: 3
  key: Eight of our 24 raised beds have rotted. Half of everything we grow goes to the Eastside Food Pantry, so every lost bed means less fresh food for families who need it.

  lines: 2
  key: We need 12 volunteers for 3 Saturdays in April, from 9 to 11. No experience needed; we bring the tools, gloves and snacks. Sign up at the library front desk by March 31.

## Write the pitch

  lines: 12
  key: Model (about 130 words): Last year, an empty lot on Maple Street grew 1,200 pounds of vegetables. Half of it went to the Eastside Food Pantry, straight to families who needed fresh food. # 33. The Robbers Cave Study
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain how in-groups form, how competition turns them against each other, and what reduces the conflict.
directions: Read the summary; its paragraphs are numbered. Answer the questions about how the groups formed, how conflict grew and what finally eased it. Cite paragraph numbers.
mistakes: Students often say that simply putting the groups together fixed the conflict. Paragraph 5 says contact alone failed; the shared problems are what worked. Watch for "the boys were bad kids": the boys were chosen to be ordinary and alike, which is the study's point. The situation, not the personalities, created the hostility.
notes: The summary is original to Curio Class. Sources: Muzafer Sherif and colleagues, Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation: The Robbers Cave Experiment (1961), and Gina Perry, The Lost Boys (2018), which reexamines the study using its archives. Details such as the burned flag, the water supply, the movie and the stalled truck are standard in accounts of the study. The study helped shape realistic conflict theory. The ethics question has no single right answer; look for reasoning about consent and harm.

## Read
passage: The Robbers Cave Experiment | numbered
  In the summer of 1954, a group of boys about 11 years old arrived at a summer camp at Robbers Cave State Park in Oklahoma. They thought it was an ordinary camp. In fact, the "camp staff" were researchers led by the psychologist Muzafer Sherif, who wanted to learn how conflict between groups begins and how it can end.
  The 22 boys had been chosen to be alike: they came from similar backgrounds, and none of them knew each other beforehand. They were split into two groups, which arrived separately and were kept apart.
  **Phase 1: building groups.** For about a week, each group hiked, swam and camped on its own. Leaders emerged, along with inside jokes, rules and favorite spots. Each group chose a name: the Rattlers and the Eagles. When the boys learned that another group was nearby, many of them wanted to challenge it.
  **Phase 2: competition.** The staff then announced a tournament of baseball, tug-of-war, a treasure hunt and other contests, with a trophy and prizes for the winning group only. Hostility grew fast. The groups traded insults and did not want to eat together. After losing a tug-of-war, the Eagles burned the Rattlers' flag. Raids on each other's cabins followed. When the researchers asked the boys to rate the groups, they described their own group in glowing terms and the other in harsh ones.
  **Phase 3: easing the conflict.** First the researchers tried simple contact: shared meals, a movie, fireworks. Being in the same place did not help; mealtimes turned into food fights. Then the staff set up problems that neither group could solve alone, which Sherif called superordinate goals. The camp's water supply suddenly failed, and boys from both groups worked together to find the blockage. They pooled their money to rent a movie they all wanted to see. When a truck bringing food stalled, they pulled it together with the same rope they had used in tug-of-war.
  After several of these shared tasks, insults dropped and friendships formed across the groups. At the end of camp, the boys chose to ride home together on one bus, and the Rattlers spent prize money they had won on malted milks for everyone.
  The study helped shape realistic conflict theory: the idea that competition over limited prizes or resources can create hostility between groups, and that working toward shared goals can reduce it. It has also been criticized. The boys and their parents were not told it was an experiment, which would not be allowed today. Later researchers who studied the archives found that staff sometimes stirred up conflict, and that an earlier version of the study in 1953 was stopped after the boys turned against the researchers instead of each other. Still, the main lesson, that shared goals can bring rivals together, has been supported by later research.

  lines: 3

## Stretch
write: The boys and their parents were not told the camp was an experiment. Was the study worth it? Explain your view using the passage.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Strong answers weigh the harm (the boys were deceived, and staff stirred up real hostility, paragraph 7) against what was learned (shared goals can ease group conflict, a lesson supported by later research). Many students argue the lesson is valuable but that the study should have been done with consent and less manipulation.
