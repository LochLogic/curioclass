# 1. Squash and Stretch Bounce
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can use squash and stretch to show weight and speed in an animation.
directions: Read the example. Answer the questions about squash and stretch. Plan the shape of the ball in each frame, then draw all 6 frames of one bounce in the box. Number each ball.
mistakes: Students often squash the ball in the air or stretch it sideways. Stretch goes along the direction the ball is moving (up and down for a straight bounce); squash happens only when it hits the ground. Watch for balls that shrink or grow: a squashed ball gets shorter AND wider, so it seems to keep the same amount of ball. Students also space every frame evenly; a real ball moves slowest near the top, so those frames sit closer together.
notes: Squash and stretch is one of the classic principles of animation. Students can test their drawing as a flip book by redrawing each frame on its own sticky note. A heavy ball (a bowling ball) barely squashes and bounces low; a light, soft ball squashes a lot and bounces higher.

## Warm up
example: **Squash:** when the ball hits the ground, it flattens: shorter and wider. **Stretch:** when it moves fast, it gets longer in the direction it is moving. At the top of the bounce it is moving slowest, so it is round.
choice: When the ball hits the ground, it should look:
  - Taller and thinner
  * Shorter and wider
  - Exactly round
  - Smaller all over
choice: While the ball falls fast toward the ground, it should look:
  * Stretched longer up and down
  - Squashed flat
  - Stretched sideways
  - Tiny
choice: Where are the frames closest together?
  * Near the top, where the ball moves slowest
  - Near the ground, where the ball moves fastest
  - The frames are always the same distance apart
fill:
  - A squashed ball gets shorter, so it must also get {wider} to look like the same amount of ball.

## Main: Plan the shapes
table: Frame | Where the ball is | Shape
  - 1 | at the top | round
  - 2 | falling fast | {stretched}
  - 3 | hitting the ground | {squashed}
  - 4 | rising fast | {stretched}
  - 5 | slowing near the top | {round}
  - 6 | at the top again | round

## Main: Draw the bounce
draw: Draw all 6 frames in one picture, left to right, like a time-lapse photo. Number each ball. | 3.4

## Stretch
write: Now picture a heavy bowling ball dropped from the same height. How would its frames be different from your bouncy ball?
  lines: 3
  key: It would barely squash or stretch because it is hard and heavy, and it would bounce very low or not at all. The frames would show little change in shape, and the drop would end quickly.
write: Why do animators use squash and stretch, even for things that do not really squash much?
  lines: 2
  key: It makes motion look alive and shows weight, speed and softness; exaggerating the shape helps the audience feel the impact.

# 7. Frame Budget
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; optional: calculator
ican: I can calculate how many frames a stop-motion film needs and plan the work.
directions: Warm up with frames and seconds. Fill in the frame budget for a 45-second film at 12 and at 15 frames per second. Then use your budget to plan the shooting days.
mistakes: Students divide when they should multiply: seconds times frames per second gives frames, and frames divided by frames per second gives seconds. When counting class sessions, students round 11.25 down to 11; the last quarter of the work still needs a session, so it rounds up to 12.
notes: 12 frames per second is a common choice for classroom stop motion. 15 frames per second looks smoother but needs 25 percent more photos (675 is 540 x 1.25). The robot story is a sample; teams can swap in their own scenes as long as the seconds add up to 45.

## Warm up
example: At 12 frames per second, 5 seconds needs 5 x 12 = 60 frames. Going back: 60 frames / 12 = 5 seconds.
fill:
  - 2 seconds at 12 fps = {24} frames
  - 4 seconds at 15 fps = {60} frames
  - 60 frames at 12 fps = {5} seconds
  - 90 frames at 15 fps = {6} seconds

## Main: The frame budget
text: **Robot Morning** is a 45-second stop-motion film. Find the frames for each scene at both speeds.
table: Scene | Seconds | Frames at 12 fps | Frames at 15 fps
  - 1. Title card | 3 | {36} | {45}
  - 2. Setup: a robot wakes up in a toy box | 12 | {144} | {180}
  - 3. Problem: its battery runs low | 15 | {180} | {225}
  - 4. Ending: a teddy bear shares a charger | 12 | {144} | {180}
  - 5. Credits | 3 | {36} | {45}
  - Total | {45} | {540} | {675}

## Main: Plan the shooting days
fill:
  - At 15 fps, the film needs {135} more frames than at 12 fps.
  - Your team can shoot 60 frames in one class session. At 12 fps, you need {9} sessions.
  - At 15 fps, you need {12} sessions.
choice: Your team has exactly 9 class sessions. Which plan makes the most sense?
  - Shoot at 15 fps and stop when time runs out
  - Skip the setup scene and hope no one notices
  * Shoot at 12 fps so the whole film fits in 9 sessions
  - Shoot the credits first and see what happens
choice: Why might a team choose 15 fps even though it takes longer?
  - It needs fewer photos
  * The motion looks smoother
  - The film gets longer
  - The camera works faster

## Stretch
fill: A team has only 8 sessions, or 480 frames. At 12 fps, the film can be {40} seconds long.
write: The team must cut 5 seconds from Robot Morning. Which scene would you shorten, and why?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer keeps the story clear, such as cutting the setup from 12 to 7 seconds because the audience only needs a moment to see the robot wake up, while the problem and ending carry the story. Cutting the title and credits to almost nothing also works if the student explains it.

# 20. Koto, Kora and Steel Pan
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; optional: recordings of each instrument
ican: I can describe instruments from different traditions and explain how film composers might use them.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions, fill in the chart, then choose an instrument for a film scene.
mistakes: Students describe an instrument only by where it comes from. Push them to describe the sound itself (bright, rippling, bending) and how it is played. Watch for students treating an instrument as a joke or a costume for a place; the passage's last paragraph is the point to discuss.
notes: If you can, play a short recording of each instrument before or after reading. Facts in the passage: the koto came to Japan from China in the 7th and 8th centuries, is about 180 cm (6 feet) long, and most often has 13 strings on movable bridges; the kora has 21 strings and a gourd body covered with cow skin and is played by jali (griot) musicians of the Mandinka people; the steel pan developed in Trinidad and Tobago in the 1930s and 1940s and is played with rubber-tipped sticks. Kora players pluck with the thumbs and index fingers of both hands.

## Read
passage: Three Instruments, Three Traditions
  The koto is a long string instrument from Japan. It is about 6 feet long. Most koto have 13 strings, and each string rests on a small bridge that the player can slide to change the tuning. Players wear picks on three fingers and pluck the strings. By pressing a string with the other hand, a player can bend a note so it slides up and sighs. The koto came to Japan from China more than 1,200 years ago.
  The kora comes from West Africa, from the Mandinka people of countries such as Mali, Senegal, The Gambia and Guinea. It has 21 strings and a body made from a gourd cut in half and covered with cow skin. Players pluck with their thumbs and first fingers, playing a repeating pattern and a melody at the same time. Many kora players come from families of jali, musicians who keep history alive through song.
  The steel pan was created in Trinidad and Tobago in the 1930s and 1940s. Musicians hammered the tops of steel oil drums into dented sections, each tuned to a different note. Players strike the notes with rubber-tipped sticks, and the sound is bright and ringing.
  Film composers choose instruments for their sound and for the story. A koto might make a quiet scene feel tense, a kora might carry a long journey, and a steel pan might make a scene feel joyful. Good composers also learn each instrument's history and often work with musicians who play it, so the music is respectful and not just a quick label for a place.

## Questions
choice: How does a koto player change the tuning of a string?
  - By twisting a knob at the end
  * By sliding the small bridge under the string
  - By hitting it with a rubber-tipped stick
  - By covering it with cow skin
choice: What were the first steel pans made from?
  - Gourds cut in half
  - Hollow logs
  - Bamboo tubes
  * Steel oil drums
choice: What do the jali do, according to the passage?
  * Keep history alive through song
  - Build steel drums
  - Write film scores
  - Teach the koto in Japan
write: Why does the passage say composers should learn an instrument's history? Use a detail from the last paragraph.
  lines: 3
  key: So the music is respectful and not "just a quick label for a place." Learning the history, and working with musicians who play the instrument, helps a composer use it for its real sound and meaning instead of as a shortcut.

## Main: Compare the instruments
chart: Where it comes from | What it is made of | How it is played | Its sound
  labels: Koto; Kora; Steel pan
  height: 0.75
  key: Koto: Japan (from China long ago); a long body with 13 strings on movable bridges; plucked with picks on three fingers, notes bent by pressing the strings; plucked notes that can slide and sigh. Kora: West Africa, Mandinka people; a gourd covered with cow skin and 21 strings; plucked with thumbs and first fingers, a pattern and a melody at once; rippling. Steel pan: Trinidad and Tobago; a steel oil drum hammered into tuned sections; struck with rubber-tipped sticks; bright and ringing.

## Stretch
write: A film scene shows a girl riding her bike to the sea at sunrise. Which of the three instruments would you choose for the music, and why? Describe the sound you want.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one instrument and connects its sound to the scene, such as "a kora, because its rippling pattern feels like the steady pedaling and the waves," or "a steel pan, because its bright, ringing notes feel like a happy morning."

# 21. Foley Technique Test
minutes: 45
materials: three sealed zipper bags (one with cornstarch, one with uncooked rice, one with dry pasta), tissue paper, pencil, a partner
ican: I can test sound effect methods and judge which one sounds most real.
directions: Your sound is **footsteps in snow**. Read about foley. Make a prediction. With a partner, test the 4 techniques the same way each time. Your partner listens with eyes closed and rates each sound. Record your results and write a conclusion.
mistakes: Students change two things at once, like squeezing one bag harder and closer to the listener, which is not a fair test. Keep the distance, rhythm and number of steps the same. Listeners who watch the object judge it by looks: eyes closed. Two people's ratings often differ; that is data, not a mistake.
notes: Foley is named after Jack Foley, a Hollywood sound effects artist who made sounds live in time with the picture. A bag of cornstarch squeezed in rhythm is a classic snow-crunch method; most classes rate it most realistic, but any result supported by the data is a good result. Use sealed bags only, double-bagged, and squeeze gently. Nothing is eaten; wash hands after.

## Warm up
example: Foley artists record everyday sounds after filming, in time with the picture: footsteps, cloth rustling, doors, splashes. The object that sounds right often looks nothing like the real thing.
choice: Why should your partner keep their eyes closed while rating?
  - So they can rest
  * So they judge only the sound, not what the object looks like
  - So the sound is louder
  - So they do not laugh
choice: What makes this a fair test?
  - Squeeze each bag as hard as you want
  - Use a different listener for each technique
  * Keep the distance, rhythm and number of steps the same for every technique
  - Test the best one twice
write: Prediction: Which technique will sound most like footsteps in snow? Why?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary; any prediction with a reason. Many students pick the cornstarch bag because it squeaks and crunches like packed snow.

## Main: Data
text: Rating: 1 = does not sound like snow, 3 = sort of, 5 = sounds just like snow.
table: Technique | My rating (1-5) | Partner rating (1-5) | What it sounded like
  - A. Squeeze the cornstarch bag | | |
  - B. Squeeze the rice bag | | |
  - C. Crumple tissue paper | | |
  - D. Squeeze the dry pasta bag | | |
choice: For technique A you gave a 4 and your partner gave a 5. What is the average rating?
  - 9
  - 4
  * 4.5
  - 5

## Conclusion
write: Which technique was most realistic? Use your data.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary with the data. A strong answer names the top technique and cites both ratings or the average, such as "A had the highest average, 4.5, because it made a squeaky crunch."
write: Which technique was least realistic, and what was wrong with its sound?
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the problem in sound words: too sharp, too loud, rattly instead of crunchy, no squeak.

## Stretch
write: How could you improve the best technique? Plan one change and how you would test it fairly.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary: add a little more cornstarch, squeeze slower for a heavy walker, or record closer. Change only one thing and have the same listener rate it again.

# 33. Source Checklist
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can use a checklist to decide how reliable a source is.
directions: Read the research question and the three pretend sources. Use the 5-question checklist on each source. Then use the writing frame to explain which source is most reliable.
mistakes: Students trust Source A because it has millions of views; popularity is not evidence. Others pick Source C because it sounds sure of itself. Point back to the checklist: who made it, when, why, where the facts come from, and whether other good sources agree.
notes: All three sources, the account name, the zoo and the product are made up. The fact itself is real: Galápagos giant tortoises often live more than 100 years. Students can keep the same 5 questions for their own research all year; consider posting them in the room.

## Warm up
text: **Research question:** How long can Galápagos giant tortoises live? Your library's animal encyclopedia says they can live more than 100 years.
example: **The 5-question checklist.** 1. **Who** made it, and do they know the topic? 2. **When** was it made or updated? 3. **Why** was it made: to inform, to sell, or to entertain? 4. **Evidence:** does it show where its facts come from? 5. **Check:** do other good sources agree?
table: Source | What it says
  - A. A video post by the account Shell_Facts_4U (no real name), 2 million views, no date | "Tortoises live to be 1,000 years old!!! Share if you love turtles!"
  - B. A fact page on a city zoo's website, written by the zoo's reptile keeper, updated March 2025 | "Galápagos giant tortoises often live more than 100 years in the wild." Below it: a list of three science articles.
  - C. An online ad for Product A, a tortoise food, no date | "Tortoises who eat Product A live longer, happier lives! Buy now and save 20%!"

## Main: Use the checklist
table: Question | Source A | Source B | Source C
  - 1. Who made it? An expert? | {unknown account, not shown to be an expert} | {the zoo's reptile keeper, an expert} | {the company selling the food}
  - 2. When? | {no date} | {March 2025} | {no date}
  - 3. Why was it made? | {to entertain and get shares} | {to inform} | {to sell}
  - 4. Shows its evidence? | {no} | {yes, three science articles} | {no}
  - 5. Do good sources agree? | {no, 1,000 years is far too high} | {yes, matches the encyclopedia} | {it does not give a number to check}

## Main: Writing frame
write: Which source is most reliable? Explain using at least three checklist questions.
  starter: The most reliable source is ____ because ____. It also ____. Source ____ is less reliable because ____.
  lines: 6
  key: The most reliable source is B, the zoo's fact page, because it was written by a reptile keeper who knows the topic and it lists three science articles as evidence. It also has a recent date and agrees with the encyclopedia. Source A is less reliable because the author is unknown, there is no date or evidence, and 1,000 years does not match other sources. Source C is less reliable because it was made to sell food.

## Stretch
choice: Source A has 2 million views. What does that tell you about whether it is true?
  - It must be true, because so many people watched it
  - It must be false, because popular videos are always wrong
  * Nothing: popularity shows that many people saw it, not that it is correct
  - It is true for young tortoises only
write: Source C might still be useful for one thing. What could you learn from it, and what should you not use it for?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows what Product A's company claims and how it tries to sell its food (useful for studying ads). It should not be used as evidence of how long tortoises live, because it gives no facts or evidence and is trying to sell something.
