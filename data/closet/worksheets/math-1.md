# 1. Pond Stories: Add To and Take From
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can write an equation for an add to or take from story and solve it.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to each pond story. Draw a picture or jump on the number line. Then write the equation and the answer.
mistakes: Children often add every story because both numbers are "in it". Ask: "Did more frogs come, or did some go away?" In items 7 and 9 the missing number is the change, not the total; children who write 8 + 11 = 19 for item 7 need to act it out with counters.
notes: Read each story twice. Act out item 3 with counters or paper frogs before the sheet. Equations may be written other ways that are still true (for item 7, 11 - 8 = 3 is fine). A number line jump: start at the first number, jump forward to add, back to take away.

## Warm up
example: 6 frogs sit on a log. 2 more frogs hop on. Start at 6 and jump 2: 6 + 2 = 8. There are 8 frogs.

# 6. Doubles Color by Code
minutes: 30
materials: pencil; crayons: light blue, red, yellow, brown
ican: I can use doubles to add near doubles.
directions: (Read aloud.) Solve each fact. Use a double to help with the near doubles. Then color each square of the picture by the letter in it: find that letter's sum, then find the sum in the color key.
mistakes: For near doubles, some children double the wrong number and miss by 2 (7 + 8 as 8 + 8 = 16, then add 1 to get 17). Ask: "Which double is hiding inside?" and have them double the smaller number, then add 1. A wrong sum shows up as a square that looks out of place in the picture.
notes: The picture is a hot air balloon: red balloon with a yellow stripe, brown ropes and basket, light blue sky. Facts A, C, E, G, I, K, M and O are doubles; the rest are near doubles, one more than a double. Warm up: let children use counters or fingers for the doubles if they do not know them yet.

## Warm up: Doubles help

# 17. Number Words to Numerals
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can read and write numbers to 120.
directions: (Read aloud.) Read each number word. Write the number with digits. Then put four numbers in order from least to greatest.
mistakes: The classic error is writing numbers the way they sound: 1005 for one hundred five, or 10012 for one hundred twelve. Show that one hundred five is 1 hundred, 0 tens and 5 ones, so it has three digits. Watch for 17 written for seventy and 70 for seventeen.
notes: Read each word aloud with the class first if your readers need it. Number word spellings to point out: forty (no u), eighteen (one t). For the order task, children can find each number on a 120 chart.

## Warm up
example: one hundred five is 1 hundred, 0 tens and 5 ones: 105.
fill:
  - ten = {10}
  - twenty-five = {25}

## Main: Words to numbers
fill:
  - forty-two = {42}
  - seventy = {70}
  - eighteen = {18}
  - ninety-nine = {99}
  - one hundred five = {105}
  - sixty-three = {63}
  - one hundred twelve = {112}
  - one hundred = {100}
  - eighty-one = {81}
  - one hundred twenty = {120}
text: Write these four numbers in order from least to greatest: 105, 63, 112, 99
text: {63}, {99}, {105}, {112}

## Stretch: Fix it
write: (Read aloud.) Ben wrote 1005 for one hundred five. What did Ben do wrong? Write it the right way.
  lines: 2
  key: Ben wrote 100 and then 5 next to it. One hundred five is 1 hundred, 0 tens and 5 ones, so it is written 105.

# 24. Add Tens to Any Number
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can add tens to a two-digit number.
directions: (Read aloud.) Add the tens. Jump by tens on the number line, or draw tens and ones. Write the sum.
mistakes: The most common error is adding to the ones: 23 + 30 written as 26. Ask: "Is 30 three ones or three tens?" Children who count by ones on the number line will lose count; show them one big jump for each ten.
notes: Only the tens digit changes in every problem, which is the pattern to notice in the stretch. A ten can be drawn as a long stick and a one as a dot. Problem 10 starts with the tens (40 + 38); children can start from 38 and jump 4 tens instead.

## Warm up: 10 more
example: 36 + 20. Start at 36. Jump 10 to 46. Jump 10 more to 56. So 36 + 20 = 56.

# 29. Paper Clip Measure
minutes: 25
materials: pencil; optional: real paper clips
ican: I can measure length by laying paper clips end to end with no gaps or overlaps.
directions: (Read aloud.) The ruler at the bottom is made of paper clips. Each space is one paper clip long. Every object starts at 0, so there are no gaps. Find where each object ends and write how many paper clips long it is.
mistakes: Children often count the tick marks instead of the spaces, and get one too many (they count the 0). Have them touch each space between marks and count "1, 2, 3." In the warm up, some pick the clips with gaps because "it goes farther": talk about why gaps make the object seem longer than it is.
notes: Adaptation: the objects are shown as bars on a printed paper clip ruler (each grid space is one clip), because the sheet cannot print pictures at exact lengths. Hold up a real pencil and a row of paper clips before the sheet. Objects 1 to 8: pencil 6, key 2, toothbrush 7, crayon 3, spoon 5, marker 4, paintbrush 8, eraser 1.

## Warm up: Good measuring
choice: (Read aloud.) Which way of measuring with paper clips is right?
  - The clips have gaps between them.
  - The clips lie on top of each other.
  * The clips touch end to end, with no gaps.

# 40. Fair Shares Cut and Sort
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue
ican: I can tell if a shape is cut into equal shares and name halves and fourths.
directions: (Read aloud.) Cut out the 12 shape cards. Look at the parts in each shape. If the shape has 2 equal shares, glue it under Halves. If it has 4 equal shares, glue it under Fourths. If the parts are not the same size, glue it under Not equal.
mistakes: Children often count the parts and stop there: a shape cut into 2 parts goes under Halves even when one part is much bigger. Ask: "If you and a friend each got one part, would it be fair?" The square cut corner to corner into 4 triangles is equal fourths even though the parts are triangles.
notes: Halves: circle, square and rectangle each cut by a line down the middle. Fourths: circle cut into 4 by two lines through the center, square cut corner to corner both ways, rectangle cut into 4 strips the same width. Not equal: the other six. "Fourths" and "quarters" mean the same thing; accept either word.

## Warm up
fill:
  - 2 equal shares are called {halves}.
  - 4 equal shares are called {fourths}.
write: (Read aloud.) Lee cut a sandwich into 2 parts. One part is big and one part is small. Did Lee cut the sandwich in halves? Tell why.
  lines: 2
  key: No. Halves are 2 equal shares. Lee's 2 parts are not the same size, so they are not halves.
