# 1. Gross to Net
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can compute net pay from gross pay after federal, state, Social Security and Medicare withholding.
directions: Each invented paycheck lists the hours (h), the pay rate and the federal income tax withheld. Overtime is paid at time and a half; Dev's yearly salary is split into 26 equal paychecks. Compute gross pay, then withhold state tax at 4%, Social Security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%. Round each amount to the cent. Subtract to find net pay.
mistakes: Students often compute the percentages from net pay or from each other. Every percentage is taken from gross pay. Watch for overtime paid at the regular rate; overtime is time and a half (1.5 x the hourly rate). Rounding each withholding to the cent before subtracting matters: compute, round, then subtract.
notes: Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) are the actual employee FICA rates; together they are 7.65%. The 4% state rate is invented (states differ, and some have no income tax). Federal amounts are invented; real ones come from the IRS withholding tables in Publication 15-T, based on pay and the worker's Form W-4. Workers and wages are made up.

## Example
example: Ava works 40 hours at $15.00 an hour. Gross = 40 x $15.00 = $600.00. Federal = $28.00 (given). State = 4% x $600.00 = $24.00. Social Security = 6.2% x $600.00 = $37.20. Medicare = 1.45% x $600.00 = $8.70.
  Net = $600.00 - $28.00 - $24.00 - $37.20 - $8.70 = **$502.10**

# 5. Form W-4 in Plain Words
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator, highlighter
ican: I can explain how Form W-4 tells an employer how much federal income tax to withhold from my pay.
directions: Read the plain-language guide to Form W-4. Then answer the questions and solve the withholding problems for the invented workers.
mistakes: Students often think the W-4 goes to the IRS or that withholding is an extra tax. The form goes to the employer, and withholding is a prepayment of the tax you owe for the year. A refund is not free money from the government; it is your own money that was withheld beyond what you owed. Watch for "exempt" being treated as a choice anyone can make; it has strict conditions.
notes: The guide is original to Curio Class and describes the current design of Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate), used since 2020, which has five steps and no longer uses "allowances." Check the current year's form and instructions on IRS.gov before teaching; amounts for credits change and are left out on purpose. Workers are invented. Math: Sam owes $1,500 - $1,200 = $300; Ana gets $2,000 - $1,650 = $350 back; Jordan: $520 / 26 = $20 extra per paycheck.

## Read
passage: Form W-4 in Plain Words | numbered
  When you start a new job, your employer will ask you to fill out Form W-4, the Employee's Withholding Certificate. Federal income tax works on a pay-as-you-go system. Instead of paying a whole year of tax at once, you have some tax taken out of each paycheck, and your employer sends it to the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) for you. This is called withholding. The W-4 tells your employer how much to withhold. You give the form to your employer, not to the IRS.
  The form has five steps. Everyone fills in Step 1 and signs Step 5. Steps 2, 3 and 4 are only for people whose situation calls for them. Step 1 asks for your name, address, Social Security number and filing status: single or married filing separately, married filing jointly, or head of household.
  Step 2 is for people who have more than one job at the same time, or who are married filing jointly with a spouse who also works. Without Step 2, each employer withholds as if its job were your only income, which often means too little is withheld in total.
  Step 3 is where you claim dependents, such as children you support, and certain other tax credits. A credit lowers the tax you owe, so claiming one lowers the amount withheld from each check.
  Step 4 lets you fine-tune. In 4(a) you can list other income that has no withholding, such as interest. In 4(b) you can list deductions beyond the standard deduction. In 4(c) you can ask your employer to withhold an extra amount from every paycheck.
  Some workers can claim to be exempt from withholding, but only if they owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year. An exemption lasts for one year, so it must be renewed with a new W-4. If you never turn in a W-4, your employer must withhold as if you were single with no other adjustments.
  Withholding is only an estimate. Each spring you file a tax return that compares the tax you actually owe for the year with the total that was withheld. If too much was withheld, you get a refund. If too little was withheld, you pay the difference, and you may owe a penalty if it was far too little. You can turn in a new W-4 whenever your life changes, such as starting a second job, getting married or having a child. The IRS offers a free Tax Withholding Estimator on its website to help, and many states have their own withholding form.

# 16. The Compound Interest Formula
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, scientific calculator
ican: I can use the compound interest formula to compute the balance of a savings account.
directions: Use A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). P is the starting deposit (principal), r is the yearly interest rate as a decimal, n is the number of times interest compounds per year, and t is the number of years. Do not round until the final answer, then round to the cent.
mistakes: The most common errors: writing r as a whole number (4 instead of 0.04), forgetting to multiply n by t in the exponent, and rounding 1 + r/n too early (with monthly compounding, 1.0041666... rounded to 1.004 changes the answer). Students also mix up "compounded quarterly" (n = 4) and "semiannually" (n = 2). Interest earned is A - P, not A.
notes: All accounts are invented. Balances were computed exactly and rounded to the cent: A $1,216.65; B $3,370.87; C $598.34; D $11,051.63; E $914.92; F $10,426.80. A calculator that rounds intermediate steps may differ by a cent or two; accept that. Daily compounding uses n = 365.

## Example
example: $1,200 at 5% compounded monthly for 2 years: P = 1,200, r = 0.05, n = 12, t = 2.
  A = 1,200(1 + 0.05/12)^(12 x 2) = 1,200(1.0041666...)^24 = **$1,325.93**. Interest earned = $1,325.93 - $1,200 = **$125.93**.

# 26. Student Loan Types
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator, highlighter
ican: I can compare subsidized, unsubsidized and private student loans and calculate the interest they build up.
directions: Read the passage about three kinds of student loans. Answer the questions, then calculate the interest for two invented borrowers. Use simple interest: interest = principal x rate x time in years.
mistakes: Students often believe "subsidized" means the loan is free or forgiven; it must still be repaid, but the government covers the interest during school and some other periods. Watch for interest on an unsubsidized loan counted only after graduation; it builds from the day the money is paid out. Students also assume private loans work like federal ones; terms depend on the lender and the borrower's credit.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class and describes federal Direct Subsidized and Direct Unsubsidized Loans in general terms. Loan limits, rates and repayment plans change with the law (including changes passed in 2025), so specific amounts are left out on purpose; check StudentAid.gov before teaching. Borrowers, rates and amounts are invented, and the math uses yearly simple interest to approximate the daily simple interest federal loans use. Whether and when unpaid interest is added to the balance (capitalized) depends on the loan and current rules, so the sheet says "if." Math: Jo: $5,500 x 0.06 x 4.5 = $1,485, balance $6,985 if capitalized; subsidized $3,500 x 0.06 x 4.5 = $945 the government pays. Kai: $10,000 x 0.09 = $900 a year, $75 a month, $3,600 over 4 years.

## Read
passage: Three Ways to Borrow for School | numbered
  Many students borrow money to pay for college or career training. Before borrowing, students fill out the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA), which is used to award grants, work-study and federal loans. Grants do not have to be repaid, but every loan does, with interest. The kind of loan you choose can change how much interest you pay by thousands of dollars.
  A Direct Subsidized Loan comes from the U.S. Department of Education. It is only for undergraduate students who show financial need. Its biggest benefit is that the government pays the interest while you are enrolled in school at least half time, during a six-month grace period after you leave school, and during certain approved pauses in payments called deferments. Your balance does not grow while you study.
  A Direct Unsubsidized Loan also comes from the Department of Education, but it is not based on financial need, and graduate students can borrow this kind too. You are responsible for all of the interest. Interest begins to build from the day the money is paid out, including while you are in school. You can pay the interest as you go. If you do not, the unpaid interest may be added to your balance, which is called capitalization, and then you pay interest on that interest.
  Both federal loans have fixed interest rates, meaning the rate stays the same for the life of the loan. Rates for new loans are set each year by law. Federal loans also come with protections, such as payment plans tied to your income and options to pause payments during hard times.
  A private student loan comes from a bank, a credit union or another lender. The lender sets the terms. Your rate depends on your credit history, and because most students have little credit, many need a cosigner, often a parent, who must repay the loan if the student does not. Rates may be fixed or variable, meaning they can rise later. Private loans usually have fewer protections than federal loans.
  For these reasons, most experts suggest that students use grants and scholarships first, then federal loans, and only then consider private loans. Borrowing only what you need keeps future payments manageable.

# 37. Quiet Year, Theft Year, Fire Year

materials: pencil, calculator, ruler
ican: I can compare insurance plans by their total yearly cost in different situations.
directions: Compare three invented renters insurance plans and no insurance. For each kind of year, find the total yearly cost to the renter: the premium plus what the renter pays for the loss. Then graph the theft year and decide which plan fits best.
mistakes: Students often compare plans by premium alone. The cheapest premium can cost the most in a bad year.
