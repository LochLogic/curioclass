# 1. Bar Model Basics
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can draw a bar model for a word problem and write an equation with a symbol for the unknown.
directions: (Read aloud.) For each problem, draw a bar model. Show the whole and the parts, and put a ? where the unknown number goes. Write an equation with ?, then solve it.
mistakes: Many children add any two numbers they see. In items 2 and 5 the total is given and a part is missing, and in item 4 the start is missing, so the answer does not come from adding the two numbers shown. In compare problems (items 3 and 6), check that the two bars are drawn one above the other, with the difference as the extra piece of the longer bar.
notes: Accept any true equation that matches the model: for item 2, 82 - ? = 47, 47 + ? = 82 and 82 - 47 = ? all show the same bar. A part-part-whole bar is one long bar split in two; a compare model is two bars of different lengths. Children may use a box or a letter instead of ?.

## Warm up: Read the bar
example: Sam has 28 stickers. He gets 15 more. The whole bar is ?. The parts are 28 and 15. 28 + 15 = ?, so ? = 43.
text: The whole bar is 50. Find the missing part.
table: Part | Part
  - 30 | {20}
text: The whole bar is 75. Find the missing part.
table: Part | Part
  - {35} | 40

## Stretch: Your turn
write: (Read aloud.) Write a word problem that matches this bar: the whole is 60, one part is 45, the other part is ?. Then solve it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary; the story has a total of 60 with one part 45, such as "60 kids are at the park. 45 are on the field. How many are at the playground?" 60 - 45 = ?, so ? = 15.

# 5. Strategy Sort Within 20
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can add and subtract within 20 using a strategy that fits the numbers.
directions: (Read aloud.) Solve each fact. Then write the strategy you used: make ten, doubles, count on or think addition.
mistakes: Some children count on by ones for every fact, even 9 + 6, and lose count. Point out that a 9 or an 8 is close to ten. For subtraction, watch for children who count back 9 from 13 one at a time; think addition (9 + ? = 13) is faster and safer.
notes: The key shows the strategy that fits each fact best: make ten for facts with 8 or 9, doubles for facts that are doubles or one away (6 + 7), count on for adding 1, 2 or 3, think addition for subtraction. Accept any strategy a child can explain, as long as the answer is right. Near doubles count as doubles here.

## Warm up
example: 9 + 4: make ten. 9 + 1 = 10, and 3 more is 13. So 9 + 4 = 13.
fill:
  - Make ten: 8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + {4} = {14}
  - Think addition: 14 - 6 = ? Think 6 + {8} = 14.

## Stretch: Two ways
write: (Read aloud.) Solve 8 + 7 two different ways. Show both.
  lines: 3
  key: Doubles: 7 + 7 = 14, and 1 more is 15. Make ten: 8 + 2 = 10, and 5 more is 15. So 8 + 7 = 15.

# 16. Skip Count Number Paths
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can skip count by 5s, 10s and 100s within 1000.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at each path. Find out what it counts by. Write the missing numbers.
mistakes: When a path crosses a hundred (190, 195, 200, 205), children often write 1100 or jump to 300. Have them say the numbers aloud. In paths that do not start on a 10 or 100 (432, 442 and 45, 145), some children change the ones digit; only the tens or hundreds digit should change.
notes: Paths 1 to 3 count by 5s, 4 to 6 by 10s, and 7 and 8 by 100s. The stretch counts back by 10s. A hundred chart or a number line to 1000 helps children who are stuck.

# 22. Open Number Line to 1000
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can add and subtract three-digit numbers by jumping hundreds, tens and ones on an open number line.
directions: (Read aloud.) Start at the first number. Jump the hundreds, then the tens, then the ones. Write where you land after each jump. The last number is the answer.
mistakes: Watch for jumps that cross a hundred, like 794 + 8 in item 4: children often write 702 or 7,102 instead of 802. In subtraction, children sometimes jump forward. Remind them that taking away means jumping back (to the left).
notes: An open number line has no tick marks: children draw a line, mark the start, and draw one hop for each jump, labeling it +200, +30, and so on. Other jump orders are fine if the landing spots add up; the key shows hundreds, then tens, then ones. Item 8 can also be done by jumping to a friendly number (903 - 3 = 900, then 900 - 365 = 535).

## Warm up: Where is the dot?
text: Write the number at each dot.
numberline: 0 1000 100
  show: 0 500 1000
  mark: A=200 B=700
numberline: 300 400 10
  show: 300 400
  mark: C=330 D=380

## Stretch: A shortcut
write: (Read aloud.) To add 456 + 298, Rosa jumps +300, then jumps back 2. Show her jumps. What is the sum?

# 27. Estimate, Then Measure
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, inch ruler, yardstick or tape measure
ican: I can estimate a length, measure it, and find how far off my estimate was.
directions: (Read aloud.) For each object, write your estimate first. Then measure it with a ruler or yardstick. Last, find how far off your estimate was: subtract the smaller number from the bigger one.
mistakes: Children sometimes measure first and then write an "estimate" that matches. Have them fill in the whole estimate column before anyone picks up a ruler. When measuring, watch for children who start at the 1 instead of the end of the ruler, and for mixed units (an estimate in inches and a measurement in feet).
notes: Answers vary with your objects. Typical lengths: a new crayon about 3 or 4 inches, a new pencil about 7 inches, a chapter book about 8 or 9 inches on its long side, a student desk about 2 feet across, a classroom door about 7 feet tall, a whiteboard about 4 to 8 feet long. Round each measurement to the nearest whole inch or foot. "How far off" is always the difference, never negative.

## Warm up: Pick the unit
choice: Which unit would you use to measure a crayon?
  * inches
  - feet
choice: Which unit would you use to measure the classroom?
  - inches
  * feet
choice: About how long is a new crayon?
  - 3 feet
  * 3 inches
  - 30 inches
choice: About how tall is a door?
  - 7 inches
  - 2 feet
  * 7 feet

## Main: Estimate, measure, compare
example: A glue stick. I estimate 5 inches. I measure 4 inches. 5 - 4 = 1, so my estimate was off by 1 inch.
table: Object | Unit | My estimate | My measurement | How far off?
  height: 0.45

## Stretch: Look back
write: Which estimate was closest? Did your estimates get better as you went? Tell why.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the closest estimate and its difference, and notices that measuring one object helps estimate the next (knowing a pencil is about 7 inches helps guess a book).

# 38. Measurement and Data Checkpoint
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can measure length, tell time, count coins and read a bar graph.
directions: (Read aloud.) Do each part. Read the ruler, the clocks, the coins and the graph carefully.
mistakes: On the ruler, children who count tick marks instead of spaces get one too many, and line 2 does not start at 0: they must count from 1 to 7 or subtract. On clocks, children often read the hour hand as the next hour when it is past the middle (9:50 read as 10:50). For coins, start with the coins worth the most.
notes: Adaptation: the two lines are read from a printed inch ruler, since a printed sheet may not be exactly to scale. Coin values: quarter 25 cents, dime 10, nickel 5, penny 1. Graph data: apples 7, bananas 4, grapes 9, cherries 5, for 25 children in all.
