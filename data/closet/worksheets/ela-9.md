# 1. Conflicting Motivations: Rainsford and Zaroff
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can analyze how characters with conflicting motivations drive the plot and develop a theme.
directions: After reading "The Most Dangerous Game" by Richard Connell (1924), read the seven excerpts. Chart what Rainsford and General Zaroff each want and value, then explain how the clash between them drives the plot.
mistakes: Students often say the two men are simply opposites. The story's point is that they start out alike: both are hunters who see animals as sport (excerpts 1 and 2). Rainsford's motivation changes when he becomes the hunted; Zaroff's never does. Watch for answers that list events without naming the motive behind them.
notes: The excerpts follow the 1924 text as printed in O. Henry Memorial Award Prize Stories of 1924 (Wikisource), with curly quotation marks made straight. The story describes a man who hunts people; keep the discussion on motive and theme. Zaroff also says cruel and racist things about the men he hunts; this sheet does not quote them, but if students raise them, name them as part of how Connell shows Zaroff's contempt for human life.

## Read: Seven excerpts
source: Richard Connell, "The Most Dangerous Game," Collier's, 1924
  [1] Rainsford, on the yacht: "Don't talk rot, Whitney," said Rainsford. "You're a big-game hunter, not a philosopher. Who cares how a jaguar feels?"
  [2] Zaroff, at dinner: "I have but one passion in my life, Mr. Rainsford, and it is the hunt."
  [3] Zaroff: "Simply this: hunting had ceased to be what you call 'a sporting proposition.' It had become too easy. I always got my quarry. Always. There is no greater bore than perfection."
  [4] Rainsford: "Thank you, I'm a hunter, not a murderer."
  [5] Zaroff: "Your brain against mine. Your wood-craft against mine. Your strength and stamina against mine. Outdoor chess!"
  [6] "The general was playing with him! The general was saving him for another day's sport! The Cossack was the cat; he was the mouse. Then it was that Rainsford knew the full meaning of terror."
  [7] Rainsford, in Zaroff's bedroom: "I am still a beast at bay," he said, in a low, hoarse voice. "Get ready, General Zaroff."

## Main: Chart the motivations
text: Row 1: what he wants at the start. Row 2: what he values most. Row 3: what he wants once the hunt begins. Row 4: excerpt numbers that prove it.
chart: Rainsford | General Zaroff
  rows: 4
  key: Rainsford: (1) to hunt for sport, without thinking about the prey [1]; (2) he still values human life: "a hunter, not a murderer" [4]; (3) to survive, then to win and stop Zaroff [6], [7]; (4) evidence 1, 4, 6, 7. Zaroff: (1) the thrill of the hunt, his "one passion" [2]; (2) challenge and his own pleasure above human life [3]; (3) to hunt a quarry who can "reason," a "worthy" opponent like Rainsford [5]; (4) evidence 2, 3, 5.
write: Zaroff's motive is the reason the plot exists. Explain how his boredom (excerpt 3) leads to the main conflict.
  lines: 4
  key: Because hunting became "too easy," Zaroff wanted quarry that could reason, so he hunts people. When Rainsford arrives, a famous hunter who refuses to join him, Zaroff makes him the quarry, and the hunt becomes the story's main conflict.
write: How does Rainsford's motivation change from excerpt 1 to excerpt 7? What causes the change?
  lines: 4
  key: At first he wants sport and does not care how the jaguar feels [1]. Once he is hunted, he knows "the full meaning of terror" [6] and wants only to survive; by the end he turns Zaroff's game against him: "Get ready, General Zaroff" [7]. Being the prey causes the change.
write: In excerpt 5, Zaroff calls the hunt "Outdoor chess." What does this reveal about how he sees the hunt, and how does Rainsford's view clash with it?
  lines: 3
  key: To Zaroff it is a game of skill, a pleasant contest. To Rainsford it is murder and a fight for his life, so their two motives (pleasure and survival) collide.

## Stretch: Theme
write: How do the clashing motivations develop a theme? State the theme and support it with two excerpts.
  lines: 5
  key: Possible theme: Seeing others only as prey can make a person cruel, and empathy may come from suffering. Rainsford dismisses the jaguar's feelings [1] but learns "the full meaning of terror" when hunted [6]; Zaroff, who never takes the prey's view, treats murder as "Outdoor chess" [5] and is destroyed by his own game. Accept other themes supported by the excerpts.

# 6. Parallel Plots Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, two colors of highlighter
ican: I can analyze how an author's choices about structure and time create suspense.
directions: Read the story, which cuts back and forth between two plot lines. Highlight each plot line in its own color, map the six sections, then explain how cutting between them builds suspense.
mistakes: Students often summarize each section without noticing where it stops. The key question for every cut is "What does the reader still not know?" Also watch for students who say the sections are in the wrong order: the time stamps show the two lines happen at the same time, and the story is told in order, just from two places.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class. Dev's line is sections 1, 3 and 5; Amara's is 2, 4 and 6; the lines meet in section 6. Each Dev section ends at a moment of doubt (will he catch the bus? will it reach the hall?), and each Amara section moves her audition closer (number nine, about 5:15), so the reader feels both clocks at once. Cross-cutting like this is common in film, where it is called parallel editing.

## Read: The Last Bus
passage: The Last Bus | numbered
  **4:31 p.m.** Dev saw the violin case the moment he walked into the kitchen. It sat on the chair by the door, exactly where his sister had left it so she would not forget it. Amara's audition for the youth orchestra started at five o'clock, across the river at Hale Music Hall, and she had left an hour early to warm up. Dev grabbed the case, checked the bus schedule taped to the fridge, and ran. The 4:40 bus was the last one that could get him there in time.
  **4:33 p.m.** Across the river, Amara sat in a hallway lined with folding chairs, her hands empty. She had checked her bag twice. Rosin, music, a pencil, a granola bar. No violin. A woman with a clipboard walked down the row, reading names. "You're number nine," she told Amara. "About five-fifteen." Amara nodded as if everything were fine. Her phone showed one bar of battery. She typed *Dev?? Violin!!* and watched the screen go black.
  **4:38 p.m.** Dev reached the corner of Fifth and Elm with his lungs burning. The bus stop was empty. Either the bus was late, or he had just missed it. He held the case against his chest and stared down the street. Far off, past the bakery, something large and blue turned the corner and came toward him. Then it stopped at a red light, and stayed there.
  **4:52 p.m.** "Number six," called the woman with the clipboard. A boy with a cello disappeared through the double doors. Amara counted on her fingers. Three more players, maybe ten minutes each. Through the doors she heard him start a scale, stop, and start again. She asked the girl next to her, a flute player, whether anyone ever borrowed an instrument for an audition. The girl gave her a sorry look. "Not a violin," she said.
  **5:06 p.m.** The bus crawled across the bridge behind a delivery truck. Dev stood by the back door, his knuckles white on the handle of the case, reading every street sign. Hale Music Hall was two stops past the bridge. When the doors finally hissed open at the first stop, he did not wait for the second. He jumped down and ran the last four blocks, the case bumping against his knees.
  **5:14 p.m.** "Number nine," said the woman. Amara stood up slowly. She opened her mouth to say she could not play, that she was sorry, that she would try again next year. Then the outside door banged open at the end of the hall, and her brother came through it, red-faced and gasping, holding up the case like a trophy. Amara laughed out loud. "One minute," she told the woman with the clipboard, and held out her hands.

  * It alternates between Dev and Amara, and the times show both lines happen at once.
  - Each section is told by a different narrator in first person.

## Main: Map the two plot lines
chart: Whose plot line | What happens | What the reader still does not know at the cut
  labels: 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6
  height: 0.55
  key: 1 Dev: finds the violin, runs for the 4:40 bus. Unknown: will he make it? 2 Amara: no violin, she is number nine at about 5:15, her phone dies. Unknown: did Dev get her message, will she play? 3 Dev: stop is empty, bus is stuck at a red light. Unknown: is that his bus, did he miss it? 4 Amara: number six goes in, three players left, no violin to borrow. Unknown: will Dev arrive in time? 5 Dev: bus slow on the bridge, he jumps off and runs. Unknown: will he reach the hall before her turn? 6 Amara: her number is called and she is about to give up when Dev bursts in. The lines meet and the question is answered.
write: Section 3 ends with the bus stopped at a red light. Why does the author cut to Amara at that moment instead of showing whether Dev catches the bus?
  lines: 3
  key: Cutting away at the moment of doubt leaves the question open, so the reader has to keep reading through Amara's section while still worrying about Dev. Her section then raises the stakes (three players left), so the suspense grows instead of resolving.
write: How do the time stamps and Amara's place in line work together to build suspense?
  lines: 3
  key: The time stamps show the clock running in both places, and Amara's slot (number nine, about 5:15) is a deadline. As the times move from 4:31 to 5:14 and the numbers count down, the reader can measure how little time Dev has left.
write: In section 6, how does the author delay the moment the two plot lines meet? Why end there?
  lines: 3
  key: Amara stands up and starts to say she cannot play, so the reader thinks Dev is too late; only then does the door bang open. The delay squeezes out the last bit of suspense, and the lines meet at the exact moment of her deadline, which is the climax and the ending.

# 17. Argumentative Essay Outline
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can plan an argument with a precise claim, counterclaims and relevant evidence from sources.
directions: Read the three sources about Riverton, a made-up city that tested a car-free downtown. Decide whether cities should ban cars downtown. Then outline your essay: a precise claim, two reasons with evidence, a counterclaim with a rebuttal, and a conclusion.
mistakes: Watch for claims that only restate the topic ("Cars downtown are an issue") or that are too broad to argue ("Cars are bad"). A precise claim takes a side and names the condition or reason. Students also tend to skip Sources B and C if they agree with A; a strong counterclaim uses the other side's best evidence and answers it, not a weak version of it.
notes: The three sources and the city of Riverton are made up by Curio Class for practice; their numbers are invented. Either side can earn full credit. A strong outline uses evidence from all three sources and cites each one by letter. The key shows one model outline for a qualified "yes" position.

## Read: Three sources
source: Source A: Riverton Department of Transportation, "One Year Car-Free: Results of the Downtown Pilot" (a made-up report)
  For one year, Riverton closed six downtown blocks to private cars. Buses, bicycles, emergency vehicles and delivery vans (before 10 a.m.) were still allowed. Compared with the year before, foot traffic on the six blocks rose 18 percent, and average sales at ground-floor shops rose 7 percent. Pedestrian injuries fell from 23 to 9. Two of the 41 shops on the blocks closed during the year.
source: Source B: a letter to the Riverton Courier from the owner of a hardware store on one of the blocks (made up)
  My customers buy paint, lumber and bags of soil. They cannot carry those onto a bus. Since the pilot began, my sales are down 12 percent, even if the city's average went up. Cafes may love the crowds, but a shop that sells heavy goods needs a place for customers to load a car. Ban cars, and you choose which businesses get to survive.
source: Source C: a statement from a Riverton disability rights group (made up)
  We support safer, quieter streets, and many of our members walk and roll downtown more often now. But the pilot removed all 14 accessible parking spaces on the six blocks, and buses stop running at 10 p.m. A car-free downtown must not become a downtown some residents cannot reach. Any permanent plan needs accessible parking at the edges and drop-off zones on every block.

## Warm up: A precise claim
choice: Which claim is the most precise and arguable?
  - Cars downtown are a big issue in many cities today.
  - Cars are bad for the environment and for people.
  * Cities should close downtown streets to private cars, while keeping drop-off zones and accessible parking, because car-free streets are safer and busier.
  - This essay will talk about whether cities should ban cars downtown.
fill:
  - The source with numbers from the whole pilot: Source {A}
  - The source that argues a ban hurts some businesses: Source {B}
  - The source that supports the goal but asks for conditions: Source {C}

## Main: Your outline
chart: Your plan (cite sources by letter)
  labels: Claim; Reason 1; Evidence for reason 1; Reason 2; Evidence for reason 2; Counterclaim; Evidence for the counterclaim; Rebuttal; Conclusion
  height: 0.6
  key: Model (qualified yes): Claim: Cities should close downtown streets to private cars, with drop-off zones and accessible parking at the edges, because car-free streets are safer and good for most businesses. Reason 1: Car-free streets are safer. Evidence: pedestrian injuries fell from 23 to 9 (A). Reason 2: More people come downtown and most shops do better. Evidence: foot traffic up 18 percent, average ground-floor sales up 7 percent (A); members of the disability group walk and roll downtown more (C). Counterclaim: A ban hurts shops that sell heavy goods and shuts out people who need a car. Evidence: hardware sales down 12 percent (B); all 14 accessible spaces removed and buses stop at 10 p.m. (C). Rebuttal: These are problems of design, not reasons to keep cars: loading and drop-off zones, accessible parking at the edges and later bus service answer them, while the safety gains stay. Conclusion: Restate the qualified claim and the stakes: a downtown should be safe and open to everyone, so ban cars but plan for access.

## Stretch: Strengthen it
write: Source B says "you choose which businesses get to survive." Is that a fair point, a weak point, or partly fair? Explain using Source A.
  lines: 3
  key: Partly fair. Source A shows average sales rose 7 percent, so most shops gained, but an average can hide losers, and two of 41 shops closed. Source B is right that the effect is uneven; it does not prove a ban hurts business overall.
write: Write a one-sentence hook that opens your essay without saying "In this essay."
  lines: 2
  key: In one year, a single downtown cut its pedestrian injuries by more than half, simply by taking the cars away.

# 24. Grammar and Style Check
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pen
ican: I can find and fix errors in parallel structure, phrases and clauses, and explain each correction.
directions: Read the essay. Each numbered sentence has one error: faulty parallelism, a dangling or misplaced modifier, a fragment, a comma splice, or a run-on. For each number, name the error and write the corrected words; item numbers match the sentence numbers.
mistakes: Students often fix a dangling modifier by moving words around without adding the missing doer: "Growing up in the city" needs "I" right after the comma. For parallelism, check every item in a list against the first one (noun, noun, noun or verb, verb, verb). A fragment that starts with "Because" or "Which" can usually be joined to the sentence next to it.

# 29. Theme Analysis Essay Planner
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter, your copy of The Odyssey
ican: I can plan an essay that develops a theme with evidence and commentary.
directions: Read the evidence bank from Samuel Butler's translation of The Odyssey. Choose a theme statement, then plan an essay with a thesis, three points, evidence for each, and commentary that explains how the evidence shows the theme.
mistakes: Students often write a topic ("self-control") instead of a theme (a complete statement about it). Watch also for commentary that only restates the quotation ("This shows he is angry"). Commentary should explain how the words build the theme. Butler uses Roman names: Ulysses is Odysseus, Jove is Zeus.
notes: Passages follow Samuel Butler's prose translation (1900), Project Gutenberg eBook #1727. Class editions use other translations, so wording differs; students may cite their own edition instead. Passage 1 comes from the poem's opening, 2 from Jove's speech to the gods in Book I, 3 from Book IX (the Cyclops), 4 from Book X (Aeolus's bag of winds) and 5 from the start of Book XX, when Ulysses, disguised in his own house, holds back his anger at the suitors' household.

## Evidence bank
source: Homer, The Odyssey, translated by Samuel Butler (1900)
  [1] Book I: "he suffered much by sea while trying to save his own life and bring his men safely home; but do what he might he could not save his men, for they perished through their own sheer folly in eating the cattle of the Sun-god Hyperion"
  [2] Book I, Jove speaking: "See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly."
  [3] Book IX, Ulysses shouting from his ship: "Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca."
  [4] Book X: "Thus they talked and evil counsels prevailed. They loosed the sack, whereupon the wind flew howling forth and raised a storm that carried us weeping out to sea and away from our own country."
  [5] Book XX: "he beat his breast and said, 'Heart, be still, you had worse than this to bear on the day when the terrible Cyclops ate your brave companions; yet you bore it in silence till your cunning got you safe out of the cave'"

## Main: Plan the essay
write: Thesis: state the theme and how the poem develops it.
  lines: 3
  key: The Odyssey argues that giving in to impulse costs people dearly while self-control brings them home, a lesson Ulysses learns as his own and his men's reckless choices cause disaster and his hard-won restraint finally wins back his house.
chart: Evidence (passage number and words) | Commentary: how this evidence develops the theme
  labels: Point 1; Point 2; Point 3
  height: 1.2
  key: Point 1: The men's impulses destroy them. Evidence: they "loosed the sack" out of greed [4]; they "perished through their own sheer folly" [1]. Commentary: Greed and hunger win over Ulysses's orders, and each time the result is loss; the word "folly" names the cause as their own choice. Point 2: Even Ulysses suffers when pride beats patience. Evidence: he shouts his full name to the Cyclops [3]. Commentary: Naming himself, "the valiant warrior Ulysses," wins him glory but lets the Cyclops call down his father Neptune's anger, which lengthens the journey home. Point 3: Restraint brings him home. Evidence: "Heart, be still ... you bore it in silence" [5]. Commentary: He remembers that patience saved him in the cave and holds back, so he can plan; the poem shows he has learned the lesson his men never did. Jove's speech [2] supports all three: people blame the gods for "their own folly."

## Stretch: Answer a counterclaim
write: Someone says the gods, not people's choices, decide what happens in The Odyssey. Use passage 2 to answer them.
  lines: 3
  key: Jove himself says men "lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly." The poem's ruler of the gods puts the responsibility on human choices, which supports the theme that self-control, not fate alone, decides who gets home.
