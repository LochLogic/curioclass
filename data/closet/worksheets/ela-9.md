# 1. Conflicting Motivations: Rainsford and Zaroff
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, highlighter, a copy of the full story
ican: I can analyze how characters with conflicting motivations drive the plot and develop a theme.
directions: After reading "The Most Dangerous Game" by Richard Connell (1924), read the seven excerpts. Chart what Rainsford and General Zaroff each want and value, then explain how the clash between them drives the plot.
mistakes: Students often say the two men are simply opposites. The story's point is that they start out alike: both are hunters who see animals as sport (excerpts 1 and 2). Rainsford's motivation changes when he becomes the hunted; Zaroff's never does. Watch for answers that list events without naming the motive behind them.
notes: The excerpts follow the 1924 text as printed in O. Henry Memorial Award Prize Stories of 1924 (Wikisource), with curly quotation marks made straight. The story describes a man who hunts people; keep the discussion on motive and theme. Zaroff also says cruel and racist things about the men he hunts; this sheet does not quote them, but if students raise them, name them as part of how Connell shows Zaroff's contempt for human life.

## Read: Seven excerpts
source: Richard Connell, "The Most Dangerous Game," Collier's, 1924
  [1] Rainsford, on the yacht: "Don't talk rot, Whitney," said Rainsford. "You're a big-game hunter, not a philosopher. Who cares how a jaguar feels?"
  [2] Zaroff, at dinner: "I have but one passion in my life, Mr. Rainsford, and it is the hunt."
  [3] Zaroff: "Simply this: hunting had ceased to be what you call 'a sporting proposition.' It had become too easy. I always got my quarry. Always. There is no greater bore than perfection."
  [4] Rainsford: "Thank you, I'm a hunter, not a murderer."
  [5] Zaroff: "Your brain against mine. Your wood-craft against mine. Your strength and stamina against mine. Outdoor chess!"
  [6] "The general was playing with him! The general was saving him for another day's sport! The Cossack was the cat; he was the mouse. Then it was that Rainsford knew the full meaning of terror."
  [7] Rainsford, in Zaroff's bedroom: "I am still a beast at bay," he said, in a low, hoarse voice. "Get ready, General Zaroff."

## Main: Chart the motivations
text: Row 1: what he wants at the start. Row 2: what he values most. Row 3: what he wants once the hunt begins. Row 4: excerpt numbers that prove it.
chart: Rainsford | General Zaroff
  rows: 4
  key: Rainsford: (1) to hunt for sport, without thinking about the prey [1]; (2) he still values human life: "a hunter, not a murderer" [4]; (3) to survive, then to win and stop Zaroff [6], [7]; (4) evidence 1, 4, 6, 7. Zaroff: (1) the thrill of the hunt, his "one passion" [2]; (2) challenge and his own pleasure above human life [3]; (3) to hunt a quarry who can "reason," a "worthy" opponent like Rainsford [5]; (4) evidence 2, 3, 5.
write: Zaroff's motive is the reason the plot exists. Explain how his boredom (excerpt 3) leads to the main conflict.
  lines: 4
  key: Because hunting became "too easy," Zaroff wanted quarry that could reason, so he hunts people. When Rainsford arrives, a famous hunter who refuses to join him, Zaroff makes him the quarry, and the hunt becomes the story's main conflict.
write: How does Rainsford's motivation change from excerpt 1 to excerpt 7? What causes the change?
  lines: 4
  key: At first he wants sport and does not care how the jaguar feels [1]. Once he is hunted, he knows "the full meaning of terror" [6] and wants only to survive; by the end he turns Zaroff's game against him: "Get ready, General Zaroff" [7]. Being the prey causes the change.
write: In excerpt 5, Zaroff calls the hunt "Outdoor chess." What does this reveal about how he sees the hunt, and how does Rainsford's view clash with it?
  lines: 3
  key: To Zaroff it is a game of skill, a pleasant contest. To Rainsford it is murder and a fight for his life, so their two motives (pleasure and survival) collide.

## Stretch: Theme
write: How do the clashing motivations develop a theme? State the theme and support it with two excerpts.
  lines: 5
  key: Possible theme: Seeing others only as prey can make a person cruel, and empathy may come from suffering. Rainsford dismisses the jaguar's feelings [1] but learns "the full meaning of terror" when hunted [6]; Zaroff, who never takes the prey's view, treats murder as "Outdoor chess" [5] and is destroyed by his own game. Accept other themes supported by the excerpts.
# 24. Grammar and Style Check
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pen
ican: I can find and fix errors in parallel structure, phrases and clauses, and explain each correction.
directions: Read the essay. Each numbered sentence has one error: faulty parallelism, a dangling or misplaced modifier, a fragment, a comma splice, or a run-on. For each number, name the error and write the corrected words; item numbers match the sentence numbers.
mistakes: Students often fix a dangling modifier by moving words around without adding the missing doer: "Growing up in the city" needs "I" right after the comma. For parallelism, check every item in a list against the first one (noun, noun, noun or verb, verb, verb). A fragment that starts with "Because" or "Which" can usually be joined to the sentence next to it.
notes: The essay is original to Curio Class (about 330 words). Six parallelism errors (1, 3, 6, 10, 13, 14), five modifier errors (2, 5, 8, 11, 15), two fragments (4, 12), one comma splice (7) and one run-on (9). Accept any correction that fixes the error and keeps the meaning.

## Read
passage: Why Every School Needs a Garden
  School gardens are more than rows of vegetables. **(1)** They teach students science, how to work as a team, and being patient. **(2)** Growing up in the city, a garden was something I had never seen until sixth grade. Now I believe every school should have one.
  First, gardens make science real. **(3)** Students measure plant growth, testing the soil, and they record the weather. Instead of reading about photosynthesis, they watch it happen. **(4)** Because plants need sunlight, water and nutrients. Students learn to plan carefully. **(5)** Hidden under the leaves, we found the first green tomatoes of the year.
  Second, gardens build teamwork. **(6)** Some students dig, others are watering, and a few of them keeping the planting chart. **(7)** Our class grew tomatoes, we sold them at the school fair. **(8)** Working together, the tomatoes grew bigger than anyone expected.
  Third, gardens teach patience. **(9)** A seed does not become a plant overnight it can take weeks. **(10)** Students learn that waiting, watching, and to care for something pays off. **(11)** On the first day, the teacher handed a seedling to each student wrapped in a wet paper towel.

  **(15)** Having seen what one garden did for my class, every school should plant one. A garden grows more than food.
