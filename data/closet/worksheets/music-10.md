# 1. SATB Ranges and Spacing
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can keep four-part chords in range and correctly spaced.
directions: Use the range chart to name the range notes and mark the four ranges on a grand staff. Then find the error in each four-part chord, if there is one, and rewrite the chord correctly.
mistakes: Students often apply the octave rule to tenor and bass too: those two may be more than an octave apart (chord 5 is correct for this reason). Watch for corrections that fix the spacing but drop the chord's third; check every corrected chord still has a root, third and fifth.
notes: Ranges used here (textbooks differ by a step or two): soprano C4 to G5, alto G3 to D5, tenor C3 to G4, bass E2 to D4. Spacing: soprano and alto no more than an octave apart, alto and tenor no more than an octave apart; tenor and bass may be farther apart. No voice crosses above the voice written above it. Pitches use scientific names (C4 = middle C). Other corrections can be right if they fix the error, keep every voice in range, and keep the chord complete. On the staffs, the range notes in order are: soprano low C4 and high G5, alto high D5, tenor high G4; then alto low G3, tenor low C3, bass low E2.

## Warm up: The four ranges
example: Soprano C4 to G5 · Alto G3 to D5 · Tenor C3 to G4 · Bass E2 to D4 (C4 = middle C)
text: Name each range note (letter only). Treble staff: soprano low, soprano high, alto high, tenor high.
staff: treble
  - C4
  - G5
  - D5
  - G4
text: Bass staff: alto low, tenor low, bass low.
staff: bass
  - G3
  - C3

draw: Draw a grand staff. Bracket each voice's range and label it S, A, T or B. | 2.4

## Main: Fix the spacing
text: Each chord is written soprano, alto, tenor, bass. Name the error (spacing, crossing, or none), then write a corrected chord.
table: Chord | S | A | T | B | Error | Corrected (S A T B)
  - 1 (C) | E5 | C5 | G3 | C3 | {alto and tenor more than an octave apart} | {E5 C5 G4 C3}
  - 2 (C) | G5 | E4 | C4 | C3 | {soprano and alto more than an octave apart} | {G4 E4 C4 C3}
  - 3 (C) | C5 | E5 | G4 | C3 | {alto above soprano (crossing)} | {E5 C5 G4 C3}
  - 4 (G) | B4 | G4 | D3 | G3 | {tenor below bass (crossing); alto and tenor also more than an octave apart} | {B4 G4 D4 G3}
  - 5 (C) | C5 | G4 | E4 | C3 | {none: tenor and bass may be more than an octave apart} | {C5 G4 E4 C3}
  - 6 (G) | D5 | B4 | G3 | G2 | {alto and tenor more than an octave apart} | {D5 B4 G4 G2}

## Stretch: Why the rules?
write: Why may the tenor and bass be more than an octave apart when the upper voices may not?
  lines: 3
  key: Low notes close together sound muddy, so wide spacing at the bottom keeps the chord clear, the way the overtone series spaces its lowest partials far apart. Wide gaps between upper voices make the chord sound hollow and the voices stop blending.
write: In chord 1, could you fix the error by moving the alto down instead of the tenor up? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Yes. Moving the alto down to G4 (E5 G4 G3 C3) puts soprano and alto a 6th apart and alto and tenor an octave apart, all in range, and the chord still has C, E and G. It doubles the fifth (G) instead of the root, which is allowed but less usual in a root-position chord, so moving the tenor up to G4 (doubling C) is the more common fix.

# 7. Modulating to the Dominant
minutes: 35
materials: pencil; a keyboard to play the progressions (optional)
ican: I can find where a phrase modulates to the dominant, label the pivot chord in both keys, and name the new key.
directions: Study the example. For each of the 3 Classical-style progressions (one chord per measure), find the first new accidental, find the pivot chord just before it, label the pivot in both keys, and name the new key.
mistakes: Students often call the first chord with the new accidental the pivot. The pivot comes before it: a chord that belongs to both keys, heard one way and then reheard the other way. Watch for Roman numerals that keep the old key after the pivot (calling D7 "II7" in C instead of V7 in G).
notes: These are short chord progressions in Classical style written for this sheet, not quotations, so students can find the pivot without reading a full score. Play each one on a keyboard so students hear the turn. Answers: 1. C major to G major, first new accidental F# in D7 (m. 6), pivot Am in m. 5 (vi in C, ii in G). 2. F major to C major, first new note B natural in G7 (m. 6), pivot Dm in m. 5 (vi in F, ii in C). 3. D major to A major, first new accidental G# in E7 (m. 5), pivot D in m. 4 (I in D, IV in A). A good real example to follow up with: the exposition of Mozart's Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, first movement, whose second theme arrives in G major, the dominant.

## Warm up: Shared chords
example: C major and G major share four triads. Am is vi in C and ii in G. When Am is followed by D7 (with F#), the ear rehears Am as ii in G: Am is the **pivot chord**.

  - It uses the old key signature.

## Stretch: Write your own
write: Write a 6 or 7 measure progression in G major that modulates to D major. Mark the pivot chord and label it in both keys.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: G, C, D7, G, Em, A7, D. Pivot: Em in m. 5 (vi in G, ii in D). A7 brings C#, and A7 to D is the cadence in D major.

# 20. Classical Style Listening Log
minutes: 45
materials: pencil; recordings of the four works
ican: I can hear and describe the traits of Classical style: balanced phrases, homophony, dynamic contrast and clear forms.
directions: Listen to each work at least once (the first 2 to 3 minutes is enough for a long one). Log what you hear: the phrases, the texture, the dynamics and the form. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students label every texture "melody" or call a melody with chords "polyphonic." In homophony, one tune leads and the other parts accompany it with chords; in polyphony, several independent tunes weave together. Watch for vague dynamics ("loud and soft"): ask where and how it changes, suddenly or gradually.
notes: The works: Haydn, Symphony No. 94 in G major ("Surprise"), second movement, a theme and variations, with a famous sudden loud chord after a very soft repeat of the theme. Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525 (1787), first movement, in sonata form, for strings. Mozart, Horn Concerto No. 4 in E-flat, K. 495, third movement, a rondo in a lively 6/8 with a recurring hunting-horn theme. Beethoven, Symphony No. 1 in C, Op. 21 (1800), third movement, marked Menuetto but so fast it is close to a scherzo, in minuet and trio form. The key gives what students should hear; accept other accurate details.

## Warm up: The traits
match: Match each Classical trait to what you hear.
  - Balanced phrases = A short "question" phrase answered by a matching "answer" phrase, often 4 + 4 measures
  - Homophony = One clear melody on top, with chords underneath
  - Crescendo = Music growing gradually louder
  - Sudden contrast = Loud changes to soft (or soft to loud) all at once
  - Rondo form = A main theme that keeps returning between new sections (A B A C A)

## Main: My log
table: Work | Phrases: what I hear | Texture | Dynamics: where they change | Form
  height: 0.9
  - Haydn, Symphony No. 94, mvt. 2 | {short, balanced 4-bar phrases} | {homophonic} | {soft theme, softer repeat, then a sudden very loud chord} | {theme and variations}
  - Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, mvt. 1 | {bold rising opening answered by a smooth phrase} | {homophonic, strings} | {loud opening, softer second theme} | {sonata form}
  - Mozart, Horn Concerto No. 4, mvt. 3 | {bouncy 6/8 hunting tune in matching phrases} | {homophonic: horn melody over orchestra} | {soft solo answered by loud full orchestra} | {rondo}
  - Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, mvt. 3 | {short phrases that rush upward} | {mostly homophonic} | {quick crescendos, sudden accents} | {minuet and trio}

## Questions
choice: In which work did you hear a main theme return again and again between new sections?
  - Haydn, Symphony No. 94
  * Mozart, Horn Concerto No. 4
  - Beethoven, Symphony No. 1
choice: Which trait makes the "Surprise" Symphony's surprise work?
  * Sudden dynamic contrast after a very soft passage
  - A change from homophony to a fugue
  - A sudden change of meter
write: Which work sounded the least like the others? Explain using two traits from your log.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Beethoven's third movement, because its phrases rush forward faster than a dance and its dynamics jump with sudden accents and quick crescendos, while Mozart's phrases stay balanced and his dynamic changes are calmer. It hints at the more dramatic style Beethoven developed later.

## Stretch
write: Pick a Baroque piece you know. Name two ways it differs from one of these Classical works.
  lines: 3
  key: Model: Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 has a basso continuo with harpsichord and long spun-out melodies in polyphonic texture, while Eine kleine Nachtmusik has short balanced phrases over simple chords. Bach's dynamics stay at one level for a while (terraced), while Mozart contrasts loud and soft themes.

# 21. History Checkpoint 10
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can compare Baroque and Classical music using traits, sources and works.
directions: Sort the traits, match works to their composers and eras, read the source, and write a paragraph comparing the two eras.
mistakes: Students often call any old-sounding music "Classical." Remind them Classical is also the name of one era (about 1750 to 1820). Watch for trait sorting by instrument alone: both eras used strings, so the deciding clue is how the music is built (continuo and one steady mood versus balanced phrases and contrasting themes).
notes: Baroque about 1600 to 1750; Classical about 1750 to 1820. Work dates: Bach's Brandenburg Concertos were dedicated in 1721; Vivaldi's The Four Seasons was published in 1725; Handel's Water Music was first played in 1717; Haydn's "Surprise" Symphony (No. 94) premiered in London in 1792; Mozart's Symphony No. 40 was finished in 1788. The source is a paraphrase, not a quotation, of Mozart's letter to his father from Augsburg in October 1777, praising the pianos built by Johann Andreas Stein; check a published translation of the Mozart family letters if you want to read the exact words aloud.

## Warm up: Sort the traits
sort: Cut out each trait and glue it under its era.
  - Baroque: basso continuo, terraced dynamics, one mood per movement, fugue, harpsichord at the center
  - Classical: balanced four-bar phrases, sonata form, gradual crescendos, piano replaces harpsichord, the string quartet

## Main: Works and a source
match: Match each work to its composer.
  - Brandenburg Concertos = Johann Sebastian Bach
  - The Four Seasons = Antonio Vivaldi
  - Water Music = George Frideric Handel
  - "Surprise" Symphony (No. 94) = Joseph Haydn
  - Symphony No. 40 in G minor = Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart
fill:
  - The Four Seasons belongs to the {Baroque} era.
  - Symphony No. 40 belongs to the {Classical} era.
  - Two of the five works are Classical: the {Surprise} Symphony and Symphony No. {40}.
source: Adapted from a letter by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart to his father, Augsburg, October 1777 (paraphrased, not his exact words)
  I used to like Späth's keyboards best, but now I prefer Stein's. [1] They stop the sound much better: whether I strike a key hard or gently, the tone is even and never jangles, and the sound stops the moment I let go. [2]
  The instrument has a special device so the hammer falls back from the string right after it strikes, which lets the player control each note. [3]
choice: What does the source show about keyboard instruments around 1777?
  - Pianos had disappeared.
  * New pianos let players control loud and soft and how long notes sound.
  - Mozart preferred the harpsichord to every piano.
write: How does a piano like Stein's help explain the gradual crescendos and contrasting moods of Classical music? Use the source.
  lines: 3
  key: The source says the tone stays even and controllable whether the key is struck hard or gently [1, 2], so a player can make each note louder or softer. That makes gradual crescendos and quick contrasts possible on a keyboard, unlike the harpsichord, whose loudness barely changes with touch.

## Stretch: Compare the eras
write: Write a paragraph comparing Baroque and Classical music. Include at least two traits, one work from each era, and one piece of evidence from the source.
  lines: 8
  key: Answers vary. A strong paragraph contrasts specific traits with works, for example: "Baroque music such as Bach's Brandenburg Concertos is built over a basso continuo, keeps one mood through a movement, and changes dynamics in sudden steps. Classical music such as Mozart's Symphony No. 40 uses balanced phrases, contrasting themes in sonata form, and gradual crescendos. Part of the change came from instruments: Mozart's 1777 letter praises Stein's pianos because their tone could be controlled hard or soft, something the harpsichord could not do."

# 33. Sixty-Second Sight-Reading Scan
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, 4 short sight-reading excerpts (8 to 16 measures, from any sight-reading book, one copy each), a timer
ican: I can scan new music in 60 seconds for its key, meter, accidentals and hardest rhythms before I play it.
directions: Learn the 5-step scan with the warm up. Your teacher hands you 4 short excerpts, one at a time. For each one, scan for 60 seconds and fill the row, then play it once without stopping. Last, reflect on what the scan caught.
mistakes: Students spend the whole minute on one hard measure; the scan is a quick sweep of the whole excerpt first. Watch for key signatures read without checking the last note (two sharps could be D major or B minor). Many forget that an accidental lasts until the bar line and must be reapplied in the next measure.
notes: Choose 4 excerpts slightly below the students' playing level with different keys and meters (for example one in 6/8 and one in cut time), and hand them out face down. Time each scan out loud. The 5 steps: Key (signature, then the last note to tell major from minor), Meter (time signature and what gets the beat), Accidentals (circle every one), Rhythms (find the hardest and tap it), Road map (repeats, endings, tempo and dynamics). Keep going when you play: no stopping to fix a wrong note.

## Warm up: The scan
example: Scan order: **K**ey, **M**eter, **A**ccidentals, **R**hythms, **R**oad map. Spend about 10 seconds on each, and 10 seconds hearing the start in your head.
fill:

  - In 6/8, count {2} big beats per measure, each a dotted quarter.
  - In cut time, there are {2} beats per measure and the {half} note gets the beat.
  - An accidental lasts until the {bar line}.
