# 1. Source Scout Field Guide
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can tell primary sources from secondary sources and say what each is good for.
directions: A primary source was made by someone who was there, at the time. A secondary source was made later by someone who studied it. For each source, write P for primary or S for secondary, then write one thing that source is good for.
mistakes: Students often decide by how old a source looks or by format ("a book is secondary"). The test is who made it and when: a diary is primary only for the events its writer lived through. Some think secondary means worse; each kind has strengths, and historians use both.
notes: The strengths column has many right answers; the key gives one model for each. Primary strengths: first-hand detail, feelings and words of the time. Secondary strengths: big picture, many sources compared, background explained. Encyclopedias are sometimes called tertiary sources; for this grade, treat them as secondary.

## Warm up: Who made it, and when?
example: A letter a soldier wrote home during the American Revolution: made by someone who was there, at the time. **Primary.**
  A video about the Revolution made in 2020: made long after, by people who studied it. **Secondary.**

## Main: Scout 10 sources
table: Source | P or S | One thing it is good for
  - A diary kept by a girl on the Oregon Trail in 1852 | {P} | {what the trip felt like day by day}
  - A social studies textbook | {S} | {a clear summary of many events}
  - An encyclopedia entry about the Gold Rush | {S} | {quick facts and dates}
  - A photo taken at a 1963 civil rights march | {P} | {seeing what really happened}
  - A blog post written this year about the Gold Rush | {S} | {an easy explanation, but check its sources}
  - A newspaper story printed the day after an event | {P} | {what people knew at the time}
  - A map an explorer drew during his trip | {P} | {what the explorer saw and believed}
  - A biography of Harriet Tubman written in 2010 | {S} | {her whole life, from many sources}
  - A speech recorded as it was given | {P} | {the speaker's exact words}
  - A documentary film made 100 years after a war | {S} | {the big picture, with experts}

## Stretch: Can it be both?
write: A newspaper from 1969 tells about the Moon landing that week. Is it primary or secondary for the Moon landing? Could the same newspaper be a secondary source for something else? Explain.
  lines: 4
  key: Primary for the Moon landing, because it was made at the time by people living through it. If the same paper ran a story retelling the Revolutionary War, that story is secondary, because the writer was not there. Whether a source is primary depends on the question you ask.

# 6. Environment Shapes Life
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, a map of North American culture regions
ican: I can explain how the environment shaped the food, homes and clothing of Native nations in different regions.
directions: Long ago, each Native nation used what its land, water and climate offered. Use the clue bank to complete the chart for 5 regions: one nation from each, and its food, homes and clothing. Then explain one pattern you see.
mistakes: Students often give every nation tipis and bison, a picture from movies. Point to each region's environment first: there were no bison herds on the Northwest Coast and almost no trees in the Arctic. Watch for students who speak of these nations only in the past tense; they are still here today, living in modern homes and keeping many traditions.
notes: The chart describes traditional ways of life before and around European contact; it simplifies a lot (each region held many nations with different ways). Facts used: Tlingit and Haida fished for salmon, built plank houses of red cedar and wove capes from cedar bark; the Lakota, after horses arrived in the 1700s, followed bison herds, lived in tipis covered with bison hides and wore tanned hide clothing; the Hopi farmed corn, beans, squash and cotton, built stone and adobe pueblos and wove cotton cloth; the Haudenosaunee grew corn, beans and squash (the Three Sisters), hunted deer, built bark-covered longhouses and wore deerskin; the Inuit hunted seals, whales and caribou, built snow houses for winter hunting trips and sod houses, and sewed caribou and sealskin parkas.

## Warm up: Use what you have
choice: Why did Plains nations cover their homes with bison hides instead of cedar planks?
  - Bison hides lasted longer than wood.
  * Bison were plentiful on the grasslands, and tall trees were few.
  - Cedar planks were against the law.
choice: What did the Northwest Coast's forests and rivers give the people who lived there?
  - bison and cotton
  - snow and ice
  * cedar trees and salmon

## Main: Complete the chart
text: **Clue bank:** salmon; corn, beans, squash and deer; bison; corn, beans, squash and cotton; seals, whales and caribou; plank houses of cedar; longhouses covered in bark; tipis covered with bison hides; pueblos of stone and adobe; snow houses and sod houses; capes woven from cedar bark; deerskin clothing; clothing of tanned hides; woven cotton cloth; caribou and sealskin parkas
table: Region and environment | Nation | Food | Homes | Clothing
  height: 0.62
  - Northwest Coast: rainy forests, rivers and ocean | Tlingit or Haida | {salmon} | {plank houses of cedar} | {capes woven from cedar bark}
  - Great Plains: wide grasslands, few trees | Lakota | {bison} | {tipis covered with bison hides} | {clothing of tanned hides}
  - Southwest: dry desert and mesas | Hopi | {corn, beans, squash and cotton} | {pueblos of stone and adobe} | {woven cotton cloth}
  - Eastern Woodlands: forests, lakes, good soil | Haudenosaunee | {corn, beans, squash and deer} | {longhouses covered in bark} | {deerskin clothing}
  - Arctic: icy, treeless, long winters | Inuit | {seals, whales and caribou} | {snow houses and sod houses} | {caribou and sealskin parkas}

## Stretch: Find the pattern
write: Choose two regions. Explain how a difference in their environments led to a difference in homes or clothing.
  lines: 4
  key: For example: "The Northwest Coast had huge cedar forests, so the Tlingit built big plank houses. The Arctic has almost no trees, so the Inuit built houses from snow and sod. Each people built with what their land gave them."

# 18. Triangular Trade Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils, a world map
ican: I can explain the three legs of the Atlantic trade and who profited from it.
directions: In the 1600s and 1700s, ships crossed the Atlantic Ocean in a pattern shaped like a triangle. Read about each leg. Draw the triangle with arrows on the map sketch, label each leg with what it carried, and then explain who profited and who paid the price.
mistakes: Students sometimes write that enslaved Africans "traveled" or "came" to the Americas. They were kidnapped and forced; use words like enslaved, captured and forced. Others put the legs in the wrong order; the ships' route ran Europe to Africa, Africa to the Americas, the Americas back to Europe. Watch for the idea that only plantation owners profited; merchants, ship owners, bankers, insurers and whole colonial economies, including in New England, profited too.
notes: Hard history: keep it factual, center the people who were enslaved, and do not role-play any part of the trade. Figures follow the SlaveVoyages database: about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships between the 1500s and the 1860s, and about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. Olaudah Equiano's 1789 autobiography describes being kidnapped as a child and the crowded, airless hold of a slave ship; the passage on this sheet is a paraphrase, not a quotation. (Some historians question whether Equiano was born in Africa; his account is still one of the best-known sources.) The routes were not one fixed triangle; many New England ships carried rum to Africa and brought molasses back from the Caribbean.

## Read: Three legs
text: **Leg 1, Europe to West Africa:** Ships from Britain, Portugal, France and other countries carried cloth, guns, iron and other goods to trade for captured African people.
text: **Leg 2, the Middle Passage, West Africa to the Americas:** Enslaved Africans were chained below deck for a voyage of many weeks. About 12.5 million people were forced onto ships, and about 1.8 million of them died on the way. Olaudah Equiano, who was kidnapped as a child, later wrote about the crowded, airless hold where many people became sick (adapted).
text: **Leg 3, the Americas to Europe:** Ships carried sugar, molasses, tobacco, rice and cotton grown by enslaved people on plantations back to Europe, where they were sold.

## Main: Map and label
draw: Map sketch: write EUROPE at the top right, AFRICA at the bottom right and THE AMERICAS on the left. Draw the triangle with arrows and label each leg. | 2.6
note: Model map: an arrow from Europe down to West Africa labeled cloth, guns, iron; an arrow from West Africa across to the Americas labeled enslaved African people (the Middle Passage); an arrow from the Americas up to Europe labeled sugar, molasses, tobacco, rice, cotton.
table: Leg | From | To | What the ships carried
  - 1 | {Europe} | {West Africa} | {cloth, guns, iron and other goods}
  - 2 | {West Africa} | {the Americas} | {enslaved African people}
  - 3 | {the Americas} | {Europe} | {sugar, molasses, tobacco, rice and cotton}
choice: What was the Middle Passage?
  - the trip carrying sugar to Europe
  * the forced voyage of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic
  - a road across Africa
fill:
  - About 12.5 million people were forced onto ships and about 1.8 million died. About how many survived the voyage? {10.7 million}

## Questions: Who profited?
write: Name 3 groups who made money from this trade.
  lines: 2
  key: Merchants and ship owners, plantation owners in the Americas, factory owners and bankers in Europe, rum makers and traders in New England.
write: Who paid the highest price, and what did they lose?
  lines: 3
  key: The enslaved African people. They lost their freedom, their families and homelands, and often their lives, and they were paid nothing for their work. Their communities in Africa lost millions of people.

## Stretch: Resistance
write: Enslaved Africans resisted on the ships and on plantations. Why is it important to include their resistance when we tell this history?
  lines: 3
  key: It shows enslaved people as people who fought for their freedom and dignity, not just as victims or cargo. Revolts on ships, escapes and the Haitian Revolution are part of the story.

# 25. Road to Revolution Timeline
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can put the events that led to the American Revolution in order and explain which mattered most.
directions: Write the letter of each event in its place on the timeline. Then circle the letters of the 3 events you think were the biggest turning points and explain one choice.
mistakes: Students often place the Declaration of Independence before the first battles; fighting at Lexington and Concord began more than a year earlier (April 1775). Watch the years 1773 to 1777, which sit close together: count tick marks carefully.
notes: Turning points have no single right answer. Strong picks: Lexington and Concord (talk turns to war), the Declaration of Independence (the goal becomes independence), Saratoga (France joins the war as an ally in 1778) and Yorktown (the British army surrenders; the Treaty of Paris of 1783 ends the war). Grade on reasoning. Worth adding in discussion: enslaved and free Black Americans fought on both sides, and many Native nations sided with Britain, hoping to keep settlers off their lands.

## Warm up: Before you place
choice: Which war ended in 1763 and left Britain deep in debt?
  - The War of 1812
  * The French and Indian War
  - The Civil War
choice: Why did Parliament pass new taxes on the colonies after 1763?
  * To help pay for the war and for soldiers in North America
  - To pay for a new navy for the colonies
  - To punish the colonies for the Boston Tea Party

## Main: Place 10 events
timeline: 1754 1781 3
  - 1754 | Fighting begins in the Ohio River Valley: the French and Indian War
  - 1763 | Treaty of Paris ends the French and Indian War
  - 1765 | Parliament passes the Stamp Act
  - 1770 | Boston Massacre: British soldiers kill five colonists
  - 1773 | Boston Tea Party
  - 1774 | First Continental Congress meets in Philadelphia
  - 1775 | Battles of Lexington and Concord
  - 1776 | Declaration of Independence is adopted
  - 1777 | American victory at Saratoga
  - 1781 | British surrender at Yorktown

## Stretch: Turning points
write: Circle 3 turning points on the timeline. Pick one and explain why it changed the direction of events.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: Saratoga, because the American victory convinced France to join the war in 1778, bringing money, soldiers and a navy that helped win at Yorktown. Also strong: Lexington and Concord (protest became war), the Declaration (the goal became independence), Yorktown (Britain gave up the fight).

# 31. Three Branches Flow Chart
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain the job of each branch of government and how each one checks the others.
directions: The Constitution splits the national government into three branches so no one branch gets too much power. Complete each branch's box. Then use the word bank to fill in the flow chart of checks: one check each branch has on each of the other two.
mistakes: Students often say the president makes laws. Congress writes and passes laws; the president signs or vetoes them. Some think the Supreme Court can strike down any law it dislikes; it rules on whether a law follows the Constitution, and only when a real case reaches it.
notes: Checks used: Congress can override a veto by a two-thirds vote in both houses; the Senate confirms or rejects the president's choices for judges (and Congress can impeach and remove a president or a judge); the president can veto a bill and nominates federal judges; the courts can rule a law or a president's action unconstitutional (judicial review, from Marbury v. Madison, 1803). Accept other true checks, such as the Senate approving treaties or impeachment.

## Warm up: The three branches
table: Branch | Who is in it | Main job
  - Legislative | {Congress: the Senate and the House of Representatives} | {makes the laws}
  - Executive | {the president, vice president and departments} | {carries out and enforces the laws}
  - Judicial | {the Supreme Court and other federal courts} | {decides what laws mean and if they follow the Constitution}

## Main: Flow chart of checks
text: **Word bank:** can veto a bill; nominates federal judges; can override a veto with a two-thirds vote; approves or rejects the president's choice of judges; can rule a law unconstitutional; can rule a president's action unconstitutional
table: From this branch | Checks this branch | How
  - Legislative | Executive | {can override a veto with a two-thirds vote}
  - Legislative | Judicial | {approves or rejects the president's choice of judges}
  - Executive | Legislative | {can veto a bill}
  - Executive | Judicial | {nominates federal judges}
  - Judicial | Legislative | {can rule a law unconstitutional}
  - Judicial | Executive | {can rule a president's action unconstitutional}
choice: Congress passes a bill. The president vetoes it. What can Congress do next?
  - Nothing; the bill is dead forever.
  - Ask the Supreme Court to sign it.
  * Vote again; if two thirds of both houses agree, it becomes law.

## Stretch: Why checks?
write: Pick one check. Explain how it stops one branch from getting too much power.
  lines: 3
  key: For example: "The veto lets the president stop a bill Congress passed, but the override lets Congress pass it anyway with a big majority. Neither one gets the final say alone."

# 43. Saving Grows, Borrowing Costs
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can find simple interest earned on savings and paid on loans.
directions: Interest is money paid for using money. When you save, the bank pays you interest. When you borrow, you pay the lender interest. Use the formula in the example to find the interest for each amount.
mistakes: Students forget to multiply by the number of years, or turn the rate into a decimal wrong (5 percent is 0.05, not 0.5). Have them find one year's interest first, then multiply. Watch the last column: savers end with principal plus interest, and borrowers owe principal plus interest.
notes: Simple interest is principal x rate x time. Real bank accounts usually pay compound interest, which grows a little faster; this sheet keeps the math simple. The rates are examples for practice, not real offers. Key: savings interest 6, 30, 80, 30 dollars; loan interest 30, 144, 96, 375 dollars. The stretch shows why borrowing usually costs more than saving earns: lenders charge more than banks pay savers.

## Example
example: Save **400 dollars** at **5 percent** a year for **2 years**.
  One year: 400 x 0.05 = 20 dollars. Two years: 20 x 2 = **40 dollars** of interest.
  Total at the end: 400 + 40 = **440 dollars**.

## Warm up: Saving grows
table: Saved | Rate per year | Years | Interest earned | Total at the end
  - 200 dollars | 3 percent | 1 | {6 dollars} | {206 dollars}
  - 500 dollars | 2 percent | 3 | {30 dollars} | {530 dollars}
  - 1,000 dollars | 4 percent | 2 | {80 dollars} | {1,080 dollars}
  - 150 dollars | 5 percent | 4 | {30 dollars} | {180 dollars}

## Main: Borrowing costs
table: Borrowed | Rate per year | Years | Interest paid | Total to pay back
  - 300 dollars | 10 percent | 1 | {30 dollars} | {330 dollars}
  - 1,200 dollars | 6 percent | 2 | {144 dollars} | {1,344 dollars}
  - 800 dollars | 12 percent | 1 | {96 dollars} | {896 dollars}
  - 2,500 dollars | 5 percent | 3 | {375 dollars} | {2,875 dollars}
choice: Which loan costs the most interest?
  - 300 dollars at 10 percent for 1 year
  - 800 dollars at 12 percent for 1 year
  * 2,500 dollars at 5 percent for 3 years

## Stretch: Two sides of the seesaw
fill:
  - Maya saves 500 dollars for 1 year at 2 percent. She earns {10} dollars.
  - Leo borrows 500 dollars for 1 year at 12 percent. He pays {60} dollars.
write: Why does the bank charge borrowers a higher rate than it pays savers?
  lines: 3
  key: The bank uses savers' money to make loans. It charges borrowers more than it pays savers, and the difference pays for the bank's costs, covers loans that are not paid back, and gives the bank a profit.
