# 1. Pathways Inside Manufacturing
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can map career pathways inside a cluster from entry-level jobs to advanced ones.
directions: A career cluster is split into pathways: groups of jobs that share the same kind of work. Read about 3 pathways inside manufacturing. Sort the 6 jobs into the chart from entry level to advanced. Then draw a pathway map.
mistakes: Students often rank jobs by how hard they sound rather than by training and experience. An entry-level job needs a high school diploma or short training; an advanced job usually needs years of experience, an apprenticeship, a certificate or a degree. Watch for the maintenance technician placed in Production; maintenance workers keep the machines running, they do not run them to make products.
notes: The pathway names follow common Career Clusters pathway names for manufacturing (production; maintenance, installation and repair; quality assurance); the 2024 Advance CTE framework groups manufacturing work into its Advanced Manufacturing cluster, and its pathway names vary a little by version. Training notes are typical, not rules: many advanced workers start at entry level and train on the job or through an apprenticeship.

## Read: Three pathways
text: **Production:** people who run the machines and lines that make products. **Maintenance, installation and repair:** people who install, fix and maintain the machines so production never stops. **Quality assurance:** people who test and inspect products to be sure they meet the standards.
text: **Word bank:** assembler (entry; high school diploma, on-the-job training), CNC machinist (advanced; programs computer-controlled cutting machines, apprenticeship or certificate), maintenance helper (entry; assists technicians), industrial maintenance technician (advanced; troubleshoots robots and machines, certificate or associate's degree), quality inspector (entry; checks parts with gauges), quality engineer (advanced; designs the testing system, bachelor's degree).

## Main: Sort the jobs
table: Pathway | Entry level | Advanced
  - Production | {assembler} | {CNC machinist}
  - Maintenance, installation and repair | {maintenance helper} | {industrial maintenance technician}
  - Quality assurance | {quality inspector} | {quality engineer}

## Main: Draw the map
draw: Pathway map: a big box for Manufacturing, 3 roads (one per pathway), each starting at its entry-level job and ending at its advanced job. Write on each road one step that moves a worker up (for example an apprenticeship). | 3.2
write: What steps can move a worker from entry level to advanced? Name two.
  lines: 2
  key: Any two: years of on-the-job experience, an apprenticeship, a certificate from a community or technical college, an associate's or bachelor's degree, employer training.

## Questions
choice: A company's robot arm stops working, and the line shuts down. Which pathway's workers are called?
  - Production
  * Maintenance, installation and repair
  - Quality assurance
choice: Which job checks finished parts with measuring tools to be sure they meet the standard?
  - Assembler
  - CNC machinist
  * Quality inspector
  - Maintenance helper

## Stretch
write: Choose one pathway. Which job in it would you rather have, and why? What would you need to do to get there?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a job, gives a reason tied to interests (working with your hands, computers, solving problems) and names a real training step (apprenticeship, certificate, degree, experience).

# 7. Which Work Style Fits?
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can describe a job's work style and compare it with the way I like to work.
directions: Read the six invented job descriptions. Highlight the clues, then decide each job's work style: team or solo, indoor or outdoor, routine or variety. Explain one choice with evidence.
mistakes: Students sometimes decide from the job title alone (a "researcher" must work indoors). Point them back to the clues in each description. Routine does not mean boring or easy; it means the tasks are mostly the same each day. Many real jobs mix styles; these descriptions were written so each has one clear answer.
notes: The job descriptions are invented, based on real kinds of work. Work styles are one piece of career fit, along with interests, skills and values. In the Stretch, students describe their own preferences only; no personal or family information is needed.

## Warm up
match: Match each work style to its meaning.
  - Team = You work closely with other people to finish a task
  - Solo = You work mostly on your own
  - Routine = You do mostly the same tasks in the same order each day
  - Variety = Your tasks change from day to day

## Main: Six job descriptions
text: **A. Wildland firefighter.** Crews of 20 hike into forests and grasslands to dig fire lines. Each day brings a new fire, new weather and new terrain, and every member depends on the others.
text: **B. Payroll clerk.** At a desk in an office, you check the same timesheets and enter hours into the same system every week. You work on your own and send one report each Friday.
text: **C. Emergency room nurse.** Inside a busy hospital, you work beside doctors and other nurses. You never know who will come through the doors next: a broken arm, a high fever or a car crash.
text: **D. Bird survey technician.** You hike alone each morning to the same 20 marked spots in a wetland and count the birds you see and hear, recording them the same way every day.
text: **E. Assembly line worker.** In a factory, you and six teammates each add one part as the product moves down the line. Your station does the same task many times an hour.
text: **F. Freelance illustrator.** Working at a desk in your home studio, you draw for a book one week, a museum sign the next and a cereal box after that. You work on your own and email your drawings to clients.

## Main: Decide the work style
table: Job | Team or solo | Indoor or outdoor | Routine or variety
  - A. Wildland firefighter | {team} | {outdoor} | {variety}
  - B. Payroll clerk | {solo} | {indoor} | {routine}
  - C. Emergency room nurse | {team} | {indoor} | {variety}
  - D. Bird survey technician | {solo} | {outdoor} | {routine}
  - E. Assembly line worker | {team} | {indoor} | {routine}
  - F. Freelance illustrator | {solo} | {indoor} | {variety}
fill:
  - Two jobs are both solo and indoor: {B} and {F}.
  - The only job that is solo, outdoor and routine is {D}.

## Main: Explain one
write: Choose one job. Explain all three of its work styles with clues from the description.
  lines: 4
  starter: Job ___ is ___, ___ and ___. The description says ___.
  key: Model: Job C is team, indoor and variety. It is team because the nurse works "beside doctors and other nurses," indoor because it is "inside a busy hospital," and variety because "you never know who will come through the doors next," from a broken arm to a car crash.

## Stretch
write: Which work style fits you best for each pair: team or solo, indoor or outdoor, routine or variety? Name one of the six jobs that comes closest, and one way it does not fit.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a choice in each pair, picks the closest job with a reason (for example, "I like team, outdoor and variety, so the wildland firefighter fits"), and names one mismatch honestly.

# 20. Snow Season Prices Review
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can find a market equilibrium, classify shifts in supply and demand, and use a graph to explain a wage change.
directions: Use the invented ski rental table to find the equilibrium. Decide how four events shift supply or demand. Then use the labor market graph to explain a change in ski instructor wages.
mistakes: Students often say a higher price "increases supply" (a shift). A price change is a movement along the curve; only an outside event (costs, new sellers, weather, tastes) shifts a curve. Watch for shortages and surpluses mixed up: below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer (shortage); above it, sellers offer more than buyers want (surplus). On the labor graph, the wage is the price and workers are the quantity.
notes: All numbers are invented. Rentals: demand 100, 80, 60, 40, 20 and supply 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 at $10 to $50, so the equilibrium is $30 and 60 rentals. Shortage at $20: 80 - 40 = 40. Surplus at $40: 80 - 40 = 40. Labor graph: supply wage = 10 + workers / 4; demand wage = 40 - workers / 4; equilibrium 60 instructors at $25. New demand wage = 45 - workers / 4 gives 70 instructors at $27.50.

## The ski rental market
table: Price per day | Rentals wanted (demand) | Rentals offered (supply) | Shortage, surplus or equilibrium?
  - $10 | 100 | 20 | {shortage of 80}
  - $20 | 80 | 40 | {shortage of 40}
  - $30 | 60 | 60 | {equilibrium}
  - $40 | 40 | 80 | {surplus of 40}
  - $50 | 20 | 100 | {surplus of 80}

## Warm up
fill:
  - The equilibrium price is {$30}, and the equilibrium quantity is {60} rentals.
  - At $20, there is a {shortage} because buyers want {40} more rentals than shops offer.

## Main: Classify four shifts
table: Event | Demand or supply? | Increase or decrease? | Price goes up or down?
  - 1. Forecasters announce a huge snowstorm for the weekend. | {demand} | {increase} | {up}
  - 2. Two new rental shops open in town. | {supply} | {increase} | {down}
  - 3. A warm winter leaves the slopes bare in January. | {demand} | {decrease} | {down}
  - 4. The cost of repairing and waxing skis doubles. | {supply} | {decrease} | {up}
choice: The rental price rises from $30 to $40, and shops offer 80 rentals instead of 60. This is...
  - an increase in supply (the curve shifts right)
  * a movement along the supply curve
  - a decrease in demand
  - an increase in demand

## Main: A wage change
text: The graph shows the labor market for ski instructors. The solid line is demand (resorts hiring) and the dashed line is supply (people willing to teach).
graph: Number of ski instructors | 0 100 10 | Hourly wage ($) | 0 50 5
  line: 0,40 80,20
  line2: 0,10 80,30
  key: 0,45 100,20
fill: Now the equilibrium wage is {$25} an hour, with {60} instructors.
text: A new resort opens nearby and needs instructors, so resorts want 20 more instructors at every wage. On the graph, draw the new demand line: 20 instructors to the right of the old one.
fill: The new equilibrium is about {70} instructors at a wage of about {$27.50}.
write: Explain the wage change. Use the words demand, shortage, wage and equilibrium.
  lines: 4
  key: The new resort increases the demand for instructors, so the demand line shifts right. At the old wage of $25, resorts now want more instructors than are willing to work, a shortage. To attract more people, resorts raise the wage until a new equilibrium is reached at about $27.50 and 70 instructors.

## Stretch
write: A new law requires all ski instructors to pass a long, costly safety course. Predict what happens to the supply of instructors and the wage.
  lines: 2
  key: Fewer people will be willing or able to become instructors, so supply decreases (shifts left); the wage rises and fewer instructors are hired.

# 21. Build a Price Index
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can build a price index from a basket of goods and compute the inflation rate.
directions: The table shows an invented basket of 5 items a family buys, with prices for 3 years. Compute the cost of each item, the basket's total each year, the price index (2023 = 100) and the inflation rate.
mistakes: Students often add the prices instead of price x quantity. Watch for inflation from 2024 to 2025 computed against 2023 (111.30 - 100 = 11.3%); inflation for a year compares with the year just before: (111.30 - 106) / 106 = 5%.
notes: Prices are invented and chosen to make the math come out clean. The real Consumer Price Index (CPI), from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, works the same way with a basket of hundreds of goods and services. Formulas: index = basket cost / base-year basket cost x 100; inflation rate = (this year's index - last year's index) / last year's index x 100.

## The basket
table: Item | Quantity | Price 2023 | Price 2024 | Price 2025
  - Bread (loaf) | 4 | $2.50 | $2.75 | $2.80
  - Milk (gallon) | 2 | $3.50 | $3.75 | $3.90
  - Apples (pound) | 3 | $2.00 | $2.10 | $2.20
  - Monthly bus pass | 1 | $45.00 | $48.00 | $50.00
  - T-shirt | 2 | $16.00 | $16.60 | $17.85

## Warm up
example: Bread in 2023: quantity x price = 4 x $2.50 = **$10.00**

## Main: Cost of the basket
table: Item | Cost 2023 | Cost 2024 | Cost 2025
  - Bread | $10.00 | {$11.00} | {$11.20}
  - Milk | {$7.00} | {$7.50} | {$7.80}
  - Apples | {$6.00} | {$6.30} | {$6.60}
  - Bus pass | {$45.00} | {$48.00} | {$50.00}
  - T-shirts | {$32.00} | {$33.20} | {$35.70}
  - **Basket total** | {$100.00} | {$106.00} | {$111.30}
fill:
  - Price index 2023 (the base year) = {100}
  - Price index 2024 = 106.00 / 100.00 x 100 = {106}
  - Price index 2025 = {111.3}
  - Inflation rate 2023 to 2024 = (106 - 100) / 100 x 100 = {6}%
  - Inflation rate 2024 to 2025 = (111.3 - 106) / 106 x 100 = {5}%

## Stretch
fill: Which item's price rose by the greatest percent from 2023 to 2025? {Bread}, by {12}%
write: Prices still went up in 2025. Why is it wrong to say "prices fell in 2025 because inflation dropped from 6% to 5%"?
  lines: 3
  key: A lower inflation rate means prices rose more slowly, not that they fell. The basket still cost more in 2025 ($111.30) than in 2024 ($106.00). Prices would only fall if the inflation rate were negative (deflation).

# 33. Investor Questions
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can use a question checklist to evaluate a startup's market, competition and risk.
directions: Read about three made-up startups that want investors. Use the checklist to evaluate each one, give it a score, and rank the three. Explain your ranking.
mistakes: Students often fall for the most exciting idea (the robot) and skip the checklist. A small market and a huge cost make Startup C risky even with no competitors. Watch for "no competition means a sure win": sometimes there is no competition because there are too few customers. Students also forget that a market can be big and still crowded (Startup B).
notes: All three startups and their numbers are invented. The key is a model; accept a different ranking that the student supports with checklist evidence. Scores: 3 = strong, 2 = medium, 1 = weak. One model scoring: A market 2, competition 3, risk 2 (total 7); B market 3, competition 1, risk 2 (total 6); C market 1, competition 3, risk 1 (total 5).

## Read the three startups
text: **Startup A: camping gear rental.** Rents tents, sleeping bags and stoves to families by the weekend. The town of 50,000 is near three state parks. The only other rental shop is 40 miles away. Needs $20,000 to buy gear. Risk: gear can be damaged, and a rainy summer could mean fewer renters.
text: **Startup B: flavored sparkling water.** Sells a new flavor of sparkling water in cans. Almost everyone buys drinks, but stores already carry hundreds of brands. Needs $60,000 for the first batch and ads. Risk: stores may not give it shelf space, and big drink makers can lower their prices.
text: **Startup C: a solar panel cleaning robot.** Building a robot that cleans solar panels on house roofs. No other company in the area does this, but only 2 out of every 100 homes nearby have solar panels. Needs $500,000 to finish the first robot. Risk: the robot has not been tested yet.

## Warm up
match: Match each checklist word to its question.
  - Market = Who will buy it, and how many of them are there?
  - Competition = Who else sells something like it?
  - Risk = What could go wrong, and how much money could be lost?

## Main: The investor checklist
table: Question | Startup A | Startup B | Startup C
  - Who are the customers? Many or few? | {families near 3 parks; a medium number} | {almost everyone; very many} | {homes with panels; few}
  - Who are the competitors? | {one shop, 40 miles away} | {hundreds of brands} | {none nearby}
  - What could go wrong? | {damaged gear; rainy summer} | {no shelf space; price war} | {robot may not work}
  - How much money is needed? | {$20,000} | {$60,000} | {$500,000}
  - Score: market (1 to 3) | {2} | {3} | {1}
  - Score: competition (1 to 3) | {3} | {1} | {3}
  - Score: risk (3 = low risk) | {2} | {2} | {1}
  - Total score | {7} | {6} | {5}
fill: In Startup C's area, {2} out of every 100 homes, or {2}%, have solar panels.

## Main: Rank and explain
fill: My ranking, best investment first: {A}, {B}, {C}
write: Explain your ranking with evidence from the checklist.
  lines: 4
  starter: I ranked ___ first because ___. I ranked ___ last because ___.
  key: Model: I ranked A first because it has customers nearby, almost no competition and needs only $20,000, so less money is at risk. B has a huge market but must fight hundreds of brands for space. I ranked C last because, even with no competitors, only 2 percent of homes have panels, the robot is untested and it needs $500,000.

## Stretch
write: Write one more question you would ask Startup C before investing, and explain why the answer matters.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Examples: "How many homeowners say they would pay to have their panels cleaned, and how much?" (shows whether the small market is still enough). "What happens to my money if the robot does not work?" (shows the size of the risk).
