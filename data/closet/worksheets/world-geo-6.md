# 1. Latitude and Longitude Locator
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencil, a world atlas or globe
ican: I can use latitude and longitude to find and describe absolute location.
directions: The grid is a flat map of the world's lines of latitude and longitude. Plot each city from its coordinates, label it, and connect the cities in order to trace a trip around the world. Then use an atlas to give the coordinates of 4 famous landmarks.
mistakes: The most common error is swapping the two numbers: latitude (north or south) goes up and down the grid, longitude (east or west) goes across. Say it as "up the ladder, then across": latitude, then longitude. South latitudes and west longitudes are below and left of zero; students who ignore N, S, E and W plot cities in the wrong hemisphere (Rio de Janeiro ends up near Cairo).
notes: Coordinates are rounded to the nearest whole degree. The grid has lines every 15 degrees, so students estimate between lines; accept plots within about 5 degrees. The key draws the route through all 10 cities in order, west to east. The grid has no coastlines: after plotting, have students compare their dots with a world map to see each city's continent. For the landmarks, accept answers within 2 degrees.

## Stretch: Why it matters
write: Two friends both say they live "near the big river." How do latitude and longitude solve their problem? Use the words absolute location and relative location.
  lines: 3
  key: "Near the big river" is relative location: it depends on another place and can be unclear. Latitude and longitude give absolute location, one exact point that means the same thing to anyone anywhere, so each friend could give coordinates no one could mistake.

# 6. Geography Tools Checkpoint
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler, a world map or atlas
ican: I can use coordinates, map projections, map scale and the five themes of geography to describe places in the world.
directions: Use the grid and a world map to answer all 12 questions. The grid is a world map with the coastlines taken off: it shows only lines of latitude and longitude. Show your work on the scale questions.
mistakes: On the grid, students still swap latitude and longitude, or forget the letters: a dot below the equator is S and left of the prime meridian is W. The grid has heavy lines every 30 degrees and light lines every 15 degrees; students who count light lines as 30 put dots twice as far out. On scale, students divide when they should multiply (map inches to real miles is multiply). On the themes, place and region get mixed up: place is what one spot is like; region is an area that shares a feature.
notes: The three dots are at 45°N 75°W (near Ottawa, Canada, in North America), 30°N 30°E (near Cairo, Egypt, in Africa) and 30°S 135°E (inland South Australia). Accept continent answers that match each dot. Questions 7 to 11 all use Egypt, so students see that the five themes are five ways to ask about one place. Count the 12 numbered answers for a quick score: 11 or 12 is ready to move on; 8 or fewer, reteach the weakest strand (coordinates 1 to 3, projections 4 and 5, scale 6 and 7, themes 8 to 12).

## Part A: Coordinates
text: Each dot on the grid is in a different part of the world. Read its latitude and longitude, then find it on your world map to name the continent.

  show: -75,45 30,30 135,-30

  - The dot in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres is at {30°S}, {135°E}, on the continent of {Australia}.

## Part B: Projections and scale
choice: On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Africa is really about 14 times larger. Why?
  - The mapmaker measured Greenland wrong.
  - Greenland is closer to the map's center.
  * The Mercator projection stretches land more and more the farther it is from the equator.
  - Africa shrank because of erosion.
choice: Every flat map of the whole world has some distortion. Why?
  - Mapmakers copy older maps that had mistakes.
  - Satellites cannot photograph the whole Earth.
  - The oceans move the continents every year.
  * A round globe cannot be flattened without stretching or tearing it somewhere.
fill:
  - A map's scale says 1 inch = 400 miles. Two cities are 2.5 inches apart on the map. The real distance is {1,000} miles.
  - A map's scale says 1 centimeter = 250 kilometers. Two cities are 1,500 kilometers apart. On the map they are {6} centimeters apart.

## Part C: Five themes, one country
match: Each sentence is about Egypt. Match it to the theme of geography it shows.
  - Cairo is at about 30°N, 31°E. = Location
  - Cairo is a crowded city of mosques, markets and desert heat beside the Nile. = Place
  - Farmers dig canals to carry Nile water to their fields. = Human-environment interaction
  - Ships carry oil and goods between Asia and Europe through Egypt's Suez Canal. = Movement
  - Egypt is part of the Arabic-speaking region called the Middle East and North Africa. = Region

## Stretch: Pick the right tool
write: A ship's navigator and a student comparing the sizes of continents need different world maps. Which projection should each one use, and why?
  lines: 4
  key: The navigator should use a Mercator map, because it keeps directions and shapes true, so a straight line on it is a steady compass heading. The student should use an equal-area map (such as the Gall-Peters), because it keeps every land area at its true size, even though shapes are stretched.

# 18. Label an Ancient Artifact
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, the fact card on this sheet (a world history textbook is a bonus)
ican: I can write a short, accurate museum label that explains what an ancient object is, where and when it was made, and why it matters.
directions: Study the picture and the fact card. Read the model label for a different Sumerian object. Plan your label with the frame, then write a final label of about 60 words for visitors your age.
mistakes: Students copy the fact card word for word, so the label reads like a list. A label is a short paragraph that answers three questions in order and ends with why the object matters. Watch for labels that skip the "why it matters" sentence or that guess at things the fact card does not say ("it was a king's letter"). Students also write "3100 BC" as "3100 years ago"; BCE dates count back from year 1, so 3100 BCE is about 5,100 years ago.
notes: The picture is a simple drawing of a cuneiform tablet, not a specific museum object. Early tablets from Uruk (about 3300 to 3000 BCE) are mostly records of goods such as barley, beer, sheep and workers' rations; writing grew out of record keeping. Cylinder seals were used across Mesopotamia from about 3500 BCE. The model label is 57 words and the key's sample is 59; accept 50 to 70 words. For the next sheet (Label Swap Critique), keep the final labels.

# 25. Greek City-States Map
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils, a map of ancient Greece (textbook or atlas) to check against
ican: I can locate the major places of ancient Greece and explain how geography shaped its city-states.
directions: Plot and label each place on the grid from its latitude and longitude. Draw the road from Sparta to Corinth to Athens. Label the seas. Then answer the questions about how mountains and sea shaped Greek life.
mistakes: Students often picture Greece as one country with one government. In this period it was hundreds of separate city-states (poleis), each with its own laws, army and gods it honored most, which sometimes allied and often fought. Students also plot Troy in Greece: it was across the Aegean Sea in Anatolia (today's Turkey).
notes: The grid is a plain latitude and longitude grid with no coastlines, so students build the map from coordinates. After plotting, have them compare with a printed map of ancient Greece and sketch the coastline. Coordinates are modern locations of the ancient sites (Troy is the site at Hisarlik, Turkey). ## Stretch: Two cities, two paths

Sparta, inland in a farming valley guarded by mountains, relied on land; its farmland was worked by the helots, enslaved people Sparta had conquered, and fear of their revolt pushed Sparta to keep a powerful army ready at all times.

# 30. Roman Republic Government
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, textbook chapter on the Roman Republic
ican: I can describe how the Roman Republic divided and checked power, and compare it to the branches of the US government.
directions: Use the word bank and your textbook to complete the diagram of Rome's government. Then show how each part checked the others, and compare Rome's government with the three branches of the US government.
mistakes: Students often say the Roman Senate was elected like the US Senate. Roman senators were mostly former officials who served for life; the Senate advised the consuls and controlled money and foreign affairs, while the assemblies voted. Students also treat the two consuls as a president and vice president: they were equals, and either one could block the other. The tribunes' veto is easy to miss; it was the plebeians' main protection.
notes: Roman government changed over five centuries; the diagram shows the middle Republic (about 300 to 133 BCE). "Assemblies" simplifies several bodies: the Centuriate Assembly (organized by wealth and army rank) elected consuls and voted on war; the Tribal Assembly and the Plebeian Council passed most laws, and after 287 BCE plebeian votes bound all Romans. The Senate had about 300 members in this period. Rome had no separate court system like the US Supreme Court; officials called praetors ran the courts.

## Stretch: Why split power?
write: The Romans had lived under a king. Explain how two features of the Republic were designed to keep any one person from becoming a king again.
  lines: 4
  key: Two consuls shared the top job, and each could veto the other, so neither could rule alone. Consuls served only one year, so no one stayed in power long. (Also accept: tribunes could veto officials; the Senate controlled money; a dictator's power ended after six months.)

# 42. Share the Water Plan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can propose a fair plan for sharing a shrinking resource among countries and explain the trade-offs.
directions: Four countries share Lake Sela, a made-up lake modeled on real shrinking lakes such as Lake Chad. Read the data, find out how much water must be saved, then plan how each country will share the water. Your plan must give everyone drinking water and stop the lake from shrinking.
mistakes: Students cut every country's water by the same amount (5 units each) without noticing that the smallest country would lose half its farm water while the largest loses an eighth. Push them to compare cuts as a fraction of what each country uses. Others protect farming fully and forget that fishing families depend on the lake staying full. A plan also needs a way to check that countries keep their promises.
notes: Lake Sela and the four countries are made up so students can work with simple numbers; a "unit" is a share of the water the rivers bring each year. The setup echoes Lake Chad, which is shared by Chad, Cameroon, Niger and Nigeria and shrank by roughly 90 percent between the 1960s and the 1990s because of drought and water taken for irrigation. Real agreements use shared river basin commissions (Lake Chad has one, set up in 1964). Many plans are good; grade on whether the numbers add up, drinking water is fully met, and the reasons address farming, fishing and drinking water.

  lines: 3
