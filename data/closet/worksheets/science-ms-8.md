# 1. Action-Reaction Pairs
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can identify action-reaction force pairs and explain that they are equal in size and opposite in direction.
directions: For each scene, sketch the two objects and draw both forces of the pair as arrows of equal length pointing in opposite directions. Label each arrow "A on B." Then fill in the table.
mistakes: Students often pair a force with a different force on the same object (weight and the floor's push on you). An action-reaction pair always acts on two different objects and is the same type of force. Students also draw the reaction arrow shorter "because the object didn't move much": the forces are always equal; the effects differ because the masses differ.
notes: Newton's third law: when object A pushes or pulls on object B, B pushes or pulls back on A with a force equal in size and opposite in direction. Accept "the gas" or "the exhaust" for the rocket; accept "the ground" or "Earth" for walking.

## Warm up
example: A hand pushes on a wall. Action: the hand pushes the wall forward. Reaction: the wall pushes the hand backward, just as hard.
choice: The two forces in an action-reaction pair act on:
  - the same object
  * two different objects
  - only objects that are moving

## Main: Six scenes
draw: 1. A rocket launches | 0.85
draw: 2. A swimmer pushes off with her hands | 0.85
draw: 3. A hammer hits a nail | 0.85
draw: 4. Two skaters push each other apart | 0.85
draw: 5. A person takes a step | 0.85
draw: 6. A balloon is let go and its air rushes out | 0.85
table: Scene | Action: A pushes B | Reaction: B pushes A
  - 1. Rocket | {rocket pushes gas down} | {gas pushes rocket up}
  - 2. Swimmer | {hands push water backward} | {water pushes hands forward}
  - 3. Hammer | {hammer pushes nail down} | {nail pushes hammer up}
  - 4. Skaters | {skater A pushes B left} | {B pushes A right}
  - 5. Walking | {foot pushes ground backward} | {ground pushes foot forward}
  - 6. Balloon | {balloon pushes air out back} | {air pushes balloon forward}

## Think
write: The forces in a pair are equal and opposite. Why don't they cancel out, so nothing moves?
  lines: 3
  key: They act on different objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the same object. Each object feels one force of the pair, so each one can accelerate.
write: In scene 4, one skater has twice the mass of the other. Compare the forces on them and how they move.
  lines: 3
  key: The forces are equal in size. The lighter skater accelerates twice as much (a = F/m), so she moves away faster than the heavier skater.

## Stretch
write: When you walk, you push Earth backward with the same force that Earth pushes you forward. Why doesn't Earth seem to move?
  lines: 3
  key: Earth's mass is enormous, so the same force gives it a tiny, unnoticeable acceleration (a = F/m), while your small mass gives you a large one.

# 6. Coils and Clips Graph
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can graph data from an electromagnet test and use the pattern to make a prediction.
directions: A class built electromagnets by wrapping wire around an iron nail and connecting it to the same battery each time. They counted how many paper clips each magnet could lift. Find the averages, graph them, and use your graph to predict the result for 40 coils.
mistakes: Students often connect the dots zigzag style instead of drawing one straight best-fit line, or start the line at the first data point instead of thinking about 0 coils. Watch for students who plot the trial results instead of the averages, or who swap the axes: coils (the variable the class changed) go on the x-axis. Some students predict 40 coils by doubling the 20-coil result (14); that only works if the line goes through 0, and it nearly does here, so 13 to 14 clips is fine.
notes: Practice data, typical of a classroom nail electromagnet with one fresh D cell. The best-fit line is close to clips = 0.34 x coils, about 1 clip for every 3 coils, so 40 coils predicts 13 to 14 clips; accept 12 to 15 with a line drawn. Real electromagnets do not stay linear forever: the iron core saturates, and more turns of thin wire add resistance, so the stretch asks students to doubt a prediction far outside the data. Safety: a nail electromagnet shorts the battery, so the wire and cell get warm. Connect only while lifting, and disconnect between trials.

## Warm up
fill:
  - The variable the class changed on purpose (independent variable): number of {coils}
  - The variable they measured (dependent variable): number of {paper clips lifted}
  - Two variables they kept the same (controlled): {the battery} and {the nail (or wire, or clip size)}

## Main: The data
table: Coils | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 | Average clips
  - 10 | 2 | 3 | 4 | {3}
  - 20 | 7 | 6 | 8 | {7}
  - 30 | 10 | 11 | 9 | {10}
  - 50 | 16 | 17 | 18 | {17}
  - 60 | 21 | 19 | 20 | {20}
text: Plot the five averages. Then draw one straight best-fit line with a ruler.
graph: Number of coils | 0 70 10 | Paper clips lifted (average) | 0 24 2
  key: 10,3 20,7 30,10 50,17 60,20
  dots: yes
note: The key shows the five average points. The best-fit line runs from about (0, 0) to (70, 24), close to clips = 0.34 x coils.

## Use the pattern
choice: Which sentence best describes the pattern?
  - The number of clips stays about the same as coils are added.
  * The number of clips goes up steadily, about 1 more clip for every 3 more coils.
  - The number of clips doubles every time 10 coils are added.
  - The number of clips goes up fast at first, then levels off.
fill:
  - Using my line, 40 coils should lift about {13 to 14} paper clips.
  - The class did not test 40 coils. Making a prediction between tested values is called {interpolation}.
write: Explain why the class ran three trials for each number of coils and used the average.
  lines: 2
  key: Each trial varies a little (clips hang differently, the battery weakens). Averaging three trials evens out these random differences, so the result is more reliable.

## Stretch
write: Use the line to predict the clips for 300 coils. Then give one reason the real magnet might lift fewer than that.
  lines: 3
  key: The line predicts about 300 / 3 = 100 clips (about 102). The real magnet would probably lift fewer: 300 is far outside the tested range, the iron core can only be magnetized so much (it saturates), and more wire adds resistance so less current flows. Predictions far outside the data are risky.

# 19. Telephone Game Signal Test
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, one original card and 5 blank cards per group (see notes), timer
ican: I can compare how analog and digital signals hold up when they are copied again and again.
directions: Work in groups of 6. Person 1 studies the original card for 10 seconds, hides it, and draws the wave and writes the code on a blank card. Person 2 studies only Person 1's card for 10 seconds and copies it, and so on, until 5 copies are made. No talking. Then compare each copy with the original. Keep this sheet face down until all 5 copies are made.
mistakes: Students often blame the digital "losers" for a mistake and miss the bigger idea: an analog copy loses a little everywhere, every time, while a digital copy is usually perfect until one symbol flips. Watch for groups that let copiers see the original or talk; the test only works if each person sees only the copy before theirs. Some students think digital means "numbers on a screen"; here it means information sent as a set of clear, separate symbols.
notes: Setup: make one original card per group by tracing the wave on this sheet onto an index card inside a box the same size as the graph, with no grid, and write the 8-digit code on the back. Give each group 5 blank cards with the same empty box. Typical results: the analog wave drifts a little more with each copy, so by copy 5 several of the 8 checkpoints are more than half a box off; the digital code usually arrives with 0 errors, sometimes 1 whole wrong digit. To score the analog copies, students lay each copy over the original and hold the pair up to a window. Real systems add extra digits so errors can be caught (the stretch's check digit), and send digital signals as two clear levels (on and off), which are even harder to confuse than 10 digits.

## Warm up
text: The same signal can be sent two ways. An **analog** signal is a continuous wave that can take any value. A **digital** signal is a set of separate symbols, here the height of the wave measured at 8 points and rounded to whole numbers.
graph: Position | 0 8 1 | Height | 0 9 1
  line: 0,5 1,7 2,9 3,8 4,5 5,2 6,1 7,3 8,5
  minor: off
table: Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8
  - Digital code (height) | {7} | {9} | {8} | {5} | {2} | {1} | {3} | {5}

## Main: Run the test
table: Card | Analog: checkpoints more than half a box off (out of 8) | Digital: digits wrong (out of 8)
  - Original | 0 | 0
  - Copy 1 | |
  - Copy 2 | |
  - Copy 3 | |
  - Copy 4 | |
  - Copy 5 | |
note: Typical results: the analog column climbs, often to 3 to 6 off by copy 5; the digital column stays at 0 or 1.
choice: In most groups, how do the two signals change from copy 1 to copy 5?
  - Both get a little worse with every copy.
  - The digital code gets worse faster than the analog wave.
  * The analog wave drifts a little with each copy, while the digital code usually stays exact.
  - Neither one changes at all.
write: Why is a digital code easier to copy exactly than a smooth wave?
  lines: 3
  key: Each digit is one of a few clear symbols. A 7 drawn a little crooked is still read as a 7, so small wobbles are ignored and the copy comes out exact. A wave can take any height, so every small wobble gets copied and added to, and the errors pile up.
write: When the digital code did go wrong, how was the error different from the analog errors?
  lines: 2
  key: A digital error is a whole wrong symbol (a 3 became an 8): rare, but a big jump at one place. Analog errors are small, everywhere, and keep growing.

## Stretch
fill:
  - A **check digit** is the last digit of the sum of the code. For 7 9 8 5 2 1 3 5 the sum is {40}, so the check digit is {0}.
  - A copier writes 7 9 8 5 2 7 3 5. That sum is {46}, check digit {6}, so the receiver {knows there is an error}.

# 26. Guarded Nests Data
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can use data to argue how a parent's behavior affects the survival of its young.
directions: Read about the study and the two data tables. Do the calculations, then write an argument using claim, evidence and reasoning.
mistakes: Students often compare raw counts without checking that both groups had the same number of nests; here they did (40 each), but percent is the fair comparison. Students also claim guarding "always" works; 9 guarded nests still failed. Push for language like "much more likely."
notes: The data are made up for practice, but typical of real field studies of nest-guarding fish such as smallmouth bass, where males fan and guard the eggs and young for days to weeks. In real studies, when guarding males are removed (for example, caught by anglers during nesting season), predators such as sunfish often eat most of the eggs within days. A good extension: why some places limit fishing for bass while they are nesting.

## The study
text: In some freshwater fish, the father builds a nest on the lake bottom and stays to guard it, chasing away egg-eating fish. Scientists watched 80 nests. At 40 nests the father stayed. At 40 others, the father had been removed. A nest was a success if young fish survived to swim away. (Practice data, typical of real studies.)
table: Group | Nests watched | Successful nests | Percent successful
  - Father guarding | 40 | 31 | {77.5%}
  - No father | 40 | 6 | {15%}
table: Day | Eggs per nest, guarded (average) | Eggs per nest, not guarded (average)
  - 0 | 2,000 | 2,000
  - 2 | 1,850 | 900
  - 4 | 1,700 | 300
  - 6 | 1,600 | 50

## Read the data
fill:
  - Percent of eggs left on day 6, guarded nests: {80}%
  - Percent of eggs left on day 6, unguarded nests: {2.5}%
  - Between day 0 and day 2, unguarded nests lost an average of {1,100} eggs.
choice: Which statement do the data support?
  - Guarded nests never fail.
  * Guarded nests were about 5 times as likely to succeed as unguarded nests.
  - Unguarded nests lost eggs slowly and steadily.
  - The number of eggs laid caused the difference.
choice: Why was it important that both groups started with about 2,000 eggs per nest?
  - So the fish had the same food
  * So the only big difference between groups was the guarding
  - So the scientists could count faster

## Argue from evidence
write: Claim: Does guarding by the father increase the survival of his young?
  lines: 1
  key: Yes, guarding greatly increases the chance that the young survive.
write: Evidence: Give at least two pieces of data.
  lines: 3
  key: 77.5% of guarded nests succeeded compared with 15% of unguarded nests; on day 6 guarded nests kept 80% of their eggs while unguarded nests kept only 2.5%; unguarded nests lost more than half their eggs in the first 2 days.
write: Reasoning: Explain how guarding leads to these results.
  lines: 4
  key: The father chases away egg-eating predators, so fewer eggs are eaten and more young survive to swim away. Without a guard, predators eat most eggs within days. Because more young survive, fathers that guard pass on more of their genes, so guarding behavior is common in this species.

## Stretch
write: Guarding fathers eat very little for days and lose weight. Why might this behavior still be worth the cost?
  lines: 3
  key: The father's cost is temporary, but guarding multiplies the number of young that survive (77.5% versus 15% successful nests), so it greatly increases his reproductive success.

# 31. Peppered Moth Story
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can use the history of the peppered moth to explain natural selection: variation, inheritance, selection and time.
directions: Read the passage. The paragraphs are numbered so you can point to evidence. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often say the moths "turned dark to hide" or "changed color to survive." No moth changed; dark moths were already in the population, and more of them survived to have young. Watch for students who think the soot itself made the moths dark. Another slip is calling the bird the thing that "selects" on purpose: birds just eat the moths they can see.
notes: Sources for the facts: the first dark peppered moth was recorded near Manchester in 1848; by the 1890s dark moths made up most of the population around Manchester (often given as about 98 percent). Bernard Kettlewell's release and recapture experiments ran in the 1950s. Britain's Clean Air Act passed in 1956. Michael Majerus watched moths in Cambridge from 2001 to 2007, and a 2016 study traced the dark color to a change in a gene called *cortex*. The dark color is inherited as a single dominant gene variant. Kettlewell's methods were criticized later, which is why Majerus repeated the work; the overall result held up.

## Read
passage: Black and White Wings | numbered
  The peppered moth lives in woods across Britain. It flies at night and rests by day on tree trunks and branches. Most peppered moths have pale wings with black speckles, like salt and pepper. On a tree covered in pale lichens, a speckled moth is very hard for a hungry bird to see.
  Not every peppered moth looks the same. A few are born almost completely black. The dark color is passed from parents to their young through a gene, the same way many other traits are passed on. For a long time, dark moths were so rare that collectors prized them.
  In the 1800s, factories in cities such as Manchester burned huge amounts of coal. Soot covered the countryside nearby. It killed the pale lichens and blackened the tree bark. The first dark peppered moth near Manchester was recorded in 1848. By the end of the century, nearly all the peppered moths around the city were dark.
  In the 1950s, a scientist named Bernard Kettlewell tested an idea: birds were eating the moths they could see. He marked and released both kinds of moths in a sooty wood and in a clean wood, then caught as many as he could again. In the sooty wood, more dark moths survived. In the clean wood, more speckled ones did.
  Then the air changed. Britain passed the Clean Air Act in 1956, and factories burned less coal. Lichens grew back, and tree bark grew lighter. Over the next decades, dark moths became rare again. A study from 2001 to 2007 watched birds eating moths in clean woods and found that birds caught more dark moths than speckled ones.
  The moths never chose a color. Each moth kept the wings it was born with. What changed, over many generations, was which moths lived long enough to lay eggs.

## Main
choice: Which sentence from the passage shows **variation**?
  - The peppered moth lives in woods across Britain.
  * A few are born almost completely black.
  - Soot covered the countryside nearby.
  - Then the air changed.
choice: Which paragraph gives the best evidence of **inheritance**?
  * Paragraph 2
  - Paragraph 3
  - Paragraph 5
  - Paragraph 6
table: Part of natural selection | What it means | Evidence from the passage (paragraph)
  - Variation | {members of a population differ in traits} | {some moths are speckled, some dark (2)}
  - Inheritance | {traits pass from parents to young} | {the dark color is passed on through a gene (2)}
  - Selection | {some traits help more of their owners survive and reproduce} | {birds ate the moths they could see; dark ones survived better on sooty bark (4)}
  - Time | {the population changes over many generations} | {dark moths rose from 1848 to the 1890s, then fell after 1956 (3, 5)}
choice: Why did dark moths become rare again after 1956?
  - The dark moths decided to stop being dark.
  - Clean air turned the dark moths pale.
  - Scientists removed the dark moths.
  * On lighter bark, birds spotted dark moths more easily, so fewer dark moths lived to reproduce.

## Explain
write: A classmate writes: "The moths turned black to hide from birds." Use paragraph 6 to correct this.
  lines: 3
  key: No moth changed color. Each moth kept the wings it was born with. Dark moths were already in the population; on sooty bark more of them survived and laid eggs, so over many generations a larger share of the population was dark.

## Stretch
write: Suppose the soot had come back for only one summer. Would the moth population have become mostly dark? Explain using the idea of time.
  lines: 3
  key: Probably not. One summer is about one generation, so dark moths would survive a little better for that year only. The big change near Manchester took about 50 years of many generations, each one shifting the mix a little.

# 44. Global Temperature Record
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator (optional)
ican: I can graph global temperature data and describe how the trend changes over time.
directions: The table gives the average global surface temperature for each decade, compared with the 1951 to 1980 average. Plot each decade at its middle year (the 1880s at 1885). Connect the points, then describe the trend in each span.
mistakes: Students often read an anomaly as the actual temperature ("it was -0.2 degrees"). An anomaly is the difference from a baseline. Watch the negative values: -0.34 is lower than -0.19. Students also judge a span by its end points alone; look at the whole span. Some say "it was colder before 1980, so warming is natural"; the stretch question addresses the rate.
notes: Values are decade averages of the NASA GISS surface temperature analysis (GISTEMP), rounded to the nearest 0.01 °C, relative to the 1951 to 1980 average; check the current GISTEMP release if exact numbers matter, since values shift slightly between versions. The year 2020 alone was about 1.0 °C above the baseline, tied for the warmest year on record at the time. Spans: 1880s to 1920s, about flat with a dip (no clear trend); 1930s to 1970s, a rise in the 1930s and 1940s then a level stretch; 1980s to 2010s, a steady rise of about 0.2 °C per decade. Scientists link the recent rise mainly to greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels.
