# 1. Action-Reaction Pairs
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can identify action-reaction force pairs and explain that they are equal in size and opposite in direction.
directions: For each scene, sketch the two objects and draw both forces of the pair as arrows of equal length pointing in opposite directions. Label each arrow "A on B." Then fill in the table.
mistakes: Students often pair a force with a different force on the same object (weight and the floor's push on you). An action-reaction pair always acts on two different objects and is the same type of force. Students also draw the reaction arrow shorter "because the object didn't move much": the forces are always equal; the effects differ because the masses differ.
notes: Newton's third law: when object A pushes or pulls on object B, B pushes or pulls back on A with a force equal in size and opposite in direction. Accept "the gas" or "the exhaust" for the rocket; accept "the ground" or "Earth" for walking.

## Warm up
example: A hand pushes on a wall. Action: the hand pushes the wall forward. Reaction: the wall pushes the hand backward, just as hard.
choice: The two forces in an action-reaction pair act on:
  - the same object
  * two different objects
  - only objects that are moving

## Main: Six scenes
draw: 1. A rocket launches | 0.85
draw: 2. A swimmer pushes off with her hands | 0.85
draw: 3. A hammer hits a nail | 0.85
draw: 4. Two skaters push each other apart | 0.85
draw: 5. A person takes a step | 0.85
draw: 6. A balloon is let go and its air rushes out | 0.85
table: Scene | Action: A pushes B | Reaction: B pushes A
  - 1. Rocket | {rocket pushes gas down} | {gas pushes rocket up}
  - 2. Swimmer | {hands push water backward} | {water pushes hands forward}
  - 3. Hammer | {hammer pushes nail down} | {nail pushes hammer up}
  - 4. Skaters | {skater A pushes B left} | {B pushes A right}
  - 5. Walking | {foot pushes ground backward} | {ground pushes foot forward}
  - 6. Balloon | {balloon pushes air out back} | {air pushes balloon forward}

## Think
write: The forces in a pair are equal and opposite. Why don't they cancel out, so nothing moves?
  lines: 3
  key: They act on different objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the same object. Each object feels one force of the pair, so each one can accelerate.
write: In scene 4, one skater has twice the mass of the other. Compare the forces on them and how they move.
  lines: 3
  key: The forces are equal in size. The lighter skater accelerates twice as much (a = F/m), so she moves away faster than the heavier skater.

## Stretch
write: When you walk, you push Earth backward with the same force that Earth pushes you forward. Why doesn't Earth seem to move?
  lines: 3
  key: Earth's mass is enormous, so the same force gives it a tiny, unnoticeable acceleration (a = F/m), while your small mass gives you a large one.

# 26. Guarded Nests Data
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator (optional)
ican: I can use data to argue how a parent's behavior affects the survival of its young.
directions: Read about the study and the two data tables. Do the calculations, then write an argument using claim, evidence and reasoning.
mistakes: Students often compare raw counts without checking that both groups had the same number of nests; here they did (40 each), but percent is the fair comparison. Students also claim guarding "always" works; 9 guarded nests still failed. Push for language like "much more likely."
notes: The data are made up for practice, but typical of real field studies of nest-guarding fish such as smallmouth bass, where males fan and guard the eggs and young for days to weeks. In real studies, when guarding males are removed (for example, caught by anglers during nesting season), predators such as sunfish often eat most of the eggs within days. A good extension: why some places limit fishing for bass while they are nesting.

## The study
text: In some freshwater fish, the father builds a nest on the lake bottom and stays to guard it, chasing away egg-eating fish. Scientists watched 80 nests. At 40 nests the father stayed. At 40 others, the father had been removed. A nest was a success if young fish survived to swim away. (Practice data, typical of real studies.)
table: Group | Nests watched | Successful nests | Percent successful
  - Father guarding | 40 | 31 | {77.5%}
  - No father | 40 | 6 | {15%}
table: Day | Eggs per nest, guarded (average) | Eggs per nest, not guarded (average)
  - 0 | 2,000 | 2,000
  - 2 | 1,850 | 900
  - 4 | 1,700 | 300
  - 6 | 1,600 | 50

## Read the data
fill:
  - Percent of eggs left on day 6, guarded nests: {80}%
  - Percent of eggs left on day 6, unguarded nests: {2.5}%
  - Between day 0 and day 2, unguarded nests lost an average of {1,100} eggs.
choice: Which statement do the data support?
  - Guarded nests never fail.
  * Guarded nests were about 5 times as likely to succeed as unguarded nests.
  - Unguarded nests lost eggs slowly and steadily.
  - The number of eggs laid caused the difference.
choice: Why was it important that both groups started with about 2,000 eggs per nest?
  - So the fish had the same food
  * So the only big difference between groups was the guarding
  - So the scientists could count faster

## Argue from evidence
write: Claim: Does guarding by the father increase the survival of his young?
  lines: 1
  key: Yes, guarding greatly increases the chance that the young survive.
write: Evidence: Give at least two pieces of data.
  lines: 3
  key: 77.5% of guarded nests succeeded compared with 15% of unguarded nests; on day 6 guarded nests kept 80% of their eggs while unguarded nests kept only 2.5%; unguarded nests lost more than half their eggs in the first 2 days.
write: Reasoning: Explain how guarding leads to these results.
  lines: 4
  key: The father chases away egg-eating predators, so fewer eggs are eaten and more young survive to swim away. Without a guard, predators eat most eggs within days. Because more young survive, fathers that guard pass on more of their genes, so guarding behavior is common in this species.

## Stretch
write: Guarding fathers eat very little for days and lose weight. Why might this behavior still be worth the cost?
  lines: 3
  key: The father's cost is temporary, but guarding multiplies the number of young that survive (77.5% versus 15% successful nests), so it greatly increases his reproductive success.
