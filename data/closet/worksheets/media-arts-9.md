# 1. Shot Types Reference
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, eraser
ican: I can name, sketch and choose shot types to tell a story.
directions: Match each shot type to a use. Then make your own reference sheet: sketch each of the 10 shot types in its box, using the same two characters (a coach and a player in a gym) for every sketch. Under each sketch, note where the frame cuts the body. Finish with a short sequence.
mistakes: Medium wide, medium and medium close-up blur together: check the frame lines (mid-thigh, waist, chest). Over-the-shoulder shots need the foreground shoulder and the back of a head in frame, not just two faces. An insert is a close shot of an object or detail that matters to the story (a stopwatch, a note), not a close-up of a face.
notes: Shot names vary a little between crews and textbooks; the medium wide is also called the "cowboy shot" because westerns framed it to show a holstered gun. A useful check: hold up a phone-sized paper frame and walk through the sizes on a classmate. Students can keep this sheet as a reference all year.

## Warm up: Shot and use
match: Write the letter of the best use next to each shot.
  - Extreme wide shot = Establish a big location and how small the people are in it
  - Wide shot = Show a whole action and the space around it
  - Full shot = Show one person head to toe: costume, posture, movement
  - Medium wide shot = Show a person from mid-thigh up, with body language and some setting
  - Medium shot = Show a person from the waist up during conversation
  - Medium close-up = Show chest and face: emotion while keeping some gesture
  - Close-up = Show a face and its emotion clearly
  - Extreme close-up = Isolate one detail, like eyes or a mouth, for intensity
  - Over-the-shoulder shot = Show a conversation from one character's point of view
  - Insert shot = Show an object or detail the audience must notice

## Stretch
write: Plan a 4-shot sequence: the player misses a free throw, then the coach reacts. Name each shot type and say what it shows.
  lines: 5
  key: Answers vary. Example: 1) wide: the player at the line in the quiet gym; 2) close-up: the player's face as they shoot; 3) insert: the ball rolling off the rim; 4) medium close-up: the coach, calm, nodding. A strong plan changes shot size for a reason and ends on the reaction.

# 21. Color Associations
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils or markers
ican: I can choose a color palette for a purpose and audience and explain my choices.
directions: Read the passage. Answer the questions, using the passage. Then choose a palette for a fictional business and explain each choice.
mistakes: Students often treat color meanings as fixed rules ("blue means sad"). The passage argues meanings are learned and depend on culture and context. Watch for palettes with no contrast (pale yellow text on white); a palette has to work in black and white and for people with color vision deficiency.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 460 words). The figures on color vision deficiency (about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women) are the commonly cited estimates for people of Northern European descent; rates vary by population. Students can test contrast by photocopying their swatches in black and white.

## Read
passage: What Color Says
  Walk down a grocery aisle and you are reading colors before you read a single word. Green labels whisper "natural." Black and gold boxes promise something fancy. Bright red and yellow shout "sale" or "fast." Designers use these associations every day, but where do they come from?
  Some color links probably start in nature. Blue skies and calm water may be part of why many people connect blue with calm and trust, and why surveys in many countries often find blue to be a favorite color. Green is the color of leaves and growing things, so it often signals health, nature or the environment. Red is the color of blood and fire, which may explain why it grabs attention and feels urgent.
  Many associations, though, are learned. Purple was linked to royalty in parts of Europe and the Mediterranean because one famous purple dye, made from sea snails, was so expensive that only the very rich could afford it. Today the dye is not rare, but the link to luxury lingers. In the United States, green also means money, because of the color of paper dollars. Traffic lights teach drivers that red means stop and green means go, and those meanings spread to buttons, apps and warning signs.
  Culture matters too. In many Western traditions, brides wear white and mourners wear black. In parts of East Asia, white has traditionally been worn for mourning, while red is the color of luck and celebration at weddings and New Year. A designer who assumes one meaning for the whole world can send the wrong message.
  Context changes meaning even inside one culture. Red on a candy wrapper feels playful; red on a hospital sign means emergency. Yellow can feel sunny and cheerful, but yellow with black stripes says caution. The same color can whisper or shout depending on how bright it is, how much of it there is and what sits next to it.
  That is why designers build palettes, not single colors. A common starting point is to choose one main color for most of a design, a second color for support and an accent color for the few things that must stand out, like a button or a headline. Some designers use a 60, 30, 10 split as a rule of thumb.
  Good palettes also work for everyone. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of color vision deficiency, most often trouble telling red from green. A design that uses only red and green to show "yes" and "no" may fail for them. Strong contrast between light and dark, plus icons or words alongside color, keeps the message clear.
  Color never speaks alone. It speaks to a particular audience, in a particular place, for a particular purpose.

## Apply: Choose a palette
example: **Pick one fictional business:** Tide Pool Swim School (lessons for young kids), Ember and Oak (a quiet bookstore cafe), or Volt Bikes (an electric bike repair shop).
write: Main color (about 60%): name it and tell why it fits the business and its audience.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example for Tide Pool Swim School: deep navy, which suggests calm, safe water and trust for parents.
write: Support color (about 30%): name it and tell why.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: sandy beige, which suggests the beach and keeps the design light and friendly.
write: Accent color (about 10%): name it and tell what it will highlight.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Example: bright coral for the "Sign up" button and class times, so they stand out against navy and beige.
