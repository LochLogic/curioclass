# 1. Drug Categories Chart
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can classify drugs by how they affect the body and brain.
directions: Read the category key. Write the category for each of the 10 substances. Then write one effect for each category and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often call nicotine and caffeine "not real drugs." Both are stimulants that change how the brain works, and nicotine is highly addictive. Opioids also slow the body like depressants; they get their own category because of how they act on pain and how easily they cause overdose. Some students think prescription drugs are always safe: oxycodone and benzodiazepines are prescription drugs that are dangerous when misused.
notes: Categories follow the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) and the DEA's drug fact sheets. Keep the sheet factual; no street names or details of use are needed. Naloxone is a medicine that can reverse an opioid overdose; many states let anyone carry it. Fentanyl is now found in many fake pills that look like prescription drugs, which is why a pill not from a pharmacy is never safe. Follow up privately with any student who shares personal or family use.

## Warm up: The category key
text: **Stimulants** speed up the brain and body. **Depressants** slow down the brain and body. **Opioids** block pain signals and slow breathing. **Cannabis** products contain THC, which changes thinking and memory. **Hallucinogens** change how a person sees, hears and feels reality.

  - Benzodiazepines (prescription sedatives) | {depressant}

## Main: One effect of each category
table: Category | One effect on the body or brain
  - Stimulants | {Faster heart rate and higher blood pressure; feeling alert, then a crash}
  - Depressants | {Slowed reactions, drowsiness, poor coordination; can slow breathing}
  - Opioids | {Slowed breathing that can stop in an overdose; highly addictive}
  - Cannabis | {Trouble with memory, attention and coordination; can harm the developing brain}
  - Hallucinogens | {Seeing or hearing things that are not there; changed sense of time; unpredictable reactions}

## Stretch
write: Fentanyl is often hidden in fake pills made to look like real prescription medicine. What is one safe rule this fact suggests? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Only take medicine prescribed to you and given by a pharmacy or a trusted adult. A pill from a friend or online could contain fentanyl, and even a tiny amount can cause a deadly overdose.

# 7. Refusal Showcase
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can judge refusal skills against clear criteria and make a weak refusal stronger.
directions: Read the 5-point refusal checklist. Read the four pretend dialogues and rate each one: write yes or no for each point, then add up the yes answers. Rewrite the weakest dialogue so it meets all five points.
mistakes: Students often count "maybe later" or "I don't know" as saying no. A clear refusal uses the word no or an equally plain statement. On point 3, students must use the stage directions in brackets: words alone cannot show body language. In Dialogue D, Jamal does many things well but stays and keeps arguing, so point 5 is no.
notes: All four dialogues and characters are made up for Curio Class. The checklist combines common refusal skill steps taught in health programs: say no clearly, give a reason, use strong body language, offer an alternative, and leave if the pressure keeps going. Totals: A 5, B 0, C 3, D 4. Pills that are not prescribed to you can be dangerous; today, fake pills may contain fentanyl.

## Warm up: The checklist
text: **1. Clear no:** says "no" or something just as plain. **2. Reason:** gives a reason (it is okay to keep it short). **3. Body language:** eye contact, steady voice, stands tall (look at the words in brackets). **4. Alternative:** suggests something else to do. **5. Walks away:** leaves or repeats no if the pressure keeps going.

## Read: Four dialogues
source: Dialogue A: At the park
  Leo: Want a hit? It's mint.
  Ana [looks right at him, steady voice]: No thanks, I don't vape. I've got a game Saturday and I need my lungs. Want to shoot hoops instead?
  Leo: Come on, just one.
  Ana: I said no. I'm heading to the court. Come if you want. [walks off]
source: Dialogue B: At a party
  Sam: Here, I poured you one.
  Riley [looks at the floor, quiet voice]: Um, I don't know... maybe later?
  Sam: Just hold it then.
  Riley [takes the cup and stays]: Okay, I guess.
source: Dialogue C: In the locker room
  Max: My brother's pills make you feel amazing. Want one?
  Jo [laughs nervously, scrolls on phone]: No, I'm not taking pills that aren't mine. They could make me really sick.
  Max: You're no fun.
  Jo: I'm going to go find Sam. [leaves]
source: Dialogue D: Behind the store
  Kai: Have a sip. Nobody will know.
  Jamal [stands tall, eye contact]: No. If I drink, I lose my spot on the team. Let's go get pizza instead.
  Kai: Wow, scared of your coach?
  Jamal [keeps standing there]: I'm not scared. Why do you even care? [They argue for ten minutes.]

  - B: Riley | {no} | {no} | {no} | {no} | {no} | {0}
  - C: Jo | {yes} | {yes} | {no} | {no} | {yes} | {3}
  - D: Jamal | {yes} | {yes} | {yes} | {yes} | {no} | {4}

## Main: Think about it
choice: Which dialogue is the weakest refusal?
  - Dialogue A
  * Dialogue B
  - Dialogue C
  - Dialogue D
choice: What could Jamal do to make his refusal score 5 out of 5?
  * Repeat "no" once and leave instead of arguing
  - Give a longer reason

write: Jo's words were strong, but her body language was not. Why does body language matter in a refusal?
  lines: 2
  key: Nervous laughing and looking at a phone can make it seem like she is unsure, so the other person may keep pushing. Eye contact and a steady voice show she means it.

## Rewrite the weakest
write: Rewrite the weakest dialogue so the refuser meets all five points. Include stage directions in brackets.
  lines: 6
  key: Model for Dialogue B: Sam: "Here, I poured you one." Riley [looks at Sam, steady voice]: "No thanks, I don't drink. I want to remember tonight and I've got practice tomorrow. Want to go get some snacks?" Sam: "Just hold it then." Riley: "No, I'm good. I'm going to go find Jess." [walks away]

## Stretch
write: Some people think refusing will cost them friends. What would you tell a younger student who feels that way?
  lines: 3
  key: Real friends respect a no. A short, friendly refusal with an alternative keeps the friendship open. If someone keeps pushing after you say no, that tells you something about the friendship, not about you.

# 20. Mental Health Check
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can show what I know about the mental health continuum, common conditions, stigma and where to get help.
directions: Answer all 10 items. Then try the stretch.
mistakes: Students often treat the continuum as two boxes, "healthy" or "mentally ill." Stress that everyone moves along it, and that a person with a diagnosed condition can be doing well. On stigma, watch for answers that blame a person for a condition; mental health conditions are not a choice or a weakness.
notes: Facts follow NIMH, the CDC and the WHO: anxiety and depression are among the most common mental health conditions in teens; about half of all lifetime mental health conditions begin by age 14 (WHO); treatment such as therapy and, for some people, medicine works for most people. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers calls and texts at 988. Person-first language ("a person with depression," not "a depressed person") is one common way to reduce stigma; some people prefer other wording. Keep this a review, not a screening of students.

## The continuum
text: Mental health is a **continuum**: a range from **thriving** (coping well, enjoying life) to **struggling** (stressed, not sleeping well, pulling back) to **in crisis** (cannot get through daily life, may be unsafe). People move along it over time.
choice: Rafi has been sleeping well, enjoying friends and handling stress from exams. Where is he right now?
  * Thriving
  - Struggling
  - In crisis
choice: Which statement about the continuum is true?
  - Once you are struggling, you stay there
  - Only people with a diagnosis are on the continuum
  * Everyone is on it, and people can move toward thriving with support

## Common conditions
match: Match each condition to a description.
  - Anxiety disorder = Worry or fear that is hard to control and gets in the way of daily life
  - Depression = Sadness or loss of interest that lasts two weeks or more
  - ADHD = Ongoing trouble paying attention, sitting still or waiting, starting in childhood
fill:
  - About half of all lifetime mental health conditions begin by age {14}.

## Stigma
choice: Which is an example of stigma?
  - Asking a friend how they are doing

  - Learning the warning signs of depression
choice: Which way of talking reduces stigma?
  - "People with mental illness are dangerous."
  - "He should just snap out of it."

## Resources
choice: Who is a good first person to talk to at school about a mental health concern?
  - A random classmate online
  * A school counselor or nurse
  - Nobody; it should stay private
fill:
  - To reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, call or text {988}.
write: Name two other places or people a teen could go to for help.
  lines: 2
  key: Any two: a parent or guardian, a trusted teacher or coach, a doctor or nurse, a therapist or counselor, a crisis text line, a faith leader.

## Stretch
write: A classmate says, "Getting help for your mental health means you're weak." Write a respectful response that uses a fact from this sheet.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: "Mental health conditions are common, and about half start by age 14, so lots of people deal with them. Getting help is like seeing a doctor for a broken arm. Treatment works for most people, and asking for help takes strength."

# 21. Healthy, Unhealthy, Abusive
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell the difference between healthy, unhealthy and abusive relationship behaviors.
directions: Relationships fall on a spectrum. Cut out the 12 behavior cards and glue each one under healthy, unhealthy or abusive. Then explain the line between two categories.
mistakes: Students often put "checks your phone" or "tells you who you can see" under unhealthy because it can look like caring. When one person controls the other, it is abusive. Watch for students who think abuse must be physical: threats, isolation and control are abuse too.
notes: The spectrum follows the healthy, unhealthy and abusive framework used by loveisrespect, the national youth dating abuse helpline (call 1-866-331-9474 or text LOVEIS to 22522; check the numbers before printing). Behaviors apply to friendships and dating relationships. Use story framing only; never ask students to describe their own relationships. Have counselor contacts ready; a disclosure means following your school's reporting policy.

## Warm up
choice: Which is the best description of a healthy relationship?
  - One person makes all the decisions
  * Both people feel safe, respected and free to be themselves
  - The two people never disagree

## Main: Sort the behaviors
sort: Glue each card under its column.
  - Healthy: Talks through disagreements calmly, Respects when you say no, Supports time with your other friends, Is happy for you when you succeed
  - Unhealthy: Gives the silent treatment after an argument, Teases in ways that hurt then says "just kidding", Often cancels plans without telling you, Pressures you to spend all your free time together
  - Abusive: Demands your passwords and checks your phone, Threatens to hurt you or themselves if you leave, Pushes or grabs you when angry, Tells you which friends you are allowed to see

## Explain the line
write: What is the difference between an unhealthy behavior and an abusive one? Use one card from each column.
  lines: 4
  starter: An unhealthy behavior like ... but an abusive behavior like ...
  key: Unhealthy behaviors (like the silent treatment) hurt the relationship but can change if both people talk and work on it. Abusive behaviors (like demanding passwords or threats) are used to gain power and control over the other person, and they can make that person feel afraid. Any physical violence is abuse.
write: What is the difference between a healthy behavior and an unhealthy one? Use one card from each column.
  lines: 3
  key: Healthy behaviors show respect and trust (like talking through disagreements calmly). Unhealthy ones show poor communication or pressure (like giving the silent treatment) even if no one means harm.

## Stretch
write: Maya notices her friend Kira's partner checks Kira's phone and tells her who she can hang out with. Write two things Maya could do to help.
  lines: 3
  key: Tell Kira she is worried and that Kira does not deserve to be controlled; listen without judging; encourage Kira to talk to a trusted adult or counselor; share the loveisrespect helpline; tell a trusted adult herself if Kira is in danger.

# 33. Pressure to Share Photos
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can explain the risks and laws about sharing private photos and know how to get help.
directions: Read the passage. Highlight the parts about the law and about getting help. Then answer the five questions.
mistakes: Students may believe a photo sent to one person "disappears" or stays private. Once it is sent, the sender cannot control it. Watch for blame: a person whose photo is shared without their consent is not at fault, and they deserve help, not judgment.
notes: The passage is written for Curio Class. Facts: in the U.S., making, sending or keeping a nude or sexual image of anyone under 18 is illegal under federal law, even when a teen took the photo of themselves; many states have separate laws for teens, and consequences vary by state. Take It Down (takeitdown.ncmec.org) is a free service from the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) that helps remove or stop the online sharing of nude or sexual images taken before age 18. Reports can also go to NCMEC's CyberTipline. The FBI's advice for sextortion: stop replying, do not pay, keep the messages, block the account and tell a trusted adult. Check your school's policy and your state's law before teaching, and tell students how to reach the counselor privately. Handle questions about real cases one on one.

## Read
passage: When Someone Asks for a Photo | numbered
  Most teens use their phones to chat, joke and share pictures with friends. Sometimes, though, someone asks for something different: a private photo, such as a nude or partly nude picture. The person asking may be a boyfriend or girlfriend, a classmate, or someone met online. They may say, "Everyone does it," "If you trusted me, you would," or "I'll delete it right away."
  A request like that is pressure, and pressure is a red flag. Once a photo is sent, the sender loses control of it. It can be saved with a screenshot, forwarded to a group chat or posted, even if it was meant for one person. Apps that promise photos disappear cannot stop someone from taking a picture of the screen.
  There are laws, too. In the United States, a nude or sexual image of anyone under 18 is illegal to make, send or keep under federal law. That is true even if the person in the photo took it of themselves. Many states also have their own laws about teens and these images, and the consequences can include school discipline or legal charges.
  Some people who ask for photos are not who they say they are. In a scam called sextortion, an adult pretends to be a teen online, gets a photo, and then threatens to share it unless the teen sends money or more pictures. The FBI says the best response is to stop replying, not to pay, to keep the messages as evidence, to block the account and to tell a trusted adult.
  If a photo has already been shared, it is not too late to get help. The person in the photo is not to blame. A parent, counselor or other trusted adult can help report it. A free service called Take It Down, run by the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, helps remove these images of people under 18 from many websites and apps.
  The safest answer to pressure is a clear no. Saying "No, I don't do that" and leaving the chat is always okay.
