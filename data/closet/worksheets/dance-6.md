# 1. BEST Elements Chart
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, open floor space
ican: I can analyze a dance phrase using Body, Energy, Space and Time.
directions: Sort the warm up observations by element. Then watch the phrase (your teacher will perform it, or read it below and try it yourself) and list 2 specific observations for each BEST element.
mistakes: Students write vague observations ("it was cool," "lots of moving"). Push for specifics: which body part, what kind of energy, which level or direction, how fast. Energy and Time get confused: "quick" is Time; "sharp" or "strong" is Energy.
notes: BEST is a common classroom frame for the elements of dance: Body (what moves, shapes), Energy (how it moves: force, flow, weight), Space (where: levels, directions, pathways, size) and Time (when: tempo, rhythm, pauses). Perform the sample phrase twice, or let students try it in small groups and observe each other. Students may also analyze a phrase they made earlier in the unit.

## Warm up: Which element?
example: "The dancer froze for 2 counts" is about **Time**: it describes when and how long.
table: Observation | Element (Body, Energy, Space or Time)
  - The arms made a wide, round shape. | {Body}
  - The punch was sharp and strong. | {Energy}
  - She traveled on a diagonal toward the audience. | {Space}
  - The turns sped up, then slowed down. | {Time}
  - He stayed in a crouch at a low level. | {Space}
  - The walk was heavy, like trudging through mud. | {Energy}

## Main: The phrase
passage: Sample Phrase: Storm Arrives
  Counts 1-4: Stand upstage center with the feet together. Slowly raise both arms out to the sides until they reach high above the head, palms facing each other.
  Counts 5-6: Bring both fists down fast and hard in front of the body while bending the knees into a deep squat.
  Counts 7-8: Hold the squat, perfectly still, with the head bowed.
  Counts 1-4: Spring up and travel forward on a zigzag path with four quick, light runs on the balls of the feet.
  Counts 5-8: Spin once to the right with the arms wrapped around the body, then unfold one arm slowly toward the audience and hold the reach until the music ends.
  Count the phrase as two sets of 8, 16 counts in all. As you watch or try it, notice what the body does, how the energy changes, where it travels and how the timing shifts from slow to sudden to still.
  The phrase is short, but almost every element changes at least once. The body grows tall, then folds small, then spirals. The energy swings from calm to forceful to light. The dancer starts far from the audience and ends close to it. The timing moves from slow to sudden, then stops completely before rushing forward again. A choreographer uses contrasts like these on purpose, because a change is what makes an audience notice.

## Main: BEST chart
text: Rows in order: **B**ody (what moves, shapes), **E**nergy (force, flow, weight), **S**pace (levels, directions, pathways), **T**ime (tempo, rhythm, stillness).
chart: Element | Observation 1 (what I saw, and on which counts) | Observation 2 (what I saw, and on which counts)
  rows: 4
  key: Body: arms rise to a high V; fists strike down; arms wrap the body in the spin. Energy: sustained rise; strong, sudden strike; light runs. Space: starts upstage center; drops to a low level; zigzag path forward toward the audience. Time: slow 4-count rise; 2-count hold of stillness; quick runs; slow final reach.
write: Which element changes the most in this phrase? Give evidence from two different counts.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: Energy changes the most: counts 1-4 are slow and sustained, counts 5-6 are sudden and strong, and the runs are light and quick.

## Stretch
write: Change one element of the phrase on purpose (for example, do the strike slowly). How does that change what the phrase means or how it feels?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names the element, the change and its effect, for example "A slow strike feels heavy and sad instead of angry, so the storm seems tired instead of fierce."

# 7. Intent Statement
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can write an intent statement that explains the idea, audience, mood and movement choices of my dance.
directions: Read the model intent statement and label its parts. Then write your own 4-sentence intent statement: the idea, the audience, the mood and the movement choices that will show it.
mistakes: Students write what the dance is "about" but never say how the movement will show it. Sentence 4 must name specific choices (levels, pathways, dynamics, formations, devices). Others name an audience that is too vague ("people"); ask who will actually watch and what they already know.
notes: An intent statement is the choreographer's plan in words: it guides choices in rehearsal and helps when giving and getting feedback. Students can write it for a dance they are making now. Revisit the statement after the first run-through and revise it if the dance has changed.

## Warm up: Label the model
example: (1) My dance is about the moment a shy new student finds a friend. (2) It is for families at our spring showcase, many of whom remember being new. (3) The mood moves from lonely and tense to warm and joyful. (4) I will start the soloist at a low level, isolated downstage left, with small, bound movements, and end with the group in a circle around her, moving with large, free, flowing movement.
fill:
  - Sentence {1} names the idea.
  - Sentence {2} names the audience.
  - Sentence {3} names the mood.
  - Sentence {4} names the movement choices.
choice: In sentence 4, which choice shows "lonely"?
  - large, free, flowing movement
  * a low level, isolated downstage left, with small, bound movements
  - the group in a circle
choice: Which is the strongest sentence for movement choices?
  - The dancers will move in ways that show the feeling.
  - The dance will be really good and creative.
  * Dancers will use sharp, sudden gestures and zigzag pathways to show anxiety.

## Main: My intent statement
write: Sentence 1, the idea.
  lines: 2
  starter: My dance is about ...
  key: Answers vary, for example "My dance is about how a city changes from night to morning."
write: Sentence 2, the audience.
  lines: 2
  starter: It is for ...
  key: Answers vary, for example "It is for younger students at the school assembly, who walk through the city every day."
write: Sentence 3, the mood.
  lines: 2
  starter: The mood is ... / The mood moves from ... to ...
  key: Answers vary, for example "The mood moves from quiet and mysterious to busy and energetic."
write: Sentence 4, the movement choices that will show it.
  lines: 3
  starter: I will use ...
  key: Answers vary; a strong answer names at least two specific choices, for example "I will use slow, sustained movement at a low level for night, then quick, sharp steps in crossing straight pathways and a canon for the morning rush."

## Stretch
write: Trade statements with a partner. Which of their movement choices best fits their mood? Which one could be stronger?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer quotes a choice, explains why it fits the mood, and suggests a specific change, such as adding a level change or a stillness.

# 20. Safety Scenario Task Cards
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, scissors (optional)
ican: I can choose safe responses to problems that come up when dancing in different spaces.
directions: Read each task card. In the table, write a safe response and the reason it is safe. Then answer the stretch question.
mistakes: Students give a response with no reason ("just be careful"). The reason should name what the response prevents: slipping, a pulled muscle, a jarring landing, a collision or a worse injury. Some students say to "dance through" pain; pain is a signal to stop and tell an adult.
notes: Cards can be cut apart for a station rotation or solved in place. Responses other than the model are fine if they are safe and the reason is sound. This sheet is about safe habits for any dancer, not about any student's own injuries. In a real injury, students tell the teacher or an adult right away.

## Warm up: Safe or not?
choice: Before a rehearsal on a cold morning, the safest first step is
  - a deep split stretch
  * a gradual warm up that raises the heart rate
  - a big jump to test the floor
choice: The safest way to land a jump is
  - on flat feet with straight knees
  - on your heels first
  * through the toes, balls and heels with bent knees over the toes

## Main: Task cards
cards: Solve each card.
  - The studio floor has a wet patch near the door after someone tracked in rain.
  - It is a cold morning, and your group wants to start right away with high kicks.
  - Your phrase ends with a jump, and dancers are landing loudly with stiff legs.
  - During rehearsal, a dancer feels a sudden sharp pain in her ankle.
  - Your group of 8 must travel across a small classroom at the same time.
table: Card | Safe response | Reason
  height: 0.7
  - 1 | {Stop and tell the teacher; dry the floor or mark it off and dance away from it.} | {A wet floor is slippery and can cause falls and sprains.}
  - 2 | {Warm up first with gentle moves that raise the heart rate, then build up to the kicks.} | {Warm muscles stretch more easily, so they are less likely to pull or strain.}
  - 3 | {Land through toes, balls and heels with soft knees over the toes; keep jumps small until it is quiet.} | {Bending spreads the landing force over more time, so less force hits the joints.}
  - 4 | {Stop dancing right away, tell the teacher, and do not keep dancing on it.} | {Sharp pain is a warning sign; dancing on it can make an injury worse.}
  - 5 | {Travel in small groups or in the same direction, with space between dancers.} | {Fewer dancers moving at once prevents collisions.}

## Stretch
write: Write your own task card about dancing safely outdoors, then answer it.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary, for example "Card: The performance is on a hot afternoon on a concrete plaza. Response: Drink water, rest in the shade, wear shoes, and make jumps smaller. Reason: Heat can cause dehydration, and concrete does not absorb landing force."

# 21. Notes into Actions
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, open floor space, a run-through of a class dance
ican: I can turn a director's notes into specific changes and check that I made them.
directions: Turn each director's note into a specific action you can do on stage: say what, where or when. Then do two run-throughs and check off each action you remembered.
mistakes: Students restate the note instead of turning it into an action ("Be bigger" becomes "be big"). A specific action names a body part, a count, a place or a direction. Students also stop checking after Run 1: the second run is where notes stick.
notes: These six notes are typical when a dance moves from the classroom to a new space (a gym, a stage, outdoors). Read each note aloud and act out the vague version, then a specific fix. Run 1 and Run 2 can be the same day or two different rehearsals. In Run 2 students can ask a partner to watch for their actions.

## Warm up: Vague or specific?
choice: Which is the most specific action for the note "Use the whole stage"?
  - Move around more.
  * On counts 1-8, travel all the way to the downstage right corner.
  - Remember the stage is big.
choice: Which is the most specific action for the note "I can't see your face"?
  - Smile.
  - Face the front.
  * On the first 8 counts, lift my chin and look over the audience's heads, not at the floor.

## Main: Translate the notes
write: Note 1: "Bigger!"
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Stretch my arms all the way to the fingertips on the reach (counts 3-4) and take wider steps on the travel.
write: Note 2: "You're late on the turn."
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Start my turn exactly on count 5 by preparing on count 4 and listening for the drum hit.
write: Note 3: "The lines are crooked."
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Before we start, check the dancer on my left with my eyes and stand on my tape mark.
write: Note 4: "The ending is fuzzy."
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Hit my final pose on count 8 and hold it, without moving, until the music fully stops.
write: Note 5: "Too quiet: we can't feel the energy."
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Make the stomps on counts 1 and 3 strong and sharp, and breathe out on each one.
write: Note 6: "This space is wider than our classroom."
  lines: 1
  key: Model: Spread the formation so there are two big steps between me and the next dancer, and travel farther on the zigzag.

## Stretch
write: Which note was hardest to fix? What will you do in the next rehearsal to make it stick?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names one note, explains why it was hard (for example, the count goes by fast), and gives a plan such as marking it slowly first or asking a partner to cue it.

# 33. Folk Dances of Many Communities
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencil
ican: I can locate folk dances on a map and explain the social purpose of each.
directions: Read about six folk dances. Fill in the table with each dance's country or region and its social purpose. Then write each dance's letter on the world map where it comes from.
mistakes: Students mix up the hora and the Kalamatianos, which are both circle or line dances in southeastern Europe: the Kalamatianos is Greek, the hora is Romanian, Moldovan and Israeli. Students may write "fun" for every purpose; push for the specific social purpose in the reading (courtship, welcoming, mourning, uniting a community).
notes: The map is simplified: rough land shapes, and islands such as Great Britain are left off, so Scotland's spot is in the sea just west of Europe. Europe is crowded at this scale; students can draw a short line from the letter to the spot. The key shows the hora at both Romania and Israel. The passage is written for Curio Class.

## Warm up: Read
passage: Six Folk Dances
  A folk dance grows out of a community's life. It is passed from person to person rather than invented by one choreographer, and it usually has a social purpose: it brings people together.
  The **polka** (A) began in Bohemia, now part of the Czech Republic, in the 1830s. This lively couple dance with quick hops spread to ballrooms across Europe and the Americas, where it became a favorite way for people to meet and dance together.
  The **hora** (B) is a circle dance of Romania and Moldova, and also of Israel and Jewish communities worldwide. Dancers join hands at weddings and celebrations; the closed circle shows a community united.
  The **Kalamatianos** (C) is a Greek line dance in an uneven seven-beat rhythm. At weddings, festivals and national holidays, a leader at the front of the line waves a handkerchief and adds fancy steps.
  The **jarabe tapatío** (D) comes from Jalisco, Mexico. This couple dance acts out a playful courtship, with the dancers' footwork answering each other, and it is danced at fiestas as a symbol of Mexican pride.
  The **Scottish reel** (E) is a social dance from Scotland in which sets of dancers weave figure-eight patterns. Reels fill community dances called ceilidhs.
  **Adowa** (F) is a dance of the Akan people of Ghana, performed to drums at funerals and festivals. Its graceful hand gestures carry meanings, so dancers can express grief, honor or respect without words.
