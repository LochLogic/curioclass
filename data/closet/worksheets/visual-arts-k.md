# 1. Line Walk: Five Kinds of Lines
minutes: 20
materials: crayons, pencil
pairs: line-safari
ican: I can name and draw straight, curvy, zigzag, wavy and spiral lines.
directions: (Read aloud.) Walk your finger along each dotted line. Then trace it with a crayon. Then draw each line again on your own in the big box.
mistakes: A zigzag has sharp points; a wavy line has round bumps. Curvy and wavy get mixed up: a curvy line bends once like a rainbow, a wavy line goes up and down again and again like water. Spirals often turn into circles that close; show how a spiral keeps going around and gets bigger, like a snail shell.
notes: Act out each line first: stand up and "draw" it in the air with your whole arm, then with one finger. Say each line name together before tracing. The sheet pairs with the Line Safari tool if you want to watch the lines draw themselves first.

## Main: Trace each line
picword:
  - trace-straight | straight
  - trace-curvy | curvy
  - trace-zigzag | zigzag
  - trace-wavy | wavy
  - trace-spiral | spiral

## Draw: Now you draw them
draw: Draw all five on your own: straight, curvy, zigzag, wavy, spiral | 1.7

## Stretch: Line hunt
write: (Read aloud.) Find a line in our room. Draw it on the back. Tell your teacher: what kind of line is it? (Your teacher can write it here.)
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. Examples: the edge of the door is straight, the clock is curvy, the radiator or a stair rail is zigzag, a curtain fold is wavy, a seashell or a pencil sharpener shaving is a spiral.

# 5. Red, Yellow, Blue: First Colors
minutes: 20
materials: red, yellow and blue crayons, pencil
ican: I can name the three first colors: red, yellow and blue.
directions: (Read aloud.) Listen to the color word. Draw one thing that is that color in its box, using that crayon. Then trace each color word with the same crayon.
mistakes: Children often draw with whatever crayon is in hand: hold up the red crayon before the red box and have everyone hold theirs up too. Some will draw an orange or pink thing for red; ask "Is it red, or a different color?" Tracing works best slowly, starting at the dot of each letter.
notes: Ideas to share before drawing: red, an apple, a fire truck or a strawberry; yellow, the sun, a banana or a duck; blue, the sky, water or a blueberry. Red, yellow and blue are called the primary colors: we cannot mix them from other paints, but we can mix them to make many other colors, such as orange, green and violet. Accept any thing a child can name in that color.

## Main: Draw it
draw: Red: draw one red thing | 1.35
draw: Yellow: draw one yellow thing | 1.35
draw: Blue: draw one blue thing | 1.35

## Trace: The color words
text: (Read aloud.) Trace each word with the crayon of that color.
trace: red yellow blue

## Stretch: Look around
write: (Read aloud.) Find something red, yellow or blue in our room. Tell your teacher what it is.
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary: a red chair, a yellow pencil, a blue book bag. The child names the object and its color.

# 15. Mola Layers from Panama
minutes: 35
materials: colored paper in 3 colors (pre-cut squares and strips help), safety scissors, glue stick, pencil
ican: I can layer three colors of paper to make an animal like a Guna mola.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at the mola picture your teacher shows. Circle the animal you will make. Glue your biggest paper in the box. Cut an animal shape from your second color and glue it on top. Then cut thin lines and shapes from your third color and glue them on the animal.
mistakes: Children often glue all three colors side by side. Show the stacking: big on the bottom, animal in the middle, small lines on top, so the colors peek out around each other like a real mola. Big, simple animal shapes work best; cut the shape first, details later. Use dots of glue, not puddles.
notes: Show a photo of a mola before starting (museum collections online have many). Molas are made by Guna women of Panama, many of whom live on the Guna Yala islands along the Caribbean coast. A mola is a cloth panel sewn onto the front and back of a blouse. The artist stacks layers of colored cloth, then cuts away parts of the top layers and sews the edges under, so the colors below show through in bright outlines. Animals, plants and patterns are favorite subjects. Say "Guna" (GOO-nah); older books spell it Kuna. Paper is our way to try the idea; real molas are cut and sewn by hand.

## Warm up: Pick an animal
text: (Read aloud.) Circle the animal you will make.
picword:
  - fish | fish
  - bird | bird
  - frog | frog
  - owl | owl

## Main: My mola
draw: Glue your layers here: 1 big paper, 2 animal, 3 little lines | 3.6

## Stretch: Count the colors
fill:
  - (Read aloud.) How many colors are in your mola? Write the number. {3}
note: Most children use 3 colors. If a child added more layers, accept the real number and praise the extra layer.

# 21. Pick a Happy Moment
minutes: 25
materials: crayons, pencil
ican: I can choose a happy moment and draw the most important part big.
directions: (Read aloud.) Look at the three happy moments. Circle the one you want to draw, or think of your own. Then draw the main thing big in the box. Fill the box!
mistakes: Many young artists draw tiny figures along the bottom edge. Before drawing, have them touch the four sides of the box and say "my picture touches the sides." A child who freezes can start with one shape: the ball, the snowman or the basket.
notes: Any happy moment works, real or pretend. Nobody needs to tell about their family or home; a pretend snow day is a fine choice. Talk first: "What do you see? Who is there? What are they doing?" Write the child's words on the line if they want a caption.

## Warm up: Choose one
text: (Read aloud.) Circle one: a game, a snow day or a picnic.
picword:
  - ball | a game
  - snowman | a snow day
  - basket | a picnic

## Main: Draw it big
draw: My happy moment: draw the main thing big | 3.3

## Stretch: Tell about it
write: (Read aloud.) What is happening in your picture? Tell your teacher one sentence.
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A good caption names the main thing and an action: "I am kicking the ball." "We built a snowman."

# 25. Feelings Faces
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
pairs: feelings-faces
ican: I can draw eyes and a mouth to show a feeling.
directions: (Read aloud.) Each face is missing its eyes and mouth. Listen to the feeling word. Draw eyes and a mouth to show it. Then pick one face for a story and draw it big.
mistakes: Many children draw a smile on every face. Make each face together in the mirror of your own face first: show a big smile, then a frown, then a round open "oh," then droopy eyes. Eyebrows help a lot; invite children to add them.
notes: Model answers: happy, open eyes and a mouth curved up; sad, eyes looking down, eyebrows slanting up in the middle, mouth curved down; surprised, big round eyes, eyebrows high, mouth a round O; sleepy, closed eyes drawn as curved lines and a small mouth or a big yawn. All faces are pretend: nobody needs to say when they felt this way. The Feelings Faces tool shows faces changing on the board if you want to warm up together.

## Main: Finish the faces
picword:
  - face-outline | happy
  - face-outline | sad
  - face-outline | surprised
  - face-outline | sleepy

## Story: Pick one
text: (Read aloud.) Pick one feeling. Draw a big picture of a pretend friend with that face. What is happening?
draw: My story picture | 3.3

## Stretch: Tell about it
write: (Read aloud.) Why does your pretend friend feel that way? Tell your teacher.
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A good answer gives a reason: "She is surprised because a frog jumped out." "He is sleepy because it is night."

# 35. My Art Label
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons, a highlighter for the teacher
ican: I can make a museum label with my name and a title for my art.
directions: (Read aloud.) Museums put a label next to each artwork. It tells who made the art and what it is called. Trace your name on the label. Then draw or tell a title for your artwork, and draw a tiny picture of it.
mistakes: Before handing out, write each child's first name in highlighter on the Artist line so they can trace it in pencil. Children may say "my picture" as a title: ask "What is in it? What is it about?" and help them find a name like "The Big Red Truck."
notes: The tracing of each child's own name is done on the teacher's highlighter letters, because each name is different. A child who cannot yet write a title can dictate it while you write it lightly for tracing, or draw a picture title. Cut out the finished label and post it next to the artwork for your class show.

## Warm up: What is a label?
choice: (Read aloud.) What does a museum label tell you?
  - What to eat for lunch
  * Who made the art and its name
  - How to get home
trace: Artist

## Main: My label
chart: My Art Label
  labels: Artist (my name); Title (the name of my art); I used (crayons, paint, clay, paper)
  height: 0.8
draw: A tiny picture of my art | 1.2

## Stretch: Tell about it
write: (Read aloud.) What do you want people to notice in your art? Tell your teacher.
  lines: 1
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer points to one part: "Look at the big wheels." "I used lots of blue."
