# 1. Neuron Label and Signal Path
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can identify the parts of a neuron and trace how an action potential travels from dendrite to synapse.
directions: Draw a neuron from the description and label its 8 parts. Match each part to its job. Then trace the signal's path in order and answer the questions about how a neuron fires.
mistakes: Students often draw the signal entering at the axon or think it travels both ways. In a typical neuron, signals come in at the dendrites, are added up at the cell body, start as an action potential at the axon hillock and travel one way down the axon. Another common mix-up: the synapse is the gap between neurons, not a part of one neuron; the electrical signal does not jump the gap, chemical neurotransmitters cross it.
notes: Typical values: resting potential about -70 millivolts; threshold about -55 millivolts. The action potential is all-or-none: a stronger stimulus makes a neuron fire more often, not harder. A model drawing: branching dendrites on the left, a round cell body with a nucleus, a cone-shaped axon hillock, a long axon wrapped in segments of myelin with small gaps (nodes of Ranvier), and branching axon terminals on the right ending near a second neuron's dendrite.

# 5. Ruler Drop Reaction Time
minutes: 45
materials: 30-centimeter (12-inch) ruler, pencil, calculator, a partner
ican: I can measure reaction time, convert a falling distance into milliseconds and trace the neural pathway from seeing to moving.
directions: Work with a partner. One person holds a ruler at the 0 mark between the catcher's open thumb and finger. Without warning, the holder lets go; the catcher grabs it as fast as possible. Record where the top of the thumb lands, in centimeters. Do 5 trials with each hand, convert to milliseconds and answer the questions.
mistakes: Catchers who watch the holder's hand and guess the moment get impossibly fast times: the holder should vary the wait from 1 to 5 seconds, and any catch under 5 centimeters is worth redoing. Students also average milliseconds by eye from the chart; average the distances first, then convert. Watch for "it's a reflex": a reflex arc runs through the spinal cord without the brain, but this catch needs the eyes and the brain to see and decide.
notes: The ruler falls freely, so distance d = 1/2 g t squared, and t = square root of (2d / g), with d in meters and g = 9.8 meters per second squared. Values: 5 cm 101 ms, 10 cm 143 ms, 15 cm 175 ms, 20 cm 202 ms, 25 cm 226 ms, 30 cm 247 ms. A catch past 30 cm (a miss) means more than about 250 ms. Simple visual reaction times for young adults are often around 200 to 250 milliseconds, so many catches fall between 15 and 30 cm. Sample data key: Jordan's dominant average (14 + 16 + 12 + 15 + 18) / 5 = 15 cm, or 175 ms; other hand (18 + 20 + 17 + 22 + 18) / 5 = 19 cm, or 197 ms; difference 22 ms. Classroom-safe: stand clear of the falling ruler and keep it below face height.

# 27. Milgram and Research Ethics
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can describe Milgram's obedience study and judge it against the ethical principles psychologists follow today.
directions: Read the summary of Stanley Milgram's study and the ethics principles. Mark places where the study meets or breaks a principle. Then answer the questions and decide whether the study could be run today.
mistakes: Students often say "nobody was really shocked, so there was no harm." The harm was the stress participants felt, and the deception about it. Others swing the other way and say the study taught us nothing; its findings on obedience are among the most cited in psychology, which is why the ethics question is hard. Watch for confusing the participant (the "teacher") with the actor (the "learner").
notes: Facts: the studies ran at Yale University in 1961 and 1962; the first report, "Behavioral Study of Obedience," appeared in 1963 in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. In that first study 26 of 40 men (65 percent) went to the final 450-volt switch; Milgram described sweating, trembling, nervous laughter and, in three men, uncontrollable seizures. Diana Baumrind criticized the study's ethics in American Psychologist in 1964. In a follow-up questionnaire, Milgram reported that about 84 percent of participants said they were glad to have taken part. The National Research Act of 1974 led to required ethics review boards (IRBs) for federally funded research. Jerry Burger's partial replication (American Psychologist, 2009) stopped at 150 volts, screened out people at risk, told participants three times they could quit and keep their pay, and debriefed them at once; 70 percent continued past 150 volts. The summary and the principles are written for Curio Class: the principles are adapted from the APA Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct, not quoted.

## Read: The study
passage: The Obedience Study | numbered
  In 1961, Stanley Milgram, a young psychologist at Yale University, wanted to know how far ordinary people would go when an authority told them to hurt someone. He placed newspaper ads and mailed letters asking men from New Haven, Connecticut, to take part in a "study of memory and learning" for $4.50.
  Each volunteer met a friendly middle-aged man who seemed to be another volunteer. A rigged drawing always made the real volunteer the "teacher" and the other man the "learner." In fact, the learner was an actor working for Milgram. The learner was strapped into a chair in the next room with an electrode on his wrist. The teacher received a real 45-volt sample shock so the machine would seem genuine.
  The teacher sat at a machine with 30 switches running from 15 to 450 volts, labeled from "Slight Shock" up to "Danger: Severe Shock." For each wrong answer, he was told to give a shock one step higher. No real shocks were given after the sample, but the teacher did not know that. At 300 volts the learner pounded on the wall, and after that he went silent. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter, a stern man in a gray lab coat, replied with a series of firm prompts telling them to continue.
  Before the study, people Milgram asked predicted that almost no one would go to the end. Instead, 26 of the first 40 men, 65 percent, pressed the final 450-volt switch. Many were visibly upset: they sweated, trembled, argued, bit their lips and laughed nervously. Three had uncontrollable seizures. Afterward, each man was told the learner was unhurt and met him again.
  Milgram ran many versions and found that obedience dropped when the learner was in the same room, when the experimenter gave orders by phone, and when other "teachers" refused. Critics, starting with psychologist Diana Baumrind in 1964, argued that the deception and stress were not justified. Milgram replied that most participants said afterward they were glad to have taken part.

## Read: The principles
source: Adapted from the American Psychological Association's Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (2002, amended 2010 and 2016), in Curio Class's words.
  [1] Do no harm. Researchers protect the welfare of participants and avoid or keep to a minimum any physical or emotional harm.
  [2] Informed consent. People agree to take part only after learning what the study involves, including anything that might make them unwilling to take part, and they are told they may stop at any time without penalty.
  [3] Deception only when needed. Deception is allowed only when the study's value is high and there is no honest way to do it, and never about things expected to cause physical pain or severe emotional distress.
  [4] Debriefing. As soon as possible, participants learn the real purpose of the study and any misunderstandings are corrected.
  [5] Respect and dignity. Researchers respect people's right to make their own decisions and protect their privacy.

# 38. How the DSM Works
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter

mistakes: Students often think the DSM explains causes or prescribes treatments; it describes and names patterns of symptoms. notes: Facts: the American Psychiatric Association published the first DSM in 1952, DSM-5 in 2013 and a text revision, DSM-5-TR, in 2022, which added prolonged grief disorder. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 took effect in 2022. Major depressive disorder requires five or more of nine listed symptoms during the same two-week period, including depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure, causing significant distress or impairment. Homosexuality was removed as a diagnosis in 1973. The National Institute of Mental Health launched its Research Domain Criteria framework around 2009 as an alternative way to study mental illness. The passage is original to Curio Class. Keep discussion general: no student is asked about their own or anyone they know's mental health.

## Read
passage: A Shared Language for Mental Health | numbered
  When a doctor in Ohio and a psychologist in Oregon both say a patient has panic disorder, they need to mean the same thing. That is the main job of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, or DSM, published by the American Psychiatric Association. The first edition appeared in 1952. The current version, DSM-5-TR, came out in 2022. Clinicians use it to diagnose, researchers use it to choose who belongs in a study, and insurance companies use its codes to decide what they pay for. Much of the world also uses a similar system from the World Health Organization, the ICD.
  The DSM groups disorders into chapters such as anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, and neurodevelopmental disorders like ADHD and autism. Each disorder has a list of criteria. For major depressive disorder, for example, a person must have at least five of nine listed symptoms, such as low mood, loss of interest, changes in sleep, and trouble concentrating, nearly every day for at least two weeks. One of the five must be depressed mood or loss of interest. The symptoms must cause real distress or make it hard to function at school, work or home, and they must not be better explained by something else, such as a medical condition or a substance.
  This checklist style makes diagnosis more reliable: two trained clinicians are more likely to reach the same answer. But the DSM describes what disorders look like, not what causes them, and it does not say how to treat them.
  The manual has many critics. Some argue that drawing a line at "five of nine" treats mental health as either-or, when most symptoms come in degrees. Two people can share the same diagnosis while having very different symptoms, and many people meet the criteria for more than one disorder. Others point out that the manual reflects the values of its time: until 1973, homosexuality was listed as a disorder, and it was removed after protests and new research. Critics also worry that each edition widens the categories, turning ordinary sadness or restlessness into illness.
  Then there is the question of labels. A diagnosis can open the door to treatment, school support and understanding, and many people feel relief at finally having a name for what they are going through. Yet a label can also bring stigma, lower others' expectations, or lead people to see every action of a person through the diagnosis. That is why many clinicians recommend saying "a person with schizophrenia" rather than "a schizophrenic."
  Some researchers want a different system altogether. In 2009 the National Institute of Mental Health began building one based on brain circuits and behaviors that vary across everyone, rather than on separate categories. For now, though, the DSM remains the common language of mental health care in the United States.
