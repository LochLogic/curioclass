# 1. Neuron Label and Signal Path
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can identify the parts of a neuron and trace how an action potential travels from dendrite to synapse.
directions: Draw a neuron from the description and label its 8 parts. Match each part to its job. Then trace the signal's path in order and answer the questions about how a neuron fires.
mistakes: Students often draw the signal entering at the axon or think it travels both ways. In a typical neuron, signals come in at the dendrites, are added up at the cell body, start as an action potential at the axon hillock and travel one way down the axon. Another common mix-up: the synapse is the gap between neurons, not a part of one neuron; the electrical signal does not jump the gap, chemical neurotransmitters cross it.
notes: Typical values: resting potential about -70 millivolts; threshold about -55 millivolts. The action potential is all-or-none: a stronger stimulus makes a neuron fire more often, not harder. A model drawing: branching dendrites on the left, a round cell body with a nucleus, a cone-shaped axon hillock, a long axon wrapped in segments of myelin with small gaps (nodes of Ranvier), and branching axon terminals on the right ending near a second neuron's dendrite.

## Main: Parts and jobs
match: Match each part to its job.
  - Dendrites = Branches that receive signals from other neurons
  - Cell body (soma) = Keeps the cell alive and adds up incoming signals
  - Nucleus = Holds the cell's DNA
  - Axon hillock = Where the action potential begins if the signal reaches threshold
  - Axon = Long fiber that carries the action potential away from the cell body
  - Myelin sheath = Fatty insulation that speeds up the signal
  - Nodes of Ranvier = Gaps in the insulation where the signal jumps ahead
  - Axon terminals = Release neurotransmitters into the synapse

## Trace the signal
fill:
  - A signal arrives at the {dendrites},
  - is added up in the {cell body},
  - and, if it reaches threshold, fires an action potential at the {axon hillock}.
  - The action potential travels down the {axon},
  - jumping from one {node of Ranvier} to the next under the myelin.
  - At the {axon terminals}, it causes the release of {neurotransmitters},
  - which cross the {synapse}
  - and bind to receptors on the next neuron.

## Check your understanding
choice: A neuron at rest has a charge of about -70 millivolts inside. What happens when stimulation raises it past threshold, about -55 millivolts?
  - The neuron fires a weaker signal that fades out.
  * The neuron fires a full action potential: all or none.
  - The neuron stops working until the next day.
  - The signal travels backward toward the dendrites.
choice: A person touches a hot stove instead of a warm cup. How do neurons signal the stronger stimulus?
  - Each action potential is bigger.
  - The signal travels on a different axon that is longer.
  * Neurons fire more often (and more neurons fire).
  - The myelin sheath gets thicker.

## Stretch: When myelin is damaged
write: In multiple sclerosis, the immune system damages myelin. Using what you know about the myelin sheath and nodes of Ranvier, predict what happens to signals and why people might have trouble with movement or vision.
  lines: 4
  key: Without myelin the signal cannot jump quickly from node to node, so it slows down, weakens or fails along the damaged stretch. Messages to muscles and from the eyes arrive late or not at all, causing weakness, poor coordination, numbness or blurred vision.

# 22. Erikson Across the Lifespan
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, colored pencils
ican: I can describe Erikson's eight psychosocial stages and recognize each stage's central conflict in an example.
directions: Place each of Erikson's stages on the timelines at an age in the middle of that stage. Then match each fictional person to the stage they are in. Finally, write your own fictional example and evaluate the theory.
mistakes: Students often treat each stage as pass or fail. Erikson saw each conflict as a balance: healthy development leans toward the positive pole (trust more than mistrust), and earlier stages can be revisited later in life. Ages are approximate; accept letters placed anywhere within a stage's range. Watch for confusing initiative (planning and starting activities, preschool) with industry (building skills and competence, school age).
notes: Approximate ages: trust vs. mistrust, birth to about 18 months; autonomy vs. shame and doubt, about 18 months to 3 years; initiative vs. guilt, 3 to 5; industry vs. inferiority, about 6 to 12; identity vs. role confusion, 12 to 18; intimacy vs. isolation, about 18 to 40; generativity vs. stagnation, about 40 to 65; integrity vs. despair, 65 and older. All examples are fictional; students write about made-up people, never about themselves or their families. Critiques worth raising: stage ages are loose and vary by culture; Erikson drew mainly on observations in Western societies; life paths today (later marriage, career changes) blur the adult stages.

## Match: Who is in which stage?
match: Match each fictional person to their stage.
  - Baby Nia, 8 months, is fed and comforted when she cries and calms quickly when held. = Trust vs. mistrust
  - Omar, 2, insists on putting on his own shoes, and his dad lets him try. = Autonomy vs. shame and doubt
  - Lena, 4, invents a pretend restaurant game and plans the menu for her friends. = Initiative vs. guilt
  - Dev, 9, practices fractions until he can do them and feels proud of his skill. = Industry vs. inferiority
  - Marisol, 15, tries debate, art and a new style while asking who she really is. = Identity vs. role confusion
  - Sam, 28, works on building a close, committed relationship and deep friendships. = Intimacy vs. isolation
  - Grace, 50, mentors new nurses and coaches a youth soccer team. = Generativity vs. stagnation
  - Henry, 78, looks back on his life and feels it was meaningful, mistakes and all. = Integrity vs. despair

## Stretch: Create and critique
write: Invent a fictional person who is struggling toward the negative side of one stage. Name the stage and describe what you would see.
  lines: 3
  key: Example: Ray, 10, keeps comparing his grades to his classmates', stops trying in math and says "I'm just bad at everything." He is leaning toward inferiority in industry vs. inferiority.
write: Give one strength and one criticism of Erikson's theory.
  lines: 4
  key: Strength: it covers the whole lifespan, not just childhood, and links development to social relationships. Criticism: the stage ages are loose and based largely on observations in Western societies, and modern life paths (later marriage, career changes) do not always follow the order.
