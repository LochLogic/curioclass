# 1. Build an Atom
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, periodic table
ican: I can find the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom or ion from its symbol, mass number and charge.
directions: Use a periodic table to find each atomic number. Then find the protons, neutrons and electrons for each atom or ion.
mistakes: The most common error is changing the protons to make an ion; the charge changes only the electrons. Students also flip the sign: a positive ion has lost electrons (fewer electrons than protons), a negative ion has gained them. Watch for neutrons found by subtracting the atomic mass from the periodic table instead of the given mass number.
notes: Protons = atomic number Z. Neutrons = mass number A - Z. Electrons = Z - charge (so a 2+ ion has 2 fewer electrons, a 3- ion has 3 more). Carbon-14 and carbon-12 make a quick isotope discussion: same element, same chemistry, different neutrons.

## Warm up
example: Sodium-23 ion, Na+: Z = 11, so 11 protons. Neutrons = 23 - 11 = 12. Charge +1 means one electron lost: 11 - 1 = 10 electrons.
fill:
  - The number of {protons} decides which element an atom is.
  - Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called {isotopes}.

## Main: Twelve atoms and ions
table: # | Atom or ion | Mass number | Charge | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons
  - 1 | carbon-12, C | 12 | 0 | {6} | {6} | {6}
  - 2 | oxygen-16, O | 16 | 0 | {8} | {8} | {8}
  - 3 | sodium ion, Na+ | 23 | 1+ | {11} | {12} | {10}
  - 4 | chloride ion, Cl- | 35 | 1- | {17} | {18} | {18}
  - 5 | magnesium ion, Mg2+ | 24 | 2+ | {12} | {12} | {10}
  - 6 | iron(III) ion, Fe3+ | 56 | 3+ | {26} | {30} | {23}
  - 7 | sulfide ion, S2- | 32 | 2- | {16} | {16} | {18}
  - 8 | uranium-238, U | 238 | 0 | {92} | {146} | {92}
  - 9 | carbon-14, C | 14 | 0 | {6} | {8} | {6}
  - 10 | calcium ion, Ca2+ | 40 | 2+ | {20} | {20} | {18}

## Look for patterns
fill:
  - Four ions in the table have 10 electrons, the same as a neon atom: {Na+}, {Mg2+}, {N3-} and {Al3+}.
  - Ions 4, 7 and 10 all have {18} electrons, the same as an atom of {argon}.
write: Carbon-12 and carbon-14 have different masses. Why do they react the same way in chemical reactions?
  lines: 2
  key: They have the same number of protons and electrons (6 each), and chemistry depends on the electrons. Only the neutrons differ, which changes mass but not chemical behavior.

## Stretch
write: An ion has 29 protons, 34 neutrons and 27 electrons. Write its symbol, mass number and charge.
  lines: 2
  key: Copper, Cu2+, mass number 63 (copper-63): Z = 29 is copper, 29 + 34 = 63, and 29 - 27 = 2 electrons lost, so the charge is 2+.

# 6. Mystery Element Placement
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can use an element's properties, valence electrons and energy levels to find its place on the periodic table.
pairs: elements-inspector
directions: The blank table shows the first four periods of the main-group elements (groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18). Read the clues. Write each mystery element's number in its box. Then name each element and answer the trend questions. Do not use a periodic table until you check your work.
mistakes: Students often mix up periods (rows) and groups (columns): the number of occupied energy levels gives the period, and the number of valence electrons gives the main group. Watch for helium placed in group 2 because it has 2 electrons; it is a noble gas with a full first level, so it sits in group 18. In clue 4, students who know "atoms get bigger down a group" place element 4 between 2 and 3 quickly; others need to draw the order.
notes: Answers: 1 helium (period 1, group 18); 2 lithium (2, 1); 3 potassium (4, 1); 4 sodium (3, 1); 5 magnesium (3, 2); 6 silicon (3, 14); 7 oxygen (2, 16); 8 fluorine (2, 17); 9 chlorine (3, 17); 10 bromine (4, 17). Mercury is the only other element that is liquid at room temperature. The stretch is Mendeleev's famous prediction: in 1871 he left a gap below aluminum and predicted "eka-aluminum"; gallium was discovered in 1875 and matched his predictions closely.

## The clues
fill:
  - Element 1 is an unreactive gas. Its atoms have only one occupied energy level, and it is full.
  - Element 2 is a soft metal that fizzes in water. Its atoms have 1 valence electron and 2 occupied energy levels.
  - Element 3 is in the same group as element 2. Its atoms have 4 occupied energy levels, and it reacts with water even faster.
  - Element 4 is also in element 2's group. Its atoms are larger than element 2's but smaller than element 3's.
  - Element 5 is a metal in the same period as element 4. It forms ions with a 2+ charge.
  - Element 6 is a metalloid in the same period as element 4. It has 4 valence electrons and is used to make computer chips.
  - Element 7 is a gas that makes up about 21 percent of air. It forms 2- ions, and its atoms have 2 occupied energy levels.
  - Element 8 is the most electronegative element of all. It is in the same period as element 7.
  - Element 9 is a yellow-green, poisonous gas in the same group as element 8, with 3 occupied energy levels.
  - Element 10 is in the same group as element 8. It is a red-brown liquid at room temperature.

## Stretch
write: In 1871, Dmitri Mendeleev left an empty box in the period 4 row, in the same group as aluminum (group 13). Predict three properties of the missing element, including the formula of its oxide.
  lines: 3
  key: It should be a metal with 3 valence electrons that forms 3+ ions, with atoms larger than aluminum's, and an oxide with the formula X2O3 (like Al2O3). The element is gallium, found in 1875, a soft metal that melts in a warm hand (about 30 °C), with the oxide Ga2O3.

directions: Each square in the maze is a conversion. Check each one on scratch paper. Start at the top left and move right or down onto true squares only. Shade your path. At the finish, do Step 12.
mistakes: Students often multiply by the molar mass when they should divide (grams to moles is divide). Watch for molar masses of diatomic gases: O2 is 32.0 g/mol, not 16.0, and N2 is 28.0, not 14.0. # 24. Bond Energy Bookkeeping
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can use bond energies to calculate the enthalpy change of a reaction and tell whether it is exothermic or endothermic.
directions: For each reaction, add up the energy needed to break all the bonds in the reactants, and the energy released forming all the bonds in the products. Then find ΔH = (bonds broken) - (bonds formed). Use the bond energy table. All substances are gases.
mistakes: The most common error is reversing the subtraction (formed minus broken), which flips the sign. Students also forget coefficients and subscripts: 2H2O has 4 O-H bonds. Watch for a single bond used in place of a double one (C=O in CO2 is not C-O).
notes: Bond energies are averages, so answers differ a little from measured values: for example, the measured ΔH for burning methane to gaseous water is about -802 kJ, for ammonia synthesis about -92 kJ, and for 2HBr to H2 and Br2(g) about +103 kJ. The ethane reaction (making ethene and hydrogen) is a real industrial step called cracking; its measured value is about +137 kJ. Bond energy values here are common textbook averages.

## Bond energy table (kJ/mol)
table: Bond | Energy | Bond | Energy | Bond | Energy
  - H-H | 436 | C-H | 413 | N≡N | 945
  - O=O | 498 | C-C | 348 | N-H | 391
  - O-H | 463 | C=C | 614 | H-Cl | 431
  - Cl-Cl | 242 | C=O (in CO2) | 799 | H-Br | 366
  - Br-Br | 193 | | | | 

## Warm up
example: H2 + Cl2 makes 2HCl. Broken: H-H (436) + Cl-Cl (242) = 678 kJ. Formed: 2 H-Cl = 2 x 431 = 862 kJ.
  ΔH = 678 - 862 = -184 kJ. Negative, so the reaction is exothermic.
fill:
  - Breaking bonds {absorbs} energy. Forming bonds {releases} energy.

## Main: Five more reactions
fill:
  - 2H2 + O2 makes 2H2O. Broken: {1370} kJ. Formed: {1852} kJ. ΔH = {-482} kJ. Type: {exothermic}
  - CH4 + 2O2 makes CO2 + 2H2O. Broken: {2648} kJ. Formed: {3450} kJ. ΔH = {-802} kJ. Type: {exothermic}
  - N2 + 3H2 makes 2NH3. Broken: {2253} kJ. Formed: {2346} kJ. ΔH = {-93} kJ. Type: {exothermic}
  - 2HBr makes H2 + Br2. Broken: {732} kJ. Formed: {629} kJ. ΔH = {+103} kJ. Type: {endothermic}
  - C2H6 makes C2H4 + H2 (ethane: H3C-CH3; ethene: H2C=CH2). Broken: {2826} kJ. Formed: {2702} kJ. ΔH = {+124} kJ. Type: {endothermic}

## Explain
write: In the methane reaction, which bonds release the most energy when they form? Why does this make burning methane a good fuel?
  lines: 3
  key: The two C=O bonds (2 x 799 = 1598 kJ) and four O-H bonds (4 x 463 = 1852 kJ). The products' bonds are much stronger than the reactants', so far more energy is released forming them than is needed to break the reactant bonds: a large negative ΔH of about -802 kJ per mole of methane.
write: Your value for N2 + 3H2 is -93 kJ, but the measured value is -92 kJ. Why do bond energy calculations give only estimates?
  lines: 2
  key: Table values are averages over many molecules; the real strength of a bond depends on the molecule it is in.

## Stretch
write: In the ethane reaction, the six C-H bonds in ethane do not all break. How could you do the calculation counting only the bonds that change? Do you get the same ΔH?
  lines: 3
  key: Only one C-C and two C-H bonds break (348 + 2 x 413 = 1174 kJ); one C=C and one H-H form (614 + 436 = 1050 kJ). ΔH = 1174 - 1050 = +124 kJ, the same, because the 4 unchanged C-H bonds cancel out.

# 29. Rates and Collisions
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, ruler, calculator
ican: I can graph reaction rate data and explain the effects of concentration, temperature and surface area with collision theory.
directions: A class timed two reactions. Experiment 1: a 2 cm strip of magnesium ribbon in hydrochloric acid of different concentrations, timed until the metal was gone. Experiment 2: a fizzing antacid tablet in 200 mL of water at different temperatures, timed until the fizzing stopped. Find each rate, graph the data, and explain each result.
mistakes: Students often graph the time instead of the rate and then say "higher concentration made it slower" because the line goes down. A shorter time means a faster rate: rate = 1000 / time here. Watch for "more collisions" as the whole answer for temperature: faster particles collide more often, but the bigger effect is that a larger share of collisions have enough energy (the activation energy) to react.
notes: Practice data, typical of classroom results; the magnesium reaction is done with 0.5 to 3 M acid in a fume hood or a well-ventilated room, with goggles. The rates here are 1000 / time, in units of "per 1000 seconds," so the numbers are easy to plot. The concentration graph is close to a straight line through the origin (rate roughly doubles when concentration doubles). The temperature rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C, so the graph curves upward. Accept reasonable rounding.

## Warm up
fill:
  - For particles to react, they must {collide} with enough energy (the {activation energy}) and the right orientation.
  - A reaction that takes 50 s has a rate of 1000 / 50 = {20}. One that takes 100 s has a rate of {10}, so it is {slower}.

## Experiment 1: Concentration
table: HCl concentration (M) | 0.5 | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 3.0
  - Time (s) | 240 | 115 | 78 | 60 | 41
  - Rate (1000 / time) | {4.2} | {8.7} | {12.8} | {16.7} | {24.4}
graph: HCl concentration (M) | 0 3 0.5 | Rate (1000 / time) | 0 25 5
  key: 0.5,4.2 1,8.7 1.5,12.8 2,16.7 3,24.4
write: Describe the pattern and explain it with collision theory.
  lines: 3
  key: The rate rises steadily, about doubling when the concentration doubles. More concentrated acid has more acid particles in the same volume, so more of them hit the magnesium surface each second, and more successful collisions per second means a faster reaction.

## Experiment 2: Temperature
table: Water temperature (°C) | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50
  - Time (s) | 80 | 42 | 23 | 13 | 8
  - Rate (1000 / time) | {12.5} | {23.8} | {43.5} | {76.9} | {125.0}
graph: Water temperature (°C) | 0 50 10 | Rate (1000 / time) | 0 140 20
  key: 10,12.5 20,23.8 30,43.5 40,76.9 50,125
choice: About how does the rate change for every 10 °C rise?
  - It stays the same.
  - It goes up by about 10.
  * It roughly doubles.
  - It goes down by half.
write: Explain the temperature result with collision theory.
  lines: 3
  key: At higher temperature particles move faster, so they collide more often, and a much larger fraction of the collisions have at least the activation energy. More successful collisions per second makes the rate rise faster and faster.

## Stretch
write: At 20 °C a whole tablet took 42 s, but a crushed tablet took 15 s. Find the crushed tablet's rate and explain the difference.
  lines: 2
  key: Rate = 1000 / 15 = about 66.7, almost 3 times faster. Crushing exposes much more surface area, so more particles of the tablet are in contact with the water at once and more collisions happen each second.

# 40. Vinegar Titration
minutes: 50
materials: goggles, white vinegar, baking soda solution (0.500 M, made by the teacher), red cabbage indicator, two droppers, 10 mL graduated cylinder, small clear cups, white paper
ican: I can titrate an acid with a base using an indicator and calculate the acid's concentration.
directions: Measure 2.0 mL of vinegar into a cup and add 5 drops of cabbage indicator. Over white paper, add baking soda solution one drop at a time, swirling after each drop, and count the drops until the pink color turns to a purple-blue that lasts 30 seconds. Do 3 trials. Then calculate.
mistakes: Students often add drops too fast near the end and overshoot to green; slow down once the pink starts to fade. Watch for students who forget to convert drops to mL, or mL to liters before multiplying by molarity. The fizzing is carbon dioxide; some students think it means the reaction is "done." Swirl until the bubbles stop and the color settles before deciding.
notes: Make the 0.500 M baking soda solution: dissolve 4.20 g of sodium bicarbonate (molar mass 84.0 g/mol) in water and add water to make 100 mL. Calibrate the droppers first: most give about 20 drops per mL; use each group's own count. Red cabbage indicator is red to pink in acid, purple near neutral, and blue to green in base. The reaction is NaHCO3 + CH3COOH makes NaCH3COO + H2O + CO2, a 1 to 1 ratio. Sample data: 67 drops at 20 drops per mL is 3.35 mL, giving 0.837 M acetic acid, about 50 g per liter, or about 5.0 percent, matching the label of most white vinegar. Safety: everything here is a household material, but wear goggles; do not taste.

## Warm up
fill:
  - Acetic acid and baking soda react in a {1} to {1} ratio, so at the end point, moles of acid = moles of {base}.
  - Cabbage indicator is {pink (or red)} in acid and turns {purple-blue} near neutral.
fill:
  - My dropper gives {20 (or your count)} drops per mL. (Count the drops in 1.0 mL of water.)

## Main: My data

  - 1 | 2.0 | |
  - 2 | 2.0 | |
  - 3 | 2.0 | |
  - Average | 2.0 | |

## Sample data: Work the calculation
table: Trial | 1 | 2 | 3 | Average
  - Drops | 68 | 66 | 67 | {67}
fill:
  - Volume of base: 67 drops ÷ 20 drops per mL = {3.35} mL = {0.00335} L
  - Moles of base: 0.00335 L x 0.500 mol/L = {0.001675} mol, so moles of acid = {0.001675} mol
  - Acid concentration: 0.001675 mol ÷ 0.0020 L = {0.84} M
  - Mass of acid per liter: 0.84 mol x 60.0 g/mol = about {50} g, which is about {5.0} percent (grams per 100 mL)
text: Now do the same calculation with your own average.
lines: 3

## Explain
choice: A group overshot and their cup turned green. How does this affect their answer?
  - Their concentration comes out too low.
  * Their concentration comes out too high.
  - It has no effect.
  - The vinegar becomes stronger.
write: Why is it important to swirl and wait for the fizzing to stop near the end point?
  lines: 2
  key: The carbon dioxide made in the reaction dissolves to form carbonic acid, which keeps the color pink for a moment. Swirling lets the gas escape and the color settle, so you do not add extra drops and overshoot.

## Stretch
write: The label says the vinegar is 5 percent acidity. Is your class result accurate? Give one source of error in this method.
  lines: 2
  key: The sample result, about 5.0 percent, matches the label. Sources of error: drops are not all the same size, judging the color change is hard (especially with bubbles), and 2.0 mL is hard to measure exactly with a small cylinder.
