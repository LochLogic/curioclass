# 1. Six Nutrients, Six Jobs
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain the main job, food sources and signs of too little for the six nutrient groups.
directions: Study the water example. Fill in the chart for the other five nutrient groups. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often say fat has no job or that all fat should be avoided. Fat stores energy, builds cell walls and carries vitamins A, D, E and K. Watch for "vitamins give you energy": vitamins help the body use energy from carbohydrates, fats and proteins but do not supply energy themselves.
notes: Facts follow the Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. Signs of too little are examples, not a way to self-diagnose; a doctor decides whether someone has a deficiency. Keep the talk on how nutrients work, not on any student's own eating or body.

## Warm up: The example row
example: **Water.** Main job: carries nutrients, controls body temperature through sweat, cushions joints. Sources: water, milk, fruits, vegetables, soups. Sign of too little: thirst, headache, dark urine, dizziness.

## Main: The nutrient chart
table: Nutrient | Main job | Two food sources | One sign of too little
  - Carbohydrates | {The body's main source of energy, especially for the brain and muscles} | {Whole grains, fruit, beans, potatoes} | {Tiredness and trouble concentrating}
  - Proteins | {Build and repair muscles, skin and other tissues; make enzymes and antibodies} | {Beans, eggs, fish, poultry, tofu, nuts} | {Slow healing, muscle loss, getting sick more often}
  - Fats | {Store energy, build cell walls, help absorb vitamins A, D, E and K} | {Nuts, seeds, olive oil, avocado, fish} | {Dry skin; low levels of vitamins A, D, E and K}
  - Vitamins | {Help the body use energy, fight infection and keep eyes, skin and bones healthy} | {Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, milk} | {Low vitamin C: bleeding gums and slow healing; low vitamin D: weak bones}
  - Minerals | {Build bones and teeth (calcium) and carry oxygen in the blood (iron)} | {Milk, leafy greens, beans, meat} | {Low iron: tiredness and pale skin (anemia); low calcium: weak bones}

## Apply it
choice: Which two nutrients supply energy?
  - Vitamins and minerals
  * Carbohydrates and fats
  - Water and vitamins
choice: Nora runs cross-country. After a hot practice she has a headache and dark urine. Which nutrient is she most likely low on?
  - Protein
  * Water
  - Vitamin D
choice: Why do vitamins A, D, E and K need some fat in a meal?
  * They are fat-soluble, so the body absorbs them with fat
  - Fat turns them into protein
  - They are found only in fat
write: Dev skips most fruits and vegetables. Name two nutrients he may be low on and one food swap that would help.
  lines: 3
  key: Vitamins (such as vitamin C and A) and minerals (such as potassium), plus fiber, a carbohydrate. Swap: fruit instead of chips at lunch, or add vegetables to pasta or a sandwich.

## Stretch
write: Plan one balanced lunch for a character named Jo that includes at least five of the six nutrient groups. Label which food gives which nutrient.
  lines: 4
  key: Example: whole grain turkey and avocado sandwich (carbohydrates, protein, fats), a side of carrots and an orange (vitamins, minerals, water), a glass of milk (protein, calcium, water).

# 7. Week of Fuel for Marcus
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can plan a week of balanced meals and snacks that fuel an athlete's training.
directions: Read Marcus's schedule and the plate model. Fill in his week of meals and snacks. In the last column, write the letters of the food groups that day includes. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often plan a huge meal right before practice. A big, high-fat meal less than 2 hours before running can cause cramps and a heavy feeling; a light snack with carbohydrates works better. Watch for weeks with no fruit or no dairy (or calcium-rich food), and for rest days with no vegetables "because he isn't training." Rest days still need balanced meals for recovery.
notes: Marcus is a character made up for Curio Class. The plate model follows MyPlate (USDA): half fruits and vegetables, about a quarter grains (at least half whole grains), about a quarter protein, plus dairy or fortified soy. Timing follows common sports nutrition advice for teens: a meal 3 to 4 hours before training, a light carbohydrate snack 1 to 2 hours before, water before, during and after, and a snack or meal with carbohydrates and protein within about an hour or two after hard training. The sheet uses no calorie numbers by design. Students plan for Marcus, not for themselves.

## Warm up: Marcus and the plate
text: Marcus is 15 and runs the 400 meters. **Track practice:** Monday, Tuesday and Thursday, 3:30 to 5:30 p.m. **Easy jog:** Wednesday. **Meet:** Saturday at 9:00 a.m. **Rest:** Friday and Sunday. He eats breakfast at 7:00, lunch at 11:30 and dinner at 6:30.
fill:
  - On the plate model, {half} of the plate is fruits and vegetables.
  - About a quarter of the plate is {grains}, and at least half of those should be whole grains.
  - About a quarter of the plate is {protein}.
  - A light snack with carbohydrates works best about {1 to 2} hours before practice.
text: **Food group letters:** F = fruit, V = vegetable, G = grain, P = protein, D = dairy or calcium-rich food.
example: **Model day, Monday:** Breakfast: oatmeal with banana and milk. Lunch: turkey and cheese sandwich on whole wheat, carrots, apple. Snack at 2:30: yogurt and granola. After practice: water and a peanut butter sandwich if dinner is late. Dinner: chicken, brown rice, broccoli, milk. **Groups: F V G P D**

## Check the plan
choice: What is the best snack for Marcus at 2:00 on a practice day?
  - A large cheeseburger and fries

  - No breakfast at all
  * A light, familiar breakfast like toast, peanut butter and a banana at about 7:00
write: Why does Marcus need balanced meals on Friday and Sunday, even though he does not practice?
  lines: 2
  key: Rest days are when muscles recover and rebuild. He still needs protein to repair muscle, carbohydrates to refill energy stores for the next workout, and fruits, vegetables and dairy for vitamins and minerals.

## Stretch
write: Marcus says, "I'll skip lunch so I feel lighter at practice." Explain why that could hurt his performance and suggest a better plan.
  lines: 3
  key: Skipping lunch leaves him low on energy by 3:30, so he may feel tired, dizzy or slow. Better: eat a normal balanced lunch at 11:30 and a light carbohydrate snack around 2:00, with water.

# 20. Lateral Reading in Action
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can read laterally to find out who is behind a health claim and whether to trust it.
directions: Read the health claim. Then read the printed search results, as if you opened new tabs to check the claim. For each source, record who is behind it and what it says. Then decide what you believe.
mistakes: Students often judge a source by how professional it looks or by its own "About" page. Lateral reading means leaving the site to see what others say about it and its claim. Watch for students who trust result 2 because it is a "review": its own disclosure says it earns money from sales. Result 4 is marked Sponsored, which means it is paid advertising.
notes: Product T, its site, and results 2 and 4 are made up for Curio Class. Results 3 and 5 describe real government guidance in our own words, not as quotations: the NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) reports little good evidence that "detox" or "cleanse" programs remove toxins or lead to lasting weight loss, and notes that some products contain laxatives and can cause harm; the Federal Trade Commission warns that claims of fast weight loss without diet or exercise are false. Check the agencies' current pages before teaching. Lateral reading is the method professional fact-checkers use (Stanford History Education Group research, 2017).

## Warm up: The claim
source: Pretend social media post (made up for this sheet)
  "Melt 10 pounds of belly fat in 7 days with Product T Detox Tea! Flushes out toxins and resets your metabolism. Doctor approved! Link in bio."
choice: What is the first thing a lateral reader does with a post like this?
  - Read every page of the seller's website
  * Open new tabs to see what other sources say about the product and the claim
  - Count how many likes the post has

## Read: Printed search results
text: **Search:** Product T Detox Tea belly fat toxins
text: **Result 1.** producttdetox.example. "Official site. Doctor approved! Order now and get 30% off. Thousands of happy customers." (About page: "We are a small team passionate about wellness." No doctor is named.)
text: **Result 2.** healthtruthdaily.example. "My honest review: Product T changed my life!" (Small print at the bottom: "This site earns a commission when you buy through our links.")
text: **Result 3.** nccih.nih.gov, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, part of the U.S. National Institutes of Health. Page on detoxes and cleanses: there is little good research showing that detox products remove toxins or lead to lasting weight loss, and some contain laxatives that can cause harm.
text: **Result 4.** Sponsored. everydaywellnessmag.example. "Why celebrities swear by detox teas."
text: **Result 5.** consumer.ftc.gov, Federal Trade Commission, the U.S. agency that protects consumers. Page on weight loss ads: products that promise fast weight loss without changing diet or exercise do not work as claimed.

## Main: Record what you find
table: Result | Who is behind it? | What it says about the claim | Trust it? (yes, some, no)
  height: 0.62
  - 1 | {The company selling Product T} | {It works and is doctor approved, but names no doctor} | {no}
  - 2 | {A site paid a commission on sales} | {It changed the writer's life} | {no}
  - 3 | {NCCIH, a federal health research agency (NIH)} | {Little evidence detoxes work; some can harm} | {yes}
  - 4 | {An advertiser (paid, sponsored post)} | {Celebrities like detox teas} | {no}
  - 5 | {The FTC, a federal consumer protection agency} | {Fast weight loss without diet or exercise does not work as claimed} | {yes}

## Decide
choice: What does "Sponsored" on result 4 tell you?
  - A doctor checked the article
  - The government approved it
  * Someone paid for it to appear, so it is advertising
write: Is the claim in the post true? Write a 2 to 3 sentence conclusion that names at least two sources.
  lines: 4
  key: No. The only sources that support it are the seller (result 1) and sites that make money from it (results 2 and 4). The independent federal sources, NCCIH and the FTC (results 3 and 5), say detox products are not proven to remove toxins and that fast weight loss without diet or exercise does not work.

## Stretch
write: "Doctor approved!" appears in the post and on the seller's site. What would you search to check that claim?
  lines: 3
  key: Search for the doctor's name (none is given, which is a red flag), then search the name plus "Product T" to see if the doctor really endorses it and whether they are paid. Check whether the doctor is licensed using a state medical board lookup.

# 21. Health Ad Appeals
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can identify the appeals in health ads and judge what they leave out.
directions: Read the five pretend ads. Name the main appeal each one uses: fear, bandwagon, expert or testimonial. Then explain what important information each ad leaves out.
mistakes: Students mix up expert and testimonial. An expert appeal uses someone with real or claimed training (a doctor, a scientist); a testimonial uses a regular person's own story. A white coat alone does not make someone an expert: ask who they are and whether they are paid.
notes: All five ads and products are made up for Curio Class. An appeal is not proof the product is bad; it is a reason to look for evidence. Good evidence questions: Who paid for this? Is there a study, and who ran it? What does a trusted source (CDC, NIH, a doctor who is not selling it) say?

## Warm up

  - Fear = Makes you worried something bad will happen if you don't buy it
  - Bandwagon = Says everyone is using it, so you should too
  - Expert = Uses a doctor, scientist or other authority to back it
  - Testimonial = Uses a regular person's own story about how it worked

# 33. Alcohol and the Body
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can describe alcohol's short-term and long-term effects on the brain, heart, liver and stomach.
directions: Read each effect. Write which organ it affects and whether it is short-term (happens during or soon after drinking) or long-term (builds up over months or years of heavy drinking). Then label your diagram and answer the questions.
mistakes: Students often think the liver can be "trained" to handle more alcohol. The liver clears alcohol at a fairly steady rate, about one standard drink an hour; tolerance means a person feels less drunk, not that less damage is done. Watch for confusion about fatty liver and cirrhosis: both are liver effects; cirrhosis is the long-term scarring.
notes: Facts follow the NIAAA and the CDC. A standard drink in the U.S. has about 14 grams of pure alcohol (12 ounces of regular beer, 5 ounces of wine, 1.5 ounces of distilled spirits). Long-term heavy drinking is also linked to cancers of the mouth, throat, esophagus, liver, breast and colon (National Cancer Institute). If a student shares a family situation, follow up privately and offer the counselor. The legal drinking age in the U.S. is 21.

## Warm up
fill:
  - The liver can break down only about {one} standard drink an hour.
  - Alcohol is a {depressant}: it slows down the brain and nervous system.

## Main: Organ and time
table: Effect | Organ (brain, heart, liver or stomach) | Short-term or long-term?
  - 1. Slower reaction time and poor coordination | {brain} | {short-term}
  - 2. Scarring that can lead to liver failure (cirrhosis) | {liver} | {long-term}
  - 3. Nausea and vomiting | {stomach} | {short-term}
  - 4. High blood pressure and a weakened heart muscle | {heart} | {long-term}
  - 5. Gaps in memory called blackouts | {brain} | {short-term}
  - 6. Ongoing irritation and swelling of its lining (gastritis) | {stomach} | {long-term}
  - 7. A fast or uneven heartbeat after heavy drinking | {heart} | {short-term}
  - 8. Damage to memory and learning, worse in a brain still developing | {brain} | {long-term}
  - 9. Busy clearing alcohol slowly, so the rest stays in the blood | {liver} | {short-term}

## Think about it
choice: Why does drinking a lot in a short time raise the risk of alcohol poisoning?
  - The stomach stops absorbing alcohol
  * The liver can only clear alcohol slowly, so the blood alcohol level keeps rising
  - Alcohol leaves the body faster when you drink quickly
choice: Which of these is a long-term effect?
  * Cirrhosis of the liver
  - Slower reaction time
  - Nausea
write: Why are teens' brains at greater risk from alcohol than adults' brains?
  lines: 2
  key: The brain keeps developing until about age 25, especially the parts for judgment, memory and learning. Alcohol can disrupt that development and has a stronger effect on memory and learning in teens.

## Stretch
write: Someone says, "I can hold my liquor, so it's not hurting me." Use the chart to explain why that is wrong.
  lines: 3
  key: Tolerance means the person feels less drunk, not that the liver clears alcohol faster or that the organs are protected. Long-term effects like cirrhosis (2), heart damage (4), gastritis (6) and memory damage (8) still build up, and needing more to feel the same effect is a warning sign of alcohol use disorder.
