# 1. Fact or Opinion? Ice Cream Edition
minutes: 20
materials: pencil, crayons
ican: I can tell a fact from an opinion.
directions: Read each sentence about Mr. Diaz's ice cream shop. Write F if it is a fact you could check. Write O if it is an opinion, what someone thinks or feels. Then fix one opinion so it becomes a fact.
mistakes: Children often call a sentence a fact because they agree with it ("Mint chip is the best flavor" feels true to a mint fan). Ask: "Could we check it with our eyes, a ruler, a clock or a price tag? Would everyone get the same answer?" Words like best, tastier, yummiest, should and fun are opinion clues.
notes: Read the sentences aloud for children still building fluency. A fact can be checked; it does not have to be something we already know. For the fix-it, any checkable sentence about the flavor is right, such as "Mint chip is green" or "Mint chip sold 20 cones on Friday."

## Warm up: Check it or feel it?
example: "The shop has a red sign." We can look and check. That is a **fact (F)**.
  "Red signs are the prettiest." That is what someone thinks. That is an **opinion (O)**.

## Main: Stamp F or O
fill:
  - The shop opens at noon. {F}
  - Mint chip is the best flavor. {O}
  - A small cone costs two dollars. {F}
  - The shop sells 12 flavors. {F}
  - Waffle cones taste better than cups. {O}
  - The shop has a blue door. {F}
  - Everyone should try the mango flavor. {O}
  - Ice cream is the yummiest snack in the world. {O}
  - The shop is next to the library. {F}
  - Sprinkles make every cone more fun. {O}

## Stretch: Fix an opinion
write: "Mint chip is the best flavor" is an opinion. Change it into a fact about mint chip.
  lines: 2
  starter: Mint chip ...
  key: Any sentence that can be checked, such as "Mint chip is green with brown chips" or "Mint chip costs two dollars."
write: Circle the word in sentence 8 that tells you it is an opinion. Write it here.
  lines: 1
  key: yummiest

# 5. Who Leads Our Town?
minutes: 20
materials: pencil
ican: I can tell what the mayor, the town council and the police chief each do.
directions: Read the story about the leaders of Pine Hill. Then answer the questions. Look back at the story to check each answer.
mistakes: Students often think the mayor makes all the laws alone. In the story the council votes on the rule and the mayor signs it; point to both sentences. Some confuse the police chief with the mayor because both are "the boss"; ask "Who is each one the boss of?"
notes: Towns are set up in different ways. In some, the mayor leads the council meetings; in others, a town manager runs the daily work. Pine Hill is a made-up town that follows a common pattern: voters elect a mayor and a council, the council passes local laws (often called ordinances) and a budget, and the police chief is hired to lead the police department. Invite students to find out who leads your own town.

## Read: Pine Hill Gets a Crosswalk
passage: Pine Hill Gets a Crosswalk
  Mayor Rosa Diaz leads the town of Pine Hill. She works with the town council. The council is five people that the voters chose. They meet each month to make town laws and to decide how to spend the town's money.
  One day, children asked the council for a crosswalk near the school. The council talked about it and voted yes. Then Mayor Diaz signed the new law.
  Chief Sam Lee leads the town's police officers. His officers helped children cross the busy street until the crosswalk was painted. The police make sure people follow the town's laws.

## Main: Who does each job?
choice: Who leads the whole town of Pine Hill?
  * Mayor Rosa Diaz
  - Chief Sam Lee
  - the children
choice: Who votes to make the town's laws?
  - the police officers
  * the town council
  - the children
choice: How did the council members get their jobs?
  - The mayor picked them.
  - They won a race.
  * The voters chose them.
choice: Who leads the police officers?
  - the town council
  * Chief Sam Lee
  - Mayor Rosa Diaz
choice: What did Mayor Diaz do after the council voted yes?
  * She signed the new law.
  - She painted the crosswalk.
  - She helped children cross the street.

## Stretch: Ask the council
write: The children asked the council for a crosswalk. What is one more thing you could ask a town council to do? Why?
  lines: 3
  key: For example, "I would ask for a new slide at the park, because the old one is broken." Any town job with a reason works: fix a road, add a bike lane, plant trees, open a library on Saturdays.

# 16. Natural, Human-Made or Both?
minutes: 25
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can tell natural resources from things people make out of them.
directions: A natural resource is something from nature that people use, like trees or water. People make new things from natural resources. Cut out the 10 cards and glue each one in the right column. Then match each made thing to the natural resource it comes from.
mistakes: Students often call bottled water natural because "it is still water." Ask: did people do work to make it (clean it, put it in a plastic bottle)? Then it is made by people from a natural resource. Coal looks like a rock someone made; it is dug out of the ground just as nature formed it.
notes: Bricks are made by shaping clay and baking it hot; glass is made by melting sand. Coal is not made into something on this sheet; it is burned in some power plants to make electricity, which the stretch asks about. Paper also comes from trees; the match leaves it out so each natural resource is used once. Students who finish early can add a card of their own (wool and a sweater, cotton and a T-shirt).

## Warm up: What is a natural resource?
choice: Which one is a natural resource?
  - a plastic cup
  * a river
  - a wooden chair
choice: Which one did people make?
  - sand
  - clay
  * a brick

## Main: Sort 10 cards
sort: Glue each card in its column.
  - Natural resource: trees, water, clay, sand, coal
  - Made by people from it: lumber, bottled water, bricks, glass, paper
match: Match each made thing to the natural resource it comes from.
  - lumber = trees
  - bottled water = water
  - bricks = clay
  - glass = sand

## Stretch: Both?
write: Coal is burned in some power plants. What does that make for people to use?
  lines: 1
  key: Electricity (and heat).
write: Is a vegetable garden natural, made by people, or both? Explain.
  lines: 3
  key: Both. The soil, water, sun and seeds come from nature, but people plan the garden, dig it, plant the seeds and take care of it.

# 23. Before, During, After
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, scissors, glue stick
ican: I can put events from a storm story in order: before, during and after.
directions: Read the story. Look at the 8 pictures. Cut out the word cards and glue each one in the right box: before the storm, during the storm or after the storm. Then add one way to help.
mistakes: Children sometimes put "the lights go out" after the storm because the power stays off afterward; ask when it first happened. Cleanup jobs (sweeping, fixing pipes) are after; getting ready jobs (boards, water, go bag) are before.
notes: The story is original to Curio Class and the town is made up. Some children may have lived through a real storm: keep the focus on the story family and on helpers, and let any child who wants to share do so by choice only. A go bag holds things a family needs if they must leave fast, like water, snacks and a flashlight.

## Read
passage: The Big Wind
  Mia lives in a town by the sea. One day the news said a hurricane was coming. Dad nailed boards on the windows. Mia helped fill water jugs. Grandma packed a go bag.
  That night the storm hit. Heavy rain pounded the roof. The wind bent the trees. Then the lights went out, so Mia held a flashlight.
  In the morning the sun came out. Mud was on the floor, so Mia and Dad swept it out. A neighbor came to help fix the broken pipes. "We can do this together," said Grandma.

## Main: Look at the pictures
picword:
  - hammer | nail boards on windows
  - jug | fill water jugs
  - backpack | pack a go bag
  - rain | heavy rain falls
  - tree | wind bends the trees
  - flashlight | the lights go out
  - broom | sweep out the mud
  - wrench | fix the broken pipes

## Sort: Before, during, after
sort: Glue each card in the right box.
  - Before: nail boards on windows, fill water jugs, pack a go bag
  - During: heavy rain falls, wind bends the trees, the lights go out
  - After: sweep out the mud, fix the broken pipes

## Stretch: One more helping action
write: After the storm, what is one more thing a helper could do for Mia's town?
  lines: 2
  key: Any helping action: bring food or water, clear branches from the road, give clothes, fix the power lines, check on older neighbors, collect toys for kids.

# 27. Not Enough Pencils: Scarcity
minutes: 25
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain scarcity and find fair ways to share when there is not enough.
directions: Scarcity means there is not enough of something for everyone who wants it. Mr. Park's class has 10 pencils and 20 students. Find 3 fair ways to share the pencils, then choose the best one and explain why.
mistakes: Students often jump to "buy more pencils," which skips the problem; say there is no money for more until next month. Others call any rule fair if it helps them. Use the test: does every student get a chance to write? Watch for "first come, first served," which leaves out slower or later students.
notes: The warm up uses division as fair sharing: 20 students and 10 pencils is 2 students per pencil. More than one "best" answer is fine if the reason is about fairness (everyone gets a turn) and about getting the work done. Good fair ways: share one pencil between two partners, take turns (half the class writes while the other half reads, then switch), lend pencils at a sign-out table, or use crayons and markers for some tasks.

## Warm up: Not enough
fill:
  - The class has 10 pencils and 20 students. How many students must share each pencil? {2}
  - If every student had a pencil, how many more pencils would the class need? {10}
choice: Which sentence shows scarcity?
  - The library has more books than readers.
  * Ten pencils for twenty students.
  - Every student has a desk.

## Main: 3 fair ways
write: Fair way 1
  lines: 2
  key: Partners share one pencil and take turns writing.
write: Fair way 2
  lines: 2
  key: Half the class writes for 10 minutes while the other half reads, then they switch.
write: Fair way 3
  lines: 2
  key: Students sign out a pencil from a table and bring it back when they finish, so the next person can use it.
write: Is this way fair? "The first 10 students to grab a pencil keep it all day." Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: No. Ten students get nothing all day, and the fastest students win instead of everyone getting a turn.

## Stretch: The best way
write: Which of your 3 ways is the best? Why is it fair, and will the class get its work done?
  lines: 3
  key: For example: "Partners sharing is best, because everyone gets to write, no one waits all day, and partners can help each other."

# 38. Changemaker Timeline
minutes: 30
materials: pencil
ican: I can put changemakers in order on a timeline using the years of what they did.
directions: Read each changemaker card. Find its year on the timeline and write the card's letter above that spot. Then answer the questions.
mistakes: Students may place cards in the order they are printed, or put 1947 and 1955 in the same spot. Count the tick marks by 25s and estimate between them: 1947 is just before 1950, and 1955 is just after it. Some think everyone on a timeline lived at the same time; point out that Benjamin Franklin died long before Malala was born.
notes: Years used: Benjamin Franklin signed the Declaration of Independence in 1776 (he also helped write it). Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in 1849 and then went back to lead others to freedom. Clara Barton founded the American Red Cross in 1881. Jackie Robinson became the first Black player in modern Major League Baseball in 1947. Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1955. Malala Yousafzai won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014 for speaking up for girls' right to go to school; she was 17, the youngest winner ever.

## Warm up: What is a changemaker?
choice: A changemaker is a person who
  - is famous on TV.
  * works to make things better for other people.
  - lived a long time ago.

## Main: Place 6 changemakers
timeline: 1750 2025 25
  - 1776 | Benjamin Franklin helps write and signs the Declaration of Independence.
  - 1849 | Harriet Tubman escapes slavery, then goes back to lead others to freedom.
  - 1881 | Clara Barton starts the American Red Cross to help people after disasters.
  - 1947 | Jackie Robinson becomes the first Black player in modern Major League Baseball.
  - 1955 | Rosa Parks will not give up her seat on a bus and helps start a bus boycott.
  - 2014 | Malala Yousafzai wins the Nobel Peace Prize for speaking up for girls' schooling.
fill:
  - Who on the timeline came first? {Benjamin Franklin}
  - Who on the timeline came last? {Malala Yousafzai}
  - Which two changemakers' years are closest together? {Jackie Robinson} and {Rosa Parks}
  - How many years after Harriet Tubman's escape did Clara Barton start the Red Cross? {32}

## Stretch: Make a change
write: Pick one changemaker. What did they change, and how did it help other people?
  lines: 3
  key: For example: "Clara Barton started the American Red Cross, so people hurt in fires, floods and wars could get food, shelter and care."
