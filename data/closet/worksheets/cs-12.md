# 1. Graph Traversal: BFS and DFS
minutes: 45
materials: pencil, ruler
ican: I can trace breadth-first and depth-first search on a graph and explain how their visit orders differ.
directions: Sketch the graph from its edge list. Then trace breadth-first search (BFS) with a queue and depth-first search (DFS) by going deep and backing up. Whenever a node has several unvisited neighbors, take them in alphabetical order.
mistakes: In BFS, students often mark a node visited when they take it out of the queue instead of when they add it, and then add it twice (E and F are both reachable two ways). In DFS, the usual slip is going back to the start after a dead end instead of backing up one step at a time: from D the search backs up through F, E, H, G, C and B, finding nothing new. The adjacency list is already alphabetical, so read neighbors left to right.
notes: The graph has 8 nodes and 10 undirected edges. Adjacency, alphabetical: A: B, D. B: A, C, E. C: B, G. D: A, F. E: B, F, H. F: D, E, H. G: C, H. H: E, F, G. BFS from A visits A, B, D, C, E, F, G, H; DFS (recursive) from A visits A, B, C, G, H, E, F, D. BFS finds the fewest edges from the start: H is 3 edges from A (A-B-E-H), but DFS first reaches H by the 4-edge path A-B-C-G-H. Stretch, from G: BFS gives G, C, H, B, E, F, A, D; DFS gives G, C, B, A, D, F, E, H.

## The graph
text: **Edges (each goes both ways):** A-B, A-D, B-C, B-E, C-G, D-F, E-F, E-H, F-H, G-H.
table: Node | Neighbors (alphabetical)
  - A | B, D
  - B | A, C, {E}
  - C | B, G
  - D | A, {F}
  - E | B, F, H
  - F | D, E, {H}
  - G | C, H
  - H | E, F, {G}
draw: Sketch the graph: 8 labeled circles with a line for each edge. | 2.4

## Breadth-first search from A
example: BFS: put A in the queue and mark it seen. Repeat: take the node at the front, visit it, and add each unseen neighbor to the back of the queue (marking it seen).
table: Step | Visit | Queue after (front first)
  - 1 | A | B, D
  - 2 | {B} | {D, C, E}
  - 3 | {D} | {C, E, F}
  - 4 | {C} | {E, F, G}
  - 5 | {E} | {F, G, H}
  - 6 | {F} | {G, H}
  - 7 | {G} | {H}
  - 8 | {H} | {(empty)}

## Depth-first search from A
example: DFS: visit a node, then go to its first unvisited neighbor and keep going deeper. At a dead end, back up one node and try its next unvisited neighbor.
fill:
  - DFS visit order: A, {B}, {C}, {G}, {H}, {E}, {F}, {D}
  - DFS reaches its first dead end at node {D}.

## Compare
fill:
  - The fewest edges from A to H is {3}, along the path {A-B-E-H}.
  - DFS first reached H along a path of {4} edges.
write: Which search would you use to find the fewest-hops route between two people in a friend network? Explain using this graph.
  lines: 3
  key: BFS. It visits nodes in order of distance from the start (A, then B and D at 1 edge, then C, E and F at 2, then G and H at 3), so the first time it reaches a node, it has found a shortest path. DFS reached H by a 4-edge path although a 3-edge path exists.

## Stretch
fill:
  - BFS from G: {G, C, H, B, E, F, A, D}
  - DFS from G: {G, C, B, A, D, F, E, H}

# 6. Versions and Deprecation
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can read a library's version number and change notes and decide how and when to update a program that depends on it.
directions: Read the passage about how libraries change. Then read four scenarios about a made-up charting library, chartkit, and decide what the team should do in each one. Name the kind of release and support your choice with the passage.
mistakes: Students treat every update as either always safe or always risky. The version number is a promise: patch and minor releases should not break working code, but a major release may, so read the change notes before moving to it. In scenario 2, students ignore the deprecation warning because the code still runs; it is a deadline, not a suggestion. In scenario 4, students wait for the major upgrade; a security fix on your current major version should go in right away.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class. It describes Semantic Versioning (semver.org), a widely used convention, not a law: some projects number versions other ways, which is a good discussion point. Real examples you can add: Python 2 reached its end of life on January 1, 2020, after years of warnings, so programs still on it stopped getting fixes; in December 2021 a severe flaw in the widely used Java logging library Log4j (called Log4Shell) sent organizations around the world rushing to update. Scenario answers: 1 patch, update after running tests; 2 minor, update and start replacing bar() with column() now; 3 major, do not update blindly: pin below 3.0, read the migration guide, change the 12 calls on a branch, test, then upgrade; 4 patch with a security fix, update to 2.4.1 at once and plan the 3.x move separately.

## Read
passage: When the Code Under Your Code Changes | numbered
  Almost every program depends on code written by someone else. A weather app might use one library to draw charts, another to read dates, and another to talk to a server. These libraries keep changing: their authors fix bugs, add features, close security holes and sometimes redesign how they work. Every change raises a question for the programs that depend on them: should we update, and what might break if we do?
  Many libraries answer with their version numbers. Under a widely used convention called Semantic Versioning, a version such as 2.4.1 has three parts: MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH. The patch number goes up for bug fixes that do not change how you call the library. The minor number goes up when new features are added in a way that does not break existing code. The major number goes up when a change can break code that used to work, such as removing a function or changing what it returns.
  Libraries rarely remove a feature without warning. First they deprecate it: the feature still works, but the documentation marks it as going away, and the program may print a warning naming its replacement. Deprecation gives programmers time to switch before the next major version removes the old feature for good.
  Careful teams protect themselves in a few ways. They read the change log, a list of what changed in each version. They pin their dependencies, writing down exactly which versions their program uses, so an update happens only when they choose. They keep automated tests, so after an update they can see right away whether anything broke. And they update on a separate branch first, not on the code their users depend on.
  Waiting too long has its own risks. Old versions eventually stop getting fixes. When a security flaw is found, the fix usually arrives as a new patch release, and programs that never update stay exposed. A program that skips several major versions can face years of changes at once. The goal is not to update everything instantly or to never update, but to update on purpose.

  - Going from 2.9.3 to 3.0.0 is a {major} release: it may break code that used to work.

## Main: Four scenarios
text: A school team's program uses **chartkit 2.3.1** and calls its bar() function in 12 places.
table: Scenario | Kind of release | What should the team do? Cite the passage.
  height: 1.0
  - 1. chartkit 2.3.2 comes out. The change log says it fixes labels that overlapped. | {patch} | {Update after running the tests; a patch should not change how the program calls the library (paragraph 2).}
  - 2. chartkit 2.4.0 adds pie(). It also deprecates bar(), with a warning: "Use column() instead. bar() will be removed in 3.0." | {minor} | {Update, since nothing breaks yet, and start replacing the 12 bar() calls with column() now, while both work (paragraph 3).}
  - 3. chartkit 3.0.0 comes out. It removes bar() and changes the default colors. | {major} | {Do not update blindly. Keep the version pinned below 3.0, read the change log, replace bar() on a separate branch, run the tests, then upgrade (paragraphs 3 and 4).}
  - 4. A security flaw is found in 2.4.0. Fixes come out in 2.4.1 and 3.0.1. | {patch (security fix)} | {Update to 2.4.1 right away: it closes the hole without breaking code. Plan the move to 3.x separately (paragraph 5).}

## Stretch
write: A teammate says, "Pinning versions is pointless; just always install the newest version." Give one benefit of their idea and one danger, and say what you would do.
  lines: 4
  key: Benefit: you get bug and security fixes as soon as they come out. Danger: a new major version can break the program without warning, possibly right before a deadline or for users. Better: pin versions so updates happen on purpose, but schedule regular updates, read change logs, run tests on a branch, and apply security patches right away.

# 17. AI, Simulation and Networks Check
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can compute a fairness metric, judge a simulation against real data, and find a single point of failure in a network.
directions: Three short parts. In Part 1, compute false positive rates for two groups. In Part 2, compare a simulation with real data. In Part 3, find the device whose failure would cut off the whole school.
mistakes: In Part 1, students divide false positives by all the essays flagged, or by every essay; the false positive rate divides by the honest essays only. In Part 2, students say the simulation is "wrong" and stop; the useful answer names an assumption that made it differ. In Part 3, students pick a core switch; there are two, linked to both buildings, so either can fail. Every path to the internet passes through the firewall.
notes: Part 1 is made up, but it mirrors a real 2023 study that found several AI-writing detectors flagged essays by non-native English writers far more often. Group A: 10 / 200 = 5%. Group B: 18 / 100 = 18%. Group B's rate is 3.6 times Group A's, so honest English learners are much more likely to be falsely accused: unfair. Part 2 (made-up data): the simulation peaks at 40% in week 6; the real data peaks at 22% in week 8. Possible reasons: the model assumes everyone mixes with everyone, while real people mix in smaller groups; people changed behavior (stayed home, washed hands); some people were vaccinated or immune; the model's spread rate was set too high. Part 3: the firewall. Fixes: a second firewall linked to both ISPs and both core switches (and two ISPs already help). Each building switch is a single point of failure for its own building only.

## Part 1: A fairness metric
text: A school tests a tool that flags essays it thinks were written by AI. It runs the tool on essays that teachers know were written honestly by students.
table: Group | Honest essays | Flagged as AI (false positives) | False positive rate
  - A: native English writers | 200 | 10 | {5%}
  - B: English learners | 100 | 18 | {18%}
fill:
  - False positive rate = false positives / {honest essays}
  - Group B's false positive rate is {3.6} times Group A's.
write: Is this tool fair to use for grading? Use your numbers.
  lines: 3
  key: No. An honest English learner is 3.6 times as likely to be falsely flagged (18% versus 5%), so the tool's mistakes fall much more on one group. The school should not use it to accuse students, and certainly not without other evidence and a human review.

## Part 2: Judge a simulation
text: A simple model simulated a flu outbreak in a town (line). The dots show what really happened.

  line: 0,1 1,2 2,5 3,10 4,20 5,33 6,40 7,35 8,25 9,15 10,8 11,4 12,2
  show: 0,1 1,2 2,3 3,5 4,8 5,12 6,16 7,20 8,22 9,20 10,16 11,12 12,8
  minor: off
fill:
  - The simulation peaks in week {6} at {40} percent. The real outbreak peaks in week {8} at {22} percent.
write: Name two assumptions of a simple model that could make it spread faster than the real flu did.
  lines: 3
  key: Any two: everyone mixes with everyone equally (real people meet mostly family, classmates and coworkers); nobody changes behavior (real people stay home, wash hands, wear masks); everyone can catch it (some are vaccinated or immune); the spread rate was set too high.
choice: What should the modelers do next?
  - Throw out simulation as a tool.
  - Report the simulation's peak, since models are more exact than data.
  * Adjust the assumptions, then test the model against this and other real outbreaks.
  - Change the real data to match the model.

# 23. Mock Legislation Hearing
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, highlighter
ican: I can plan testimony that supports claims about a technology policy with evidence.
directions: Read the bill and the evidence cards. Choose a role and a side. Plan three claims, each with evidence and reasoning, plan for the toughest question, and write a short opening statement.
mistakes: Students often pick evidence that sounds strong but does not match their claim, such as using the San Francisco ban (a policy) as proof that the technology is inaccurate. Check that each piece of evidence actually supports its claim. Others ignore the cards that cut against their side; strong testimony addresses them.
notes: The bill and the hearing are made up for Curio Class; the evidence cards summarize real, checkable events and studies: NIST's 2019 Face Recognition Vendor Test report on demographic effects (NISTIR 8280, 189 algorithms); the Gender Shades study by Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru (2018); San Francisco's May 2019 ban on city agencies using facial recognition; Robert Williams's wrongful arrest in Detroit in January 2020 and the 2024 settlement that changed Detroit police rules; Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (2008). Roles can be assigned so both sides are heard. Either side can earn full credit.

## The bill
text: **Senate Bill 12 (mock).** Government agencies in this state, including police and public schools, may not use facial recognition technology to identify or track people. The bill does not cover private businesses, or people using face unlock on their own phones. Anyone harmed by a violation may sue.

## Evidence cards
table: Card | What it says (summarized)
  - A | In 2019, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tested 189 face recognition algorithms. For many of them, false matches were 10 to 100 times more common for Asian and African American faces than for white faces.
  - B | The same NIST study found that the most accurate algorithms showed much smaller differences between groups, and accuracy depends heavily on which algorithm and which photos are used.
  - C | The 2018 Gender Shades study tested three commercial systems that guess gender from a face. Error rates reached 34.7% for darker-skinned women, but stayed at or below 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.
  - D | In January 2020, Detroit police arrested Robert Williams after a face recognition search wrongly matched him to a theft suspect. In a 2024 settlement, Detroit police agreed not to arrest people based only on face recognition results.
  - E | In May 2019, San Francisco became the first major U.S. city to ban city agencies, including police, from using facial recognition.
  - F | Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (2008) requires private companies to get written permission before collecting face scans, and lets people sue if they do not.
  - G | Supporters argue that face recognition can help investigators find suspects or missing people much faster than people searching hours of video by hand, if a human checks every match.

## Plan your testimony
write: My role (for example: student, parent, police chief, civil rights lawyer, software engineer) and my position on Senate Bill 12 (support or oppose):
  lines: 1
  key: Any role and either position, stated clearly, such as "a high school student who supports the bill."
chart: Claim | Evidence (card letter and detail) | Reasoning: how the evidence proves the claim
  rows: 3
  key: Support example. Claim 1: The technology makes more mistakes for some groups. Evidence: A (10 to 100 times more false matches) and C (34.7% vs 0.8%). Reasoning: unequal error rates mean unequal risk of being wrongly accused. Claim 2: Mistakes cause real harm. Evidence: D (Robert Williams). Reasoning: a false match led to an arrest of an innocent person. Claim 3: A ban is workable. Evidence: E (San Francisco). Reasoning: a major city has run without it since 2019. Oppose example: B (the best algorithms are far more accurate), G (speed in finding missing people), D's settlement (rules requiring human checks can fix misuse without a ban); reasoning: regulate and require accuracy testing instead of banning.
write: What is the hardest question the other side could ask you? How will you answer it, using a card?
  lines: 3
  key: Support side: "Card B says the best algorithms are much more accurate. Why not require those instead of a ban?" Answer: accuracy is only one problem; Card D shows misuse by people, and tracking people in public raises privacy issues even when matches are right. Oppose side: "What about Robert Williams?" Answer: Card D's settlement shows rules requiring human checks and other evidence before an arrest can prevent that harm without banning a tool that can help (Card G).
write: Write a 60-second opening statement: who you are, your position, and your strongest claim with its evidence.
  lines: 6
  starter: Thank you, Senators. My name is ... and I am here as a ... I urge you to ...
  key: Model (support): Thank you, Senators. I am here as a high school student, and I urge you to pass Senate Bill 12. Face recognition makes more mistakes for some people than others: a federal study of 189 algorithms found false matches 10 to 100 times more common for Asian and African American faces in many systems. Those mistakes are not small. In Detroit, Robert Williams was arrested for a theft he did not commit because of a false match. Students should not have to wonder whether a camera at school will mistake them for someone else. Please pass this bill.

## Stretch
write: Suggest one amendment that could win votes from the other side without giving up your main goal.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Support side: allow use only to find a missing child, with a warrant and a human check of every match. Oppose side: keep the tools but require accuracy testing across groups, a warrant, and a ban on arrests based only on a match.

# 28. Energy Behind a Search
minutes: 40
materials: pencil, calculator, ruler
ican: I can estimate the energy used by everyday internet activities and show which ones use the most.
directions: Use the per-task estimates to find the energy one student uses in a school day, then the whole class of 30. Graph the class total for each activity as a bar. Then explain what the estimates show and how sure we can be.
mistakes: Students multiply the hours of video by 80 and forget the half hour (0.5 x 80 = 40, not 80). Others add up per-student numbers and forget to multiply by 30 for the class. Watch for the leap to "searching is the problem": with these figures, the laptop itself uses far more than all the searches combined. Units: 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh.
notes: The per-task numbers are rounded classroom estimates, not measurements, and published figures vary a lot by study, device and year. Sources for the teacher: Google estimated about 0.3 Wh per search in 2009; an Electric Power Research Institute report (2024) estimated about 2.9 Wh per AI chatbot request, while some later estimates are about ten times lower; an International Energy Agency analysis (2020) put an hour of streaming video at around 0.08 kWh, including data centers, networks and the device; laptops commonly draw about 20 to 60 watts. Answers: per student 6, 15, 40 and 120 Wh = 181 Wh. Class: 180, 450, 1,200 and 3,600 Wh = 5,430 Wh = 5.43 kWh, about 5.4 hours of a 1,000-watt microwave. Stretch: at 0.3 Wh per AI question, the class AI total falls from 450 to 45 Wh, and the day's total from 5,430 to 5,025 Wh.

table: Activity | Energy (estimate) | What one student does in a school day
  - 1. Web search | 0.3 Wh per search | 20 searches
  - 2. AI chatbot question | 3 Wh per question | 5 questions
  - 3. Streaming video | 80 Wh per hour | half an hour
  - 4. Laptop running | 30 Wh per hour | 4 hours
text: Wh means watt-hours. A 10-watt LED bulb uses 10 Wh in one hour. 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh.

  lines: 3

# 39. Computing Pathway Plan

materials: pencil

directions: Choose one computing career from the list, or another your teacher approves. Plan the steps from now to an entry-level job: courses, projects, skills, routes after high school and a backup route. Use real resources you know of at your school or in your area.
