# 1. Periodization Basics
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain macrocycles, mesocycles and microcycles and use them to read an athlete's season plan.
directions: Read the passage and Lucia's season plan. Then answer the questions, using details from both.
mistakes: Students reverse meso and micro. Memory hook: micro is the smallest (one week), meso is the middle (a block of weeks), macro is the whole plan. Students also read a taper as "getting lazy before the race"; push them to explain that the athlete keeps some fast work while the total amount drops.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 420 words) and Lucia is fictional. Typical lengths vary by coach and sport: a microcycle is usually a week, a mesocycle about 3 to 6 weeks, a macrocycle a season or a year. Lucia's plan is a simplified cross-country season; ask a cross-country or track coach to share a real plan for comparison.

## Read
passage: Training in Cycles
  No athlete can train at full effort every day of the year. Muscles, bones and the nervous system need time to recover and adapt, and a body pushed too hard for too long gets injured, sick or stale. Coaches solve this problem with periodization: planning training in cycles, each with its own purpose, so the athlete improves step by step and is at their best on the days that matter most.
  The largest cycle is the macrocycle. It covers the whole plan, from the first day of training to the end of the season, and often lasts about a year. A macrocycle is split into phases: a preparation phase to build fitness, a competition phase when the important events happen, and a transition phase of rest and light activity before the next plan begins.
  Inside the macrocycle are mesocycles, blocks of training that usually last about three to six weeks. Each mesocycle has one main focus. An early mesocycle might build a base of endurance and general strength. A later one might sharpen speed and the skills of the sport. Many coaches end a mesocycle with a lighter week so the body can absorb the work before the next block begins.
  The smallest cycle is the microcycle, which is usually one week of training. A microcycle sets out each day: which days are hard, which are easy, and which are for rest. Hard days are spread out so the athlete does not stack one hard session on top of another without time to recover.
  Two ideas tie the cycles together. The first is progressive overload. The workload rises a little at a time, by adding distance, weight, speed or repetitions, so the body keeps adapting without being overwhelmed. Jumping from easy training straight to very hard training is one of the most common causes of overuse injuries, such as shin pain in runners.
  The second idea is the taper. In the last week or two before the most important competition, the athlete cuts back the total amount of training but keeps some short, fast work. The body recovers fully but stays sharp, so the athlete arrives at the big event rested and ready.
  Periodization is not only for professionals. A high school runner, swimmer or soccer player can use the same thinking: build a base first, sharpen next, peak for the big event, and then rest. A plan made of cycles turns a long, tiring season into a set of clear steps, and it gives the athlete a reason for every workout.
table: Lucia's mesocycle | Weeks | Main focus | A typical week (microcycle)
  - 1. Base | 1 to 6 | Easy running to build endurance; general strength | 5 easy runs, 2 strength sessions, 1 rest day
  - 2. Build | 7 to 12 | Hill repeats and tempo runs; a longer long run | 2 hard days, 3 easy runs, 1 long run, 1 rest day
  - 3. Racing | 13 to 18 | Races most weekends; fast interval workouts | 1 interval day, 1 race, easy runs between, 1 rest day
  - 4. Peak and taper | 19 to 21 | Less total running, short fast efforts; state meet in week 21 | Shorter runs, short sprints, 2 rest days
  - 5. Transition | 22 to 24 | Rest and light activity of any kind | 3 or 4 easy sessions Lucia chooses

  lines: 2
  key: The work gets harder in steps: after 6 weeks of easy base running, mesocycle 2 adds hill repeats, tempo runs and a longer long run, raising intensity and distance gradually instead of all at once.
write: Lucia is sick and misses all of week 9. Her coach could make her do double workouts in week 10 to catch up. Using the passage, explain why that is a poor idea and suggest a better plan.
  lines: 2
  key: Doubling up breaks progressive overload: a sudden jump in workload after illness raises the risk of overuse injury. Better: return with easy runs in week 10, then rebuild over the rest of the build block, or shift the block a week later; skip, don't cram, the missed hard days.

# 7. Cross-Training Matrix
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can match activities to the fitness components they build and design a cross-training week that covers every component.
directions: In the matrix, write X for each component an activity builds well and a dash for the rest. Use the matrix to answer the questions. Then design a 7-day cross-training week that follows the rules.
mistakes: Students mark every activity for every component ("swimming uses muscles, so it builds strength"). Mark only what an activity builds well: lap swimming at a steady pace trains endurance, not maximal strength. Watch for weeks with strength on back-to-back days or no recovery day at all, and for weeks that cover every component once but give the heart and lungs only one day.
notes: Matrix key, with the main components only: run X - X - -; squats - X - - -; yoga - - X X X; swimming X - X - -; circuit - - X - -; basketball X - - - X; stretching - - - X -; bouldering - X - - X. Accept a reasoned extra X (a fast-paced circuit raises heart rate, bouldering builds grip endurance) if the student can explain it. No two activities cover all five components: covering heart and lungs (run, swim, basketball), strength (squats, bouldering) and flexibility (yoga, stretching) takes three, because no activity is in two of those groups. The week rules follow adult activity guidance: aerobic activity on most days, muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days, and rest so muscles recover. Model week: Mon run 30 min; Tue squats and upper-body strength 45 min plus stretching 10 min; Wed swim 30 min; Thu yoga 45 min; Fri bouldering 60 min; Sat pickup basketball 60 min; Sun rest or an easy walk.

# 20. Inclusion Audit
minutes: 35
materials: pencil
ican: I can rate how inclusive a class game is using clear criteria and recommend changes that give everyone a real chance to play.
directions: Read the description of a class game. Rate it on each of the 6 criteria from 1 (missing) to 4 (strong) and write the evidence. Then recommend two changes and predict what each one would fix.
mistakes: Students rate the game on how fun it was for the strong players. Point them to the evidence: 7 of 22 players never touched the ball. Others give every criterion a 1; the halftime huddle and the safety rules earned credit, and an audit should name what works too. Watch for recommendations that are vague ("make it more fair") instead of a rule, grouping or equipment change.
notes: The game is fictional. Key ratings (accept 1 point off with evidence): Touches 1; Roles 1; Voice 3; Safety 3; Team selection 1; Challenge fit 2. Strong recommendations: small-sided games (for example, four 5 v 5 or 6 v 5 games on smaller fields) to multiply touches; teams set by the teacher or by a quiet count-off; rotate positions every 5 minutes; a rule such as "three different players touch it before a shot"; a second ball or softer ball. 7 of 22 is 31.8 percent, about 32 percent. This sheet pairs well with Modify the Game (#19).

  - Everyone plays the same position all game.
  * Every player has real chances to touch the ball, have a role and be heard, and feels safe doing it.

  - 7 of the 22 players never touched the ball. That is about {32} percent of the class.

## Main: The game
passage: Period 4 Soccer
  Two students who play club soccer were named captains, and they picked teams aloud in front of the class. For the third week in a row, the same four students were picked last. The game was 11 against 11 on one full-size field, with one ball, for 30 minutes.
  A student with a clipboard counted touches. Six players had about 70 percent of all the touches, and 7 players never touched the ball. Players chose their own positions; both captains took striker, and two newer players were told to "stay back and guard the corner" for the whole game. One of them later said she did not know what that job was for.
  The teacher set boundaries well away from the wall, everyone wore shin guards, and slide tackles were not allowed. Teammates cheered every goal. A few players groaned when a beginner missed an easy pass, and nobody said anything about it.
  At halftime the teacher asked each team to huddle and agree on one change. The Blue team added a rule that everyone has to touch the ball before a shot. On the Gold team, a student suggested adding a second ball; a captain said, "That's not real soccer," and the idea was dropped. Nothing else about the rules, ball or field changed for beginners.
table: Criterion | What a 4 looks like | Rating (1 to 4) | Evidence
  - Touches | Every player gets many touches | {1} | {7 of 22 never touched it; 6 had about 70 percent}
  - Roles | Every role matters; roles rotate | {1} | {Captains took striker; newer players stuck at the back all game}
  - Voice | Players help shape the rules and are heard | {3} | {Halftime huddle; Blue changed a rule, but Gold dropped an idea}
  - Safety | Safe space and rules; no put-downs | {3} | {Boundaries, shin guards, no slide tackles; groans not addressed}
  - Team selection | Teams formed fairly and privately | {1} | {Captains picked aloud; same 4 picked last}
  - Challenge fit | Rules and equipment let all abilities succeed | {2} | {Only Blue's touch rule; full field, one ball}

## Main: Recommend
write: **Change 1.** Name one specific change and predict which criterion it raises and how.
  lines: 3
  key: Play four small-sided games (5 v 5 or 6 v 5) on smaller fields. With fewer players per ball, every player gets many more touches, so the Touches rating should rise from 1.
write: **Change 2.** Name a second change that fixes a different criterion.
  lines: 3
  key: The teacher sets balanced teams before class, or students count off, and positions rotate every 5 minutes. No one is picked last in public, and every player gets to try an attacking role.

## Stretch
write: The Gold captain said a second ball is "not real soccer." Write a respectful reply that explains why a modified game can still build real soccer skill.
  lines: 3
  key: "The goal in class is for all of us to get better, and we only get better by touching the ball. A second ball doubles the passing and shooting chances, and we can still play the full game at the end of the unit."

# 21. Ethics in Sport Cases
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can analyze real sport ethics cases and judge the difference between what the rules allow and what is fair.
directions: Read the three case summaries. Answer the questions after each one. Then compare the cases.
mistakes: Students often treat "legal" and "fair" as the same thing. Push them to separate three questions: Did it break a rule? Did it break the spirit of the game? Who was hurt by it? Watch for answers that judge the person instead of the action.
notes: The case summaries are written for Curio Class from widely reported facts; they are not quotations. Before class, check the details against a current reference if you plan to discuss them in depth: the 1981 underarm delivery (Australia against New Zealand, Melbourne Cricket Ground, 1 February 1981), the "Hack-a-Shaq" strategy and the NBA's 2016 change to its away-from-the-play foul rule, and the Uruguay against Ghana quarterfinal at the 2010 World Cup (2 July 2010). Keep the discussion on the choices and the rules, not on attacking the players. Extension to ask aloud: write a one-sentence rule of thumb a captain could use to decide whether a tactic is fair, and test it on one case (for example, "fair if we would accept the other team using it on us and both teams still compete with their skills"; the underarm ball fails it).

## Case 1: The underarm ball
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports of the match, Melbourne, 1 February 1981
  In a one-day cricket final between Australia and New Zealand, New Zealand needed six runs from the very last ball to tie the match. A six can only be scored by hitting the ball over the boundary in the air. Australia's captain, Greg Chappell, told the bowler, his brother Trevor, to roll the ball underarm along the ground, so it could not be hit in the air.[1]
  The delivery was legal under the rules at the time. The batter blocked it, and Australia won. The crowd booed, and leaders in both countries criticized the decision. Underarm bowling was soon banned in one-day international cricket.[2]
write: Did Australia break a rule? Did they break the spirit of the game? Explain both answers.
  lines: 2
  key: No rule was broken: underarm bowling was legal then. Many people argue it broke the spirit of the game, because it removed any chance for the batter to compete for the result instead of winning through skill.
write: Why do you think the rule was changed after this match?
  lines: 2
  key: To stop a legal tactic that made the contest unfair and unpopular; lawmakers decided the game should protect a fair chance to compete.

## Case 2: Hack-a-Shaq
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports, National Basketball Association, late 1990s to 2016
  Shaquille O'Neal was one of the most powerful centers in basketball history, but he often missed free throws. Some coaches began fouling him on purpose, even when he did not have the ball, so he would have to shoot free throws instead of getting a close, easy shot. Fans called the tactic "Hack-a-Shaq."[1]
  Teams later used it against other poor free-throw shooters. The tactic was allowed by the rules, since a foul simply gives the other team free throws, but critics said they slowed games down and made them dull to watch. In 2016 the league changed its rules so that away-from-the-ball fouls in the last two minutes of every quarter give the other team a free throw and the ball.[2]
write: Is intentional fouling a smart strategy, gamesmanship, or both? Defend your view.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Strategy: it follows the rules and gives the other team a lower-percentage chance to score. Gamesmanship: it uses fouls, meant as penalties, as a tool, and it stops the game rather than competing in it. A strong answer addresses both sides.

## Case 3: The handball on the line
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports of the match, 2010 World Cup quarterfinal, Johannesburg, 2 July 2010
  Uruguay and Ghana were tied 1 to 1 in the last minute of extra time. A Ghana header was about to cross the goal line when Uruguay forward Luis Suárez, who was not the goalkeeper, knocked it away with his hands.[1]
  The referee sent Suárez off with a red card and gave Ghana a penalty kick. Ghana's shot hit the crossbar, and Uruguay won the penalty shootout that followed. Some people said Suárez took the punishment the rules set; others said he cheated Ghana out of a fair win.[2]
write: Suárez broke a rule and accepted the penalty. Does accepting the penalty make the action fair? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Yes: the rules set the cost of a handball, and Ghana got its penalty kick. No: a deliberate rule break that takes away a sure goal is not fair just because there is a punishment; the penalty was a smaller cost than the goal it prevented.

## Compare the cases
choice: In which case was a player sent off for his action?
  - Case 1, the underarm ball
  - Case 2, Hack-a-Shaq
  * Case 3, the handball on the line
choice: What do Cases 1 and 2 have in common?
  * A tactic the rules allowed at the time was criticized as unsporting, and the rules were later changed.
  - A player was sent off for a deliberate foul.
  - The team using the tactic lost the game.

# 33. Feedback Fading
minutes: 35
materials: pencil, calculator
ican: I can plan how to reduce feedback over time for learners at different stages and explain why it helps them learn.
directions: Read the feedback terms and the three learners. For each learner, plan the feedback for 4 sessions, from more support to less. Then explain your plan with what you know about how people learn motor skills.
mistakes: Students cut feedback to zero for the beginner in session 2; fading is gradual, and beginners still need frequent help at first. Others fade at the same rate for all three learners; the stage sets the starting point and the speed. Watch for the belief that more feedback always means more learning: it can raise performance during practice while lowering what the learner keeps later.
notes: Background: learners move through stages (cognitive, associative, autonomous, from Fitts and Posner). The guidance hypothesis says feedback after every attempt can guide performance but makes learners depend on it, so retention suffers when it is taken away. Reduced, faded, bandwidth, summary and learner-requested (self-controlled) feedback all help learners build their own sense of the movement. The table key is a model; accept any plan that starts higher for earlier stages, fades gradually, and ends with the learner detecting errors. Math key: 20 x 0.75 = 15; 20 / 5 = 4; 15 - 4 = 11.

## Warm up
text: **Terms.** *Frequent feedback:* after most attempts. *Summary feedback:* one comment about a set of attempts, after the set. *Bandwidth feedback:* only when an error is bigger than an agreed range. *Learner-requested feedback:* the learner asks when they want it.
choice: Why can feedback after every attempt hurt learning?
  - Learners stop listening to their coach.
  - It takes too much time.
  * Learners come to depend on it instead of their own feel for the movement, so they struggle when it is gone.
fill:
  - A learner takes 20 serves and gets feedback after 75 percent of them: {15} times.
  - In session 4 the learner gets summary feedback after every 5 of 20 serves: {4} times.
  - That is {11} fewer feedback moments than session 1.

## Main: Three learners
text: **Mai** (first week of badminton, cognitive stage): thinks through every step, serves vary a lot, cannot tell why a serve went wrong. **Theo** (associative stage): most serves land in, makes small errors, can sometimes say what went wrong. **Rina** (autonomous stage): smooth and consistent, can talk while serving, can describe her own errors.
table: Learner | Session 1 | Session 2 | Session 3 | Session 4
  height: 0.9
  - Mai | {After most serves, one cue at a time} | {After every other serve} | {Summary after every 5 serves} | {Bandwidth: only big misses; Mai guesses first}
  - Theo | {After every other serve} | {Summary after every 5} | {Bandwidth: only big errors} | {Theo asks when he wants it}
  - Rina | {Summary after each set of 10} | {Bandwidth, tight range} | {Rina asks when she wants it} | {Rina self-assesses; coach only confirms}
write: Why does Mai's plan start with more feedback than Rina's?
  lines: 3
  key: Mai is in the cognitive stage and cannot yet detect her own errors, so she needs frequent, simple feedback to know what to change. Rina is autonomous and already senses her errors, so extra feedback would add little and could make her depend on the coach.
write: Why does every plan end with the learner judging their own attempts?
  lines: 3
  key: In a game or on their own, no coach is there after every attempt. Asking the learner to estimate first builds their error detection, which is what lets the skill hold up later (retention) and transfer to new situations.

## Stretch
write: In session 3, Theo's serves get worse as feedback drops. What would you do, and what would you check in session 4?
  lines: 3
  key: A small dip is expected when support is removed and does not mean less learning. I would hold the fade steady (not jump back to feedback after every serve), add a brief summary cue, and check his serves at the start of session 4 with no feedback, a retention test, to see what he kept.
