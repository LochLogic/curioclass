# 1. Periodization Basics
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can explain macrocycles, mesocycles and microcycles and use them to read an athlete's season plan.
directions: Read the passage and Lucia's season plan. Then answer the questions, using details from both.
mistakes: Students reverse meso and micro. Memory hook: micro is the smallest (one week), meso is the middle (a block of weeks), macro is the whole plan. Students also read a taper as "getting lazy before the race"; push them to explain that the athlete keeps some fast work while the total amount drops.
notes: The passage is original to Curio Class (about 420 words) and Lucia is fictional. Typical lengths vary by coach and sport: a microcycle is usually a week, a mesocycle about 3 to 6 weeks, a macrocycle a season or a year. Lucia's plan is a simplified cross-country season; ask a cross-country or track coach to share a real plan for comparison.

## Read
passage: Training in Cycles
  No athlete can train at full effort every day of the year. Muscles, bones and the nervous system need time to recover and adapt, and a body pushed too hard for too long gets injured, sick or stale. Coaches solve this problem with periodization: planning training in cycles, each with its own purpose, so the athlete improves step by step and is at their best on the days that matter most.
  The largest cycle is the macrocycle. It covers the whole plan, from the first day of training to the end of the season, and often lasts about a year. A macrocycle is split into phases: a preparation phase to build fitness, a competition phase when the important events happen, and a transition phase of rest and light activity before the next plan begins.
  Inside the macrocycle are mesocycles, blocks of training that usually last about three to six weeks. Each mesocycle has one main focus. An early mesocycle might build a base of endurance and general strength. A later one might sharpen speed and the skills of the sport. Many coaches end a mesocycle with a lighter week so the body can absorb the work before the next block begins.
  The smallest cycle is the microcycle, which is usually one week of training. A microcycle sets out each day: which days are hard, which are easy, and which are for rest. Hard days are spread out so the athlete does not stack one hard session on top of another without time to recover.
  Two ideas tie the cycles together. The first is progressive overload. The workload rises a little at a time, by adding distance, weight, speed or repetitions, so the body keeps adapting without being overwhelmed. Jumping from easy training straight to very hard training is one of the most common causes of overuse injuries, such as shin pain in runners.
  The second idea is the taper. In the last week or two before the most important competition, the athlete cuts back the total amount of training but keeps some short, fast work. The body recovers fully but stays sharp, so the athlete arrives at the big event rested and ready.
  Periodization is not only for professionals. A high school runner, swimmer or soccer player can use the same thinking: build a base first, sharpen next, peak for the big event, and then rest. A plan made of cycles turns a long, tiring season into a set of clear steps, and it gives the athlete a reason for every workout.

  lines: 2
  key: The work gets harder in steps: after 6 weeks of easy base running, mesocycle 2 adds hill repeats, tempo runs and a longer long run, raising intensity and distance gradually instead of all at once.
write: Lucia is sick and misses all of week 9. Her coach could make her do double workouts in week 10 to catch up. Using the passage, explain why that is a poor idea and suggest a better plan.
  lines: 2
  key: Doubling up breaks progressive overload: a sudden jump in workload after illness raises the risk of overuse injury. Better: return with easy runs in week 10, then rebuild over the rest of the build block, or shift the block a week later; skip, don't cram, the missed hard days.

# 21. Ethics in Sport Cases
minutes: 40
materials: pencil
ican: I can analyze real sport ethics cases and judge the difference between what the rules allow and what is fair.
directions: Read the three case summaries. Answer the questions after each one. Then compare the cases.
mistakes: Students often treat "legal" and "fair" as the same thing. Push them to separate three questions: Did it break a rule? Did it break the spirit of the game? Who was hurt by it? Watch for answers that judge the person instead of the action.
notes: The case summaries are written for Curio Class from widely reported facts; they are not quotations. Before class, check the details against a current reference if you plan to discuss them in depth: the 1981 underarm delivery (Australia against New Zealand, Melbourne Cricket Ground, 1 February 1981), the "Hack-a-Shaq" strategy and the NBA's 2016 change to its away-from-the-play foul rule, and the Uruguay against Ghana quarterfinal at the 2010 World Cup (2 July 2010). Keep the discussion on the choices and the rules, not on attacking the players. Extension to ask aloud: write a one-sentence rule of thumb a captain could use to decide whether a tactic is fair, and test it on one case (for example, "fair if we would accept the other team using it on us and both teams still compete with their skills"; the underarm ball fails it).

## Case 1: The underarm ball
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports of the match, Melbourne, 1 February 1981
  In a one-day cricket final between Australia and New Zealand, New Zealand needed six runs from the very last ball to tie the match. A six can only be scored by hitting the ball over the boundary in the air. Australia's captain, Greg Chappell, told the bowler, his brother Trevor, to roll the ball underarm along the ground, so it could not be hit in the air.[1]
  The delivery was legal under the rules at the time. The batter blocked it, and Australia won. The crowd booed, and leaders in both countries criticized the decision. Underarm bowling was soon banned in one-day international cricket.[2]
write: Did Australia break a rule? Did they break the spirit of the game? Explain both answers.
  lines: 2
  key: No rule was broken: underarm bowling was legal then. Many people argue it broke the spirit of the game, because it removed any chance for the batter to compete for the result instead of winning through skill.
write: Why do you think the rule was changed after this match?
  lines: 2
  key: To stop a legal tactic that made the contest unfair and unpopular; lawmakers decided the game should protect a fair chance to compete.

## Case 2: Hack-a-Shaq
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports, National Basketball Association, late 1990s to 2016
  Shaquille O'Neal was one of the most powerful centers in basketball history, but he often missed free throws. Some coaches began fouling him on purpose, even when he did not have the ball, so he would have to shoot free throws instead of getting a close, easy shot. Fans called the tactic "Hack-a-Shaq."[1]
  Teams later used it against other poor free-throw shooters. The tactic was allowed by the rules, since a foul simply gives the other team free throws, but critics said they slowed games down and made them dull to watch. In 2016 the league changed its rules so that away-from-the-ball fouls in the last two minutes of every quarter give the other team a free throw and the ball.[2]
write: Is intentional fouling a smart strategy, gamesmanship, or both? Defend your view.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Strategy: it follows the rules and gives the other team a lower-percentage chance to score. Gamesmanship: it uses fouls, meant as penalties, as a tool, and it stops the game rather than competing in it. A strong answer addresses both sides.

## Case 3: The handball on the line
source: Case summary written for Curio Class from published reports of the match, 2010 World Cup quarterfinal, Johannesburg, 2 July 2010
  Uruguay and Ghana were tied 1 to 1 in the last minute of extra time. A Ghana header was about to cross the goal line when Uruguay forward Luis Suárez, who was not the goalkeeper, knocked it away with his hands.[1]
  The referee sent Suárez off with a red card and gave Ghana a penalty kick. Ghana's shot hit the crossbar, and Uruguay won the penalty shootout that followed. Some people said Suárez took the punishment the rules set; others said he cheated Ghana out of a fair win.[2]
write: Suárez broke a rule and accepted the penalty. Does accepting the penalty make the action fair? Explain.
  lines: 2
  key: Answers vary. Yes: the rules set the cost of a handball, and Ghana got its penalty kick. No: a deliberate rule break that takes away a sure goal is not fair just because there is a punishment; the penalty was a smaller cost than the goal it prevented.
