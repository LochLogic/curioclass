# 1. Global Concert Dance Survey
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, video clips or demonstrations of each form chosen by the teacher
ican: I can compare concert dance forms from around the world by technique and cultural context.
directions: Match each form to its home and a key feature. Then complete the survey chart on technique and context, and answer the questions comparing two forms.
mistakes: Students describe forms as "exotic" or "traditional" without specifics. Ask for the trained technique (stance, footwork, hands, use of the face) and the context (where it began, who performed it, what music goes with it). Bharatanatyam and Kathak are often confused: Bharatanatyam is from South India, with a deep bent-knee stance; Kathak is from North India, with fast spins and footwork in an upright stance.
notes: Key facts: Bharatanatyam comes from Tamil Nadu in South India, grew from temple and court dance, and was revived for the concert stage in the 1930s; it uses the araimandi half-sitting stance, precise footwork, hand gestures (mudras) and expressive storytelling (abhinaya), with Carnatic music. Kathak comes from North India, from storytellers (kathakars), and developed further in Hindu and Mughal courts; it is known for fast spins (chakkars) and footwork sounded with ankle bells (ghungroo), with Hindustani music and tabla. Butoh began in Japan in the late 1950s with Tatsumi Hijikata and Kazuo Ohno, in the years after World War II; it uses very slow, controlled movement, often white body paint and intense, transformed images of the body. Flamenco comes from Andalusia in southern Spain, shaped above all by Romani (Gitano) communities along with other influences; dance (baile) joins song (cante), guitar (toque) and handclaps (palmas) with strong footwork (zapateado). Kabuki began in Kyoto around 1603, credited to Izumo no Okuni; after women were banned from the stage in 1629, men played all roles, including women's roles (onnagata); it features stylized poses (mie), elaborate costumes and makeup, and shamisen music. Chinese classical dance was shaped as a concert form in the 20th century from Chinese opera movement and martial arts, with round, flowing paths, jumps and turns, and sleeve dances. Present every form through its own dancers where possible.

## Warm up: Match the form
match: Match each form to its home and one feature.
  - Bharatanatyam = South India; half-sitting stance and hand gestures called mudras
  - Kathak = North India; fast spins and ankle bells
  - butoh = Japan, late 1950s; very slow, controlled movement
  - flamenco = southern Spain; footwork with guitar, song and handclaps
  - Kabuki dance = Japan, early 1600s; stylized poses called mie
  - Chinese classical dance = China; movement drawn from opera and martial arts, with flowing sleeves

## Main: Survey chart
text: Write one form in each row, in this order: Bharatanatyam, Kathak, butoh, flamenco, Kabuki dance, Chinese classical dance.
chart: Form | Technique (stance, footwork, hands, face) | Music | Context (origin, who performed it, purpose)
  rows: 6
  key: See the teacher notes for each form's technique, music and context; accept other accurate details.
write: Which two forms rely most on rhythmic footwork? What makes the sound in each?
  lines: 2
  key: Kathak and flamenco (Bharatanatyam footwork also counts). Kathak dancers sound rhythms with bare feet and the ankle bells (ghungroo); flamenco dancers strike the floor with the heels, toes and soles of their shoes (zapateado).

## Compare
write: Butoh began after World War II. How might its historical context explain why it looks so different from the other forms on this chart?
  lines: 4
  key: Butoh artists rejected both Western dance and older Japanese forms, searching for a new way to show the body after the war's destruction and rapid change in Japan; that is why it uses slow, transformed, sometimes unsettling images instead of codified steps or graceful lines.
write: Choose two forms. Compare how each uses the hands or arms to communicate.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: Bharatanatyam uses mudras, codified hand gestures that can show words, objects or feelings while telling a story; flamenco uses curving arm and wrist movements (braceo) and handclaps that add to the rhythm and mood rather than telling a story word by word.

## Stretch
write: Many of these forms changed when they moved from temples, courts or communities onto concert stages. What can be gained and what can be lost in that change?
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Gains: wider audiences, preservation, new training and new works. Losses: the original setting and purpose, community participation, or meaning that depends on a ritual or occasion. Strong answers give an example such as Bharatanatyam's revival in the 1930s.

# 7. Professional Review
minutes: 60
materials: pencil, a live or recorded concert dance performance chosen by the teacher, lined paper for the full draft
ican: I can write a dance review for general readers with a lead, context, description, analysis and verdict.
directions: Match each part of a review to its job. Plan your word budget and your notes for each part. Draft your lead and verdict here, then write the full 500-word review on lined paper.
mistakes: Reviews for general readers fail in two ways: jargon with no explanation ("the tendus were clean") or plot summary with no judgment. Each technical term should be explained in passing or swapped for a plain description. Watch for description and analysis blurring: description says what happened; analysis says how and why it works or does not.
notes: The five-part shape is common in newspaper and magazine arts criticism, though professional critics bend it freely. 500 words is a typical length for a short published review. Have students read one published review first and label its parts. A good lead does not start with "On Friday night I saw"; it starts with an image, a claim or a question. The verdict can be mixed; it should be clear.

## Warm up: The five parts
match: Match each part of a review to its job.
  - Lead = hooks a general reader with a vivid image, a claim or a question
  - Context = tells who made the work, when and where, and what tradition it comes from
  - Description = shows what the audience saw and heard, in plain, precise words
  - Analysis = explains how the choices work together and why they succeed or fail
  - Verdict = gives the reviewer's overall judgment and who should see it
fill:
  - If the lead is 50 words, context 75 and verdict 75, that leaves {300} words of a 500-word review for description and analysis.
  - Split evenly, description and analysis get {150} words each.
choice: Which sentence is written for a general reader?
  - The adagio's développés were impeccably controlled.
  * In the slow duet, the dancers unfold their legs to full height so gradually that the movement seems to float.
  - The épaulement was very good.

## Main: Plan the review
text: Performance: ______________________________ Choreographer or company: ____________________
chart: My notes | Words
  labels: Lead; Context; Description; Analysis; Verdict
  height: 0.95
  key: Answers vary. Notes should give a hook for the lead, who, when, where and tradition for the context, specific observable moments for the description, a claim about how the choices work for the analysis, and a clear judgment and audience for the verdict. The word column should total about 500.
write: Draft your lead (2 or 3 sentences).
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: "For eleven minutes, eight dancers try to cross a stage that keeps tilting under their feet, or so it seems. The new piece by the city's youth company turns a simple walk into a fight against gravity, and it is the most gripping thing on the program."
write: Draft your verdict (2 or 3 sentences).
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. Model: "The middle section drags, but the final crossing earns its standing ovation. Anyone who thinks modern dance is hard to follow should start here."

## Stretch
write: Find one technical term you plan to use. Write the sentence you will use to explain it to a reader who has never taken a dance class.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "The piece is built on canon, where dancers start the same phrase one after another, like singers in a round."

# 20. Collaborators' Master Score
minutes: 50
materials: pencil, colored pencils (optional), your issue piece, its music and any spoken text, a stopwatch
ican: I can align movement, music, spoken text and light cues by timing in one master score that all collaborators can follow.
directions: Work out the timing in the warm up. Then build the master score for your issue piece: each row is 16 seconds of the piece. Write what happens in each layer and number your light cues. Answer the questions about how the layers fit together.
mistakes: Students write vague entries ("dancing," "music plays"). Each cell should let a collaborator act: name the section or motif, the musical landmark (a drum entrance, a silence), the exact words, and the cue number with what changes (LQ 3: cold side light only). Watch for light cues placed on the same count as a big entrance with no time to see it; a cue often lands a count or two before the moment it supports.
notes: The times printed assume 120 beats per minute, so one 8-count phrase is 4 seconds and each row is 4 phrases. For a different tempo, students cross out and rewrite the time column: seconds per phrase = 480 divided by the tempo. LQ is the common shorthand for a lighting cue number; in performance the stage manager calls each cue ("Lights 3, go"). If there is no lighting board, plan cues as changes with available light (house lights, a lamp, a doorway) or as imagined cues for a future staging.

## Warm up: Timing
fill:
  - At 120 beats per minute, one count lasts {0.5} seconds.
  - One 8-count phrase lasts {4} seconds.
  - A light cue on count 1 of phrase 5 happens at {0:16} after the piece starts.
  - A 2-minute piece at 120 beats per minute has {240} counts, or {30} eight-count phrases.
choice: During a performance, who usually calls the light cues so the board operator knows exactly when to press go?
  - The choreographer, from the audience
  * The stage manager
  - The lead dancer, by giving a signal on stage

## Read the score
write: Find one moment where two layers support each other. What do they do together?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: At 1:04 the music builds with added drums while the dancers move from scattered positions into one tight group, and LQ 6 brightens the whole stage, so sound, movement and light all say "coming together."
write: Find one moment where two layers compete for attention. How will you fix it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: The spoken line at 0:40 happens during a fast unison section, so no one will hear it; we will move the line to the held shape in phrase 12 and cut the music to piano there.

## Stretch
write: Choose one spoken line. Should the dancers move or hold still while it is spoken? Defend your choice.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer weighs clarity (stillness lets the words land) against meaning (movement under the words can contradict or deepen them) and ties the choice to the issue.

# 21. Running a Rehearsal
minutes: 30
materials: pencil, timer, your issue piece and cast
ican: I can run an efficient rehearsal segment that warms up, teaches, cleans and gives notes.
directions: Plan a 20-minute rehearsal segment for your issue piece. Answer the timing questions, fill in the checklist, then run the segment with your cast and record what happened.
mistakes: Student directors spend 15 minutes teaching and skip cleaning and notes. Watch the clock and move on at each time marker. Notes should be specific ("arms at shoulder height on count 3") and include something that went well, not only corrections.
notes: The segment shape: warm up (prepare bodies and focus), teaching (new material), cleaning (make the same material sharp and together: counts, spacing, unison), notes (specific feedback and the plan for next time). Rotate student directors so everyone runs one segment. The issue piece is the dance each group is creating about an issue in this module.

## Warm up: Plan the clock
fill:
  - Warm up 5 minutes, teaching 7 minutes and cleaning 5 minutes leaves {3} minutes for notes in a 20-minute segment.
  - If teaching runs 4 minutes long, and notes must stay at 3 minutes, cleaning shrinks to {1} minute.
  - A 32-count section taught 8 counts at a time takes {4} chunks to teach.
choice: Which is the most useful cleaning note?
  - That looked kind of messy.
  * Everyone, the arm on count 5 goes straight up, not forward. Let's run counts 1 to 8 again.
  - Try harder next time.

## After the rehearsal
write: Write the two most important notes you gave your cast.
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Strong notes are specific and name counts, body parts or spacing, for example "Counts 5-8: the diagonal line drifts; stay on your marks" and "The ending pose was strong and together: keep that."
write: Did you stay on time? What would you change in the next segment?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. A strong reflection compares planned and actual minutes and names a fix, for example "Teaching ran 3 minutes over because I taught 16 counts at once; next time I will teach 8 counts, then run them before adding more."

## Stretch
write: A cast member keeps doing a move that hurts their knee. What do you say and do as the rehearsal director?
  lines: 3
  key: Stop that move for that dancer right away, let them rest and tell the teacher; offer a safe alternative (change the level or take out the deep bend) and adjust the choreography so no one has to dance through pain.

# 33. Daylight Study
minutes: 60
materials: pencil, a meter stick, a tape measure, a sheet of white paper, a compass (or a map of the site showing north), your site
ican: I can study how daylight changes at a performance site and use the evidence to choose a performance time.
directions: Answer the warm-up questions. Visit your site at three times of day. Each time, rate the light with the shadow test, record where the sun is and measure the shadow of a meter stick. Then choose the best performance time and defend it with your data.
mistakes: Students look toward the sun to find it. Never look at the sun: find its direction from shadows, which point directly away from it. Watch for students who pick the brightest time without thinking about glare: an audience that faces the sun cannot see the dancers, and dancers who face it squint and lose their focus.
notes: Plan three site visits of about 10 minutes each (morning, midday, late afternoon) and 30 minutes to analyze. The shadow test needs no device: hold a hand about 30 cm above white paper. Sharp, dark edges mean direct sun (5); soft edges mean hazy or light cloud (3); no shadow at all means full cloud or shade (1). A school light meter or meter app can add numbers, but the sheet does not need one. Facts for the warm up: in the continental United States, the sun is due south at solar noon and shadows point due north and are shortest then; solar noon is often near 1:00 pm during daylight saving time. A 1 m stick casts a 1 m shadow when the sun is 45 degrees above the horizon. Do all three visits within a few days so the season does not change the results.

## Warm up: Reading the sun

  - A meter stick casts a shadow 1 m long. The sun is {45} degrees above the horizon.

## Choose a time
write: Which time is best for the performance? Use at least two pieces of data from your log.
  lines: 4
  key: Answers vary. A strong answer names a time and cites data: for example "Visit 1 (9:30 am): shadow test 3 and the sun behind the audience in the east, so the dancers are lit from the front without glare; at visit 3 the sun was low in the west right behind the dancers, so the audience would squint."
write: What is one problem with your chosen time, and how will you work around it?
  lines: 3
  key: Answers vary. Model: "Morning light is softer, so the far end of the site is in shadow; we will move the opening phrase into the sunlit half and use the shaded corner for the quiet solo."

## Stretch
write: Your performance moves from fall to late spring. How might the best time change? Explain using what you know about the sun's height in different seasons.
  lines: 3
  key: In late spring the sun rises higher and sets later and farther to the north, so shadows are shorter at the same clock time and evening light lasts longer; a later performance may work, and the angle of glare will shift. Students should plan another daylight study closer to the date.
