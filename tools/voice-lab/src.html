Voice Lab / Manual

Through the glass

Press MIC, then say ahhh. Or press a sound pad: no microphone is needed. Higher frequencies appear higher on the ruler. Time enters at the right and moves left. Brighter means a stronger frequency component, not a better sound.

Hold freezes the ribbon. Click or drag on it to measure a point. Arrow keys move the crosshair; Shift takes bigger steps. The cursor reports seconds back from the held moment and frequency in hertz. A and B store the spectrum slice at that time. Their outlines share the frequency ruler: left is quieter, right is stronger. Solid A and dashed B also have text labels.

The front panel

Input has MIC, CLIPS and TONE detents. Switching away from MIC releases the microphone. TONE analyses its own signal directly. Sound mutes synthesized playback and narration, but analysis continues; Read still speaks. A microphone is never played back.

Gain changes analysis amplitude by −20 to +20 dB. Range changes the top frequency, not the sound. Linear gives equal room to equal hertz intervals. Musical uses a logarithmic ruler from 20 Hz: each octave gets equal room. Wide uses a 256-sample FFT; Narrow uses 4096 samples. Short windows blur frequency and reveal changing vowel bands; long windows separate harmonics and blur time. Sweep shows the most recent 2, 5, 10 or 20 seconds.

Voices and Instruments select two banks of eight pads. A pad loops an original synthetic sound; press it again or Stop to stop. There are men's and children's ah, ee and oo, a whistle, whispered ah, flute, violin, clarinet, sss, shh, siren and C-major chord. Flute, violin and clarinet share C4 (about 262 Hz). The last instrument-bank pad is silence. The frequency knob in TONE spans 20 to 8,000 Hz; start at a quiet output.

Stop pauses the generator; its pad then reads Start. Press Start to hear and analyse the tone again.

Readouts

PITCH estimates a repeating fundamental from 50 to 1,000 Hz using YIN, with clean single-line tracking up to 4,000 Hz. NOTE is the nearest equal-tempered note with cents above or below it, referenced to A4 = 440 Hz. PEAK uses a separate 4096-sample FFT with parabolic interpolation in both bands; unresolved low peaks show dots. LEVEL is RMS amplitude in dBFS, clamped to −60 through 0; it is not a calibrated sound pressure reading. Red at the top means clipping risk.

F1 and F2 are approximate vocal-tract resonances estimated with order-12 linear predictive coding (LPC), after averaging down to about 10 kHz and pre-emphasis. They follow a usable voiced estimate live, with gentle smoothing; Hold freezes the numbers at the cursor. They may wobble on high voices and fail on whispers or hisses. A pure tone is not a vowel, and the chart does not grade anyone.

The vowel chart shows three vowels for men (circles), women (squares) and children (triangles), from published American English averages. F2 increases to the left; F1 increases downward. The cross follows the live estimate; Hold freezes it at the cursor's time. The numerical F1/F2 readout follows that same estimate.

Privacy and honest edges

Nothing is recorded or kept. The sound never leaves this device. Analysis uses a rolling, temporary buffer in RAM; Hold and A/B retain spectra until cleared or the tab closes. No audio files, recording, export or network code exist here. The microphone is released on switching input, switching it off, hiding this tab or closing it. Motion is the only saved preference.

Permission requires a secure context and an iframe that allows microphone access. If unavailable, use the sound pads or open the downloaded file directly. Speech uses the browser's installed voice when available; recordings of the chosen news-reader voice are not bundled. Offline systems without a local voice can still use all visible controls and readouts.

Built-in sounds are computer-made approximations, not recordings of people or instruments. Formant numbers are historical averages, not boundaries for accents, languages, genders or individual voices. Laptop microphones and speakers roll off low and high frequencies. Pitch can jump an octave on creaky or breathy voices and is unreliable for chords. The actual sample rate determines FFT resolution: at 48 kHz, bin spacing is 187.50 Hz (Wide) or 11.72 Hz (Narrow). Blackman window bandwidth is wider than one bin. A C-major chord's thirds are four and three semitones, so its lines are not equally spaced on a log ruler.

Things people have found

  • Holding a note while changing ee to oo moves the bright vowel bands while the harmonic spacing stays similar.
  • Historical men's ee and oo second-formant averages are 2,290 and 870 Hz. Children's ee average is 3,200 Hz.
  • A whisper can carry a vowel's broad resonances without a periodic ladder.
  • In the low clarinet register odd harmonics tend to be stronger. Real instruments have more detail than these recipes.
  • Doubling frequency makes an octave; multiplying the same pitch by 2, 3, 4 and so on locates its harmonics.
  • Sss tends to put noise higher than shh; use the 8 or 16 kHz range to see it.
  • About 6 dB up doubles amplitude and quadruples relative energy for the same wave and duration. The generator shows AMP = 10^(dB/20) and ENERGY = AMP squared, relative to its 0 dB setting for the same waveform. These are signal ratios before input Gain, not calibrated room energy (UNSW, Decibels; OpenStax 17.3).
  • The C-major pad can make PITCH settle near 65 Hz (C2), below any of its three tones. A 4:5:6 chord would repeat at that low fundamental. These equal-tempered C4, E4 and G4 frequencies only approximate 4:5:6, so they do not repeat exactly once per C2 period. This is a periodicity estimate, not a fourth played note (UNSW, Acoustics FAQ; de Cheveigné and Kawahara, YIN).
