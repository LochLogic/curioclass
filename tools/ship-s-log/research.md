# Research packet: ship-s-log
Concept: Plan a 1600s voyage: supplies, route and crew choices; meet the consequences for sailors and Indigenous people.
Grade: 5   Standards: D2.His.14.3-5, D2.Geo.7.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.His.14.3-5: "Explain probable causes and effects of events and developments." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to trace how voyage decisions (rations, routes, season) caused specific outcomes for ship crews and neighboring Native nations.
- D2.Geo.7.3-5: "Explain how cultural and environmental characteristics affect the distribution and movement of people, goods, and ideas." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain how wind currents, ocean storms, and navigational geography guided colonial ships across the Atlantic to specific coastal homelands.

## Real data

- Library of Congress Digitized Collections: William Bradford's *Of Plimoth Plantation* manuscript: https://www.loc.gov/item/03001851/ (Public domain). Firsthand journal documenting the Mayflower crossing, rations, beam cracks, and first encounters.
- National Park Service Historic Jamestowne Research Archive: https://www.nps.gov/jame/learn/historyculture/ (Public domain). Archeological records and manifests for the 1607 voyage of the Susan Constant, Godspeed, and Discovery.
- Smithsonian National Museum of American History: On the Water: Stories from Maritime America: https://americanhistory.si.edu/onthewater (Smithsonian Institution). Navigational astrolabes, cross-staffs, and colonial ship logs.

## Common mistakes

- Imagining European ships reached an empty wilderness: Fifth graders often picture unpopulated forests, missing that Native nations had inhabited and cultivated the land with extensive towns, cornfields, and governments for millennia (Smithsonian NMAI NK360).
- Viewing the crossing as easy sailing: Students frequently overlook the misery of weeks confined to pitch-dark holds, rancid food, contaminated drinking water, and crippling scurvy (Library of Congress / NPS).
- Assuming colonists survived independently: Students assume settlers cleared land and grew food immediately, forgetting they relied critically on corn traded from Native nations (NPS Jamestowne).
- Thinking sailors used modern GPS navigation: Students assume 1600s captains could pinpoint their exact location, unaware that determining longitude at sea remained an unsolved problem in the seventeenth century (Smithsonian NMAH).

## Sensitivity

- Dual-perspective accountability: Decisions made by colonial voyagers carried severe consequences for both sailors and Indigenous peoples. Highlight that European land claims and introduced diseases caused devastating demographic crises for Native nations.
- Native sovereignty: Depict the Powhatan, Wampanoag, and other coastal nations as organized sovereign nations with skilled leaders and complex diplomacies.
- Consult living tribal resources: Reference Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian (NK360).

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Plimoth Patuxet Museums "You Are the Historian" (https://plimoth.org): Interactive multi-perspective investigation examining English colonist and Wampanoag experiences.
- Historic Jamestowne "Voyage to Virginia" simulation (NPS / Preservation Virginia): Educational simulation where students select cargo and crew for the 1607 voyage.
- BBC Bitesize Primary History: Life on board a 17th-century sailing ship.

## Gaps

- Precise ration rationing mathematics: Historical ships carried food calculated in pounds and barrels, which spoiled unpredictably; Curio Class tools should represent supplies through simple status gauges (Food, Water, Crew Health, Hull) suitable for branching fifth-grade narrative choices.
