## What it teaches

Students use their own population counts to explain how inherited variation and different chances of survival can change a population.
They compare a small population they hunt with a larger computer simulation, then distinguish that model from peppered moth history.

## Key skills

- Notice camouflage and compare a moth with its surroundings.
- Read a histogram and a percent graph together.
- Connect the moths that survive with the next generation.
- Explain a result with counts from the population being discussed.
- Compare a classroom model with evidence from real research.

## Support at home

- On a walk, look for a leaf or stone that blends with its surroundings. Talk about who might find it hard to see.
- Discuss a family resemblance in a familiar animal. Separate inherited features from a stain or something learned.
- Ask your child to describe a graph that rose and fell and explain what changed around the moths.

## A 5-minute start

1. Open Hunt. Find one moth and peck it. A bare-bark tap starts the clock too, with a one-second miss penalty.
2. Eat up to fifteen. Look at the survivors during the breeding beat, then compare the next histogram.
3. Keep hunting through the first story card. Ask which moths are easier to find on soot.
4. Open Run and press History. It runs five clean, twenty-five soot and twenty-five clean generations.
5. Open Explain, choose the explanation and start a sentence with Use my numbers.

## The four modes

**Hunt:** fourteen generations, thirty moths at birth, up to fifteen pecks in twenty-five seconds. First tap starts each round.
The player causes selection. Misses freeze pecking for one second. P or Pause covers and blurs the field. Window blur pauses too.
Switching modes preserves Hunt in a paused state. Restart uses the same seed and returns the same starting population.

**Run:** two hundred moths, with forty drawn as a sample. Birds take one hundred by contrast-weighted sampling without replacement.
Play runs three generations a second at 1x and six at 2x. Step advances once. All controls remain live during playback.
Changing Bark during History takes over its environment sequence. Predictions lock at the first Play, Step or History action.
Reset returns two hundred pale moths and unlocks prediction. The run stops at generation 120.
Each Reset and History starts a fresh random trial, so watching an earlier run cannot reveal the next prediction result.

**Explain:** both misconceptions give reasons. The correct card opens Variation, Survival and Inheritance writing boxes.
Use my numbers inserts evidence from the latest Hunt with a completed round, otherwise the latest Run with a completed generation.
Editing is the student's job. The tool awards no mastery or completion score for selecting a card or inserting a sentence.
Text is stored under bug-hunt-explain on this device, with storage failures handled quietly. Copy falls back to selecting text.

**Real story:** eight stops and four painted scenes. The historical chart shows rare in 1848 and about 98 percent by the 1890s.
There is no connecting historical line: these are selected records, not annual measurements. Stop 7 states the lichen debate.
Stop 8 adds the student's Hunt record when available. Model generations are never relabeled as historical years.

## Talk about it

- Which moths did you eat on lichen? Which on soot? What counts support that?
- Did any individual moth change its wings, or did different moths become parents?
- Why do two students sometimes get different results even when both hunt carefully?
- What does Birds off remove? What do mutation and incoming moths still add?
- Why is a stain different from an inherited gene copy?
- How is the historical chart different from the simulated line?

## A class period

Allow about 7 minutes for Hunt, 5 to 10 for Run, 5 for Explain and 5 for Real story.
Pairs compare screens directly. No pooled counts, transfer codes or device-to-device copying are needed.
MS-LS4-4 is practiced in the misconception comparison and written evidence. MS-LS4-6 is practiced with both population graphs.

## Good to know

Game numbers. Real birds eat a smaller share, so in England the change took decades, not a few rounds. Real dark copies were far rarer: one change, around 1819.

The dark form has at least one dark gene copy. Pale darkness is 5 + 40 times speckle; dark darkness is 78 + 20 times speckle.
The ten bins therefore separate the forms exactly: bins 70 through 100 contain all and only dark-form moths.
Young inherit one copy from each of two survivors, with speckle near the parents' mean. Individual moths never recolor during life.
Six scheduled dark copies support variation in Hunt. One pale incomer arrives each generation. Run has four incomers, each with a one-in-ten chance of a dark copy.
New dark copies in Run affect each inherited pale copy with probability one in five hundred. These are teaching assumptions, not measured natural rates.

Birds off removes selection but does not turn off incomers or mutation. Dark frequency can drift upward, sometimes above 25 percent.
The spec's stronger statistical expectations did not reproduce exactly; see codex-report.md for the measurements and retained rules.
The game makes no claim that a particular hunt must make a hill. End text describes the observed record honestly.
The band under a birth population names the bark the student hunts on in that generation. The parents were hunted in the previous generation.
A new cohort can therefore still reflect soot survival just as the bark changes back to lichen.

Keyboard Hunt: Tab to the field, arrows move a crosshair forty pixels, Shift with arrows moves ten, Space or Enter pecks.
Moths are never separate focus targets, so keyboard focus does not reveal their hiding places.
Predict supports pointer drag and left/right keys before the run starts. Motion off removes animation and keeps the twenty-five-second hunt clock.
The spec explicitly makes the breeding beat and bark transition instant with Motion off. Story narration still finishes before Continue acts.
