Field manual

An ecologist's study tank. Add, net, feed, shade, then watch. The pond keeps running while this drawer is open.

At the rim

Tap a specimen to drop it in. Drag one into the water to choose its place. Shake in fish food with the shaker. Tap a creature in the water to see its food arrows. Net changes the tray to Net 1 and all; swipe through the water to scoop at most six.

Space holds or runs. Period advances one week. < and > change speed. Keys 1–7 stock the tray kinds; arrows on the tank move the drop point; Enter inspects the nearest creature in Life. N picks up the net; Escape puts it down. L changes lenses; W shows the whole chart. M opens this drawer.

Reading the recorder

The dashed line is the pond you did not change. Compare it with your darker line. Floating algae and algae on stems have separate lines. Shade is off until you tap its key; its own scale runs from 0 to 7.

Recorder details

Numbers on the line keys are counts. Algae: one clump is 5 matter units; snails and nymphs 0.5; minnows 2. Darker traces are your pond. Paler long-dashed traces project the pond just before your latest change, unchanged for 104 weeks. They are a counterfactual, not a prediction after further changes. A vertical mark records an action. Each trace has its own dash and end label. The vertical scale is shared counts; oxygen has its own meter in mg/L.

Dense populations share a specimen: each tray key reports its drawing multiplier. The model retains every fraction of matter.

Matter

Tap a dot to follow some matter. It may stay in one place for weeks. The trip tells you how long it has waited, and where it goes next.

Matter details

Tap any dot, or press Enter on the tank in Matter, to follow a small sample. The trip follows actual transfers with flow-weighted probabilities. Dots are samples, not individual atoms. Their unit key adapts above 750 units. All model transfers conserve matter; additions, netting, and turtles walking away are in the ledger.

A fixed adult turtle has 30 units. A dot in that fixed body stays there; the turtle's food passes through to water, air and muck. A heron is a visitor outside the matter total.

Energy

Algae catch sunlight and keep some after they breathe. Plant eaters keep part of that food. Heat leaves the pond. Compare this year with the last full year.

Energy details

Sunlight enters. Algae store chemical energy. Eaters keep some and breathe some away; all living pools release heat. The ledger accumulates this year. Caught is gross algae growth × 10. Algae net production subtracts their respiration; grazer net production subtracts body maintenance from growth. A negative net value means stored chemical energy is being used, not negative energy being passed along. The share is shown when algae net production is positive and grazers are not using stores. Heat is actual respiration × 10. Heat and caught need not balance over a short interval because biomass is stored. Fish food and stocked organisms bring stored chemical energy from outside; the sunlight comparison is not a closed energy budget after stocking or feeding.

Model and honesty

This is a made-up pond that follows a set of rules. Algae build their bodies from carbon dioxide (air) and water using sunlight. Nutrients from muck are a small share of their bodies; this model puts them in the same Water & air pool.

This is one made-up pond, not a forecast for a lake. Matter uses arbitrary units, not grams. Water, air and nutrients are one pool; oxygen is one pond-wide number. Turtle and heron are fixed adults. Rates and thresholds are chosen model values, including the oxygen warning thresholds, not universal fish tolerances. Weekly steps contain seven daily substeps; outgoing flows share a proportional limit when a pool is short. No randomness changes population calculations.

Minnow inheritance and predation are simplified and sped up. Colour is not a full account of natural selection. One illustrative oxygen fit is used for fresh water; at 20 °C it is about 0.5 mg/L above the sea-level reference saturation value. Actual saturation also depends on pressure and salinity. Some fish food may cause winterkill after years. Mild years prevent ice here. Decomposers off is a hypothetical switch, not a pond-management suggestion.

Things people have found

Green water can hide a second change

Real lakes can show chains of food-web changes too (Carpenter, Kitchell and Hodgson, 1985; EPA, Nutrient Pollution). This is a model result.

Removing minnows frees floating algae. Their shade can reduce algae on stems and the snails that graze there. This instrument calculates that cascade; real food webs have more links and vary among ponds.

Food can become an oxygen debt

Real ponds can lose oxygen under ice (Minnesota DNR, Winterkill; USGS, Dissolved Oxygen and Water). This is a model result.

Uneaten food sinks. Decomposers use oxygen while breaking down organic material. Ice restricts exchange with the air. A pond can lose fish when that oxygen runs short.

The background matters to a hunter

Real fish populations can change colour under predation (Endler, 1980; OpenStax Biology 2e 19.3). This is a model result, with inheritance sped up.

A heron takes more visible minnows. In this model, inherited shade shares drift toward the bottom colour over many simulated generations.

Matter returns, energy leaves

Real food webs recycle matter while energy passes through (OpenStax Biology 2e 46.2 and 46.3; Lindeman, 1942). This is a model result.

The matter ledger closes even when populations crash. Energy enters as light and eventually leaves as heat; it does not follow matter around a closed loop.

A small share of sunlight is caught

Real producers catch a small share of incoming sunlight (Lindeman, 1942; OpenStax Biology 2e 46.2). This is a model result, not a universal percentage.

In year 10 of this Starter model, algae caught 1.03 percent of the incoming sunlight. This is a calculated model result, not a measurement of a real pond.

Another share reaches the grazers

Real plant eaters pass on only part of the energy they eat (Lindeman, 1942; OpenStax Biology 2e 46.2). This is a model result.

In that same model year, grazer net production was 9.65 percent of algae net production after respiration. This is a model result. Early in a year either group can use its stored energy; a negative net value is labelled using stored food.

A missing hunter changes the snail trace

Real food webs can respond when predators change (Carpenter, Kitchell and Hodgson, 1985; OpenStax Biology 2e 46.1). This is a model result.

After nymphs were netted at year 1, week 13, the snail peak over the next 104 weeks was about 73. The unchanged copy peaked at about 33 over that interval.

Muck can hold most of the matter

Real decomposers return material from dead organisms to the environment (OpenStax Biology 2e 46.3; USGS, Dissolved Oxygen and Water). The switch and these amounts are model results.

With decomposers off from the start, muck held 92.1 percent of the pond's matter after a year. Switching them on then brought algae above 20 clumps (100 units) after 11 weeks. If algae have gone extinct, decomposers cannot create new algae.

Food can slow the shade drift

Real herons hunt fish by sight (Cornell Lab, Great Blue Heron; Audubon, Great Blue Heron). Real fish colour can respond to predators (Endler, 1980). These feeding comparisons are model results.

After ten winters in this model, unfed minnows on sand averaged shade 1.22. With Some feeding, the average was 2.10, but oxygen fell into winterkill conditions in 6 of those 10 winters. By year 10 fewer than one minnow remained (0.86 in the model). On mud without feeding it was 5.78. These are separate runs from the Starter pond.

Shade and cold reach a hunter

Real blooms can shade underwater plants, and cold water holds more oxygen (EPA, Nutrient Pollution; USGS, Dissolved Oxygen and Water). These canopy and year comparisons are model results.

Starting with 25 percent canopy shade kept all six kinds for ten winters here. At 50 percent shade, nymphs disappeared in the third year. Cold years also lost nymphs in the third year; Mild years kept all six kinds and formed no ice. Each run conserved its 742 units.
