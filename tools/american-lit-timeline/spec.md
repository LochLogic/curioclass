# American Lit Timeline

## One idea
American literature from Plymouth to 1929 laid out as pairs: two works written within a few years of each other on the same theme, pinned to one moment on the timeline with the history lane under them, so you see two people looking at the same years from different places (a revolutionary pamphleteer and an enslaved poet on freedom, Thoreau at Walden and Douglass escaping slavery in the same summer) and say how each one treats the theme.

## Not like
- satire-decoder (ela 11, wave 4): one satirical text read for what it means. Here the unit is a pair, and the frame is time.
- premise-inspector and founding-words-explorer (ela 11 and 9, wave 4): founding documents and responses to them. Here the texts are literature (poems, stories, essays, a novel); founding documents only appear as dated events in the history lane.
- world-lit-atlas (ela 10, this group): place and what readers already knew. Here it is time and what was happening.
- idea-threads (ela 9-12, this group): how ideas develop inside one text. Here two texts are set side by side across one theme.
- art-through-time (arts, live): an art history timeline. Here every stop is a pair of written works with an excerpt and a theme.
- history timelines in social studies: events are only the backdrop here; the student's work is reading two texts against each other.

## Who and how
- Grade 11 ELA, module American Literature: "Foundational works of American literature and their historical moments".
- Standards: RL.11-12.9.
- Driver: students on laptops, alone or in pairs; a teacher can project the timeline and open one pair for discussion. 25 to 45 minutes.
- tool.json: driver "students", minutes "25-45", modes ["Pairs", "Place it"], grades_label "Grade 11". Kit starter with `kit:theme-912` (dark) and an accent override.

## Screens and modes
Kit frame. Header: "American Lit Timeline", small line "1620 to 1929"; Motion, Sound, Read. Mode switch: **Pairs** and **Place it**.

**The timeline** (the whole stage until a pair opens; then a one-third strip at the top, still live for panning and tapping): a horizontal axis from 1620 to 1930 with decade ticks, zoomable (buttons and pinch) from the full span to about 40 years. Three layers:
- **Movement bands** across the top, with soft edges because the dates are approximate (Content); tap a band for a card: name, rough dates, three features to look for, and "Dates are approximate; movements overlap."
- **Work pins** on the middle row: a book or quill icon, a short title, the year. Pairs are drawn as two pins joined by a bracket with the theme word on it (**Freedom**, **Providence**, **Nature**, **Self**, **Being civilized**, **Indifferent world**, **Who speaks**). Solo works are single pins.
- **History lane** along the bottom: dated events as small flags (Content). When a pair is open, events within ten years of it light up.

**Pairs mode.** Focus: the timeline until a pair opens, then the two excerpts. Tap a pair bracket: the **pair reader** opens below the timeline strip, taking about two thirds of the stage, with the two excerpts side by side, each under a small painted picture of its work's world (Art) and with author, title, year, where it first appeared, and a one-line "who was writing, from where" note. Under them, the **lens** asks three questions in turn, each answered by tapping words in either excerpt and choosing a chip:
1. "What does each one say about [theme]?" (chips per text, for example for Freedom: **a right**, **a cause**, **a wish for others**, **something taken from me**).
2. "Where is each writer standing?" (chips: **free**, **enslaved**, **leaving**, **arriving**, **inside the system**, **outside it**, **a woman in 18__**, and others per pair).
3. "Same moment, different view: finish the sentence." A frame: "Both [A] and [B] write about [theme] around [year], but [A] ___, while [B] ___, because ___." with the tapped words dropped in as quotes. **Show ours** reveals the tool's sentence for that pair beside it, never a score.
A pair is "read" once the sentence is saved; the bracket fills in. Solo works open a one-excerpt reader with the first lens question only.

**Place it.** Focus: the timeline, full stage, with the current card above it. A deck of eight unlabeled excerpt cards (from works not in the pairs, plus some from the pairs with names hidden). Drag a card onto the timeline (or focus it and press arrow keys, then Enter). The card lands at the year under the pointer; then the tool shows the real year and how far off the guess was, which band features the student could have used ("Plain style, God's providence: Puritan"), and the work's painted picture (Art), shown only after the reveal so it never hints at the date. A row of eight dots tracks the deck. No score; the end shows "within 25 years: n of 8".

Panel (right): `#hint`, **Pairs read** (n of 8), the open pair's history events as a list, **Who was left out** (a short note for the open pair, Content), and **Clear my work** (asks Yes / No).

## Content
Movement bands (approximate; source Britannica, "American literature"): Puritan and Colonial, 1620 to 1750; Revolutionary and Early National, 1750 to 1820; Romanticism and Transcendentalism, 1820 to 1865; Realism, 1865 to 1900; Naturalism, 1890 to 1915; Modernism, 1910 to 1930 (and beyond); Harlem Renaissance, 1918 to 1930 (as a band inside Modernism). Three features each, for example Puritan: plain style, God's providence in daily events, the community over the self; Transcendentalism: the self, nature as a teacher, distrust of institutions; Naturalism: forces larger than people (nature, heredity), indifferent universe, ordinary people in extremes.

Excerpts are quoted from the editions in Sources; each is under about 60 words. Where the source has an em dash, show two hyphens (--).

Pairs (theme; works; excerpts; who was writing, from where; our sentence; who was left out):
1. **Providence**, 1620s to 1666. William Bradford, Of Plymouth Plantation (written 1630 to 1651), modern English by Harold Paget: "they fell upon their knees and blessed the God of Heaven who had brought them over the vast and furious ocean," Anne Bradstreet, "Upon the Burning of Our House, July 10th, 1666": "It was his own; it was not mine / ffar be it that I should repine." and "My pleasant things in ashes lye, / And them behold no more shall I." Ours: "Both see God's hand in every event, but Bradford reads it in a whole colony's survival, while Bradstreet has to accept it in the loss of her own house." Left out: Bradford's history calls the Native people "wild men"; the Wampanoag, whose land Plymouth was built on, wrote no account the Pilgrims kept.
2. **Freedom**, 1773 to 1776. Phillis Wheatley, "To the Right Honourable William, Earl of Dartmouth" (Poems on Various Subjects, 1773): "Should you, my lord, while you peruse my song, / Wonder from whence my love of Freedom sprung, / Whence flow these wishes for the common good, / By feeling hearts alone best understood, / I, young in life, by seeming cruel fate / Was snatch'd from Afric's fancy'd happy seat:" and "And can I then but pray / Others may never feel tyrannic sway?" Thomas Paine, Common Sense (1776): "Freedom hath been hunted round the globe." and "The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind." Ours: "Both write about freedom on the eve of the Revolution, but Paine speaks for a people claiming it, while Wheatley, enslaved in Boston, speaks from having had it taken." Left out: Wheatley's book came out in London with an attestation signed by Boston men assuring the world that she wrote the poems, and its title page calls her a "Negro servant" to John Wheatley; she was enslaved in his household.
3. **Self**, 1841 to 1850. Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (Essays, 1841): "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist." Nathaniel Hawthorne, The Scarlet Letter (1850): "The scarlet letter was her passport into regions where other women dared not tread." Ours: "Both prize the person who stands apart, but Emerson calls nonconformity a choice and a duty, while Hawthorne shows Hester forced apart by her town and finding freedom there." Left out: Emerson's "man" was the usual word for everyone and was also how he pictured the thinker; the tool says both.
4. **Nature and freedom**, 1845. Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854; he moved to the pond on 4 July 1845): "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived." Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (1845): "You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man." Ours: "In the same summer, Thoreau chooses to step out of society to find out how to live, while Douglass, who had no such choice, writes to prove his own freedom and humanity." Left out: Douglass's book had to carry prefaces by white abolitionists to vouch for it.
5. **A woman's place**, 1892 to 1899. Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wall-paper" (New England Magazine, 1892; Gutenberg titles it "The Yellow Wallpaper"): "Personally, I disagree with their ideas. Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement and change, would do me good." Kate Chopin, The Awakening (1899): "Mrs. Pontellier was beginning to realize her position in the universe as a human being, and to recognize her relations as an individual to the world within and about her." Ours: "Both show a married woman told what is good for her, but Gilman's narrator argues quietly against her doctors, while Chopin's Edna begins to see herself as a person apart from her roles." Left out: women could not vote in national elections until 1920 (history lane).
6. **Being civilized**, 1884 to 1900. Mark Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884): "But I reckon I got to light out for the Territory ahead of the rest, because Aunt Sally she's going to adopt me and sivilize me, and I can't stand it. I been there before." Zitkala-Ša, "The School Days of an Indian Girl" (Atlantic Monthly, 1900; in American Indian Stories, 1921): "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!" and "Among our people, short hair was worn by mourners, and shingled hair by cowards!" Ours: "Both resist being 'civilized', but for Huck it is a joke he can run from to the Territory, while for Zitkala-Ša, at a boarding school, it is the forced cutting of her hair, with nowhere to run." Left out: the "Territory" Huck runs to was Native land; the lane shows the 1879 founding of the Carlisle boarding school and the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act.
7. **Indifferent world**, 1897 to 1910. Stephen Crane, "The Open Boat" (1897): "None of them knew the colour of the sky." and "She did not seem cruel to him then, nor beneficent, nor treacherous, nor wise. But she was indifferent, flatly indifferent." Jack London, "To Build a Fire" (in Lost Face, 1910): "The trouble with him was that he was without imagination." Ours: "Both put a person against a nature that does not care, but Crane's men survive by working together, while London's man dies alone because he cannot imagine the danger."
8. **Who speaks**, 1915 to 1921. T. S. Eliot, "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915; Prufrock and Other Observations, 1917): "I have measured out my life with coffee spoons;" Langston Hughes, "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" (first published 1921; in The Weary Blues, 1926): "I've known rivers: / I've known rivers ancient as the world and older than the flow of human blood in human veins. / My soul has grown deep like the rivers." Ours: "Both are modern poems in one voice, but Eliot's speaker measures a small, doubtful life, while Hughes's speaker claims a history as deep and old as the rivers." Left out: the word "Negro" in the title was the respectful term of its time; the tool says so on the card.

Solos (one excerpt each): Jonathan Edwards, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741): "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you,". Walt Whitman, "I Hear America Singing" (Leaves of Grass, 1860): "I hear America singing, the varied carols I hear,". Emily Dickinson, "Hope is the thing with feathers" (written about 1861, published in Poems, Second Series, 1891): "Hope is the thing with feathers / That perches in the soul,". Paul Laurence Dunbar, "We Wear the Mask" (Lyrics of Lowly Life, 1896): "We wear the mask that grins and lies, / It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,--". F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925): "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

Place it deck: Edwards, Whitman, Dickinson, Dunbar, Gatsby, and three pair excerpts with names hidden (Bradstreet, Crane, Hughes). Years used for the check: 1741, 1860, 1891 (published), 1896, 1925, 1666, 1897, 1921.

History lane (each with its source): 1620 Mayflower arrives, Plymouth founded; 1692 Salem witch trials; 1776 Declaration of Independence; 1787 Constitution signed; 1830 Indian Removal Act; 1845 Douglass's Narrative published (also a pin); 1848 Seneca Falls Convention; 1861 to 1865 Civil War; 1865 Thirteenth Amendment ends slavery; 1869 transcontinental railroad completed; 1879 Carlisle Indian Industrial School founded; 1890 the Census Bureau reports no clear frontier line; 1896 Plessy v. Ferguson; 1917 the United States enters World War I; 1920 Nineteenth Amendment; 1924 Indian Citizenship Act; 1929 stock market crash.

## Interactions in detail
- Timeline: drag or arrow keys to pan, plus and minus to zoom; pins never overlap (they stack upward when close). Every pin, bracket and flag is a button with its full label for screen readers; a **List view** toggle shows the same items as a table sorted by year.
- Pair reader: tapping words in an excerpt selects whole words (up to 12 per text) and places them in the lens frame as a quote. Chips are toggles; more than one may apply. Show ours appears only after the student's sentence has at least 20 characters.
- Place it: dropping snaps to the year under the pointer; the reveal animates the card to its true year in 400 ms (instant with Motion off) and draws the gap as a line labeled "off by n years". The next card can be picked up during the animation, which then finishes at once.
- Store: lens answers and sentences per pair, and the Place it deck state, in localStorage `amlit-work` (try/catch). Clear my work (after Yes) empties it.
- Sizes: at 1366x768 and 1280x720 the timeline shows the full span with bands, pins and lane, and the pair reader fits below without page scroll. At 680x425 the reader opens as a full-height sheet over the timeline. At 760 and 375 wide the timeline turns vertical (years down the left edge, bands as colored edge stripes), the reader stacks the two excerpts, and Place it uses a year picker instead of drag.

## Family engine
lit-timeline (`kit/lit-timeline.js`), described in full here. No other tool is in this family yet; the engine is written so a British or world literature timeline can reuse it.
- **Timeline.Axis(svg, {from, to, ticks})**: horizontal or vertical axis by width, pan and zoom (buttons, keys, pinch), year-to-pixel mapping, a **List view** fallback table.
- **Timeline.Bands(axis, bands)**: movement bands `{name, from, to, fuzzy, features: [3], tint}` with soft edges, labels that stay on screen while panning, and a card on tap.
- **Timeline.Pins(axis, works)**: works `{id, author, title, year, firstIn, excerpt: [lines], note, pairId}` as pins that stack when close; `open(fn)`.
- **Timeline.Pairs(axis, pairs)**: brackets joining two pins `{id, a, b, theme, ours, leftOut}`, with the filled state when read.
- **Timeline.Lane(axis, events)**: dated events `{year, to, label, source}` as flags; `light(range)`.
- **Timeline.Reader(el, works, lens)**: one or two excerpts side by side with word tapping, chip rows and a sentence frame; `Show ours` after a length check.
- **Timeline.Place(axis, deck)**: drag or key-place cards, snap, reveal with the gap and the band features.
- **Timeline.Store(key)**: localStorage wrapper with try/catch and Clear.
Fixed by the engine: axis style, the fuzzy band rule (approximate dates always say so), pin stacking, the vertical layout under 760 px, list view, and that every excerpt carries its source edition. Each tool brings its own works, bands, lens questions and events.
