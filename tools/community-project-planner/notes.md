## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Run a Project** and pick **Riverside Cleanup (start here)**. Point out the team row: six made-up volunteers, each with a strong skill (filled icon), an okay skill (outline icon), hours a week and an energy bar with a word.
2. Week 1: click a volunteer, then a task card (or press 1 to 6, then Q W E A S D). Put people on their strong skill. Pick **Check in** as the lead move, then **Run week 1**.
3. Read the week summary together: points per task, each person's energy change and why, and the check-in notes. "Cares about" is now revealed on every card.
4. Keep going for six weeks. Events come up at the start of weeks 2, 4 and 5. On a projector, let the class vote on each lead move and each event choice before you click.

## The two modes

- **Run a Project:** lead one of three made-up Maple Street projects for six weeks: Riverside Cleanup (start here), Library Lot Garden (some tasks wait on others) and Winter Coat Drive (tight budget). Three resources run at once: volunteer hours, dollars and team energy. Each week you assign people (or let them rest), buy supplies, pick one lead move (Thank the team, Ask for ideas, Check in, Planning meeting, Push harder, No lead move) and run the week. A volunteer who starts a week at 0 energy is Stepping back for two weeks and then returns at 40. The report gives one of four outcomes, from "Finished, and the team wants to do another one" to "Not this time. Try leading it a different way," with what each finished task means and three discussion questions.
- **Plan Your Own:** a one-page planning form for a real or imagined project: name, who it helps, what done looks like, budget, up to 8 tasks (skill, role, weeks, supplies) and "How I'll keep the team going" checkboxes. The timeline and budget update as you type, and gentle notes appear only when true (over budget, a crowded week, an empty last week, nothing checked to keep the team going). **Start from an example** loads one of the three projects. Students use roles (Build lead, Outreach lead), not classmates' names.

## Talk about it

- Which week did the team feel best, and what did you do that week?
- Who did you learn about by checking in, and what changed after?
- If you led it again, what is one thing you would do sooner?
- Push harder gets more done this week. What does it cost, and when does that cost show up?
- Why might the same thank-you land softer the second week in a row?

## Good to know

- Keys in Run a Project: 1 to 6 pick a volunteer, then Q W E A S D a task card, F Fundraise, R Rest; L cycles the lead move; Enter runs the week; Escape cancels a selection (and closes the Start over card). Every control also works with Tab and Enter. Event cards hold focus until a choice is made.
- Run a Project has no text box. Plan Your Own saves nothing until **Save on this device**, which keeps the plan in this browser only; **Clear** asks first, then removes it. The only other thing stored is the Motion setting shared by Curio Class tools. Nothing is sent anywhere. **Print plan** and **Print report** print one light page.
- The projects, volunteers, prices, needs and energy numbers are a made-up model, a simplification for discussion, not a measurement of how real volunteers behave. The tool says "usually" and "can", never "proven". The energy bar measures the made-up team, not the student.
- If a scenario stirs something up for a student, or a student is upset, the school counselor is the right next step.
- In very small embeds (under about 520 px tall) the board drops the banner and the "Working on" menus to fit everything on one screen: pick a volunteer, then a task card. On a phone, the lead moves and Run week stay in a bar at the bottom of the screen.
- Motion off stops the "Fired up" bob and makes every change instant. Read speaks the current hint; Sound off keeps the tool silent.
- What happens if you play it the hard way: everyone on one task with Push harder every week drives the whole team to 0 energy by the end of week 4, so they are Stepping back for weeks 5 and 6 and the project ends partly done. (In the Winter Coat Drive, choosing "Take the week off" in week 4 forces a rest week and delays this.) The careful path (strong skills, Check in in week 1, then alternating Thank the team and Ask for ideas, resting people when they get tired) finishes every core task with the top outcome in all three projects. These were checked with a separate Python model of the rules; no task sizes were changed from the spec.
- Small differences from the written plan: in Riverside Cleanup the week 2 "Re-map the hot spots" choice adds work to the map, so in the example plan Cleanup day runs in weeks 3 and 4 and recycling in week 2 (core work still finishes in week 5). Task cards show three across whenever there is room, so the whole board fits on smaller screens.
- Sources: CASEL Relationship Skills (showing leadership in groups, teamwork and collaborative problem-solving, seeking or offering support) and Responsible Decision-Making (reflecting on one's role in community well-being). Eisner, Grimm, Maynard and Washburn, "The New Volunteer Workforce" (Stanford Social Innovation Review, Winter 2009), names not matching volunteers' skills to their assignments and not recognizing their contributions among the reasons volunteers don't come back; the game's Check in move is our own addition, not from that article. The banners are generated (FLUX.1-schnell); the volunteers, faces and timeline are drawn in code.
