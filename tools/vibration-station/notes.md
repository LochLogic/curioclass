## What it teaches

Children test how shaking makes sound and how sound can make things shake. They use their evidence to build, repair and choose devices that send sound to a friend far away.

## Key skills

- Say a prediction before trying a test.
- Watch what changes when a vibrating object is held still.
- Choose loose materials that help reveal a shake.
- Compare a touching test with a test that leaves a gap.
- Build and repair a cup phone, then invent a distinct beat pattern for each message.

## Support at home

- Hum gently together and rest fingers lightly on your own throat. Ask what you feel.
- Listen for a quiet sound nearby, then farther away. Talk about what changes.
- Make a little family drum code on a cushion: one pattern for lunch, another for play. Take turns sending the messages.

## A 5-minute start

1. Tap a band. Watch its blur and the teal sound arcs.
2. Tap Hold still. Ask what stopped together.
3. Compare the finger tap and mallet on the rice drum.
4. Hold Hum and watch the sprinkles. Sound works without microphone permission.
5. Open Find out. Say a guess before tapping a prediction picture.

## The three modes

**Bench** is free exploration with four stations. A band rings for four seconds, giving time to stop it. Pull a band sideways before releasing for a bigger pluck. Hold Hum with the pointer, Space or Enter. Sound arcs make every outcome visible with Sound off.

**Find out** has five prediction-and-test investigations. Prediction accuracy earns no points. The lab book records what the class thought beside what it saw. The sticker trial and pan-touching trial are useful attempts, but they do not complete the investigation. A filled book page can be opened and tested again. Next keeps the evidence visible until its narration finishes. A material choice highlights at once, then its test controls appear after the planning explanation.

**Reach Sam** builds a cup phone in order, repairs three randomly drawn faults, tests six combinations of place and device, chooses a private way to tell a secret, and designs three distinct drum counts. Wrong counts make Sam perform the message they actually mean, or shrug when there is no such message. Wait one and a half seconds after the last beat to send.

With Sound on, a selected control can keep an outline while a whole explanation finishes. Its scene change follows the explanation; wait for the pan to move before the next Bang.

## Talk about it

- What stopped when we held the band still?
- Why could loose rice help us see a shake, when a sticker could not?
- Could we tell what moved the rice when the pan touched the bowl?
- Where did the shake stop in the broken cup phone?
- Which test helps us choose a way to tell only Sam?
- Why does each drum message need a different count?

## Good to know

Teacher driver, Grade 1. NGSS 1-PS4-1 is taught through predictions, two planned investigations and an evidence book. NGSS 1-PS4-4 is taught by building, testing, repairing and using sound communication devices.

The bench sounds and slow motion are explanatory models. The rice, blur and arcs show that something happens; they do not measure vibration speed. The chart describes this story's yard and park, not a universal distance limit. The cup-phone yard stop is 12 story steps; the park phone runs out before Sam. No distance or decibel measurement is shown to children.

Mic is optional. It samples sound level while on, records nothing, saves nothing and sends nothing. Room sounds can also move the sprinkles. Turn Mic off to release the device. If permission is refused, use Hold Hum. The application saves no lab book, predictions or Sam progress; everything lasts only for this page load. The shared kit remembers the Motion preference on this device.

Narration uses the browser voice. The requested curious-librarian / Aoede bank and all fixed lines are authored in voice/lines.json; no recorded clips were made in this Build-only run.

For a real cup phone, an adult should prepare holes and use cups or cans with smooth rims. Make quiet classroom sounds rather than loud bangs near ears. Physical wrap, rice and hum demonstrations depend on their materials and setup. The tool deliberately makes small effects easier to see.
