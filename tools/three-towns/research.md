# Research packet: three-towns
Concept: Sort homes, jobs and transport into a city, a suburb and a farm town, then trace one breakfast in each to see what comes from nearby and what from far away, and how each town's land shapes its work.
Grade: 2   Standards: D2.Geo.8.K-2, D2.Geo.9.K-2

## Standards in plain words
- D2.Geo.8.K-2: "Compare how people in different types of communities use local and distant environments to meet their daily needs." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Explain how people living in cities, suburbs, and farm towns depend on both nearby goods and items shipped from far away to eat their daily meals.
- D2.Geo.9.K-2: "Describe the connections between the physical environment of a place and the economic activities found there." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Describe how physical land (open fertile fields versus dense paved streets) shapes the kinds of jobs people do, such as farming versus office work.

## Common mistakes
- Thinking cities only have office buildings and no homes: Second graders often picture cities purely as skyscrapers, forgetting that millions of people live in city apartments, row houses, and condos (Source: California History-Social Science Framework Grade 2).
- Believing farm towns are isolated from modern goods: Students frequently think rural residents produce 100 percent of what they use, not realizing rural families buy groceries, clothes, and electronics shipped from around the globe (Source: C3 Framework Geography Indicators D2.Geo.8.K-2).
- Assuming suburban neighborhoods have no jobs: Children often think suburbs are only for sleeping, missing suburban schools, medical offices, parks, and retail centers (Source: U.S. Census Bureau Statistics in Schools).
- Thinking food originates inside the supermarket: Students often fail to trace breakfast items back to soil, cows, orchards, and transport trucks (Source: USDA Ag in the Classroom).

## Sensitivity
- Balanced community respect: Avoid portraying any community type as superior, cleaner, or more desirable than another. Treat urban density, suburban neighborhoods, and rural farmland with equal appreciation and economic dignity.
- Representation of diverse housing: Ensure urban homes include walk-ups and apartments, suburban homes include townhomes, and rural homes reflect various agricultural settings without stereotyping living conditions.

- U.S. Census Bureau Statistics in Schools: "Mapping Community Types". URL: https://www.census.gov/schools/. Core mechanic: Visual comparison of census block maps showing population density and open space across regions.
