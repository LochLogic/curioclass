## A 5-minute start

1. Picture 1 shows a cup on a table. Ask: which one stays still? Tap **The table**.
2. Choose the handshape for the table (**B**, a flat hand), then for the cup (**C**).
3. Tap in the signing space to place the table, then the cup on top of it. Watch it play back, then press **Viewer's view**.

## The big idea

ASL describes where things are by placing them in the space in front of the signer. Signers usually name an object first, then set it up with a **classifier**: a handshape that stands for a kind of thing (a flat surface, a vehicle, a person). The **ground**, the larger object that stays still, usually goes first and stays on the non-dominant hand while the **figure** is placed or moves on the dominant hand. Signers show the scene from their own point of view, so a viewer sees it mirrored.

## The handshapes in this tool

- **B (flat hand):** flat surfaces such as a table, floor, wall, bed or slope.
- **C:** round things you hold, such as a cup or can.
- **1:** one whole person standing or walking.
- **3:** vehicles, such as cars, bikes and boats.
- **Bent V:** an animal, or a person sitting or crouching.
- **Upside-down V:** a person's legs: standing, jumping, falling.

## Talk about it

- Why does the table go first?
- In picture 3, the blue car is on the right. Why does the signer put it on their right too?
- What changes in the viewer's view? How does the viewer know where things really are?
- Watch your teacher or a Deaf signer describe one of these pictures. What did they do the same? What was different?

## Good to know

- This tool practices the **spatial grammar** of classifiers. It does not show how full signs are made, and the hand pictures are drawings, not a signing model. Learn and check signs with your ASL teacher and with Deaf signers.
- Classifier choices can vary between signers and regions (some signers use different handshapes for an animal, for example). Treat the tool's answers as one common way, and compare with Deaf signers' own descriptions.
- The gloss under the picture writes signs in capital letters, with the classifier and its meaning. A gloss is a study aid, not how the signs look.
- **Left-handed** swaps which hand is labeled dominant.
- Checks are generous: they look at above or below, left or right, and start and end points, not exact spots.
