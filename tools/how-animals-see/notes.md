## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Look through** with the **Honeybee** chosen. Move the circle over the yellow flowers. Ask: "What do you notice in the circle that you can't see outside it?" (Dark bullseyes in the middle of the flowers.)
2. Switch to **Dog** and move the circle over the three balls. Which one disappears into the grass? (The red one.)
3. Try **Owl** and **Rattlesnake** on the night meadow. Why does the toad barely show for the snake?
4. Open **Inside the eye** and press **Turn the Sun off**. No light, nothing to see.

## The four modes

- **Look through**: one meadow by day and by night. Inside the circle you see through the animal's eyes; outside, through a person's. **Whole picture** fills the frame; **Bigger circle** makes the lens bigger.
  - Bee: ultraviolet patterns on flowers (nectar guides) and a mosaic from the compound eye.
  - Dog: two kinds of color cones, so red and green look alike.
  - Eagle: about four times sharper, so the rabbit by the fence is clear.
  - Owl: bright night vision with little color.
  - Rattlesnake: heat from warm-blooded animals, sensed by the pits.
- **Inside the eye**: labeled diagrams of a human, owl, honeybee, rattlesnake and dog eye. Tap a part to learn what it does. The human, dog and owl diagrams show light traveling from the Sun to the apple, into the eye, through the lens and onto the retina upside down.
- **Field of view**: seen from above, drag a fox around a person, owl, dog, horse and rabbit. Is the fox seen with both eyes, one eye, or not at all?
- **Who's looking?**: eight rounds. Look at a view and choose whose eyes it is.

## Teaching notes

- 4-PS4-2: we see an object when light reflects off it and enters our eyes. The diagram and the Sun switch make that visible.
- 4-LS1-1 and 4-LS1-2: each animal's eye structure helps it survive: finding flowers, hunting at night, spotting prey from far away, or watching for predators.
- Predators tend to have forward-facing eyes (depth for catching prey); prey animals tend to have eyes on the sides (watching all around).

## How the views are made

These pictures are simplified models of what scientists have measured, not exactly what an animal experiences. We can't see ultraviolet, so the bee view uses the false colors scientists use, with ultraviolet shown as purple. The dog view uses a standard model of two-cone color vision. The eagle view shows the detail an eagle can make out as if a person stood four times closer. Field-of-view angles are typical published values and vary by breed and source.

## Talk about it

- Which animal would be best at finding food at night? In a flower garden?
- Why might a rabbit need eyes on the sides of its head?
- If you could borrow one animal's eyes for a day, which would you pick and why?

- Keys 1 to 4 switch modes. Arrow keys move the circle and the fox.
