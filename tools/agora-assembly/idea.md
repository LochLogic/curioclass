# Agora Assembly (idea card)

- Module: social-studies-6-ancient-greece, grade 6. Codes: D2.His.3.6-8, D2.Civ.8.6-8, D2.Civ.9.6-8
- Format / family: roleplay / none. Fit: good
- One idea: As an Athenian citizen in 483 BCE, debate and vote on a real question (share out the new silver from the mines at Laurion or build warships, as Themistocles urged), then carry the same question to Sparta's council of elders, ephors and shouting assembly, and see who decided, how, and who never got a say.
- How it plays (solo student by default):
  - First screen: the Pnyx hill at dawn, a citizen's token and a speaker's platform.
  - Core loop: hear speakers (illustrated), pick arguments to answer, vote by show of hands; then replay in Sparta, where elders set the question and the assembly shouts yes or no. A side bench holds the people left out: an Athenian woman, an enslaved worker, a metic merchant.
  - The aha or the end state: a compare board of the two processes; then the student asks questions of the people on the side bench and writes why Athens' assembly is remembered as important, and what that memory leaves out.
  - Class mode or extras (optional): the class votes together as the assembly.
- Standards, one line per code:
  - D2.His.3.6-8: questions the student asks of Themistocles and the side bench decide why the assembly is seen as significant.
  - MOVE D2.Civ.8.6-8: constitutional-convention (founding documents of the United States; delegates argue from the Declaration's principles and the finished plan shows how they shape government today). Athens cannot teach the founding documents well.
  - D2.Civ.9.6-8: the side-by-side compare of the Athenian and Spartan ways of deciding.
- Not like: motion-on-the-floor (modern meeting procedure); rome-republic-to-empire (a story of one senator); constitutional-convention (negotiating a written plan).
- Art: people (illustrated) for speakers and the side bench; public domain Greek pottery and objects (Met open access, ids to check at spec).
- Proposed title: keep
- Proposed concept line: As an Athenian citizen in 483 BCE, debate and vote on spending new silver, then take the same question to Sparta's council and assembly; compare who decided and how, and ask the people left out why Athens is remembered.
- Flags: needs a data source (the Laurion silver debate and Spartan institutions, checked)
