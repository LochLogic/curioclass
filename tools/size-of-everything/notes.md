## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Zoom**. You start at about the size of a person: 10<sup>0</sup> meters.
2. Press **÷10 Smaller** a few times. Each press makes the screen ten times narrower. Read the card: the size in scientific notation, the same number written out, and the units.
3. Press **▶ Tour** to fly from a proton to the whole observable universe. It slows down where there is something to see.
4. Ask: "How many powers of ten did we cross?" (About 42.)

## The four modes

- **Zoom**: one slider through 43 powers of ten with 38 real things drawn to scale, side by side. Each thing sits on the same line, so heights and widths compare fairly. Drag the slider, use the mouse wheel, drag across the picture, or press + and -. The scale bar shows a power of ten that fits on screen.
- **Compare**: pick any two things. The card works the division in scientific notation, subtracting the exponents, then makes a quick single-digit estimate and says how many of the smaller thing would line up to match the bigger one. When the small one is too tiny to see, a magnifier shows it.
- **Place it**: ten things, one at a time. Drag the pin to the power of ten you think is closest. Three stars for landing within one power, two for two, one for four. Landmarks (an atom, you, Earth, the Milky Way) sit above the line.
- **Write it**: eight numbers from the catalog. Slide the decimal point to turn standard form into scientific notation, or the other way around. The exponent counts the hops as you go, and padding zeros appear in gray.

## Teaching notes

- Hop left, the exponent goes up; hop right, it goes down. The value never changes, only the way it's written.
- A negative exponent means a small number, not a negative number. Ask students where they see that on the Zoom slider.
- In Compare, the estimate rounds each coefficient to one digit first (8.EE.A.3): 8 × 10<sup>2</sup> ÷ 5 × 10<sup>-3</sup> is about 2 × 10<sup>5</sup>.
- The jump from a proton (10<sup>-15</sup> m) to an atom (10<sup>-10</sup> m) is empty on purpose: atoms are almost all empty space.

## About the sizes

Sizes are typical published values, rounded: living things vary, and the Oort cloud's size is an estimate. Distances like Earth's orbit are measured across. The "you" figure is a typical 13-year-old at 1.6 m.

## Talk about it

- Which comparison surprised you the most?
- Why do scientists use scientific notation instead of writing all the zeros?
- The observable universe is about 10<sup>27</sup> m and a proton about 10<sup>-15</sup> m. How many times bigger is the universe?

## Good to know

- Keys 1 to 4 switch modes. In Zoom, + and - jump one power of ten.
