## What it teaches

Students build a literary thesis and test what it promises about a whole text. They connect quotations to an interpretation and notice passages that challenge it.

## Key skills

- Distinguish a summary from a claim a reader could debate.
- Narrow or widen a claim by checking its reach across a text.
- Explain how particular quoted words support an interpretation.
- Address evidence that challenges a claim.
- Revise an assembled paragraph in your own words.

## Support at home

- After a shared story or film, ask what it suggests about people, then ask which moment supports that idea.
- Offer a different reading and invite your student to explain one detail that helps their case.
- Ask what a claim leaves out. You do not need a right answer to have a useful conversation.

## Five-minute start

Open Build with The Open Window. Choose Vera and A story inside the story, then choose the meaning about stories beating truth. Read a lit passage and ask what the claim now obliges the writer to explain. Try Any for both topic and move to see the scope expand. Nothing scrolls until Show me is pressed.

## The modes

Build uses editorial tags to light passages sharing the selected topic and move. Meaning adds fit checks and contradiction crosses. Scope is Too narrow at zero or one tagged passage, Thin at two, Workable at three through eight, and Too broad at nine or more. These thresholds are workshop heuristics, not rules about every essay. The passage count measures tagged ranges, not lines or word count. Some ranges contain several sentences.

My own words lets students rewrite the sentence, but the light still follows the chips. A drift note checks only whether the chosen topic is mentioned. It cannot tell if the rewritten claim means the same thing. Compare up to five saved theses per text; saving a sixth asks which to replace.

Prove asks students to choose three different lit passages and explain each in a Because box. The pinned picker uses the passage ids in the text margin; hover a choice to preview its quotation, or choose it to open the full card. Its two checks are deliberately modest: one quote word of at least four letters, excluding the tool's stop list, and one meaning keyword. They do not assess the quality or truth of an explanation. Very short word matches and copied quotations receive an additional explanation nudge. Summary chips have no meaning-keyword check.

Draft combines the claim, evidence with passage ids, Because writing, and any response to a contradiction. The checklist is a revision aid, not a grade. Edited paragraphs stay intact when students return to other modes. If evidence later changes, the draft flags the need to reconcile it. Copy has a local selection fallback for restricted school browsers.

## Talk questions

- What changes when Vera becomes Any? Does more evidence necessarily make a claim better?
- How do Frost's lines R9 through R12 complicate the familiar reading about a better road?
- Can a line contradict a claim yet still become useful evidence in a thoughtful paragraph?
- Which quoted word does the most work in your explanation? Why?
- What could another reader fairly object to in your own version of the thesis?

## Good to know

The Open Window includes an invented account of death by drowning and a graveyard story. Both are Vera's fabrications within the fiction. This mild ghost-story fright is retained for grade 10. The final invented story includes the historical phrase "pariah dogs" and a reference to the Ganges; discuss the narrator and Vera's framing rather than treating her account as information about a place or people.

The popular Frost interpretation is deliberately available. Choosing The two roads, Roads described as equal, and the better-road meaning marks R9 through R12 against the claim. The tool shows a tension in the text, rather than declaring one complete interpretation universally correct.

The full texts are from Project Gutenberg #269 and #29345. Whitespace, italic markup and long-dash typography are normalized; words are preserved. Frost's line 18 keeps two hyphens. Saki's pen name and the 1914 edition are checked against Gutenberg; Frost's August 1915 magazine appearance is checked against The Atlantic's original issue.
