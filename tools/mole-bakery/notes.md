## What it teaches
Students convert grams into moles, use a balanced equation to find the limiting reactant, and predict gas and leftovers. They account for every atom and gram through a bake.

## Key skills
- Convert between mass and amount of substance.
- Read coefficients as mole ratios.
- Compare the product each reactant could make.
- Apply percent yield and identify excess reactants.
- Reconcile a mass balance including escaped gases.

## Support at home
- Ask your student why doubling only one ingredient might leave extra material rather than double the result.
- Use pairs of socks or place settings to explain what runs out first when combining things in a fixed ratio.
- Ask how a loaf could lose mass while the total mass of bread and escaped gas stays constant.

## Five-minute start
Press Bake on the ready biscuit mix. Change soda to 6.00 g and bake again. Compare the gas and taste, then explain why extra gas does not guarantee a good bake. Open Follow every gram.

## Modes
Test kitchen gives immediate calculations for five balanced reactions. Mix exact shows its conversion chain and matches the second reactant to the first; it does not choose an order's target amount.

Bakery is a six-day resource game. An order is paid once, on Bake, and stock is spent immediately even if the animation is interrupted. Every order must be baked or declined before End day. Full price needs 95 to 105 percent of the target and clean taste; any other valid bake gets half price. Declining is free. Progress stays only on this device; New week clears it.

## Talk questions
- Why can extra baking soda produce gas and still ruin the taste?
- How many batches can six grams of cream of tartar supply?
- Why do cookies need one third as many moles of ammonium bicarbonate as their target gas?
- Why does a 90 percent yield require making more gas than the dough keeps?

## Good to know
This is a quantitative model, not a real recipe or an invitation to taste reagents. Other food ingredients are outside the model. Vinegar is treated as 5 percent acid by mass using density near 1 g/mL, although acidity labeling is grams per 100 mL. All supplied glucose is fermented; 90 percent represents gas retention in this fictional bakery, not a universal yeast efficiency. Ethanol escapes during baking and is separately listed in the mass ledger.

Taste changes when more than 2 percent of a starting reactant remains. Extra soda is then heated by R5; its pre-oven amount counts as excess, while the final mass ledger contains sodium carbonate and water. Rise for R5 counts carbon dioxide, as specified. R4 counts ammonia, water vapor and carbon dioxide together. The 25 °C gas-volume readout is an idealized comparison for those hot-oven gases, not a claim that all water stays vapor at room temperature.

The model uses unrounded atomic sums internally and displays molar masses to two decimals. Ammonium bicarbonate is explicitly displayed as 79.06 g/mol. Tiny rounded-recipe excesses below the taste threshold remain in the mass ledger.
