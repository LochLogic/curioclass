# Research packet: symbol-spotter
Concept: Find national symbols on everyday things (a coin, a stamp, a flag, a postcard), hear the idea each stands for (freedom, fairness, welcome), then open holidays that honor people who changed the country and ask your own questions.
Grade: k   Standards: D2.Civ.8.K-2, D2.His.3.K-2

## Standards in plain words
- D2.Civ.8.K-2: "Describe democratic principles such as equality, fairness, and respect for legitimate authority and rules." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Explain how everyday national symbols (like the Statue of Liberty, flag, or eagle) represent principles like freedom, fairness, and welcome.
- D2.His.3.K-2: "Generate questions about individuals and groups who have shaped a significant historical change." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Ask curious questions about national changemakers (like Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., Abraham Lincoln, and civil rights leaders) honored by holidays.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing Memorial Day and Veterans Day: Young children often confuse honoring those who died in military service (Memorial Day in May) with celebrating all living and past service members (Veterans Day in November) (Source: USA.gov Federal Holidays Guide).
- Viewing national symbols as merely decorative: Kindergarteners frequently think eagles or stars are just neat pictures rather than symbols standing for abstract principles like unity and freedom (Source: C3 Framework Civics indicators D2.Civ.8.K-2).
- Treating national holidays as simply days without school: Children regularly miss the civic purpose of holidays, associating them primarily with candy, parades, or barbecues rather than changemakers (Source: California History-Social Science Content Standards K.6).
- Believing the Statue of Liberty was manufactured in America: Students often assume the monument was created in New York rather than designed and sculpted in France as a collaborative international gift (Source: National Park Service Statue of Liberty History).

## Sensitivity
- Honest treatment of enslavement and civil rights: When introducing holidays like Juneteenth and MLK Day, present the reality of racial inequality and the end of slavery accurately at an age-appropriate level, emphasizing the courage of leaders who expanded freedom for all.
- Inclusive civic identity: Emphasize that national symbols and democratic ideals belong to all people regardless of race, background, religion, or country of origin.

## Similar tools elsewhere
- Smithsonian Learning Lab: "Symbols of the United States" interactive gallery. URL: https://learninglab.si.edu/. Mechanic: Children click on physical museum artifacts (coins, stamps, portraits) to reveal the values they symbolize.
- PBS KIDS: "Xavier Riddle and the Secret Museum" historical adventure. URL: https://pbskids.org/xavier/. Mechanic: Interactive narrative where kids travel back in time to interview figures like George Washington and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.
- Ben's Guide to the U.S. Government: "Learning About Symbols" interactive portal. URL: https://bensguide.gpo.gov/. Mechanic: Guided clickable book introducing symbols, monuments, and patriotic songs for early readers.

## Gaps
- Exact historical records of the very first child-addressed postcard bearing the American flag: Postcard and stamp records document early patriotic mailings in the late 19th century, but earliest classroom-specific postcards are not indexed in public digital archives.
