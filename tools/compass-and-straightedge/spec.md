# Compass and Straightedge (Curiosity No. 09)

## The real thing
The two tools Euclid allowed himself around 300 BCE: a compass that draws a circle from a centre through a point, and a
straightedge with no marks that draws a line through two points. For two thousand years geometers, architects,
masons and draughtsmen built exact figures with nothing else, and geometry students still do. Here they lie on a
drafting table with an endless sheet of cream paper, a brass compass that really swings, and pencil lines that stay.
Every point you can use is one you made: a given point or a place where two lines or circles cross.

## The first two minutes
- On open: a sheet of cream paper under a soft desk lamp. Two pencil dots, A and B, about a hand apart. A brass compass
  lies closed near the top edge; a steel straightedge lies along the bottom. One pencilled line in the margin: "Drag
  from A to B."
- First action, nothing read: press on A and drag to B. The compass lifts, its needle leg sets into A, the legs open
  to B with a faint metallic tick, and when you let go the pencil leg swings a full circle in about 0.8 s with a soft
  graphite scratch, leaving a grey line. Drag from B to A: a second circle. Where they cross, two small dots appear.
- First reaction: drag from the top crossing to A, then to B. The straightedge slides over and a darker pencil line is
  drawn each time. A perfect equilateral triangle sits on the page; the readouts say 1.0000, 1.0000, 1.0000 and
  60.00 degrees, and a pencilled margin note appears: "Euclid, Book I, Proposition 1." Nothing else happens.

## Things to do
1. Make the equilateral triangle from two circles, then make a second one on the other side.
2. Bisect a line: two circles of the same size from its ends, and the line through their two crossings.
3. Bisect an angle and measure both halves.
4. Drop a perpendicular from a point to a line, then raise one from a point on it.
5. Walk the compass around a circle at its own radius and find it closes in exactly six steps: a hexagon.
6. Inscribe a square in a circle, then an octagon by bisecting its sides' arcs.
7. Build a regular pentagon with Euclid's method from the Book, and measure its diagonal against its side.
8. Copy an angle onto another line, then draw a parallel through a point.
9. Switch on Euclid's rule, where the compass closes when lifted, and copy a length anyway (Book I, Proposition 2).
10. Try to cut any angle into three equal parts; read in the Manual why no one ever can.
11. Ink the lines you want to keep and leave the construction in light pencil, as draughtsmen do.
12. Read your construction back as the written log, in Euclid's style, then print or save the sheet.
13. Zoom far out and keep building on the endless sheet.

## Controls
- Compass: drag from a centre point to a point on the circle; release draws it. Straightedge: drag from point to
  point; a segment, or a full line with the extend toggle (X). Keys: C or S, then arrows move a point cursor between
  points, Enter sets the first point, Enter again the second.
- Only made points can be used: given points, crossings (they appear as dots, 44 px hit area) and points dropped with
  the point tool (O), which places a free point anywhere, marked as free in the log.
- Euclid's rule switch (a small brass toggle on the compass case): off is a modern compass that keeps its opening;
  on, the compass closes when lifted (Euclid's collapsing compass).
- Pencil or ink: a two-position switch; ink draws a dark line over a chosen pencil line (I on a selected line).
- Erase (E, or Delete on the selected item); undo and redo through the whole log (Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Y). Paper: drag
  empty paper to pan, wheel or pinch to zoom (+, -, 0 to fit everything).
- Measure: tap any segment for its length, two lines for their angle, three points for an angle (M toggles).
- The Book (a cloth-bound volume on the table edge): 15 pages, 14 constructions from Euclid and one on trisecting.
  Each has Heath's wording, a small finished figure, and "show the next step", which draws one faint ghost step per
  press. Nothing is checked, ticked or counted.
- Paper menu: new sheet with A and B, blank sheet, a given angle, a given circle; 4 save slots on this device; print.
- Power panel, a lamp switch plate at top right: Motion, Sound, Read toggles.

## Readouts
- On small paper tags, monospace: a selected segment's length in units of AB (AB = 1), 4 decimals; an angle in
  degrees, 2 decimals, at its vertex; a selected circle's radius in AB units, 4 decimals, on its rim.
- The log, a lined index card at the right: each move as a numbered sentence ("3. Circle with centre C through A.";
  free points marked "(free)"), with plain counts of circles, lines and points at its foot, never compared or rated.

## The Manual
Lower-left drawer: the two tools and the made-points rule; Euclid's postulates 1 to 3 in Heath's words; reading the
log; honesty notes; sources. "Things people have found":
- Two circles, each centred on one end of a line and passing through the other, cross at the corner of an
  equilateral triangle (Euclid I.1, Heath 1908).
- A compass set to a circle's radius steps around it exactly six times, which is why a hexagon's side equals its
  radius (Euclid IV.15 and its porism, Heath 1908).
- Euclid's compass closes when lifted, but his Proposition I.2 shows how to copy a length anyway, so a compass that
  holds its opening adds no new power (Heath 1908, note on I.2).
- Halving any angle is easy (I.9), but no compass and straightedge construction can cut every angle into three equal
  parts; Pierre Wantzel proved it in 1837 (MacTutor, "Pierre Wantzel").
- At 18, in 1796, Carl Friedrich Gauss found how to construct a regular 17-sided figure; a regular 7-sided figure
  cannot be built with these tools (MacTutor, "Carl Friedrich Gauss"; Gauss-Wantzel theorem).
- Making a square with exactly the area of a given circle is impossible with these tools; Ferdinand von Lindemann
  settled it in 1882 by proving pi transcendental (MacTutor, "Ferdinand von Lindemann").
- A compass alone, with no straightedge, can find every point that both tools together can: Georg Mohr in 1672 and
  Lorenzo Mascheroni in 1797 (MacTutor, "Lorenzo Mascheroni").
- In a regular pentagon a diagonal divided by a side is the golden ratio, 1.6180 (Euclid IV.11 builds the pentagon;
  XIII.8 shows the diagonals cut in extreme and mean ratio, Heath 1908).

## Honesty notes
In the Manual: crossings are computed to about 15 digits, far finer than any pencil, so readouts are more exact than a
drawing could be; measuring is the tool's help, not Euclid's method (he never measured); free points stand in for
Euclid's points "taken at random" (as in I.9); the Book's ghost steps follow Heath's figures in our own drawing.
