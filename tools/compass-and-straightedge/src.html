    Curio Class Curiosities · No. 09Compass and Straightedge

    Manual

    A compass makes a circle. An unmarked straightedge joins two points. The paper keeps what you make.

    At the table

    Drag from A to B with the compass. Drag from B to A. Choose Edge and join either crossing to A and B. The two circles meet at the third vertex of an equilateral triangle. Draw AB too, or measure the three points.

    Only made points can anchor an instrument: given points, calculated crossings, or your free points. Choose Point to place a free point. Drag empty paper to pan; wheel or pinch to zoom. Fit brings the drawing back.

    Euclid’s rule closes the compass after each circle. With it off, Keep opening carries a radius to another made centre. Extend draws an infinite line. Ink darkens a selected object; Erase removes it and anything depending on it.

    Keys and measurements

    C compass, S straightedge, O free point, E eraser, M measure, I ink, X extend. Arrows cycle through made points; Enter chooses one. Two choices draw. In Measure, choose three points for the angle at the middle point, or two lines for their smaller angle.

    Tab to the paper first. For a keyboard free point, press O, move the crosshair with arrows (Shift for a larger move), then Enter. [ and ] select drawn objects for ink, erasure or line-angle measurement. Escape cancels. Delete erases the selected object. Ctrl+Z undoes; Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes. + and − zoom; 0 fits. On the paper, Shift+arrows pan in other tools. Measure tags report lengths in AB units and angles in degrees.

    The record, the Book, and saving

    The log records moves in order, including free points, ink and erasures. Undo and redo restore the whole drawing. The Book has fifteen independent pages. Its faint diagrams are references, never drawing anchors. Press Show next step for another ghost stroke. Choose any page, any time. Clear ghosts removes the reference.

    Paper offers four save slots, kept only on this device. Nothing leaves the device. Browser storage can be cleared or blocked. Print makes a clean sheet with the construction record. New paper starts a fresh sheet; undo can bring the old sheet back.

    Euclid’s first three postulates

    “To draw a straight line from any point to any point.”

    “To produce a finite straight line continuously in a straight line.”

    “To describe a circle with any centre and distance.”

    Things people have found

    Equilateral triangle. Two circles, each centred at one end of AB and passing through the other, give an equilateral triangle (I.1).

    Hexagon. A circle’s own radius walks round it six times (IV.15).

    Collapsing compass. A collapsing compass can copy a length by I.2.

    Pentagon. A regular pentagon’s diagonal divided by its side is 1.6180 (IV.11; XIII.8).

    Trisecting. Some angles trisect, but no compass and straightedge procedure trisects every angle. Pierre Wantzel proved the general impossibility in 1837.

    Seventeen sides. Gauss found the regular 17-gon construction in 1796, aged 18; a regular 7-gon cannot be constructed.

    Squaring the circle. Lindemann proved pi transcendental in 1882, settling its impossibility with these instruments.

    Compass alone. Mohr (1672) and Mascheroni (1797) showed that a compass alone can locate every point constructible with both instruments.

    Honesty notes

    This is floating-point geometry, not symbolic proof. Double precision has about 15 significant decimal digits; ill-conditioned or very large constructions can lose accuracy. Crossings within 10⁻⁷ times the largest stroke size (at least one sheet unit) are merged; given and free points use 10⁻⁹ sheet units. Tangencies merge the two roots when their half-separation is within 10⁻⁷ times the larger radius. Pencil thickness and 44-pixel selection areas are visual aids.

    AB is the original unit. Blank paper keeps that unit, labelled u; its new point names do not redefine it. Measuring is our aid, not Euclid’s construction method. Free points stand for points taken at random. Book diagrams are our own analytic drawings of the constructions, with compound steps identified in words; they are not facsimiles of Heath’s plates. No marks on the straightedge.
