## What it teaches
Students connect changes in the appearance of art with inventions, trade, and artistic ideas. They distinguish a historical connection from a comparison across places or centuries.

## Key skills
- Read dates and travel along a timeline with a changing scale.
- Look at an authentic artwork for evidence of a new visual approach.
- Connect an event with a later work and explain the connection.
- Tell the difference between influence and a shared idea.
- Reconsider an explanation when the dates rule it out.

## Support at home
- Look at an old household object together. Ask what materials or inventions made it possible.
- Compare two packages, buildings, or pictures. Notice a shared shape without assuming that one copied the other.
- Tell a family story in time order. Ask which things happened before a change and which came afterward.

## Five-minute start
1. Open The Rhinoceros, dated 1515, in the Timeline.
2. Look closely at the authentic print. Read its Around it line, then tap its Invention thread.
3. Follow the dashed connection to the printing press. Explain what printing changed about how pictures traveled.
4. Open The Birth of the Virgin. Compare its deep street with the Boscoreale room and Summer Mountains.
5. Enter What Changed? Select a work, then Place, or drag the event onto the work. Use the picture as well as the date.

## Modes
**Timeline:** Drag the walnut band sideways, scroll, use the Era buttons, or jump with the mini-map. Zoom out to clusters, then tap a cluster to reopen that part of the gallery. Spotlight visits a work not yet opened. The four thread types have different icons and strokes. All threads shows the complete network faintly. A check marks a work opened in this session.

**What Changed?:** Eleven shuffled event cards connect to later artworks. Photography, Cezanne's ideas, and abstraction each accept either of two works and draw both connections. Japan's trade opening accepts The Seasons, not the earlier Mishima print. After two misses, Show me highlights three candidate works. Next event becomes available after placing the current card. The eleventh placement opens an illustrated record of the student's own connections. Tap a record entry to revisit its thread. There is no timer or competitive score.

## Talk questions
- What changed about the way art could be made, seen, or carried?
- Which evidence comes from the picture, and which comes from its historical context?
- Why can a date rule out an explanation without proving another explanation?
- How are the Compare threads different from Learned from threads?
- How does showing a Chinese handscroll from a traveling viewpoint differ from a single vanishing point?

## Good to know
- Grades 6-8, visual arts. NCAS VA:Re7 is practiced by inspecting the works and choosing visual evidence. VA:Cn11 is practiced by connecting the works to cultural, social, and technological context.
- The scale changes at 1000 CE. The earlier 2,500 years take 30 percent of the band; the following 925 years take 70 percent. The double slash marks the change. In crowded overview positions, zoom in to restore omitted tick labels. The ruler uses the spec's representative numeric years; wall labels retain approximate date ranges.
- Compare means a shared idea, never a claim of direct influence. The lost-wax technique card has no date and is never dealt in What Changed?. Its generated model is a vessel, avoiding a generated human figure.
- The history of art involves many causes. These selected threads are brief teaching explanations, not exhaustive accounts. The camera-to-Monet wording, the press-to-woodcut connection, and the abstraction labels follow the supplied spec and should be discussed as simplified context.
- Cold review identified three prescribed wording conflicts: the Met now dates Hatshepsut about 1473 to 1458 BCE; the mihrab combines geometry with sacred writing and a prayer function; Cannons still contains partly hidden objects. The tool retains the spec's text pending a specification correction. Do not use those descriptions as exact definitions or updated museum dates.
- On an earlier source, an outgoing Learned from thread is labeled Influenced. On its later recipient, the button reads Learned from. Compare keeps its separate meaning in both directions.
- The plaque's work view always includes the calm note about the British looting of Benin in 1897 and requests for return. This history is not scored or timed. Read includes that note. Consult the [Met object page and provenance](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/312302), rather than assuming the provenance of every Benin object is identical.
- The Smithsonian's [Benin Bronzes: Ambassadors of the Oba](https://africa.si.edu/exhibitions/benin-bronzes-ambassadors-oba) documents the October 11, 2022 return to Nigeria. Checked November 1, 2026. The museum's indexed 2022 news release also describes the ownership transfer; its direct news route returned 404. This supports the statement that some objects have been returned; it does not claim that the illustrated Met plaque itself was returned.
- Supplied authentic museum works can contain people. No people, faces, or body parts were generated. All 12 event pictures were generated with OpenAI gpt-image, edited for Curio Class.
- Everything runs offline. Opened works, placed events, and the student's record last only for the page session. The kit remembers only the shared Motion setting on this device. Nothing is collected or sent.
- Mouse: drag or scroll the band, use Ctrl with the wheel to zoom, and drag a dealt event onto a work.
- Keyboard: panel controls precede the timeline. Left and Right move between cards, Enter opens one. In What Changed?, Enter on a work places the event directly. The mini-map also supports Left, Right, Home, and End. Escape closes a work view or restores the whole picture after zooming.
- Sound controls narration and tones. Read speaks the current context and hint. Motion removes animation without changing the activity. No voice recordings were made; speech uses the kit's browser fallback.
