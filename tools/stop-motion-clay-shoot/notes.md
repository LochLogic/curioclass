## What it teaches
Students plan a four-beat clay story, capture small changes, and diagnose what moved between photographs. They can explain how a steady camera, consistent light, clear frame and small poses affect a stop-motion film.

## Key skills
- Compare neighboring photographs using onion skin and a difference view.
- Separate a camera shift from a lighting change or a hand in the shot.
- Place an in-between pose to split a large move into smaller moves.
- Plan a beginning, event, action and ending, then shoot and revise a film.
- Make presentation choices with a title, end card and original music.

## Support at home
- Make a tiny story using a pebble and a leaf. Name four things that happen, in order.
- Put an object on a table and move it a little at a time. Notice which things around it should stay still.
- Look at shadows in one room at two different times. Talk about why a cloud might change a film's light.

## Five-minute start
Open Rough cut and tap Play. Use the frame arrows to compare photographs. Turn on Jump Finder, find an unusual change, and tap Something jumped here. Choose its cause, then use the matching repair.

## Modes
Rough cut contains a made-up after-school club film. Four repairs teach camera stability, lighting consistency, keeping hands clear, and small movements. For the large move, students place the missing halfway pose themselves.

Studio has Plan, Set up, Shoot and Check. Pick one of three four-beat stories, reorder or swap beats, choose filming conditions and a set, then drag Moss or Wren and select poses. Snap captures the current set, including any simulated mistakes. Check identifies problems from the saved photographs. Fix them or reshoot a frame.

Premiere screens the student's own frames with a title board, one of three original synthesized loops (or silence), and an end card. Work saves only in this browser on this device. No webcam is used and nothing is uploaded.

## Talk questions
- Which parts should change between two pictures? Which should stay still?
- What did the Jump Finder show that the normal picture hid?
- How did your studio choices affect the next photographs?
- Does your four-beat film show what you planned? What would you shoot again?

## Good to know
Students can work alone or in pairs, swapping the posing and camera roles. Keyboard: Tab to a figure; arrows move one floor unit, Shift plus arrows moves five. Number keys choose a pose. Space snaps when a figure or the background has keyboard focus. Comma and period step photographs. The frame strip scrolls horizontally. Focus a placed prop's button and use arrows to move it, with Shift for five units.

This tool follows the spec's explicit timing model: on twos displays six captured photographs per second; on ones displays twelve. Therefore 24 captured frames last 4 seconds on twos and 2 seconds on ones. In common production terminology, shooting on twos at 24 playback frames per second means 12 unique photographs per second. The tool's wording and timing are a simplified model, and that distinction should be discussed if moving into real production.

The set is 100 floor units wide. The checks use teaching thresholds: camera movement greater than 1 unit, brightness change greater than 8 percent, any hand layer, and figure travel greater than half its length (9 units for Moss, 6 for Wren). These are model rules, not published professional limits. A remote avoids the simulated hand; otherwise wait at least 1.5 seconds after posing. Untaped bumps have a seeded 1-in-8 chance per snap. A window's simulated cloud changes brightness by up to 12 percent per snap.

The technical-check message requires at least two photographs, an intentional figure or prop change, and no configured camera, light, hand or figure-spacing problems. Props are visible in Jump Finder, but their travel is not automatically graded; watch them during Play. Empty and entirely still sequences receive a next-step hint instead. This does not grade storytelling or student mastery. There are no points, trophies or saved best scores.
