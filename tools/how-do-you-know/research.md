# Research packet: how-do-you-know

Concept: Help a talking backpack answer "What do pandas eat?" by choosing between a zoo fact page and a toy poster, calling each line fact or opinion, and asking "How do you know?" until you know which to trust.
Grade: 2
Standards: D3.1.K-2, D3.2.K-2, D4.4.K-2

## Standards in plain words

- D3.1.K-2 (Gather relevant information from one or two sources while using the origin and structure to guide the selection): Second graders compare where two documents came from (a zoo science institute versus a toy store advertisement) to choose the best source for answering a factual question.
- D3.2.K-2 (Evaluate a source by distinguishing between fact and opinion): Second graders test statements to decide if a line is a verifiable scientific fact (proven by evidence) or a personal opinion (a feeling or preference).
- D4.4.K-2 (Ask and answer questions about arguments): Second graders repeatedly ask "How do you know?" to evaluate evidence and defend why one source is reliable while another is not.

## Real data

Contrasting Source Comparison:

| Feature | Source A: National Zoo Wildlife Fact Sheet | Source B: Toy Town Cuddle Panda Poster |
|---|---|---|
| Origin / Creator | Wildlife biologists and zookeepers at the National Zoo | Marketing department at CuddlePaws Toy Company |
| Intended Purpose | Educate the public with scientific facts from animal observation | Persuade families to purchase plush stuffed toys |
| Structure | Informational headings, measurements, wildlife photographs | Slogans, bright cartoon illustrations, price discount tags |
| Primary Diet Statement | "Giant pandas eat 70 to 100 pounds of fresh bamboo every day." | "Pandas love to eat strawberry cupcakes and chocolate ice cream at tea parties!" |
| Statement Type | Verifiable Fact (proven by daily feeding measurements and biology) | Fiction / Imaginary Story (contradicted by animal science) |
| Credibility Rating | High (trusted scientific institution studying living animals) | Low for animal science (commercial advertisement for a toy) |

Line-by-Line Detective Evaluation:

| Source | Statement | Fact or Opinion? | Test: "How do you know?" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zoo Fact Sheet | "Pandas eat between 70 and 100 pounds of bamboo daily." | Fact | Zookeepers weigh the bamboo before and after feeding each day. |
| Zoo Fact Sheet | "A special wrist bone acts like a thumb to grip bamboo stems." | Fact | X-rays and anatomical examinations confirm the enlarged wrist bone. |
| Zoo Fact Sheet | "Giant pandas are the most fascinating animals to observe." | Opinion | This is a feeling or preference; another person might prefer otters. |
| Toy Poster | "Pandas prefer sweet chocolate cookies over plain plants." | Fiction / Opinion | Biologists test panda digestion and find they cannot eat chocolate. |
| Toy Poster | "This plush toy bear is 12 inches tall and machine washable." | Fact | Measurable physical dimensions and fabric care testing. |
| Toy Poster | "Every kid will be much happier with a cuddly toy panda." | Opinion | Emotional sales claim that cannot be measured or proven as a universal rule. |

Inquiry Detective Flow in Curio Class:
1. Talking Backpack poses the mystery: "What do real giant pandas eat?"
2. Inspect the sources: Student examines the headers, authors, and logos of both documents.
3. Apply the "How do you know?" magnifying glass: Each line is tagged as either "Fact" (provable with evidence) or "Opinion" (feeling or advertisement).
4. Evidence verdict: Backpack asks, "Why should we trust the zoo sheet rather than the toy poster?" Student selects evidence: "Because zookeepers study live animals and measure real bamboo, while the poster wants to sell toys."

## Common mistakes

1. Assuming that because a statement is printed on paper or looks colorful, it must be true.
2. Confusing an opinion you agree with (e.g., "Pandas are cute!") with a scientific fact.
3. Believing all statements in an advertisement are lies; ads often mix real product facts (size, material) with exaggerated opinions and fictional stories.
4. Forgetting to ask who wrote the document and what goal they had in creating it.
5. Relying on intuition rather than concrete evidence when asked "How do you know?".

## Sensitivity

- Keep media literacy lessons empowering, teaching curiosity and discernment rather than cynicism.
- Clarify that fictional toy advertisements are fun for imaginative play, but different from scientific research tools.
- Provide simple audio support so non-fluent second grade readers evaluate reasoning without getting stuck on technical vocabulary.
