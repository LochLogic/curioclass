arc: teach-then-practice
minutes: 45
focus: 6.NS.B
goal: I can write a number as a product of primes and use prime factorizations to find the GCF and LCM of two numbers.
materials: The tool on the projector, and on student devices if you have them. Whiteboards.
grouping: Whole class, then pairs on devices (or teams taking turns at the projector).
---
## Before class

- Open **Smash** on the projector.
- Write the divisibility rules for 2, 3 and 5 on the board.
- Have one real-world problem ready for Apply (below).

## Hook (5 min)

1. "Every number has a secret recipe made only of primes, and each number has exactly one." Write 84 on the board.
2. "Can anyone break 84 into smaller pieces?" Take ideas like 4 × 21. "Can we keep breaking?"

## Teach (10 min)

1. In **Smash**, ask: "Which prime goes into 84?" Strike with the **2** prime hammer. A prime coin breaks off and the **factor tree** grows.
2. Strike the wrong hammer on purpose. It clangs and shows why: 84 ÷ 5 = 16.8, not a whole number.
3. Finish the tree: 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 = 2² × 3 × 7.
4. Try 60, starting with 5 this time. "Does it matter which prime we strike first?" (No. The coins at the end are the same.)
5. Switch to **GCF & LCM** and model one pair. Smash both numbers, then tap a coin from the first and the **same prime** from the second. Press **Melt it!** The **GCF** is the shared primes multiplied. The **LCM** is the shared primes once, times every leftover coin.

## Practice (15 min)

1. Pairs do three more **GCF & LCM** pairs. Before each **Melt it!**, they predict the GCF on a whiteboard.
2. Point out the check that appears every time: GCF × LCM = the two numbers multiplied. "Why would that always work?"
3. If the tool warns that a shared prime is left unpaired, use **Undo last pair** and look again.
4. Finish with one round of **Forge Rush**: 60 seconds, and a wrong hammer costs 2 seconds. "Who used a divisibility rule to go faster?"

## Apply (10 min)

1. Pose a GCF problem: "You have 36 pencils and 48 erasers and want identical kits with nothing left over. What's the most kits you can make?" (12, the GCF.)
2. Pose an LCM problem: "Hot dogs come in packs of 8 and buns in packs of 6. What's the fewest of each that come out even?" (24, the LCM.)
3. Pairs explain which tool they used and why: GCF for splitting into equal groups, LCM for things lining up again.

## Exit check (5 min)

On a card: write 90 as a product of primes (2 × 3² × 5), then find the GCF and LCM of 12 and 18 (6 and 36). Cards that mix up GCF and LCM show who needs the crucible again.

## Support and stretch

- **Support:** use the 💡 **Hint** button, and keep the divisibility rules on the board.
- **Support:** stay in **Smash** with numbers under 100 before moving to GCF and LCM.
- **Stretch:** "Two numbers share no primes at all. What is their GCF? Their LCM?" (1, and their product.)
- **Stretch:** beat the class **Forge Rush** record using only mental divisibility checks.

## Short on time

- Teach one factor tree in **Smash** (5 min).
- One **GCF & LCM** pair together (8 min), then the exit card (5 min).
