## What it teaches

Children copy and describe movements in seven dances, explain a favorite part, and connect dances to people, places and occasions. This is an introduction to noticing movement, not a lesson in performing a complete traditional dance.

## Key skills

- Watch a movement closely and copy its shape and timing.
- Describe what you see with dance words such as high, low, smooth and sharp.
- Choose a favorite part and explain why you like it.
- Connect a dance with its community, place and occasion.

## Support at home

- Share a dance your family enjoys. Tell your child who taught it to you and when you dance it.
- Take turns making one gentle movement and copying it like a mirror. Try describing it as big, small, high or low.
- Ask which part of a dance your child likes and why. There is no single right favorite.

## Five-minute start

Open one table and tap Play. Watch both moves, then tap Copy and one move. Leave comfortable space around each dancer, or copy with seated arm movements. Tap Words and choose a word the class notices. Open Where from, then vote for a Favorite part and explain why. Return to the gym to light that family's string.

## The gym and dance views

The teacher drives the screen. Tables can be visited in any order and revisited. Two or three tables in a day give the class time to copy and talk; the full activity can take 15 to 25 minutes.

Watch plays an original 16-count musical sketch with two eight-count moves. Slow uses 60 percent of the original tempo. Copy plays one eight-count move at that slower tempo, mirrored to the class. Count me in adds four counts before the selected move. The large count shows when to begin. Space plays or stops, Escape returns to the gym, and Tab plus Enter reaches every control. Arrow keys move among the four tabs.

Words responds to the move actually shown. Only after a complete Watch loop do both moves count. A short or interrupted Watch run does not unlock words from an unseen move. Yes and look-again responses are invitations to observe and discuss, not a score. Where from shows the geographic home and the two spoken sentences. Favorite part records one class choice per dance for this page visit and asks children to explain it to a friend.

Each first return lights a string. All seven brighten the gym. Lights count tables visited, not movement skill or correct answers. Lights count tables visited, not movement skill or correct answers. Revisited tables have a check mark as well as a lit color. Nothing about the class's visits or votes is saved. The shared Motion button remembers only its setting on this device.

## Talk questions

- Which part of the move looked high or low? Show us the part you noticed.
- How do the feet and arms move differently in these two dances?
- Which part do you like best? What makes you say that?
- Who dances this dance, and when? Is there a dance your family shares?

## Good to know

The standards are NCAS Dance DA:Re8, DA:Re9 and DA:Cn11 for K-2. Children describe actual displayed moves, make and explain a favorite choice, and open each community card themselves. The tool is also usable alone, with spoken next actions and Read.

These are simplified movement studies. Costume colors and objects are examples, not a claim that everyone in a community dresses or dances alike. Invite a child's connection without asking them to represent a whole people. The Korean fan dance often makes group formations; this tool isolates two movements rather than reproducing a group performance. The lion is one painted costume with four performers' legs visible. The seven painted dancers include different ages and body shapes. Two key poses per move make its main contrast clear; these are movement studies, not a complete choreographic lesson.

The music is original synthesis suggesting each ensemble's rhythm and timbre. It is not a recording or an authentic traditional tune. Sound off silences music and speech; Read still speaks by request. Motion off freezes each count's pose without shortening the clock. Recorded clear-teacher narration is not included in this build, so fixed lines use browser speech.

## Checked cultural context

- Southern lion dance: [Singapore Chinese Cultural Centre, Culturepaedia](https://culturepaedia.singaporeccc.org.sg/social-practices/southern-lion-dance/) identifies the Guangdong tradition, festive settings and wishes for good luck. [National Museums Scotland, traditions and celebrations teacher notes](https://files.nms.ac.uk/production/Legacy/Images/National%20and%20International/ea-traditions-and-celebrations-teachersnotes-hlf.pdf?dm=1712512715) describes two performers controlling the front and rear of one costume. This card concerns the southern style, not every lion-dance tradition.
- Jarabe Tapatio: [Tamaulipas Secretariat of Education](https://comunidaddelsaber.tamaulipas.gob.mx/contenido_mimurales/14) identifies Jalisco and festive performances. [Mexico's Secretariat of Culture, Ballet Folclorico del Edomex lesson](https://contigoenladistancia.cultura.gob.mx/detalle/clase-de-danza-jarabe-tapatio-ballet-folclorico-del-edomex) provides a movement reference. The skirt sweep is one movement study, not the whole partner dance.
- Irish step dance: [Irish Dance Teachers Association of North America](https://idtana.org/types-of-irish-dancing/) distinguishes solo, ceili/team and show dancing and supports the still arms and active feet of solo step dance. [Irish Traditional Music Archive, Tubridy dance collection](https://www.itma.ie/playlists/tubridy-dance/) also documents an older, more relaxed style. Still arms are not a rule for every form of Irish dance.
- Bhangra: [BBC School Radio, Dance Workshop, programme 3](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/schoolradio/pdfs/danceworkshop_autumn_2009.pdf) supports Punjab, harvest celebrations, weddings and parties, shoulder shrugs and hops. Punjab spans India and Pakistan. Modern staged and diaspora forms continue to change; the harvest summary is not a complete origin history.
- Buchaechum: [Korea.net and the Korean Cultural Centre's National Gugak Center workshop](https://www.korea.net/Events/Overseas/view?articleId=26726) supports hanbok, large fans, flowers, waves and stage performance. [Korea.net's learning account](https://www.korea.net/NewsFocus/HonoraryReporters/view?articleId=202577) names Kim Baek-bong and the first 1954 performance. This is a modern staged work with older influences, not an anonymous ancient dance. The single dancer here demonstrates shapes that are often made together in a group.
- Second line: [National Park Service, Jazz Origins in New Orleans](https://www.nps.gov/jazz/learn/historyculture/history_early.htm) explains African American mutual aid and benevolent societies, brass bands and the people dancing behind them. The origin card now names Black communities rather than presenting the tradition as anonymous street dancing.
- Kpanlogo: [Ghana Ministry of Education / NaCCA, Physical Education and Health, p. 40](https://nacca.gov.gh/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Physical-Education-and-Health-Core.pdf) identifies the Ga people and Greater Accra, communal drumming and dancing, arm swings, torso movement and footwork. The [University of Ghana, Ghana Dance Ensemble brochure](https://ias.ug.edu.gh/sites/ias/files/styles/thumbnail/GDE-Brochure_reduced.pdf) identifies its 1960s development. It is not a timeless stand-in for all Ghanaian or African dance.

The family sharing is the tool's fictional school-night setting. Costumes, poses and synthesized musical sketches are illustrative choices, not documentary recordings or a claim of exact traditional technique. Map dots are city-area anchors for the named places, not cultural or political boundaries. Visits and favorite choices stay only in memory for the current page visit; they never leave the device. The shared Motion preference stays on this device.


## Grade-specific depth

For kindergarten, name and demonstrate a noticed movement. For grade 1, explain an idea the movement suggests using a dance word: the lion's low-to-high head can suggest waking up, and the smooth fan sweep can suggest a wave. For grade 2, use Where from to connect that movement and idea to the people, setting or occasion. The Favorite part reflection now asks what the moves show; students can answer aloud alone or to a partner. If the favorite is the music or a prop, they can still discuss the moves they watched. Ask for a reason, not only a tap.

These distinctions follow the [NCAS Dance at a Glance, responding and connecting](https://www.nationalartsstandards.org/sites/default/files/2021-11/Dance%20at%20a%20Glance.pdf). The tool supports this practice; its lights and chip feedback do not assess a spoken interpretation or certify mastery.
