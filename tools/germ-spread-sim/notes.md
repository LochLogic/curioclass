## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Classroom outbreak**. One student (circled when Germ vision is on) has come to school with cold germs and feels fine.
2. Ask the class to predict: how many of the 24 will catch the cold in two weeks?
3. Press **Start 2 weeks** (**Fast** if time is short). Watch the day: desks, story time on the rug, snack, recess. Coughs show as green puffs, and sick students get a 🤒.
4. When it ends, turn on one habit (try **Cover coughs: Into elbow**) and press **Run again**. The same student brings the same germs, so the comparison is fair. The run list keeps score.

## The two modes

- **Classroom outbreak**: ten school days with 24 students. Germs spread through uncovered coughs, hands (sharing supplies, then touching your face or eating snack), and shared things: the door handle, the pencil sharpener, the crayon bin and the water fountain. Five habits can be switched on: wash hands with soap (rarely, sometimes, always), cover coughs (don't, with hand, into elbow), stay home when sick, wipe shared things every day, and flu shots for half or most of the class.
- **Wash up**: the five steps of handwashing (wet, soap, scrub, rinse, dry) on a hand covered in germs you can see under a black light, like the glow lotion many classrooms use. Scrubbing counts real seconds toward 20. **Turn hand over** shows the back. At the end the black light shows what was missed. Try it with **water only** to see how much soap matters.

## What the model shows

- **Germs spread before anyone feels sick.** Students are contagious the day before symptoms. Germ vision shows who is carrying germs.
- Each habit helps on its own. With no habits, about 20 of 24 get sick in a typical run. Covering coughs, washing hands every time or staying home when sick brings that down to around 10 to 13. Wiping shared things helps a little. All four together usually stops it at the first student.
- Washing "sometimes" barely helps, because one missed wash before snack is enough.
- Coughing into your hand blocks the spray but puts germs on your hands, so it helps less than coughing into your elbow.

**This is a simplified teaching model of one classroom, not a medical prediction.** The rates are tuned so the effect of each habit shows clearly in 24 students. Real illnesses differ: a stomach bug spreads mostly by hands, and the flu more by the air.

## Talk about it

- Why didn't anyone know the first student was sick?
- Which habit helped the most in your runs? Which was easiest to do?
- Why does staying home when you're sick protect other people more than it protects you?
- When should you wash your hands? (Before eating, after the bathroom, after coughing or blowing your nose, after recess.)

## Good to know

- **Skip** jumps to the end of the two weeks. **New start** picks a different first student.
- The flu shot option is included because vaccines are part of disease prevention in health standards. It is off by default and can be skipped.
