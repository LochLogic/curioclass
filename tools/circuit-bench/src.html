Circuit Bench · Manual

On the mat

Drag from a copper terminal to another terminal or hole to fit a jumper. Or choose Wire and tap two holes. In each column, holes a to e share one copper strip; holes f to j share a separate strip across the centre trench. Each coloured rail runs the full width. Crossing wires do not connect unless their ends share a hole or strip.

Choose a drawer, then tap the board. Drag a part by its body to move it. Select a part for its paper tag and adjustment controls. On a compact bench, tap Adjust to unfold the whole tag. Shift-drag a part's copper leg to replug that leg; a plain drag fits a jumper. Drag empty board to pan. The + and − controls zoom; the centre control returns to the opening view. A dark part marked × has burned: tap its body or Replace on its tag.

Without a mouse

Tab to the breadboard. Arrows move the hole cursor. W wire, B battery, L bulb, D LED, R resistor, P potentiometer, S three-leg slide switch, U push button, M motor, Z buzzer, C coil, K compass. Enter places the chosen part. W then Enter at each end fits a wire. Enter on a leg picks it up; arrows and Enter replug it. Enter on a body selects it. T rotates the selected part by 90 degrees. Delete removes it. Escape puts down the tool.

On a selected part, F reverses a battery, I moves an iron core, 1 to 4 picks an LED colour. Press H or Hold shaft to latch the motor shaft; press again to release. Hold Space on the push button's tag to conduct. Switches toggle with Enter or Space. Tab reaches all adjustments; ranges use arrows, Page Up and Page Down. Use Move on the tag, then arrows and Enter to place a whole part.

The meter

The dial has OFF, volts, fused milliamps (200 mA), amps (10 A), ohms and continuity. With the dial focused, keys 0 to 5 select it. For voltage, put the probes across two points. For current, remove a wire and put the meter into that gap. Amps across a battery makes a short. The mA fuse opens over 200 mA; tap its fuse cover to replace it. Use A for bulbs, whose current exceeds 200 mA. Ohms and continuity use a 1 mA test source: disconnect batteries first. LIVE means the circuit has power, OL means out of range, and - - means a probe is in an empty hole or floating off the circuit. Continuity sounds below 30 Ω, only with Sound on.

On a real bench a short heats the wire fast and can burn you; never short a real battery, and never use mains power.

Pick Red probe or COM probe, then tap a hole. You can also drag a connected probe's tip. A focused probe uses arrows to move, Enter to attach at the cursor. Read announces the current context and meter value. A minus sign means the red probe is lower.

Parts and model values

AA cells: 1.5 V and 0.15 Ω inside each, 1 to 4 in series; 9 V pack: 1.5 Ω inside. Estimated runtime uses 2000 mAh per AA or 500 mAh for 9 V, rounded to 10 minutes. The batteries do not drain; real cells give less at high draw.

Bulb: 3 V, 0.3 A. Tungsten resistance is 1 + 9√(P/0.9 W) Ω, capped at 10 Ω, warmed over 80 ms; it opens after more than 2 W for 0.5 seconds. Cold ohms uses 1 Ω. Red/yellow/green/blue LEDs turn on at 2.0/2.1/2.2/3.0 V with 10 Ω dynamic resistance; over 50 mA for 0.2 seconds opens them. Replace a burned part on its tag.

Resistors: E12, 10 Ω to 100 kΩ, 49 detents over four decades. Potentiometer: 0 to 10 kΩ in 100 steps with a real third wiper leg; the endpoints keep 0.01 Ω for numerical stability. Switch and push contacts: 0.01 Ω. Wires: 0.01 Ω; over 3 A they glow red. The motor has 2 Ω winding resistance, back voltage 0.0022 V per rad/s and 0.15 A running load, with a smoothed speed response. Hold its shaft to stall it.

Coil: 4 Ω, length 4 cm, radius 1 cm, 50/100/200/400 turns. Centre field uses the finite solenoid formula B = μ₀NI / (2√(r² + (L/2)²)). The core multiplies this by 100, a design value; real iron varies and saturates. Every coil here is 4 Ω, as if wound with thicker wire as turns rise; a real coil from the same wire gains resistance with its turns. The coil is a DC resistance model, with no inductive transient. The buzzer is a 100 Ω load with a quiet 2.4 kHz tone.

What the instrument assumes

Every electrical connection enters a conductance matrix with Norton battery sources and 1 nS leakage to reference. The diode's conducting states iterate up to 20 passes. Volts has 10 MΩ input resistance, A has 0.01 Ω and mA 0.1 Ω. Readouts update five times each second; part tags report voltage, signed current and electrical power magnitude. Battery power is delivered power; motor power is electrical input, including mechanical output. A potentiometer's tag reports voltage across its outer legs, current entering leg A and total power in both sections.

The compass samples nearby part and wire fields, at a minimum 1 cm separation, plus a fixed 20 μT northward Earth field. The long straight wire limit is μ₀I/(2πr); jumpers use Biot-Savart summed along their drawn path, with the compass 1 cm above them, as drawn. A coil uses a dipole far field with moment NIπr², multiplied by 100 with the core; its radial and axial components return beside the coil. Inside one radius the distance is softened to avoid a singularity. This is not an exact near-field model. The 1 mA ohms test source can turn an LED on: its displayed resistance is a test-source ratio, not a fixed diode resistance. The compass ignores nearby steel. This is a local DC field approximation, not a full 3D magnetic simulation or a live WMM location. Dots represent conventional current; electron view reverses them. Neither speed is the real electron drift speed.

Things people have found

  • Two bulbs in a row share the voltage and dim. Every point in a single loop carries the same current (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 21.3).
  • Two bulbs side by side each receive the terminal voltage; the battery supplies both branch currents (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 21.1).
  • A reversed LED stays dark. Its anode, marked +, needs the higher potential (Georgia State University HyperPhysics, Diode).
  • A hot filament has much more resistance than its cold ohms reading (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 20.3).
  • A wire swings a compass, as Hans Christian Oersted found in 1820 (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 22.9; Britannica, Hans Christian Orsted).
  • A wire lined up with the needle swings it most; one across it barely moves it (OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, 12.2, right-hand rule).
  • More turns or more current strengthen a coil, and an iron core increases its field (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 22.2 and 22.9).
  • A spinning motor makes back voltage and draws less current than a stalled motor (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 23.6).
  • The ammeter itself conducts. Across a source it can heat a wire or open its own fuse (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 21.4).
