## What it teaches
Students compare ten musical traditions by hearing and seeing where notes lie and how rhythmic cycles repeat. They connect each example to its place, instruments and musical context, using the piano as a reference rather than a universal rule.

## Key skills
- Read pitch positions in cents and recognize notes between piano keys.
- Follow layered rhythmic patterns and distinguish pulse counts from strokes.
- Compare cycles at a shared pulse speed and explain when they meet.
- Hear how drones, interlocking parts, tuning and ornament change a musical sketch.
- Connect musical organization to particular traditions and instruments.

## Support at home
- Clap a steady four-beat pattern together. Let someone tap a different repeating pattern over it and listen for where they meet.
- Listen to music your family knows. Talk about which sounds stay steady and which change.
- Hum a held note while another person hums a short phrase. Notice how the held note changes what you hear.

## A five-minute start
Open Syria and press Play scale. Tap E half-flat, read its offset, then switch Piano notes and listen again. Open Ghana and play its cycle, then add Hear the four beats. Compare Ghana with India: their 12- and 16-pulse cycles meet every 48 pulses at the common comparison speed.

## Modes
Map offers ten stops, map layers, zoom, pitch rulers and cycle diagrams. Each Try this setting changes the sound, and a ruler note can sound over a running cycle. Closing a stop resets its switches except Drone and Slides, which persist for the session.

Compare aligns two rulers in cents and runs two cycles at 200 pulses per minute. The meeting interval is their least common multiple. The second picker excludes the first stop.

Where in the world? hides the tradition and pitch names. Listen, inspect the diagrams, then pick a place. Two misses give different clues; the third reveals the answer. Only correct identifications increase the found count. No timer or timing score is used.

## Talk questions
- What changes when the Rast notes move onto piano keys? Does the piano explain all the original notes?
- Ghana has seven bell strokes and four main beats. Why are these different counts?
- How does isolating one mbira part or siku row change the phrase?
- What does a pitch ruler leave out about a raga, a particular gamelan, or a musical tradition?
- Which pair of cycles meets most often? Show how you know.

## Good to know
Sounds are synthesized sketches, not instrument recordings or traditional pieces. Phrases and parts labeled sketch were written for Curio Class. Hear the real thing opens a Smithsonian Folkways search only when clicked. No automatic network requests occur, and no student work is collected or saved.

Slendro uses an evenly spaced model; real gamelans differ. The mbira sketch uses piano notes, while real tunings differ. Bagpipe cents are a simple-ratio model; pipers tune by ear. Ewe bell tones are a fifth apart only in this sketch. Yaman is more than its scale. Koto's alternate setting is explicitly a tuning for comparison.

The supplied Met sitar is a historical six-string specimen without sympathetic strings, unlike the later sitars described in the general fact. Its card identifies that exception. The Cuba portrait shows the tres's six strings grouped into three paired courses.

Instrument classes follow Hornbostel-Sachs: chordophones use vibrating strings; aerophones use vibrating air; idiophones use the body's vibration; membranophones use a stretched skin. Mixed ensembles show multiple icons.

Scales badges count notes per octave, with Scotland's badge showing all nine notes of the full chanter range. The Ewe example counts its two bell tones.

The diagrams use filled shapes for hits and open shapes for rests. Each part has a distinct shape as well as color. Motion off removes the sweeping hand while retaining pulse highlights, sound timing and numbers. The tool supports Tab, Enter and arrow keys on the ruler. Space in the card starts or stops a cycle; Escape stops sound. Back to map closes the card and returns to Map mode; the mystery found count is kept for the session.

NCAS MU:Cn11 is practiced through place, instrument and cultural context at each stop. MU:Re7 is practiced directly through tuning and cycle comparisons and the live Try this experiments.
