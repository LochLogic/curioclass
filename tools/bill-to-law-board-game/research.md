# Research packet: bill-to-law-board-game
Concept: Carry a kid-proposed bill around a state capitol board through committees, both chambers, a veto and an override; learn what each office can do and where the rules come from, then see what the law changes.
Grade: 4   Standards: D2.Civ.1.3-5, D2.Civ.5.3-5, D2.Civ.12.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Civ.1.3-5: "Distinguish the responsibilities and powers of government officials at various levels and branches of government and in different times and places." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to tell what powers belong to lawmakers, committee chairs, and governors when creating state laws.
- D2.Civ.5.3-5: "Explain the origins, functions, and structure of different systems of government, including those created by the U.S. and state constitutions." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain how state constitutions create legislative chambers and executive offices with distinct rules.
- D2.Civ.12.3-5: "Explain how rules and laws change society and how people change rules and laws." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain how everyday citizens and students can propose ideas that become binding laws.

## Real data

- National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) Legislative Process Overview: https://www.ncsl.org/about-state-legislatures/learning-the-game (Public domain educational material). Documents steps, committee roles, and veto override thresholds.
- Kansas State Legislature House Bill 2644 (2022 Session): https://www.kslegislature.org (Public domain). Legislative history of a real student-initiated bill passing committees, both chambers, and being signed into law.
- Georgia General Assembly Senate Bill 102 (2005 Session, Green Tree Frog): https://www.legis.ga.gov (Public domain). Complete legislative roll call and committee steps for a student-sponsored state symbol law.

## Common mistakes

- Believing the governor writes and creates all laws: Fourth graders often view the governor as a king who dictates laws alone, rather than the legislature authoring and voting on statutes (NCSS C3 Framework).
- Thinking a bill becomes law as soon as one chamber approves it: Students frequently overlook that bills must pass both legislative chambers in identical text (NCSL).
- Assuming a gubernatorial veto is the absolute end of a bill: Students miss that constitutions grant the legislative branch the power to override vetoes with a supermajority vote (NCSL).
- Believing children cannot participate in legislation: Students assume only adults or professional politicians can influence state law, unaware of real elementary classes passing state statutes (Kansas Legislature).

## Sensitivity

- Partisan neutrality: Frame kid-proposed bills around relatable, civic topics (such as establishing a state fruit, a bike helmet safety day, or school playground shade funding) rather than divisive political wedge issues.
- Respect for checks and balances: Present the governor's veto and committee scrutiny as legitimate constitutional checks rather than unfair obstacles.
- None seen for religion or living culture.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- iCivics LawCraft (https://www.icivics.org/games/lawcraft): Interactive game where players draft a federal bill, select sponsors, negotiate amendments, and vote.
- Kids in the House (https://kids-clerk.house.gov): Educational portal explaining how legislative bills advance through committees and votes.
- Oregon State Legislature Kids Page / State Capitol Virtual Tours: Interactive walkthroughs of state legislative chambers and lawmaking steps.

## Gaps

- State-to-state variations: Nebraska is unicameral (single house), and veto override thresholds range from a simple majority in Tennessee to three-fifths in Maryland and two-thirds in California and Texas. Curio Class should model the standard bicameral two-thirds override structure found in most states, with an informational card noting state variations.
