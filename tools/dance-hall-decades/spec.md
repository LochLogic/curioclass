# Dance Hall Decades

## One idea
A century of social dancing as rooms you walk into: at each of eight halls on the timeline you learn one short pattern on your feet in the classroom, then turn the room over to see who made the dance and who was kept out of the room or the credit, and at the end you set your own way of moving and lay two dances beside it.

## Not like
- art-through-time (6-8, timeline): threads tie artworks to causes. Here each stop is a place to dance in, a pattern to learn with your body, and a question of who got in.
- music-history-radio (9-12, timeline): one tune through eleven eras on a dial. Here the dances, rooms and people are the content, not arrangements.
- dance-styles-explorer (9-12): concert and street styles traced and critiqued. Here social dances in their halls, with access and credit as the lens.
- choreography-board (6-8), dancers-on-tour (this band): making and adapting choreography. Nothing is choreographed here.
- world-music-map (6-8): music traditions on a map. This is one country's social dance halls over time.

## Who and how
- Grade 6-8, dance, module "Dance in Culture and History (Dance)". Topic line: social dance across a century.
- Driver: either (a teacher on a projector with the class dancing, or students on laptops). 20 to 40 minutes; stops can be split across days.
- Standards: NCAS DA:Re7 (find patterns and their cultural context), DA:Re8 (interpret meaning), DA:Re9 (evaluate with criteria), DA:Cn10 (relate dance to personal experience), DA:Cn11 (dance in history and culture).
- Hard history (segregation and exclusion) is part of every stop: explorer format, no points, no timers, no win state. Teacher notes give guidance for discussion.
- Header: title, topic line, modes **Halls** (default), **My way of moving**, then kit `motion`, `sound`, `read`.

## Screens and modes

### Halls
- Top: the timeline, 1920 to 2000, with eight stops as painted object markers (Content) and the decade labels in code. Tap a marker or use **Previous hall** and **Next hall**.
- Focus: the hall view, the largest thing on the stage: the painted empty hall for that stop (Art), with three tabs over it: **The dance**, **The room**, **Who made it, who was kept out**.
  - **The dance**: a footprint diagram over the hall floor (code: left and right footprints numbered by count, arrows for travel) and a simple code figure (the dance family's figure) doing the steps. **Play** (becomes **Stop**) loops an original groove (code) at **Learn** speed or **Party** speed (toggle), lighting each footprint on its count and showing the count big. **Mirror** flips the diagram for a class facing the screen. Students do it in the room.
  - **The room**: the place, the years, the music that played, what it was like (3 to 4 short lines, Content).
  - **Who made it, who was kept out**: the people who created the dance, and who was kept out of the room, the TV show or the credit (Content), with one primary-source-style quote card or fact card and a link in Sources. A "Talk about it" prompt sits at the bottom (one question).
- Panel: `hint`, the three tab buttons, Play, Learn / Party, Mirror, Previous hall, Next hall.

### My way of moving
- Focus: a comparison chart (code): four rows (Content), each a scale from one end to the other, with three markers: Me, and the two dances the student picks.
- The student sets **Me** on each row with a slider. **Pick two dances**: two dropdowns of the eight.
- Under the chart: **Judge with a criterion**: pick one criterion (Easy for newcomers to join, Lets you invent, Works without a partner, Fits a crowded room), then for each of the two dances choose **Strong**, **Some** or **Weak** and pick a reason chip from that dance's facts. A sentence frame closes it: "I move most like ___ because ___. By my criterion, ___ is stronger because ___." Saved on this device; **Copy** copies the text.

## Content

### The eight halls (Learn and Party speeds are the tool's choices for learning, in beats a minute)
1. **1923, the Charleston, Harlem nightclubs** (marker: a 78 rpm record on a gramophone). The dance: (1) step forward on the left, (2) kick the right foot forward, (3) step back on the right, (4) tap the left foot back; repeat; arms swing opposite. Learn 100, Party 200. The room: Harlem clubs in the 1920s; jazz bands; the Charleston swept the country after the 1923 Broadway show "Runnin' Wild". Who made it: Black Americans in the South, with roots traced to Charleston, South Carolina. Kept out: the Cotton Club in Harlem (opened 1923) put Black dancers and musicians on its stage but admitted white customers only. Talk about it: "Who could dance there, and who could only perform there?"
2. **1930s, the Lindy Hop, the Savoy Ballroom** (marker: a big band music stand). The dance (6-count basic): (1, 2) rock step back and forward, (3 and 4) triple step, (5 and 6) triple step; partners hold one hand. Learn 110, Party 160. The room: 596 Lenox Avenue, Harlem, open 1926 to 1958, a block long, up to 4,000 people; the best dancers had "Cats' Corner". Who made it: Black dancers at the Savoy developed the Lindy Hop around 1927. Kept out: the Savoy kept a no-discrimination policy and crowds were mostly Black with white dancers too, while other famous rooms, like the Cotton Club, turned Black customers away. Talk about it: "Dancer Frankie Manning said people were judged by their dancing, not their skin. What would that change?"
3. **1950s, the mambo, the Palladium Ballroom, Manhattan** (marker: a pair of timbales). The dance (on 1): (1) step forward left, (2) replace weight right, (3) step left back to center, (4) pause; (5) step back right, (6) replace left, (7) right to center, (8) pause. Learn 90, Party 180. The room: Latin big bands (Tito Puente, Machito, Tito Rodriguez) played mambo nights. Who made it: Cuban musicians first shaped the mambo, and Puerto Rican, Cuban, Black and Jewish and Italian New Yorkers danced it together on the same floor. Kept out: many hotel ballrooms of the time were closed to Black and Latino customers. Talk about it: "Why might a dance spread faster in a room open to everyone?"
4. **1957, the Stroll, "Bandstand", Philadelphia** (marker: a 1950s TV camera). The dance: two lines face each other; everyone steps side, touch, side, touch in place (counts 1 to 8) while one couple at the top struts down the middle together, then joins the end. Learn 100, Party 110. The room: a TV studio; the show began locally in 1952 and went national on ABC as "American Bandstand" on August 5, 1957. Who made it: line dances like the Stroll came from Black teen dancing and rhythm and blues. Kept out: from 1954 the producers' ticket rules (favoring repeat guests, promoting in white suburbs, answering mail only from some surnames) kept Black teenagers out of the studio audience; footage shows it stayed almost all white until the show moved to Los Angeles in 1964. Talk about it: "Black musicians played on the show. Why did it matter who was dancing in the audience?"
5. **1960, the Twist, a teen sock hop** (marker: a 45 rpm record on a portable record player). The dance: feet in place, twist the hips and shoulders in opposite directions, as if drying your back with a towel, on every beat; dip lower and come back up over 8 counts. Learn 120, Party 156. The room: a school gym, shoes off to save the floor. Who made it: Hank Ballard and the Midnighters, a Black rhythm and blues group, recorded "The Twist" in 1959; Chubby Checker's 1960 version made it a national craze. The twist was danced apart, no partner needed, which was new. Kept out: many adults called it improper. Talk about it: "Who gets the credit for a dance: the first one to do it, or the one who makes it famous?"
6. **1973, toprock, a Bronx block party** (marker: two turntables). The dance (toprock basic, called the cross step here): (1) cross the right foot in front of the left, arms open, (2) step back to center, (3) cross the left foot in front, (4) back to center; bounce on every count. Learn 100, Party 110. The room: a community room and the street outside 1520 Sedgwick Avenue, where DJ Kool Herc played a back-to-school party on August 11, 1973. Who made it: Black and Puerto Rican young people in the Bronx, dancing on the long drum breaks Herc looped. Kept out: these parties were free and open; the dancers' own neighborhood had been cut through by highways and left with few places to go. Talk about it: "When a place leaves you out, how do you make your own?"
7. **1975, the Hustle, a New York disco** (marker: a mirror ball). The dance (partner basic): (and 1) tap the left foot and step on it, (2) step back on the right, (3) step in place left; repeat. Learn 100, Party 120. The room: dark floor, colored lights, a DJ mixing records. Who made it: disco grew at parties like David Mancuso's Loft (from 1970) where Black, Latino and gay dancers were welcome at a time many clubs turned them away; the Hustle was danced by Latino teens in the Bronx and Brooklyn before the 1975 hit song. Kept out: in 1979 "Disco Demolition Night" at a Chicago ballpark blew up disco records; many saw it as a backlash against the people disco belonged to. Talk about it: "What does it mean when a crowd destroys another crowd's music?"
8. **1976 to today, the Electric Slide, wedding receptions** (marker: a boombox). The dance (the 18-count version most people do): (1 to 4) step right, left behind, step right, touch; (5 to 8) the same to the left; (9 to 12) step back right, left, right, touch; (13, 14) step forward left, touch right; (15 to 18) step forward right, brush left, a quarter turn left, and repeat facing the next wall. Learn 104, Party 116. The room: any party where everyone gets up. Who made it: choreographer Ric Silver made the first version in 1976 (22 counts); it became famous to Marcia Griffiths's song "Electric Boogie". Kept out: no one; a line dance lets anyone join. But in 2007 Ric Silver claimed copyright in his dance and asked people not to post wrong versions online, which started an argument about who owns a dance. Talk about it: "Can anyone own a dance everyone does?"
- Teacher note (notes.md, not on screen): the common name for this toprock step comes from a stereotype of Native Americans, so the tool calls it the cross step.

## Interactions in detail
- Play keeps every control live: switching Learn and Party changes the tempo from the next beat with no jump; Mirror flips at once; changing tab keeps the groove playing; Previous and Next hall stop it.
- The groove is original, made in code per stop (a ragtime-style stride on piano for 1, swing with a walking bass for 2, a clave pattern with bass for 3, a shuffle for 4 and 5, a drum break loop for 6, four-on-the-floor with an offbeat hi-hat for 7, a steady pop beat for 8). Each pattern loops; no auto-play runs longer than the student lets it.
- Footprints light on their counts in step with the audio clock (lookahead scheduling).
- My way of moving: Me sliders start in the middle (2); the chart redraws at once. The two dropdowns cannot pick the same dance.
- No scores, stars or completion badges anywhere; visited halls get a small dot on the timeline, nothing more.
- Saved on this device: Me profile, picks and the judging text (localStorage, try/catch). **Reset** clears it.
- Motion off: the figure holds one pose per count (no sliding); footprints still light in time.
- Sizes: at 1366x768 the timeline, the hall view with the footprint diagram and Play are visible without scrolling. Preview 680x425: timeline and hall view, count numbers 14 px or more. Projector use: the count number is at least 72 px at 1920x1080. At 375 wide: timeline scrolls sideways in its strip; the hall view under it.
