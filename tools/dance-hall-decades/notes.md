## What it teaches

Students compare the patterns of eight American social dances and connect them to places, communities, access and credit.
They describe their own movement preferences, then evaluate two dances using an explicit criterion and evidence.

## Key skills

- Follow a counted pattern and notice steps, touches, pauses and changes of facing.
- Connect movement to the people and places that developed it.
- Distinguish making a culture from being allowed to participate in a venue.
- Compare personal preferences with dance characteristics.
- Support a judgment with a criterion and a relevant fact.

## Support at home

- Ask someone which dance they remember learning from another person. Ask who taught them and where it happened.
- Try a comfortable side-touch pattern together. Describe whether you prefer set steps or inventing your own.
- Talk about a gathering that helped someone feel welcome. Name one thing the hosts did that made a difference.

## A 5-minute start

1. Open Charleston and press Play. Copy the four-count pattern or trace it with a finger while seated.
2. Try Mirror when facing the screen. Stop and open The room, then Who made it, who was kept out.
3. Read the Cotton Club fact card. Distinguish being a performer from being allowed to enter as a customer.
4. Open the Savoy and compare its policy. Ask how different access could change the dances people shared.
5. In My way of moving, set Me, choose Charleston and Lindy Hop, select a criterion, and make a claim with evidence.

## The modes

**Halls:** eight stops with painted imagined settings, counted teaching patterns and original code-generated grooves.
The images evoke places and periods; they are not photographs or exact architectural reconstructions. Learn and Party
are Curio Class teaching tempos, not measurements of historical performances. A dance phrase can cross musical bars:
six-count Lindy, three-count Hustle and eighteen-count Electric Slide do not imply those musical meters.
Timeline markers are ordered stops spaced for legibility, not a proportional scale of elapsed years.

Play loops until Stop. Learn / Party changes the next scheduled interval, Mirror updates immediately, and tabs keep
the groove going. Previous hall, Next hall, mode changes, Read and hiding the page stop music. Read stops the groove
before narrating the current action hint. A story opened during play waits for Stop, if that hall and tab still fit.

**My way of moving:** four Me sliders, two distinct dances, a criterion, two strength judgments, two fact choices,
and a sentence frame. The dance positions are authored descriptions, not measured scores or cultural hierarchies.
The student decides the judgment; the software does not mark cultural preferences right or wrong. Copy copies the
sentence; browser restrictions instead select the text for manual copying. Me, dance picks and writing are saved
only on the current device with guarded localStorage. Reset clears the comparison. There are no accounts or uploads.

## Discussion and care

This is an explorer with no points, timer, badges or win state. Visited dots record navigation only. Segregation,
racism, anti-Latino exclusion, hostility toward gay communities, commercial credit and ownership are explicit
content in DA:Cn11 and interpretation and evaluation work in DA:Re8 and DA:Re9 here. Do not reenact exclusion or ask
students to represent a racial group. Focus on institutions, choices, evidence, and the agency of the people who
created welcoming spaces. Students can describe observations privately and decline personal disclosure.

Ask: Who made the dance? Who received visibility or money? Who could join this particular room? What evidence makes
you think so? Distinguish a welcoming dance format from a claim that nobody has ever been excluded from its settings.
The Electric Slide does not prove universal access. The Twist did not invent every form of dancing apart.

Give students room for small steps, observe rather than touch partners, and allow seated marking or watching. The
footwork is a short introduction, not a full social-dance course. Learn speed can still feel quick. No acrobatics,
floorwork or lifts are taught. The toprock step's common name invokes a stereotype of Native Americans; this tool
uses **cross step**. Historical names of songs and venues appear only when relevant to the lesson.

## Teaching adaptations and source conflicts

- **Charleston:** four-count forward, kick, back, touch, as specified. CLDAA's dance terminology describes this basic.
- **Lindy Hop:** six counts with swung triples on 3-and-4 and 5-and-6. Eight foot placements occupy six beats.
- **Mambo:** the requested on-one learning adaptation is labeled on screen. Common ballroom teaching instead breaks
  on two and six. This is not presented as a reconstruction of a single Palladium dancer's timing.
- **Stroll:** the requested side-touch version is labeled a simplified eight-count adaptation. Richard Powers's
  historical reconstruction describes six-count side patterns and personal invention in the central aisle.
- **Twist:** feet remain in place, hips and shoulders oppose each other, and eight counts dip and rise.
  Ballard's polished recording was made in November 1958 and released in January 1959. The screen says released,
  correcting the spec's conflation of the recording year with the release year.
- **Toprock:** alternating crosses and returns, not a claim that this basic was invented on August 11, 1973.
- **Hustle:** the spec's short tap-and-step introduction is retained, with the lead-in shown as & before 1.
  Historical Hustle basics vary. It does not teach a complete partner lead-and-follow technique.
- **Electric Slide:** the supplied counts 15-18 were incomplete and disagreed with the common eighteen-count sheet.
  The build uses 15 right back, 16 left touch, 17 left forward, 18 right scuff with a quarter turn left. Counts 4 and 8
  use touches, an accepted teaching variant. The original twenty-two-count version is identified separately.
- **Access claims:** the closed-hotel-ballroom generalization was softened because the named source did not establish
  it. The 1973 Sedgwick event was a fundraiser, so the spec's claim that these parties were free is not repeated.
  Universal "no one excluded" wording for wedding receptions is also qualified.
