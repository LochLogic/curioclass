## What it teaches

Students design a zipline that works for three rider masses, then use an energy ledger to explain where the starting
height energy went. They compare constant-force and generator brakes against three explicit lab criteria.

## Key skills

- Predict how height, drag and rolling friction affect a ride.
- Explain why the same air drag matters more per kilogram for a light rider.
- Account for energy in motion, height, thermal energy and a battery.
- Test a design against several constraints rather than one successful example.
- Use measured results to support a written explanation.

## Support at home

- On a walk, point out a hill. Ask where energy goes when a bicycle coasts and slows without pedaling.
- Rub your hands gently and notice warmth. Talk about motion transferring energy to thermal energy.
- Compare a gentle stop with an abrupt stop in an everyday movement. Ask why stopping distance matters.

## A five-minute start

1. Open Design and press Ride immediately. The default 70 kg ride passes.
2. Press Test all three. Watch the light rider stop too abruptly with the same 800 N pad.
3. Choose Generator and test again. Keep the default strength initially.
4. Open Ledger. Drag the scrubber back and forward to follow the same conserved total.
5. Open Notebook. Compare the mass plot and explain how the generator adapts its force.

## Modes

Design: change anchor height, sag, trolley wear, posture, mass and brake. Every edit cancels the active test.
Ride uses physical time. Test all three launches two seconds apart and plays each ride at four times speed.
Cards opens three optional briefs. Z1 uses the default cable; Z2 sets a 25 m tree anchor; Z3 uses a worn trolley.
Keep each brief's cable, trolley and upright posture while changing the brake. Passing all three records the brief.

Ledger: replays the last completed ride beside a fixed-total energy column. Before a first ride, an explicitly labeled
default calculation is available. Zero height is the lowest point on that cable, not the valley floor.
Brake work is split into brake heat and battery energy for a generator. Battery efficiency is 80 percent.

Notebook: stores completed rides on this device using localStorage. The conclusion box is unsaved.
Copy table exports tab-separated text to the clipboard or reveals selectable text if clipboard permission is unavailable.
Clear notebook clears ride history; earned challenge cards remain. No data leaves the device.

## Talk questions

- Why can one 70 kg success hide a problem for a 35 kg rider?
- Why do friction-off speeds coincide even though the starting energy depends on mass?
- How does tucking change drag and the brake needed at the end?
- If the motion energy becomes zero, where has its energy gone?
- Which numbers from your notebook support your conclusion about the two brakes?

## Good to know

This is a physics lab model, not a construction or operation guide. The 1.5 g deceleration limit, 1 m/s minimum zone
speed, rolling coefficients, drag areas, brake settings and battery share are lab settings rather than industry standards.
The model omits cable stretch and bounce, wind, rider swing and trolley spin. The trolley follows the lower branch
of an ellipse with anchors as foci. It starts at x = 4 m, the brake begins at 276 m, and the platform edge is 296 m.
A ratchet holds a rider who stops in the brake zone. A padded bumper catches a rider who arrives moving.
Friction off disables rolling friction, drag and braking so the ledger contains height and motion only; no lab verdict applies.
The rider is drawn at five times physical scale for visibility. Rider paintings are pending; code silhouettes preserve posture.
Motion off preserves ride duration and displays still launch, brake and finish poses. Sound controls narration and synthesized ride audio.
Space rides, T tests all three, and R resets when the page itself has focus. Native controls retain their usual keyboard actions.

## Standards in student actions

HS-PS3-1: students use the computed energy ledger, change model inputs and quantify redistribution of energy.
HS-PS3-2: students describe height, motion, heat and stored electrical energy using the ledger and notebook.
HS-PS3-3: students design and evaluate a brake that converts motion energy within stopping and deceleration constraints.
