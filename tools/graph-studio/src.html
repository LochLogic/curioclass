The plotter manual

Put a thought on paper

Type on the equation tape. Enter adds a line; Delete on an empty line removes it. There are eight pens. The little number tabs under an equation are part of its tape: drag vertically, scroll, or use Up and Down. A decimal changes by its last digit; Shift changes ten times as much, and Alt gives finer drag or scroll adjustments.

y = x^2, y = 2x + 1, x = 3, f(x) = x^2 - 3x, then f(4). Functions f and g can call each other; circular definitions are left undefined. Type y = a(x-b)^2 + k to make three rulers.

Operators: + − * / ^. Multiplication can be implicit: 2x, 3(x+1), xy. Powers bind before a leading minus. Constants: pi, π, e. Functions: sqrt, abs, sin, cos, tan, asin, acos, atan, ln (natural log), log (base ten), exp, floor, round. Trigonometry uses the RAD/DEG switch; it applies to inverse functions too. Round takes the nearest integer, with exact halves going away from zero.

r = sin(4t) is polar. (cos(3t), sin(2t)) or x = cos(3t), y = sin(2t) is parametric. Both use t from 0 to 2pi radians (0 to 360 in degrees), adjustable in Axes. (2, 3) is a point: drag it, or select its line and use Alt with the arrow keys on paper.

The rulers and paper

A free letter gets a ruler just below its first line. Its end caps set minimum, maximum and step. Arrows step; Page Up and Page Down take ten steps. Play sweeps back and forth. Motion off parks all moving rulers. Drag the paper to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom. + and − zoom; 0 restores both axes to −10 through 10. Square gives equal physical distances per axis unit (per decade on log axes). log x and log y use base ten and show positive coordinates only.

Measure, without guessing

Tap an equation-tape curve or press T to trace. Left and Right move the trace; Up and Down change pens. The brass tag gives the parameter and both coordinates to four significant figures. For ordinary y curves, the parameter is x. P drops a pin; drag it along its curve. Two pins on the same curve give Δy/Δx, the average rate of change. Pins snap together when dragged onto each other; when Δx is zero the rate is undefined. Escape clears the pins. G shows the numerical tangent.

K marks numerically found zeros, turning points, the y intercept and intersections of visible equation-tape y curves. A numbered cross matches its pen on the tape; an × with two pen numbers is an intersection. Open circles mark sampled holes with a finite limiting value. A pole is a break, never a joining stroke.

Table prints values for the chosen line, with your own start and step. It lists t for polar and parametric ink, y for a vertical equation, and x for an ordinary equation. Previous and Next move through the table.

Measurements in, curves out

Data accepts two columns separated by a tab, comma or space, at most 1,000 pairs. An optional first header row is ignored. Non-numeric rows are reported. A line and a quadratic use ordinary least squares, with centered, scaled x for stability. Exponential fits log(y) against x; power fits log(y) against log(x). Those fits require positive values in the logged columns. Rescale columns larger than 1e100 before fitting.

R² measures the share of variation described by the fitted curve. For exponential and power it is measured in log space, so it is not directly comparable to an ordinary-space R². Constant data have undefined R². Coefficients have four significant figures in the readout; plotting keeps full precision. A fit is a description, not a prediction or a cause.

The Notebook has six setups and four save slots. Equations, rulers, data and paper settings stay on this device; they never leave it. Private browsing or clearing browser storage can remove them. No account, upload or recording.

Honesty notes

A literal fraction exponent p/q, reduced to an odd denominator, uses the real root for negative bases: x^(1/3) and x^(2/3) work on both sides of zero. Decimal exponents keep the usual real-power domain.

This is a real numerical plotter, not symbolic algebra. It accepts explicit equations, points, polar and parametric forms; inequalities and general implicit equations are outside this build. Real-number arithmetic uses double precision. Very large values overflow; undefined values are left blank.

Every curve starts with at least one sample per paper pixel and is refined where it bends. Very fast wiggles can alias; a warning is shown for detectable high-frequency trigonometric inputs. No finite sampler can detect every hidden oscillation or hole. Key points and intersections are numerical candidates, typically about six significant figures for well-scaled curves; repeated roots and closely spaced features can be missed. The tangent is a symmetric numerical derivative, checked on both sides. At corners and jumps it may be undefined. No physical units are inferred from equations.

Things people have found

On the positive x axis, 2^x and x^10 cross near 1.078 and 58.77. There is also a negative crossing near −0.9371. Eventually the exponential outgrows the polynomial.

The planets have a power-fit exponent near 1.500 for orbital period against distance. A log-log plot makes that relation nearly a straight line. Squaring period and cubing distance is Kepler's third law.

The bundled Mauna Loa annual CO₂ means bend upward; the Data drawer computes line and quadratic R² directly from those values.

The US population from 1790 to 1860 grew roughly three percent per year. A later span tells a different story.

For positive integer n, a polar rose sin(nt) has n petals when n is odd and 2n when n is even. In radians, sin(x)/x has a hole at (0, 1); in degrees its limiting height is pi/180. (1 + 1/n)^n approaches e as n grows; set the ruler n's maximum to 1000 to watch it approach. cos(3t), sin(2t) makes the 3:2 Lissajous figure.
