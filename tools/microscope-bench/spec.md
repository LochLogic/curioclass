# Microscope Bench (Curiosity No. 06)

## The real thing
A compound light microscope on a lab bench, with a tray of things to put under it and a sketchbook beside it. Biology
students, pathologists, pond ecologists and hobby microscopists all work this way: make a wet mount, find the specimen
at low power, focus, close the iris for contrast, move up the objectives, calibrate the eyepiece scale, sketch. The
slides are real micrographs, large enough to scan, and the optics (focus, depth of field, the flipped image, oil
immersion, the iris) behave as they do on a real instrument.

## The first two minutes
- Open: a matte bench seen from above. The round eyepiece view fills the middle, glowing but blurry: a drop of pond
  water is already on the stage. Focus knobs to the right, turret and lamp dimmer below, the tray on the left, the
  closed sketchbook at the edge. One quiet line on the bench: "Turn the focus knob."
- First action with nothing read: turn the coarse focus knob (drag, wheel or arrows).
- First reaction: the blur snaps sharp. Algae strands, debris, then something moves: a paramecium crosses the field.
  Chasing it with the stage knobs, the student finds the image moves the opposite way, like a real microscope.

**What keeps it from feeling like a regular tool** (Cory's worry, 2026-11-04). A lesson tool shows picture 1, then
picture 2, then asks a question. This bench never does:
- The student chooses what goes on the stage; every choice (stain, cover slip, water or dry) changes what is seen.
- Each slide is far bigger than the eyepiece and nothing is centred for you. Finding things is the activity.
- The tool never asks "What is this?" and never says right or wrong. A thing is named only after the student has
  zoomed to 40x or more and tapped it, like checking a field guide.
- The sketchbook is the only record, written by the student. Nothing counts what has been seen.

## Things to do
1. Focus on the pond drop and follow a paramecium around the slide with the stage knobs.
2. Hunt the pond slide for the rotifer hiding in the debris, then go to 40x and watch its crown of cilia.
3. Make an onion-skin wet mount with no stain, then again with iodine, and compare what appears.
4. Make a cheek-cell mount with methylene blue, find a nucleus, and compare it with the onion cells.
5. Drop the cover slip carelessly and find the air bubbles: thick black rings that are not cells.
6. Put salt and then sugar on dry slides and compare the crystals' shapes.
7. Lay a hair under the cover slip, go to 40x, and focus up and down along its scaly surface.
8. Look at a feather and find the tiny hooks that zip the barbs together.
9. Scan a fly's wing for the veins and the field of fine hairs between them.
10. Swing in the 100x objective without oil, then with a drop of oil, and compare.
11. Close the iris on a pale onion cell and watch contrast rise while fine detail softens.
12. Calibrate the eyepiece scale with the stage micrometer, then measure a cheek cell in micrometres.
13. Open the prepared-slide box, scan the onion root tip, and hunt for a cell caught in each stage of division.
14. Sketch what you saw with a label and the magnification, and print the page.

## Controls
- Coarse focus knob: stage height 0 to 10 mm, 50 µm steps; drag, wheel, or Up and Down. Shift with arrows is fine.
- Fine focus knob: plus or minus 50 µm around the coarse setting, 1 µm steps; Up and Down.
- Stage knobs X and Y: 0.05 mm steps, arrows when focused, Shift for 1 mm. The image moves opposite to the slide;
  dragging in the eyepiece view moves the slide the same inverted way.
- Objective turret: 4x, 10x, 40x, 100x oil; click or keys 1 to 4. Parfocal: focus stays nearly put between them.
- Lamp dimmer: fader 0 to 100 percent, 5 percent steps. Iris lever: 0 to 100 percent open, 10 percent steps.
- Tray: specimens (pond water, onion skin, cheek swab, hair, salt, sugar, sand, leaf, feather, fly's wing), a
  prepared-slide box (onion root tip, blood smear), iodine, methylene blue, water dropper, immersion oil, cover
  slips, a stage micrometer. A mount is built by drag, or Enter on a tray item then Enter on the slide: specimen,
  water or dry, stain or none, cover slip or none. Key X washes the slide.
- Eyepiece reticle: toggle with E, rotate with R.
- Tap or Enter on something at 40x or more: a small field-guide note names it. Below 40x: "Look closer."
- Sketchbook (key K): pencil, eraser, label pins, notes, magnification (filled from the turret), date; pages saved on
  this device with the kit's save (kit/curio.js), with the line "Your sketches stay on this device"; Print
  prints the page alone.
- Power panel (Motion, Sound, Read): lower-left beside the plate, styled as a lab power strip.

## Readouts
Monospace, black on a cream lab label on the microscope's arm.
- MAG: total magnification (objective times the 10x eyepiece), whole number.
- FIELD: field of view in mm, two decimals (4.50, 1.80, 0.45, 0.18 for field number 18).
- SCALE: µm per reticle division, one decimal, marked "nominal" until calibrated.
- STAGE X and Y: mm, two decimals. FOCUS: µm from best focus, signed whole number. DEPTH: depth of field in µm, one
  decimal. LAMP and IRIS: percent.

## The Manual
Contents: the parts and what each knob does; making a wet mount; why the image is flipped; magnification and field
of view; oil at 100x; calibrating the reticle; the stains; each slide's source, licence, real scale and how it was
assembled; honesty notes.

Things people have found:
- Robert Hooke named the "cells" he saw in cork in Micrographia (1665), after small rooms (Hooke 1665, public domain).
- Antonie van Leeuwenhoek described tiny moving "animalcules" in water in a 1676 letter to the Royal Society
  (Philosophical Transactions 1677).
- Rotifers are named for the crown of cilia on the head, which beats like turning wheels (OpenStax Biology 2e,
  CC BY 4.0).
- A compound microscope flips the image, so moving the slide right moves the image left (Nikon MicroscopyU,
  "Introduction to microscope optics").
- At 100x the objective needs a drop of oil, which bends light about as glass does (refractive index about 1.52) and
  lets more light in (Nikon MicroscopyU, "Immersion oil").
- Closing the iris makes a pale specimen easier to see but softens fine detail (Nikon MicroscopyU, "Condenser
  aperture").
- A red blood cell is about 7 to 8 µm across (OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e, CC BY 4.0).
- Salt crystals are cubes because sodium and chloride ions stack in a cubic lattice (OpenStax Chemistry 2e, CC BY 4.0).
- In a root tip most cells are between divisions at any moment, so the dividing ones are rare finds (OpenStax
  Biology 2e, the cell cycle).

## Real material and sources
- Micrographs: only images marked public domain, CC0 or CC BY, checked one by one and logged in
  tools/microscope-bench/art/manifest.json (URL, licence, credit, magnification, µm per pixel and how that is known:
  scale bar, stated magnification, or a known-size object in frame).
  - CDC Public Health Image Library: status is per image; only "Public Domain" ones, credited to CDC and the
    photographer (PHIL FAQ).
  - Cell Image Library (UC San Diego): licence is per image (five kinds); only Public Domain or CC BY. Example: onion
    root tip CIL:43552, public domain (doi 10.7295/W9CIL43552).
  - Wikimedia Commons: only PD, CC0 or CC BY files; most Commons micrographs are CC BY-SA and are not used.
- Per slide: one base image up to 2400 px square (WebP, 150 to 220 KB) for 4x and 10x, plus two to four 512 px detail
  tiles (about 40 KB each) at the places worth zooming to.
- Pond life: paramecium, rotifer and others cut from public-domain micrographs as sprites (about 100 KB), moved by code.
- Blur: computed from distance to best focus and the objective's depth of field (Nikon MicroscopyU formulas); a real
  focal stack is used where the source has one.
- Unstained views: a real photo when one exists, otherwise the stained photo with the stain colour removed.
- Stage micrometer and reticle: drawn in code at exact scale (0.01 mm divisions).
- Budget: 12 slides at about 300 KB plus sprites and code, about 4 MB. Over the 2 MB goal, under the 6 MB ceiling,
  because real micrographs must be large enough to scan; notes.md says so in a `Size:` line.

## Honesty notes
Said in the Manual:
- Motion is animated: the organisms were photographed still and set swimming by code along realistic paths.
- Some slides are assembled from several photos, and some unstained views come from stained photos; each slide's
  page says which.
- Blur is computed, not optical, though it follows the real depth of field.
- Each slide is one particular specimen someone else made; real slides have more debris and smears.

## Done when
1. Opens on the blurry pond slide with no welcome screen; the first focus turn sharpens it.
2. Every tray choice changes the view, and only to an image that really matches it (spot-check every mount).
3. Every image is in manifest.json with a PD, CC0 or CC BY licence and credit; the Manual lists them all.
4. A calibrated measurement of a cheek cell or red blood cell is within 20 percent of the textbook size.
5. The image moves opposite to the stage knobs; 100x without oil is dim and soft.
6. The tool never asks a question and never names a thing below 40x or before it is tapped; the lesson creep check
   also searches for "what is" and "collected".
7. Sketchbook pages save on this device, survive a reload and print; the screen says they never leave the device.
8. Every control works by keyboard and announces its value; panning a full slide holds 60 frames a second at
   1366x768 on a school Chromebook profile.
9. Layout checked at 1366x768 and 375x812; plate and Manual drawer lower-left at both; contrast AA; Motion off stops
   the swimming (organisms rest in place).
10. No network requests; short test passed and written in the report, with a frame beside Elements Inspector.
11. No score, goal, level, sticker, collection count or finish screen anywhere.

## Open questions
- Finding PD or CC BY micrographs with known scale for every tray item is the biggest risk. Lean: a curation pass
  before the Codex session writes manifest.json; a tray item with no qualifying image leaves the tray, never painted.
- Unstained views made from stained photos. Lean: allowed, said on the slide's Manual page.
- Dragging in the eyepiece: inverted like the knobs, or map-style. Lean: inverted, the real behaviour.
- Short video clips for swimming. Lean: no video; sprites keep the file small.
