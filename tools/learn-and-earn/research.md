# Research packet: learn-and-earn
Concept: Run a kid's bike repair business for a year: spend weeks learning new skills or buying better tools, and watch how many bikes you fix and what you earn compared with a friend who never trains.
Grade: 4   Standards: D2.Eco.6.3-5, NSPFE.Earning Income

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Eco.6.3-5: "Explain the relationship between investment in human capital, productivity, and future incomes." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain how dedicating time and resources to learning new skills increases work output per hour and leads to higher future income.
- NSPFE.Earning Income (Standard 4-2): "People may be able to improve their ability to earn income by gaining new knowledge, skills, and experience." (https://www.councilforeconed.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/2021-National-Standards-for-Personal-Financial-Education.pdf). The student must be able to give concrete examples of how acquiring technical skills and equipment enables a worker or small-business operator to earn more money.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing revenue with profit: Fourth graders commonly think all incoming cash from customers is pure profit, overlooking the money spent on replacement tubes, patches, and tools (Council for Economic Education National Standards).
- Thinking productivity only means physical exertion: Students often believe increasing output requires working longer hours or sweating harder, rather than using better equipment and smarter methods (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Page One Economics).
- Ignoring opportunity cost: Students frequently view training as free if there is no upfront class fee, forgetting the income lost while spending time learning instead of working (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Page One Economics).
- Assuming higher prices always boost total income: Students often assume charging twice as much always doubles earnings, ignoring that customers may reject poor service or take bikes to competitors (NCSS C3 Framework Economics).

## Sensitivity

- Socioeconomic sensitivity: Be thoughtful that some students come from families with limited financial buffers where time away from earning is difficult to afford. Present the bike repair business as an empowering neighborhood project without moral judgment on families facing financial strain.
- Safety standards: Emphasize standard bicycle safety protocols (NHTSA brake and tire checks, wearing properly fitted helmets).
- None seen for religion or living culture.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Lemonade Stand (classic economics simulation): Teaches pricing, inventory costs, and weather-driven demand for a youth business.
- Practical Money Skills Peter Pig's Money Counter (https://www.practicalmoneyskills.com): Interactive youth games teaching basic earning, spending, and saving.
- BizWorld Elementary Entrepreneurship Simulation (https://www.bizworld.org): Classroom business simulation where students design products, purchase materials, track expenses, and calculate net profit.

## Gaps

- Specific bike repair pricing for youth: Real neighborhood kid bike repair businesses operate on informal cash rates (e.g. $5 to fix a flat tire, $10 for a full tune-up), so Curio Class spec writers can use realistic round dollar amounts suitable for fourth-grade mental math.
