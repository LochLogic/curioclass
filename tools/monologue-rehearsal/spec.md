# Monologue Rehearsal

## One idea
Score a monologue the way actors do (given circumstances, beats, a want and a tactic for each beat), perform it out loud for a partner who taps the words where they felt you change and names what they think you wanted, and the overlay shows which of your choices actually read and which stayed in your head.

## Not like
- director-s-chair (9-12, story): you direct one scene across five theaters and watch blocking from the seats. Here you are the actor, alone with one speech, and the response comes from a real listener in the room.
- gallery-critique (9-12, roleplay): argues claims about a painting. The roleplay here is performing a character, and the evidence is what your partner felt.
- character-voice-booth (3-5) and speech-coach: change how lines sound. Nothing here is recorded or played back; the actor speaks, and the tool records only the plan and the partner's taps.
- stage-lighting-plot (this band): the same era of realist theater from the designer's side. Here the text, the want and the delivery are the subject.

## Who and how
- Grade 9-12, theater, module "Acting a Monologue (Theater)".
- Topic line: preparing and performing a monologue
- Driver: students in pairs on one laptop (the actor preps alone, then hands the laptop to a partner for Perform). 30 to 45 minutes; work is saved on this device.
- Standards: NCAS TH:Pr4 (explore and refine a character through given circumstances and choices), TH:Pr6 (perform for a specific audience), TH:Cn10 (relate a character's beliefs to your own).

## Screens and modes
Header: title, topic line, `Read`, `Motion`, `Sound`. Kit theme `theme-912`. Modes (`.seg`): `Choose` (default), `Prepare`, `Rehearse`, `Perform`, `Compare`. Prepare, Rehearse and Perform open once a monologue is chosen; Compare once a performance is logged.

**Choose.** Focus: four monologue cards, large, each with its painted setting. Each card: who speaks, the play, the author and year, length in words and about how long it runs, one line on the situation. Tap a card to read the full text in a reader; `Choose this one`.

**Prepare.** Focus: the script, center and largest, with the current tab's questions beside it. Two tabs.
- `Circumstances`: six questions, each a text box (200 characters): Who am I? Where am I? When is it? Who am I talking to? What do I want? What is in my way? Under the first four, `What the play says` reveals the facts from the play (Content) to check your answer against.
- `Score`: the script as words you can mark. Tap the space between two words to put a beat change there (a "/" mark; tap again to remove); each beat gets a row in the panel with `Tactic` (picker from 16 verbs), `Pace` (slow, middle, fast), `Volume` (quiet, middle, full), `Body` (still, move toward, move away, sit, stand, turn away). Tap a word and choose `Stress` (underlined) or `Pause after` ("//"). `Clear marks`. One `Want` box for the whole speech (from Circumstances, editable here too) and a `Main obstacle` box.
- Three versions: `Version A`, `Version B`, `Version C` buttons above the script; each holds its own marks and tactics (the circumstances are shared). Copy buttons: `Copy A to B`, and so on.

**Rehearse.** Focus: the prompter (the script in large type with the version's marks: beat bars in the accent, tactic verbs in the margin, stresses underlined, pauses as "//", pace and volume as small icons), filling the stage. Pick a version. `Start` shows the first beat highlighted; `Next beat` / `Back` (also Space and Shift+Space) move the highlight while you speak aloud; a timer shows elapsed time (no limit, no score). `Switch version` keeps the beat position. Panel: the beat's tactic, pace, volume and body choice in large words, and a one-line rehearsal tip per tactic (Content).

**Perform.** Focus: the partner's script, filling the stage. First the actor picks an audience (four cards, Content; each shows its note) and the version to perform, then taps `Hand to partner`. The screen turns to the partner view: the plain script (no marks), large, and a line: "Listen. Tap any word where you feel the actor change what they're doing." Each tap puts a small flag on that word (tap again to remove). `Done` asks the partner two things: `What did they want?` (pick one of six wants, Content, shuffled, plus `Something else` with a box) and `One word for how it felt` (a text box, 20 characters). `Save` logs the performance and returns the laptop to the actor (Compare opens).

**Compare.** Focus: the overlay script, filling the stage: the actor's beat marks above each line and the partner's flags below, in two shapes and colors. Matches are joined by a short bracket. Panel: "Your partner felt 3 of your 5 changes." / "They also felt a change at 'Be gone!' that you didn't plan." / "You wanted: to make them ashamed. They heard: to make them ashamed." (or the mismatch). A list of performances (date, audience, version) to compare any of them. Under it, `Connect`: three reflection boxes (300 characters each): "What does your character believe?", "Where do you agree or disagree with them?", "What did this audience change about how you played it?". `Save on this device` is automatic; `Clear all` (confirm).

## Content
**Monologues** (text from Project Gutenberg editions, public domain; Shakespeare line breaks kept; double hyphens in Glaspell set as commas or "...", house rule; [...] marks a cut).
1. Viola, Twelfth Night, William Shakespeare (about 1601), Act 2, Scene 2. 207 words, about 1 minute 25 seconds. Situation: "Malvolio has just thrown a ring at her feet, saying it is from Olivia." Text: Gutenberg 1526 from "I left no ring with her; what means this lady?" to "It is too hard a knot for me t'untie!" (24 lines, exactly as in the eBook).
   What the play says: Who: "Viola, a young woman shipwrecked in Illyria, disguised as a young man called Cesario, serving Duke Orsino." Where: "A street in Illyria." When: "Just after leaving Olivia's house, where she carried Orsino's love message." To whom: "Herself (and the audience)."
   Wants (partner's choices): to figure out what just happened; to warn herself; to blame the disguise; to pity Olivia; to hand the problem to time; to get back to Orsino.
2. Marullus, Julius Caesar, William Shakespeare (1599), Act 1, Scene 1. 179 words, about 1 minute 10 seconds. Situation: "Ordinary Romans have taken the day off to cheer Caesar's victory over Pompey's sons." Text: Gutenberg 1522 from "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?" to "That needs must light on this ingratitude." (24 lines).
   What the play says: Who: "Marullus, a tribune, an official who speaks for the common people." Where: "A street in Rome." When: "The day of Caesar's triumph." To whom: "A crowd of workers dressed in their best."
   Wants: to make them ashamed; to send them home; to remind them of Pompey; to warn them; to win them to his side; to show off his power.
3. Helena, A Midsummer Night's Dream, William Shakespeare (about 1595), Act 1, Scene 1. 208 words, about 1 minute 25 seconds. Situation: "Her friend Hermia has just told her she will run away tonight with Lysander." Text: Gutenberg 1514 from "How happy some o'er other some can be!" to "To have his sight thither and back again." (26 lines).
   What the play says: Who: "Helena, a young woman of Athens who loves Demetrius, who used to love her." Where: "Athens, by the Duke's palace." When: "Just after Hermia and Lysander leave her." To whom: "Herself (and the audience)."
   Wants: to make sense of love; to win Demetrius back; to feel less alone; to get even with Hermia; to make a plan; to stop caring.
4. Mrs Hale, Trifles, Susan Glaspell (first performed 1916). About 160 words (the cut below), about 1 minute 5 seconds. Situation: "In a neighbor's empty farmhouse kitchen, while the men search upstairs for evidence." Teacher note on the card: "The play is about a woman held for her husband's death; this speech is about loneliness and neighbors." Text (Gutenberg 10623, Plays by Susan Glaspell, two speeches joined): "I could've come. I stayed away because it weren't cheerful, and that's why I ought to have come. I, I've never liked this place. Maybe because it's down in a hollow and you don't see the road. I dunno what it is, but it's a lonesome place and always was. I wish I had come over to see Minnie Foster sometimes. I can see now... [...] I wish you'd seen Minnie Foster when she wore a white dress with blue ribbons and stood up there in the choir and sang. Oh, I wish I'd come over here once in a while! That was a crime! That was a crime! Who's going to punish that? [...] I might have known she needed help! I know how things can be, for women. I tell you, it's queer, Mrs Peters. We live close together and we live far apart. We all go through the same things, it's all just a different kind of the same thing."
   What the play says: Who: "Mrs Hale, a farm wife, neighbor of Minnie Wright (once Minnie Foster)." Where: "The Wrights' farmhouse kitchen, cold and left half cleaned." When: "A winter morning, the day after John Wright was found dead." To whom: "Mrs Peters, the sheriff's wife."
   Wants: to confess she failed a friend; to make Mrs Peters understand; to defend Minnie; to accuse the men; to forgive herself; to keep a secret.

**Tactics** (16 verbs, each with a rehearsal tip under 80 characters): to beg ("Lower, closer, smaller."), to scold ("Short sentences, hard consonants."), to tease ("Lift the ends of lines."), to warn ("Slow down on the danger word."), to shame ("Let silence do the work after the question."), to charm ("Smile into the vowels."), to puzzle out ("Let the thought arrive on the line, not before."), to reassure ("Warm and steady; no rush."), to accuse ("Point the words at one person."), to confess ("Quiet, and don't look away."), to rally ("Build each line bigger than the last."), to dare ("Stillness, then a push."), to mock ("Copy their voice, then drop it."), to plead ("Reach with the voice and the hands."), to comfort ("Soften the ends of lines."), to challenge ("Lean in; make them answer.").

**Audiences** (card note shown in Perform):
- Audition panel: "Three strangers at a table. Say your name and the piece, then begin. They're deciding."
- Your class: "People who know you. Commit fully; they'll notice if you hold back."
- Younger students: "Grade 6 visitors. Say who the character is and what just happened before you start."
- Community night: "Families in a school theater. Speak to the back row."

## Interactions in detail
- Words are tokens split on spaces; punctuation stays with its word. Beat marks sit between tokens (an index); a mark before the first word is not allowed (the start is not a change).
- Matching: a partner flag matches a planned beat change when it sits from 2 words before to 6 words after the mark (listeners react a moment late). Each flag matches at most one mark, the nearest. Unmatched flags are reported as "felt but not planned". The want matches when the partner picked the want the actor chose from the same six (the actor's Want text box is free text, so in Score the actor also picks `Closest want` from the six; the box stays their own words).
- Extremes: no beat marks at all gives "You planned no changes. Your partner felt (n)." No partner flags gives "Your partner didn't feel a change. Try bigger contrasts between beats." Every word flagged is capped at 30 flags ("That's a lot of flags; pick the strongest moments.").
- Rehearse: Space never scrolls the page; the highlight scrolls the prompter so the current beat sits in the upper third.
- Perform hides the actor's marks completely in the partner view (the DOM holds no mark text, so a curious partner can't peek with Read either: Read speaks only the instruction line there).
- Nothing is recorded: no microphone, no camera. A line in Perform says so.
- Saving: localStorage `curio-monologue-rehearsal` (choice, circumstances, three versions, performances with audience, version, flags, want, word, reflections). `Clear all` empties it.
- Sizes: the prompter uses 28 px type at 1366 x 768 and never less than 20 px at 680 x 425; at 375 wide Score uses a bottom sheet for the beat row.

Not used by any 9-12 arts neighbor or another spec in this band. `Curio.voice`, scope = mode. The monologues themselves are never spoken by the voice (the student speaks them).
- welcomeChoose (how; Choose entered; stale once a card is tapped): "Tap a monologue to read it. Then tap Choose this one."
- welcomeCirc (how; Prepare entered with empty boxes; stale once a box is typed in): "Answer the six questions. Tap What the play says to check yourself."
- scoreHow (how; Score tab first opened; stale once a beat mark is placed): "Tap between two words where your character changes tactics."
- welcomeRehearse (how; Rehearse entered; stale once Start is tapped): "Tap Start, then say it out loud. Tap Next beat as you go."
- breath (how; the first Start in Rehearse; stale after the first Next beat): "Breathe out first. Then begin."
- handHow (how; Perform entered; stale once Hand to partner is tapped; needs `#hand`): "Pick an audience and a version, then tap Hand to partner."
- partnerHow (how; the partner view opens; stale once a flag is placed): "Listen. Tap a word whenever you feel the actor change."
- landed (event; each Compare result where every planned change matched; stale when another performance is opened): "Every change you planned, they felt."
- wantRead (event; each Compare result where the want matched; stale when another performance is opened): "They heard exactly what you wanted."

## Done when
1. Focus: the cards in Choose, the script in Prepare, the prompter in Rehearse, the partner script in Perform and the overlay in Compare are each the largest thing on screen at 1366 x 768 and 680 x 425.
2. The four texts match their Gutenberg eBooks word for word (except the stated dash changes and cuts).
3. Beat marks, stresses and pauses can be placed and removed by pointer and keyboard; each version keeps its own.
4. The partner view shows no marks, and Read there speaks only the instruction.
5. The matching example in Sources gives "1 of your 4 changes" with the right "felt but not planned" list; the extremes give their lines.
6. Next beat and Back move the prompter highlight at once; Space never scrolls the page.
7. Performances survive a reload and can be compared one after another; Clear all empties storage.
8. How lines once each in five runs through the modes; landed and wantRead every time they happen.
9. All 9 images load with no people and no lettering.
