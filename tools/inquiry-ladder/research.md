# Research packet: inquiry-ladder
Concept: A big question about the past (Was the railroad good for our town?) sits at the top; students climb by adding supporting questions drawn from old photos, timetables and letters, mark words people disagree about, and see each rung help.
Grade: 4   Standards: D1.2.3-5, D1.3.3-5, D1.4.3-5, D2.His.12.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D1.2.3-5: "Identify disciplinary concepts and ideas associated with a compelling question that are open to different interpretations." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to spot that big historical questions involve concepts like progress, fairness, or cost that different people understand differently.
- D1.3.3-5: "Identify the disciplinary concepts and ideas associated with a supporting question that are open to interpretation." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to see that smaller supporting questions also contain ideas open to interpretation.
- D1.4.3-5: "Explain how supporting questions help answer compelling questions in an inquiry." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain how answering smaller factual questions builds evidence to resolve a large overarching question.
- D2.His.12.3-5: "Generate questions about multiple historical sources and their relationships to particular historical events and developments." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to ask questions about how multiple historical records (photos, timetables, letters) connect to a historical event.

## Real data

- Library of Congress Railroad Maps Collection (1828 to 1900): https://www.loc.gov/collections/railroad-maps-1828-to-1900/ (Public domain). Contains over 600 maps showing routes, depots, and land grant parcels.
- Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Online Catalog: https://www.loc.gov/pictures/ (Public domain). Holds 19th-century street scenes, depot construction, and locomotive imagery.
- National Archives Milestone Documents: https://www.archives.gov/milestones/pacific-railway-act (Public domain). Original 1862 legislative text, maps, and land grant records.

## Common mistakes

- Believing historical questions have only one correct answer: Fourth graders often assume historical questions have a single right answer in a textbook, rather than interpretations based on evidence (NCSS C3 Framework).
- Treating all primary sources as unbiased facts: Students frequently view letters or newspapers as objective truths rather than expressions of personal viewpoints or commercial promotions (Library of Congress Primary Source Teacher Guides).
- Assuming railroads benefited every resident equally: Students tend to overlook that while depot merchants prospered, local craftspeople faced factory competition and Indigenous communities lost homelands (National Museum of the American Indian NK360).
- Imagining time was always standardized: Students assume identical clock times existed across towns, unaware that communities kept separate solar noon times before 1883 (Smithsonian NMAH On Time).

## Sensitivity

- Native American displacement: Railroad expansion was directly linked to military campaigns, treaty violations, and the destruction of bison herds that supported Plains nations. Spec writers should reference Indigenous resources such as the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian (Native Knowledge 360).
- Immigrant labor conditions: Chinese and Irish rail laborers faced dangerous working conditions, unequal wages, and discrimination. Portray their contributions and struggles with historical respect.
- No graphic violence: Focus on economic and social change rather than disaster details.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Library of Congress Primary Source Analysis Tool (https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/primary-source-analysis-tool/): Interactive observe, reflect, and question scaffolding for inspecting historical documents.
- C3 Teachers Inquiry Design Model (https://c3teachers.org/inquiry-design-model/): Inquiry units that scaffold a compelling question with supporting questions and source tasks.
- Stanford History Education Group / Digital Inquiry Group (https://sheg.stanford.edu): Lesson units that evaluate competing historical documents to answer an overarching question.

## Gaps

- Specific town names and case studies: Curio Class tools can use either a composite 1880s American rail town or a specific historical town (such as Promontory, Utah, or Abilene, Kansas), which the spec writer can select based on available public-domain photographs and archival letters.
