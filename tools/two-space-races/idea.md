# Space Race (idea card)

- Module: social-studies-11-cold-war-america, grade 11. Codes: D2.His.1.9-12, D2.His.6.9-12
- Format / family: race / none. Fit: change to timeline, because a beat-the-clock race teaches speed, while D2.His.6 needs the student to compare how each side wrote the same events
- One idea: Put the space race from Sputnik to Apollo 11 on two scoreboards, one written by Soviet sources and one by American sources, and find where the two histories disagree, what each left out, and how later historians filled the gaps.
- How it plays (solo student by default):
  - First screen: two scoreboards side by side, Moscow and Washington, at October 4, 1957, with Sputnik's beep.
  - Core loop: place each milestone (Sputnik, Explorer 1, Gagarin, Shepard, the first spacewalk, Apollo 1, Apollo 11) on both timelines; each comes with a period account from that side's press. The student flags what each account stresses or hides (Gagarin's parachute landing kept secret, Korolev's name kept secret until his death, failures on both sides, the women and Black mathematicians left out of US accounts for decades).
  - Context strip: each milestone links to Cold War politics of that moment (the missile gap, Kennedy's 1961 challenge, Vietnam's budget pressure).
  - The aha or the end state: a third "historians later" timeline; the student writes how each side's writers shaped the story and what was unique about the times that made space a battlefield.
  - Class mode or extras (optional): a race-style quiz round for review, as a side mode only.
- Standards, one line per code:
  - D2.His.1.9-12: the context strip shows how each milestone was shaped by its moment in the Cold War.
  - D2.His.6.9-12: the two scoreboards and the later timeline show how each set of writers shaped the history they produced.
- Not like: cold-war-crisis-room (decisions in 1962, grade 10); decades-explorer (since 1980).
- Art: public domain NASA photographs and generated Soviet-era scenes, plus people (illustrated) for figures without free photos, because NASA's archive is public domain and vivid.
- Proposed title: Two Space Races (the plain name matches older video games and many products)
- Proposed concept line: Place the space race from Sputnik to Apollo 11 on two timelines written by Soviet and American sources; find where they disagree, what each hid or left out, and how historians later filled the gaps.
- Flags: none
