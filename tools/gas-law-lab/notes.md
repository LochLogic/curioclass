## What it teaches

Students use an ideal gas model to measure how pressure, volume, amount and temperature relate. They collect two sets
of volume and temperature data, fit straight lines, and compare their extrapolated zero-volume temperatures.

## Key skills

- Change one condition while keeping a fair comparison.
- Read pressure, volume, temperature and amount with units.
- Record data and choose useful graph axes.
- Distinguish measurements from extrapolation beyond the measured range.
- Connect gas particle motion and the energy stored in lifted weights.

## Support at home

- Remember a tied balloon you have seen. Talk about how its size might change between a warm room and a cooler room.
- Ask your student why a line can tell us about temperatures outside the measured range, and what assumptions that needs.
- Discuss how a pump compresses air and why squeezing air feels different from squeezing water.

## A 5-minute start

Open Bench. Tap Hot water, watch the piston rise and the particles move, then Record. Add a weight and watch the gas
shrink. Record again and open Notebook. These two rows change more than one factor: use them to discuss a fair test,
then start Absolute zero for a controlled experiment.

## Modes

**Bench:** explore Piston, Can and Balloon. Use Pump and Valve for the first two. A tied balloon contains 0.100 mol;
altitude sets its outside pressure. The can's volume stays at 250 mL. The piston has a 300 mL stop and holds at most
four 5.00 kg weights. Record captures the current gas temperature, even while it approaches the bath temperature.
Notebook stores rows on this device only, with no transmission. Delete row and Clear notebook remove saved data.
Deleting a bench row leaves the guided fits intact; deleting a set B row keeps the valid set A fit for comparison.

**Absolute zero:** collect at least four distinct gas temperatures spanning 60 °C for set A. Fit V against T in °C.
For set B, change just the amount, weights or gas and collect another set with the same span. Changing gas alone
keeps the same slope. Changing amount or pressure makes a different slope. The result card reports both crossings
and their mean against the accepted −273.15 °C.

Real lab is on by default in Absolute zero. A seeded error adds at most ±0.1 percent to volume and ±0.2 °C to
temperature. Exact mode turns this reading error off. Repeating a measurement at one temperature does not count as
four temperatures. Four distinct bath settings are required as well, preventing repeated taps during a single warm-up
from completing a set. Mixed setups and piston-stop rows are saved as bench rows but do not qualify the guided sets.
No points, timer or competitive score is attached to this measurement activity.

## Talk questions

- Which conditions must stay fixed for volume to be proportional to temperature in kelvin?
- Why does helium move faster than nitrogen at the same temperature, despite equal mean translational energy?
- Where does bath energy go when a weighted piston rises? Compare the particle-motion and weights-lifted bars.
- Which part of your fitted line is measured, and which part is extrapolated?
- Why does changing gas alone leave the volume unchanged in this ideal model?

## Good to know

All physical readouts use PV = nRT, with R = 8.314462618 J/(mol·K), T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15, N_A = 6.02214076 × 10²³/mol,
k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K and g = 9.80665 m/s². One weight adds 24.516625 kPa on a 20.0 cm² piston. Atmospheric pressure
at sea level is 101.325 kPa. This is a frictionless, weightless piston model. Real balloons need a pressure difference
to stretch their rubber; here inside pressure equals outside pressure. The can's 200 kPa cutoff is a model limit,
not a safe rating for any real can. Never heat or pump a real sealed container as a classroom imitation.

The particle magnifier is a scaled, two-dimensional picture. Wall-hit counts are only for the picture, not an SI
pressure measurement. Every other number is independent of it. Helium and argon are atoms; the conventional
kinetic-theory label “molecules” includes these gas particles. Color and size both distinguish the three species.
Motion off freezes the picture with speed arrows, while temperature changes become immediate.
To reproduce the R check with the shown kPa and mL, first divide P × V by 1000 to obtain joules.

Mean translational kinetic energy is (3/2)kT per molecule, and (3/2)nRT for the gas. Nitrogen also stores rotational
energy, shown as a lighter bar segment so the combined bar is (5/2)nRT over this temperature range. The weights bar
is m g Δh since the last Record; a negative value means energy was released as the weights descended.
The motion bar uses one fixed scale for each apparatus, so adding gas visibly increases the bar. The weight bar shows
the magnitude up to 2 J, with the signed numeric value always shown; larger magnitudes fill the bar.

The dashed graph is an ideal gas extrapolation. Real gases condense before their volume becomes zero. This tool
does not model condensation, quantum motion or a physically reachable zero-volume gas. It teaches classical gas
laws as a route to HS-PS3-2: represent large-scale energy as particle motion and position (the lifted weights).
