# Research packet: barter-bay

Concept: Fishers, bakers and potters on Barter Bay trade by swapping until shells become money and trading speeds up; then the class adds too many shells, too few, or closes the cannery to see inflation, deflation and unemployment.

Grade: 4   Standards: D2.Eco.5.3-5, D2.Eco.11.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Eco.5.3-5 asks students to explain the role of money in making exchange easier. Swapping goods directly (barter) requires finding someone who wants what you have, whereas money acts as a widely accepted medium of exchange.
- D2.Eco.11.3-5 asks students to explain how macroeconomic shifts (inflation, deflation, and unemployment) affect different groups of people across a community.

## Real data

- Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis: Three functions of money: medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.
- Federal Reserve Bank of Boston History of Colonial Money: Massachusetts Bay colony adopted wampum shell beads as legal tender in 1637 before commodity oversupply and wear caused demonetization in 1661.
- Bureau of Labor Statistics: Definition of unemployment requiring lack of work, active job search, and availability.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing price increases for one item with inflation: Fourth graders often mistake one expensive item for inflation; inflation requires a sustained increase across the general price level.
- Assuming barter is simple: Students often underestimate the difficulty of a "double coincidence of wants" (e.g., a potter needing fish must find a fisher who specifically wants a pot right now).
- Viewing falling prices as purely good: Students often think deflation is ideal because items cost less, missing that deflation can cause businesses to lay off workers and freeze investment.

## Sensitivity

- Economic hardship: Portrayals of cannery closures and job loss should remain grounded in economic mechanics without sensationalizing family distress or poverty.
- Cultural handling of shell currencies: Cowrie shells and wampum held deep ceremonial and cultural meanings for Native American and West African nations, not merely arbitrary tokens.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta: "Econ Express" interactive modules on money and inflation.
- Council for Economic Education (EconEdLink): Elementary barter vs. money simulations ("Why Do We Need Money?").

## Gaps

- Fourth graders understand the three functions of money and why money is easier than barter, but Curio Class tools should keep the mathematical ratios simple (1 shell = 1 loaf of bread) so arithmetic does not obscure macroeconomic cause-and-effect.
