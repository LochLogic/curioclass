## What it teaches
Students use indicator colors, conductivity, dilution and carbonate reactions as evidence for identifying clear liquids.
They compare equal pH with very different acid amounts and interpret a virtual neutralization graph.

## Key skills
- Choose a test that separates two possible liquids.
- Read a color result together with its written name.
- Keep a fair comparison by taking fresh samples.
- Distinguish the acid currently split into ions from the total acid available to react.
- Read the sharp change and plateau in a neutralization graph.

## Support at home
- Ask your student why two bottles with the same pH might need different amounts of baking soda in a virtual test.
- Talk about how a label and adult instructions keep a household chemical safe. Leave all containers closed.
- Ask your student to describe one test result that ruled out a possible identity. No home chemistry experiment is needed.

## Modes
Cases has three independently accessible investigations. The identification case has a seeded New shelf option.
The second case compares 10 mL of vinegar and dilute hydrochloric acid, first by baking soda amounts, then by tenfold
dilution. Take a fresh sample before the dilution comparison. The third case requires Tell the teacher before any
addition. Its graph uses 50 mL of 1.0 mol/L HCl. Reset spill clears the virtual spill and reinstates that report gate.

Bench exposes all seven named liquids and tests, with a reference ladder of food pH ranges. It does not award case
completion. The ladder values are references, not predictions for every brand or sample.

## Talk questions
- Which test separated two liquids that looked identical?
- Why were the conductivity bulbs similarly dim for vinegar and dilute HCl?
- Why did a tenfold dilution change the two acid readings differently?
- What happens to pH when baking soda is added beyond the modeled acid endpoint?

## Good to know
This is a virtual investigation, not an instruction to handle or neutralize a real chemical spill. Students tell a
teacher about every real spill. Use goggles, never taste or smell a chemical, and follow school laboratory rules.
Never mix bleach with acids or ammonia. ACS secondary school safety guidance supports these rules.

All concentrations, volumes and readings are a simplified model at 25 °C. Weak acids and bases use exact quadratic
solutions. Strong acid calculations include water at extreme dilution. Weak-acid/base quadratics are floored at neutral
under extreme dilution. Baking soda and borax buffer pH stays fixed, including at dilutions where a real buffer would
fail. The conductivity thresholds are an instructional model based
on total ions, not measured brightness or electrical conductivity. Different ions have different mobilities.

During soda addition the specified model consumes acid stoichiometrically, uses the remaining acid concentration for
pH, ignores volume changes and lets carbon dioxide escape. It does not solve a complete acetic acid/acetate titration
equilibrium. Methyl orange turns yellow before acid exhaustion in real titrations, so the modeled "used up" endpoint
and fizz status, rather than the indicator alone, define the task's endpoint. Other additions and indicators are
treated as diagnostic tests and do not form a general mixture simulator. Vinegar drops mark a reacted sample; take a
fresh sample before dilution.

The sodium bicarbonate molar mass is 84.007 g/mol. In C2 the exact calculated amounts are about 0.699442 g and
0.00336028 g. Displayed reference amounts are rounded to 0.699 g and 0.0034 g; with 0.001 g steps, the first exhausted
sample occurs at 0.700 g and 0.004 g. The ratio is 208.15, shown as approximately 208. C3 needs 4.20035 g, rounded
to 4.2004 g; 4.200 g is still acidic at pH 4.08 and 4.201 g is pH 8.35. No finer step is provided.
"Harmless overshooting" is not a claim about real spill cleanup: only the modeled pH plateau is shown.

## Cold-review clarifications
The equal-pH case's paired bars show the student's measured button endpoints, 0.700 g and 0.004 g, on one scale.
The model amounts and the approximately 208 ratio still use the unrounded stoichiometric calculation.
After an incorrect explanation, each dilution must be repeated from a newly taken fresh sample.
Vinegar drops consume the diagnostic sample; every later test or soda addition requires Fresh sample.
Conductivity after acid and soda includes the sodium and counterions left in solution. This remains an ion-count
brightness model; the pH calculation retains the spec's simplified residual-acid approximation.
