## What it teaches
Students compare a message's sender and real link destination with known addresses, notice pressure and requests for secrets, and explain why a message deserves reporting. They distinguish an expected attachment or normal deadline from a suspicious one.

## Key skills
- Read the actual sender address, beyond the display name.
- Inspect a link destination before trusting its label.
- Check the full site name in a sign-in address bar.
- Use context to distinguish ordinary reminders from pressure.
- Report suspicious requests and check through a route you already know.

## Support at home
- Discuss who a child could tell if a message feels worrying or asks for secrecy.
- Invent a message together and spot what makes its request unexpected.
- Ask how you would check a request in person, using a trusted number or opening an app yourself.

## A 5-minute start
1. In Practice, inspect the school email's sender and hover its link.
2. Press Report, tap the evidence, and press Done. Use the Why arrows to revisit each tell.
3. Press Next. Compare a normal deadline or an expected attachment with a suspicious request.
4. Open Tells, then try an example. Return to Arcade and press Start round.

## The three modes
- Practice has no clock and no score. Report allows multiple evidence selections before Done; Why compares distinct tell types found and other parts selected. Empty selections and selecting everything do not earn the evidence-success message. Repeated phrases for the same tell count once; a deadline in the subject is valid evidence too. The highlighted tells remain available for learning.
- Arcade lasts 60 seconds or 20 messages, whichever comes first. Keep a safe message for 1 point. Report a phish for 1, or 2 if your first tell attempt is right within 4 seconds. Wrong calls get 0. Streak has no multiplier. Maximum 30.
- Tells gives eight examples and the safe next steps. Its Opened labels record visits this page load, not correctness.

## Talk about it
- Why can a normal deadline be fine while an urgent threat is suspicious?
- Why is a familiar name or logo insufficient evidence?
- What changes between the real and fake sign-in pages?
- How could you check an alert without using its link?

## Good to know
All six places, people and messages are invented. Addresses end in reserved .example or .test domains; all phone numbers are fictional. Links never navigate and sign-in fields cannot accept typing. Best is stored on this device only; Clear best asks Yes or No. The kit also remembers Motion.

K keeps, R reports, keys 1 to 8 tap spots in reading order, and Enter activates the focused button. Read speaks the current hint. Sound uses browser speech in this Build-only version; voice recordings are not made here. Motion off removes animation without shortening the clocks or result pauses.

These 20 scenarios are a practice set, not a universal test of safety. A familiar-looking sender, saved contact or expected file is context, not proof that a real message is safe. When unsure, check independently and tell a trusted adult. A score is not a security certification.

## Sources and scope
FTC Consumer Advice, How To Recognize and Avoid Phishing Scams and verification-code guidance; CISA phishing guidance; NIST, Phishing; RFC 2606/6761; NANPA fictional numbers. No attack procedure or real credentials are used.

This tool practices the phishing and social-engineering part of CSTA MS-SYS-SE-33. The module also lists MS-SYS-SE-32, which requires explaining failures of confidentiality, integrity and availability; this tool does not teach that whole standard. It needs a separate activity elsewhere in the module. The spec's 2017 crosswalk 2-NI-05 is broader than this tool's digital-message scope.
