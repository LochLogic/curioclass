# Research packet: storm-helpers
Concept: After a big storm the class walks a street to see who is affected and how (no power, a flooded road, a closed school), meets the people already helping, then picks a way kids can help and makes a poster about it.
Grade: 2   Standards: D2.Geo.12.K-2, D4.6.K-2, D4.7.K-2, D4.3.K-2

## Standards in plain words
- D2.Geo.12.K-2: "Identify ways that a catastrophic disaster may affect people living in a place." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Explain how severe storms disrupt daily community life, such as knocking down power lines, flooding streets, and closing schools.
- D4.6.K-2: "Identify and explain a range of local, regional, and global problems, and some ways in which people are trying to address these problems." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Identify community problems caused by a storm and describe the jobs done by emergency crews and community volunteers to fix them.
- D4.7.K-2: "Identify ways to take action to help address local, regional, and global problems." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Choose an age-appropriate, safe action kids can take to help their neighborhood recover after a storm.
- D4.3.K-2: "Present a summary of an argument using print, oral, and digital technologies." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Create an informational poster that clearly communicates a storm safety rule or a way neighbors can support one another.

## Common mistakes
- Underestimating moving water: Second graders often think shallow floodwater is harmless to play in, not realizing 6 inches of water can knock an adult off their feet (Source: National Weather Service Flood Safety Guide).
- Believing downed power lines are safe if quiet: Children often think electric wires must hiss, hum, or throw sparks to be live, when deadly lines can appear completely inert (Source: NWS Electrical Storm Safety).
- Assuming kids cannot help after a storm: Students frequently assume disaster response belongs exclusively to adults with heavy trucks, overlooking community actions like safety awareness posters and canned food drives (Source: FEMA Ready Kids Ready 2 Help Program).
- Confusing storm response with personal danger: Children sometimes try to act as direct first responders rather than following safety rules to keep themselves and others out of harm's way (Source: American Red Cross Prepare with Pedro).

## Sensitivity
- Trauma and natural disaster anxiety: Some children have lived through terrifying hurricanes, tornadoes, or house fires. Frame the walk and story around calm recovery, community solidarity, and cooperative helpers rather than fear, panic, or horrific devastation.
- Socioeconomic disparities in storm recovery: Lower-income families and renters often have fewer resources (no backup generators, older housing, food loss from power outages). Avoid framing that blames victims or implies suffering damage was due to personal carelessness.
- Clear adult supervision boundary: Make it clear that children must never handle electrical wires, tree-cutting tools, or deep floodwaters; their contribution is communicative, supportive, and safe.
