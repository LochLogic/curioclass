# Living Earth (Curiosity No. 04)

## The real thing
A seismologist's map station: a globe of every large earthquake since 1970 and the world's volcanoes, laid over the
real shape of the sea floor, with the tools a seismologist uses on it. Seismologists at the USGS National Earthquake
Information Center locate quakes from station records within minutes; geologists slice the quakes into
cross-sections to see sinking plates; geophysicists send waves through model Earths; plate modellers rewind the
continents. Living Earth puts all five on one globe, driven by the real USGS catalog.

## The first two minutes
- Open: a dark station room. The globe turns slowly in the middle, sea floor in deep blues with the ridges showing as
  pale seams, land in muted earth tones, no dots yet. Under it a brass time dial reads 1970. A drum seismograph along
  the bottom scratches a quiet trace. One quiet line on the glass: "Drag to spin. Press play."
- First action with nothing read: drag the globe, or press the big play pad.
- First reaction: fifty-six years of quakes flash on in about twenty seconds, each a dot that blooms and settles,
  sized by magnitude, coloured by depth. The plate edges draw themselves out of the dots: the Ring of Fire, the
  thin line down the middle of the Atlantic. The drum trace jumps with each big one.

## Things to do
1. Press play and watch the plate edges draw themselves out of fifty-six years of quakes.
2. Spin to Japan, set the depth fader to 300 km and deeper, and see the deep quakes sitting inland of the trench.
3. Slice across the Peru-Chile Trench: the quakes trace the sinking plate down hundreds of kilometres.
4. Slice across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and see the opposite: a line of shallow quakes with nothing beneath.
5. Raise the magnitude floor to 8 and find where the giants happened; tap one for its date, depth and place.
6. Find the quake: drop three stations, let a real past quake strike, read each S-P gap, and watch the circles meet.
7. Send a wave through the Earth and find the far-side shadow zone the S waves never reach, wherever you start.
8. Rewind 200 million years into Pangaea and watch the Glossopteris and Mesosaurus fossil belts join up.
9. Stop the rewind at 100 million years ago: the Atlantic is a narrow sea.
10. Pin your town and read its nearest plate edge, how fast your plate moves, and the nearest volcano.
11. Turn on plate-motion arrows and find two plates heading straight at each other.
12. Find Hawaii, far from any plate edge, and follow the chain of older islands to the northwest.
13. Compare the visible-quake count around the Pacific rim with the rest of the world.

## Controls
Controls sit around the globe like a station console; the active instrument is chosen from a toolbelt of five brass
tool tabs on the right edge (Slice, Stations, Wave, Rewind, Pin). Choosing a tool never hides the globe.
- Globe: drag to spin, wheel or pinch to zoom (1x to 6x). Arrow keys spin 5 degrees, Shift 20; + and - zoom.
- Time dial: brass dial 1970 to 2025, one-year detents, drag or Left and Right (Shift for 10 years). Play pad: Space.
- Speed knob: 1, 3 (default), 10 or 30 years per second, by detent. Keys [ and ].
- Magnitude floor fader: 5.0 to 9.0, step 0.1. Focused: Up and Down, Shift for 0.5.
- Depth fader: two handles, 0 to 700 km, step 10 km. Tab between handles, Up and Down to move.
- Layer switches: toggles for Quakes (Q), Volcanoes (V), Plate edges (P), Sea floor relief (F), Motion arrows (A).
- Slice tool: drag a line up to 3,000 km (keyboard: Enter sets each end at the globe centre); the section opens with
  its vertical exaggeration shown.
- Stations tool: tap three places to drop stations; Strike pad (key S) picks a real past quake from a curated set;
  each station's trace has a draggable P marker and S marker (Left and Right nudge 0.5 s); Circles pad draws the
  three circles; Reveal pad shows the catalog epicentre.
- Wave tool: tap a source; Fire pad (Enter); a scrub fader for elapsed time 0 to 25 min; P and S toggles.
- Rewind tool: deep-time dial 0 to 200 Ma, 1 Ma steps (Shift 10); toggles for fossil belts and mountain belts.
- Pin tool: tap the globe or type a place in a search box (bundled city list); Escape removes the pin.
- Power panel (Motion, Sound, Read): lower-left beside the plate, styled as station toggle switches.

## Readouts
Monospace, typewriter amber on dark slate, in a survey-label strip along the top and in each tool's panel.
- YEAR (four digits) and QUAKES (visible count). Rewind: MA (whole) and the model name.
- Selected quake: date (YYYY-MM-DD), M (one decimal), DEPTH km (whole), LAT and LON (two decimals, N/S, E/W).
- Slice: LENGTH km (whole), DEEPEST km (whole), QUAKES in section (whole).
- Stations: S-P per station in s (one decimal), DIST km (nearest 10), OFFSET to catalog epicentre km (after Reveal).
- Wave: T+ minutes and seconds; ARRIVAL at the cursor for P and S in min:s, or SHADOW.
- Pin: PLATE name, EDGE distance km (nearest 10), SPEED cm per year (one decimal) and heading degrees, QUAKES within
  300 km since 1970 (whole), nearest VOLCANO and its distance km.

## The Manual
Contents: what the dots are and what is left out; reading depth colours and magnitude sizes; how each tool works
(slice, stations, wave, rewind, pin); how the S-P gap gives distance; honesty notes; sources.

Things people have found:
- The deepest quakes, under Tonga and Fiji, are nearly 700 km down, inside the sinking Pacific plate (USGS, Earthquake
  Hazards Program, "Deep earthquakes").
- About 81 percent of the world's largest earthquakes happen around the rim of the Pacific (USGS FAQ, "Where do
  earthquakes occur?").
- The largest earthquake ever recorded was magnitude 9.5, in Chile on 22 May 1960 (USGS, 1960 Valdivia event page).
- S waves cannot cross the liquid outer core, which leaves an S-wave shadow beyond about 104 degrees from a quake;
  that is how we know the outer core is liquid (USGS, "This Dynamic Earth"; Gutenberg 1914).
- Inge Lehmann found the solid inner core in 1936 from faint P waves arriving inside the P-wave shadow zone (USGS,
  "Inge Lehmann" profile; Lehmann 1936).
- The Atlantic widens about 2.5 cm a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow (USGS, "This Dynamic Earth").
- Hawaii's volcanoes are thousands of kilometres from any plate edge; the island chain gets older to the northwest as
  the plate moves over a hot spot (USGS, "This Dynamic Earth", hotspots).
- The same fossil reptile, Mesosaurus, is found only in southern Africa and South America (USGS, "This Dynamic
  Earth", historical perspective).

## Real material and sources
- Earthquakes: USGS ANSS Comprehensive Catalog (ComCat), every event of magnitude 5.0 and up from 1970-01-01 to
  2025-12-31 (about 75,000 events). USGS data is US public domain (USGS copyright policy; credit USGS). Packed as
  binary (time as days, lat and lon at 0.01 degree, depth in km, magnitude in tenths: about 9 bytes each), gzipped
  and base64-encoded, unpacked with DecompressionStream. About 650 KB. If over budget, M5.5 and up (about 25,000
  events, about 250 KB) and the Manual says so.
- Relief: NOAA NCEI ETOPO 2022 (CC0 1.0, verified on the NOAA dataset record; cite DOI 10.25921/fd45-gt74). A shaded
  2048x1024 equirectangular texture as WebP (about 300 KB) and a 0.5 degree int16 elevation grid for slice profiles,
  gzipped (about 330 KB as base64).
- Coastlines and places: Natural Earth 50m coastline and populated places (public domain, verified on
  naturalearthdata.com terms). About 120 KB.
- Plates, plate speeds and Rewind: Merdith et al. 2021, "Extending full-plate tectonic models into deep time",
  Earth-Science Reviews 214; model files on Zenodo (doi 10.5281/zenodo.4485738), CC BY 4.0 (verified on the record).
  Present-day plate polygons at 0.1 degree plus each plate's rotation pole every 5 Ma to 200 Ma, rotated in the
  browser. About 120 KB.
- Volcanoes: NOAA NCEI Global Volcano Locations Database, about 1,500 volcanoes (CC0 1.0 on data.gov, verified),
  compiled from the Smithsonian's Volcanoes of the World. About 25 KB. The Smithsonian GVP data itself is flagged and
  not used: its terms bar commercial use without written permission.
- Earth model: P and S speeds by layer from PREM (Dziewonski and Anderson 1981) and travel times from iasp91
  (Kennett and Engdahl 1991), as published numbers. Ray tracing in code; a 0 to 180 degree travel-time table, about
  5 KB. Station traces are drawn in code from those arrival times; they are not recorded seismograms.
- Fossil and rock belts: hand-drawn region outlines after the USGS "This Dynamic Earth" figure (public domain,
  Kious and Tilling 1996). About 10 KB.
- Total goal 2 MB (estimate 1.8 MB with code). Hard ceiling 6 MB; the only reason to pass 2 MB is keeping every M5.0
  quake, and notes.md says so in a `Size:` line.

## Honesty notes
Said in the Manual:
- Only quakes of magnitude 5 and up are shown (or 5.5, whichever ships); millions of smaller ones are left out.
- Magnitudes before the 1970s-80s were measured with older scales; a few early values are less exact.
- Slice depths are true; the surface relief in the section is exaggerated so it shows, and the panel says by how much.
- Station traces are drawn from model travel times, not recorded; real records are noisier.
- The cut-away Earth uses a layered model (PREM); the real interior has lumps it smooths over.
- Rewind is one published model (Merdith et al. 2021); other models differ in the details, especially further back.
- Disasters are shown as data: place, date, depth and size, never casualty counts or pictures of harm.
