# Research packet: ripple-street

Concept: Make one kid's Saturday choices on Ripple Street (rake leaves for pay or go to the park, plant flowers, play loud music) and see the costs and benefits to the kid, the rewards and fines that nudge each choice, and the ripples on neighbors.

Grade: 3   Standards: D2.Eco.1.3-5, D2.Eco.2.3-5, D2.Eco.8.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Eco.1.3-5 asks students to compare the benefits and costs of individual choices. Every Saturday decision involves weighing personal gains against personal trade-offs.
- D2.Eco.2.3-5 asks students to identify positive and negative incentives that influence the decisions people make. Rewards like pay nudge behaviors forward, while rules and fines discourage disruptive actions.
- D2.Eco.8.3-5 asks students to explain how external benefits and costs (externalities) affect neighbors and community life, leading choices to ripple beyond the person making them.

## Real data

- EPA 1974 Levels Document recommendations: 55 dBA outdoor day-night average level ($L_{dn}$) to prevent activity interference and annoyance in residential neighborhoods; 45 dBA indoor residential guideline.
- USDA Forest Service / US Fish and Wildlife Service: 75% to 80% of flowering plants depend on animal pollinators to reproduce.
- Council for Economic Education Standard 4: predictable response to positive (rewards) and negative (penalties) incentives.

## Common mistakes

- Treating incentives as only monetary: Third graders often think incentives are only money, missing non-monetary incentives like social praise, satisfaction, tiredness, or community goodwill.
- Confusing personal costs with externalities: Students often confuse personal sacrifices (getting blisters raking leaves) with spillover costs on neighbors (blowing leaves onto a neighbor's sidewalk).
- Assuming all actions affect only the actor: Students frequently ignore unintentional ripples on bystanders, such as noise disturbing a sleeping infant next door.

## Sensitivity

- Neighborhood socioeconomic disparities: Avoid portraying yard upkeep, lawns, or equipment as universally available; keep homes varied and relatable (apartments with balconies, shared sidewalks, yards).
- Financial situations: Raking leaves for pay should not frame earning money as trivial or excessive; respect that kids and families have varied financial contexts.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- EconEdLink (Council for Economic Education): Elementary interactive lessons on opportunity cost and choices ("The Grasshopper and the Ant", positive vs. negative incentives games).
- Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis: "Ella's Adventures: Opportunity Cost" and Economic Lowdown video modules on externalities and incentives.

## Gaps

- Grade 3 students can understand immediate neighborhood cause-and-effect (noise bothers neighbor, flowers please neighbor), but formal terminology like "marginal analysis" or "Pigovian subsidy" should be simplified into plain terms like ripples, nudges, costs, and benefits in Curio Class tools.
