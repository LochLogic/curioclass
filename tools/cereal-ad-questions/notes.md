## What it teaches

Children identify music, pictures and words as parts of an advertisement. They compare their own reactions, discuss what an ad wants them to think, and distinguish selling messages from useful everyday information.

## Key skills

- Notice how sound, images and language work together.
- Compare a reaction before and after one part changes.
- Give a reason for an interpretation of a message.
- Ask whether an ad can support what it suggests.
- Find selling messages in familiar places.

## Support at home

- At breakfast, look at a package you already have. Ask what its picture wants you to notice.
- On a walk, find one sign that sells something and one that helps people stay safe.
- Make up a cheerful tune for an ordinary household object. Talk about whether the tune changes the object itself.

## A 5-minute start

1. Tap Play ad. Zoom-O's and Comet are made up for this activity.
2. Ask who wants it and who is not sure. Tap the plus buttons to enter the hands. A child alone can add one vote.
3. Tap Music, then Play ad. Vote again. Compare the two pairs of bars.
4. Continue taking away Pictures and Words until only the plain bowl remains.
5. Ask when the class wanted it most. Accept unchanged votes as well as changing votes. The class's answers are evidence, not a required result.

## The three modes

**The ad:** five three-second shots form a fifteen-second commercial. Each switch works during playback. Votes belong to the most recently completed version, including when switches changed during it. A version's latest votes replace its old votes. Each bar holds zero to forty hands; the scale uses the largest count with a floor of ten. There is no combined class-size limit.

**What it wants:** choose one of five possible messages, then replay Music, Pictures or Words in isolation. True? opens the exact discussion answer. The Words clip plays the opening slogan for three seconds. The supplied spec also asks for all five slogans in that three-second clip, which cannot be spoken clearly; the full ad contains all five.

**Ads in my day:** explore Breakfast, Street and Store. Each scene contains four ads and two objects serving other purposes. Objects become outlined after a tap. Only ads add to the counter; the activity has no timer or penalties. The family photograph shows people together. The tablet banner, billboard and bus poster have fictional selling messages, so children can point to evidence before tapping.

## Talk about it

- Did taking out the music change your vote? Why might someone else's vote stay the same?
- Did the ad say the cereal makes you strong, or did its pictures suggest that?
- Does a free toy tell us what the cereal tastes like?
- What makes a stop sign different from a store sign?
- Could an ad be fun to watch without making us want the product?

## Good to know

- Teacher led for K-2, but all controls also work for a child exploring alone.
- M, P and W toggle Music, Pictures and Words. Space plays or stops. Enter activates a focused button. Up and Down change Want it votes after a completed ad.
- Motion off preserves the full timing and sound while holding each picture still.
- Sound off mutes music and automatic narration. Read speaks the current hint or open answer card. Read stops an ad before speaking.
- Start over asks before clearing the ad votes. Switching modes keeps work in memory. Reloading clears it. The shared kit remembers the Motion preference on this device.
- No recordings were created in this Build. Fixed narration uses the browser fallback until the requested news-reader and announcer recordings are supplied.
- Interpret the research examples as examples of selling placement, not claims about every shop or every package.

## Sources and standards

The source of lesson content is the supplied spec: NCAS Media Arts K-2, MA:Re7 (parts), MA:Re8 (messages), MA:Re9 (evaluate the changed ad), and MA:Cn11 (ads in daily life).

Advertising context: American Psychological Association, *Report of the APA Task Force on Advertising and Children* (2004); Common Sense Media K-2 media literacy lessons. Placement examples: Musicus, Tal and Wansink (2015), *Eyes in the Aisles*, Environment and Behavior 47(7), 715-733; Center for Science in the Public Interest, *Temptation at Checkout* (2015). These named sources were independently checked in the cold review. The shelf-height study is a historical sample of ten Eastern U.S. stores, not a rule for every store.

All products, slogans, music and mascot identities in the commercial are fictional. Illustrations generated with OpenAI gpt-image, edited for Curio Class. Sound synthesized in the browser.

## Review source links and limits

- APA (2004), Advertising and Children: https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/advertising-children. Selling intent, mascots and persuasive messages need adult discussion at this age; this activity does not prove protection against advertising.
- Musicus, Tal and Wansink (2015 issue; online 2014), Eyes in the Aisles: https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916514528793. The lower-shelf example is supported by this specific sample, without generalizing its exact heights.
- CSPI (2015), Temptation at Checkout: https://www.cspi.org/resource/temptation-checkout. Checkout placement encourages purchases; shops vary. The spec dates the report to 2016, but it appeared in 2015.
- NCAS Media Arts: https://www.nationalartsstandards.org/content/media-arts. K-2 activities address identifying components, interpreting messages, comparing effects and daily-life contexts.
- Votes are opinions entered by the teacher, not a knowledge score. One child alone can enter one hand per replay.
- The day counter records objects explored, not demonstrated skill. Tapping every object reveals all six explanations. Ask children to name what each ad wants before tapping.
- Here we use ad to mean a selling message. Publicity can also promote a free event; the free library poster is treated as community information in this activity.
- Do not require tasting cereal: taste preferences cannot be verified by an ad, and no food is needed for this activity.

Safety-sign wording was checked against FHWA, MUTCD Section 2B (https://mutcd.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/11th_Edition/mutcd11thedition.pdf), and OSHA, Young Worker Safety, Clean-up (https://www.osha.gov/etools/young-workers-restaurant-safety/clean-up). The stop sign directs drivers to stop; the wet-floor sign warns about a hazard, rather than preventing a slip by itself.

Common Sense Education, Understanding Ads (Grade 1, updated July 2025): https://www.commonsense.org/education/digital-literacy/understanding-ads; Family Tips: https://www.commonsense.org/education/family-tips/k-5-news-and-media-literacy. These support looking for intent and evidence in packages, billboards and other media, without promising a particular change in preference.
