## What it teaches

Students use drops at four places to support an argument that Earth's gravity pulls toward its center.
They then predict the direction of down at unfamiliar places and compare each prediction with the plumb line.

## Key skills

- Compare a falling object's path with a plumb line.
- Transfer a direction from a local yard to a globe.
- Choose evidence that tests a claim about direction.
- Explain why several observations support an argument.
- Test a prediction once, then learn from its measured error.

## Support at home

- Ask your child to point toward the ground and explain where that direction leads on a round Earth.
- Hold a small object still, release it safely over a soft surface, and describe its path together.
- Imagine a family on the opposite side of Earth. Ask where their feet, ground and down would be.

## A 5-minute start

1. Tap Drop in Anchorage. Compare the trail and brass plumb bob.
2. Tap Mark on globe. Turn the arrow with the buttons or drag its head, then tap Record.
3. Visit Quito and Sydney. Record their arrows too. A miss offers a turned yard as a scaffold.
4. In Argue, choose a claim, attach the three places and tap Check claim.
5. Explain the reasoning and try a new-place prediction. Each place allows one prediction.

## The four modes

**Drop:** apple, rubber ball and evacuated feather tube at four school locations. Complete all four records to unlock Zoom out.
The local yards deliberately keep ground at the bottom of the screen. The paintings and children are illustrative, not portraits
of actual pupils or verified views from particular schools. Anchorage, Quito and Sydney are town locations; Esperanza has a real school.

**Globe:** spin Earth, turn X-ray and Extend lines on or off, and stand up to eight extra children on its surface.
Use the arrow keys to spin a focused globe; Enter stands a child at the facing point. Replay repeats the four-record reveal.

**Argue:** attach at least three place records. Feather tube and Other worlds are supplementary evidence about speed,
and never replace place records about direction. Dashed white arrows show the selected claim; solid arrows show recorded evidence.
After a fitting claim, Test it offers new places with alternating sides. Turn, Predict, then Next place.
Strong requires a fitting claim, three place records, at least eight words of reasoning and three consecutive successful predictions.
The word count does not judge scientific reasoning. The teacher must read it. Filler can satisfy the word count, but cannot replace
the independent direction predictions. A wrong prediction resets the streak; changing the claim or attached evidence starts a new argument.

**Other worlds:** apples drop from 1.5 m on Earth, Moon and Mars. Slow motion starts on here and scales time by ten.
The readouts always show real fall times, not slow-motion viewing times. Tap Drop all again to restart all three.

## Talk about it

- Why did every object look as if it fell the same way in the yard?
- Can arrows pointing different ways in space describe the same rule?
- Which records distinguish a pull toward the center from a pull toward the South Pole?
- Why does evidence about how fast something falls not settle which way it falls?
- If a prediction misses, what can it tell you about your claim or your arrow?

## Good to know

The spherical Earth model follows the NGSS 5-PS2-1 clarification. Earth's mean radius is 6,371 km in this model.
Real Earth is slightly flattened and rotating. The local vertical need not pass exactly through the geometric center;
the geodetic/geocentric latitude difference reaches about 0.2 degrees, with additional local gravity deflections.
The tool's 8-degree tolerance is much larger than these refinements.

The drop model uses constant gravity, no air resistance and y = g t squared divided by two.
Earth uses 9.81, Moon 1.62 and Mars 3.71 meters per second each second. The display rounds these to 9.8, 1.6 and 3.7.
Their 1.5 m fall times round to 0.55, 1.36 and 0.90 seconds. Other-world scenery and planets are not to scale.
The evacuated feather tube stays on its stand while its feather and apple fall together inside it.
It shows equal falling acceleration without air resistance; it does not model pressure or air drag.

Saved records, reasoning and tests stay only in this browser on this device. Storage failures do not prevent play.
Motion off removes movement but preserves the real landing times. Fixed narration currently uses browser speech;
the upbeat-guide manifest is ready for the separate recording pipeline. No recordings were made in this build.
