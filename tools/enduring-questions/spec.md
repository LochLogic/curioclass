# Enduring Questions

## One idea
Something you wonder about today ("Should voting be required?") sits on top of an argument people have had for centuries: the tool lifts the wonder to the lasting tension underneath it, lets you walk that argument's long thread of voices from 1788 to 1928, seats four experts at a table to show they mostly agree on the facts and split on what matters most, and ends with the three supporting questions you would research next.

## Not like
- Question Sharpener (ela 12, this group): prices a project question in hours and sources. Here the question is lifted, not narrowed: from a wonder to the enduring issue, then down to supporting questions.
- Source Portfolio (library 9-12, this group): builds a balanced set of sources. Here there are no sources to collect; experts and historical voices are read to find where disagreement really lies.
- panel-of-experts (6-8, not specced): a radio panel of experts from different disciplines. Here the experts sit at one table and the point is the line between facts they share and values they don't.
- founding-words-explorer and premise-inspector (ela 9 and 11, wave 4 argument group): read founding documents for their structure and later echoes. Here founding-era voices are short entries on a thread of one tension, beside later voices.
- Socratic Seminar Board (ela 10, wave 4): runs a live class discussion. Here the student works alone or in pairs before any discussion.
- fake-or-fact-lab (6-8 library, wave 1): judges one page. Here every voice is real or clearly labeled composite; judging is not the task.

## Who and how
- Library, grades 9-12, module Enduring Questions: "From a wonder to a compelling question".
- Topic line: "finding the enduring issue behind a question".
- Standards: AASL.Explore, D1.1.9-12, D1.2.9-12, D1.3.9-12.
- Driver: students on their own laptops or in pairs; projectable for a class launch. 20 to 35 minutes.
- tool.json: driver "students", minutes "20-35", modes [] (five steps, pure explore), grades_label "Grades 9-12". Kit starter with `kit:theme-912` (dark) and an accent override. No family.

## Screens and modes
Kit frame. Header: "Enduring Questions"; Motion, Sound, Read. Every step sits over the painted night ridge (art eq-night-ridge). A path of five painted lanterns (art eq-lantern-lit and eq-lantern-dark) across the top, one per step, each lit when visited: **Wonder**, **Underneath**, **The long argument**, **Expert table**, **Supporting questions**. Any lantern can be opened at any time once a wonder is chosen.

Focus per step: the step's own centerpiece is the biggest, central thing, with the lantern path small above it: the three wonder cards (1), the beams (2), the thread of voice cards (3), the table and then the agreement map (4), the eight question cards with the set check beside them (5).

**1 Wonder.** Three wonder cards on the night sky, each with its painted picture (art eq-wonder-ballot, eq-wonder-phones, eq-wonder-camera): "Should voting be required?", "Should schools collect phones at the door?", "Should the town put cameras on Main Street?". A fourth card, **My own wonder** (art eq-wonder-own), opens a text box (one line, up to 120 characters, stays on this device). Choose one to go on.

**2 Underneath.** The wonder sits on a ledge at the top; below it, four **tension beams**, each a painted wooden plank (art eq-beam-plank) resting on a painted stone (art eq-beam-stone), with a code word at each end: **Liberty and order**, **Individual and community**, **Security and privacy**, **Majority rule and minority rights**. Tap the beam you think holds the wonder up (one or two). Then **Show the usual reading** marks the beams most writers see under it (Content), with one plain sentence on why. (For My own wonder there is no usual reading; the button is hidden and the hint says "Your call. Say why in the frame below.") Below the beams, the **compelling question builder**: "How should a democracy balance [A] and [B] when [situation]?" with A and B filled from the chosen beam (swappable) and the situation chosen from three chips per wonder (Content) or typed. Under it, the **Why it lasts** sentence: "This question reflects the enduring issue of [tension] because [reason chip]." (three reason chips per tension, Content). The finished question and sentence show in the panel from here on.

**3 The long argument.** A thread across the sky for the chosen tension: 3 or 4 **voice cards** in date order, each with the speaker, year, a short context line, the quotation, and a **Who was left out** line where the speaker's world excluded people (Content). Tap a card to open it full; **Then and now** under each asks the student to pick which side of the beam the voice leans to (two buttons), and the beam at the top tips a little with each answer. A note at the end of the thread: "People have argued this for centuries because both sides protect something real."

**4 Expert table.** A painted round table with four empty chairs (art eq-table); a code seat card sits at each chair; each seat is a composite expert (a labeled role, not a real person) with a card of two or three short claims. Each claim has a tag to set: **Fact** (could be checked with evidence) or **Value** (about what matters most). After the student tags, **Show the map** draws the **agreement map**: claims as rows, experts as columns, each cell Agrees, Disagrees or No view, with the reading "They agree on n of n facts and split on n of n values." The student's tags and the key are both shown; differences are explained, never scored.

**5 Supporting questions.** Eight candidate questions for the wonder, each with a discipline tag (History, Civics, Economics, Geography, Data). Pick three. The **set check** reads four lines: three chosen; at least two disciplines; at least one about facts and one about values or views; none answered by a single fact and none off the question. Decoy questions explain themselves when picked ("A single search answers this one."). **Copy my plan** copies the compelling question, the why sentence and the three supporting questions as plain text.

Panel (right): `#hint`, **My question** box (the compelling question and why sentence, once built), and **Start over** (asks Yes / No; clears this device's saved work).

## Content
Wonders, usual readings and situation chips:
- Voting: usual beams Individual and community (main), Majority rule and minority rights. Why: "A duty to vote asks each person to give up a choice for the sake of everyone being counted." Situations: "turnout is low", "some groups vote much less than others", "people disagree about whether staying home is a choice".
- Phones: usual beams Liberty and order (main), Individual and community. Why: "A school rule trades students' freedom for a calmer room for everyone." Situations: "phones distract in class", "families want to reach students", "students are learning to manage their own time".
- Cameras: usual beam Security and privacy (main), Liberty and order. Why: "Cameras may deter crime, and they record everyone who walks by." Situations: "crime has risen downtown", "the cameras would store video for months", "shop owners and walkers disagree".

Reason chips (Why it lasts), per tension: Liberty and order: "every government needs power and needs limits", "each new tool raises the old question again", "people trust power differently in calm and in crisis". Individual and community: "people owe something to each other and also own their own lives", "the balance shifts as a community changes", "both selfishness and conformity cause harm". Security and privacy: "safety and being left alone both matter", "new technology changes what can be seen", "courts have changed their answers over time". Majority rule and minority rights: "democracy counts votes and also protects people who lose them", "today's majority can be tomorrow's minority", "who counts as the people has changed".

The long argument (public-domain texts quoted exactly from the named editions; context lines and left-out lines are written for Curio Class):
- Liberty and order:
  - Federalist No. 51, 1788 (published under the name Publius; generally credited to James Madison): "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary. In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself." Context: an essay urging New York to ratify the Constitution. Left out: "Madison enslaved people; the Constitution he defended counted enslaved people as three fifths of a person for representation and gave them no rights."
  - Henry David Thoreau, "Resistance to Civil Government" (later called "Civil Disobedience"), 1849: "The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right." Context: written after a night in jail for refusing a tax he linked to slavery and the war with Mexico.
  - Abraham Lincoln, message to Congress, July 4, 1861: "It forces us to ask, Is there in all republics this inherent and fatal weakness? Must a government of necessity be too strong for the liberties of its own people, or too weak to maintain its own existence?" Context: less than three months after the Civil War began.
- Individual and community:
  - Alexis de Tocqueville, Democracy in America, vol. 2 (1840), Henry Reeve translation, book 2, ch. 2: "Individualism is a mature and calm feeling, which disposes each member of the community to sever himself from the mass of his fellow-creatures; and to draw apart with his family and his friends; so that, after he has thus formed a little circle of his own, he willingly leaves society at large to itself." Context: a French visitor describing Americans. Left out: "The America he toured in 1831 held about two million people in slavery and was removing Native nations from their lands."
  - John Stuart Mill, On Liberty, 1859: "That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." Context: an English philosopher on how far society may push a person.
  - Franklin D. Roosevelt, fireside chat, September 6, 1936: "In a physical and a property sense, as well as in a spiritual sense, we are members one of another." Context: a radio talk after touring drought states.
- Security and privacy:
  - Fourth Amendment, ratified 1791: "The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized." Left out: "In 1791 these protections did not reach enslaved people."
  - Olmstead v. United States (1928), Chief Justice Taft for the Court, on police wiretaps: "The amendment does not forbid what was done here. There was no searching. There was no seizure. The evidence was secured by the use of the sense of hearing and that only."
  - Justice Brandeis, dissenting in the same case: "Experience should teach us to be most on our guard to protect liberty when the government's purposes are beneficent." Context line: "The Court later overruled Olmstead in Katz v. United States (1967)."
- Majority rule and minority rights:
  - Federalist No. 51, 1788: "It is of great importance in a republic not only to guard the society against the oppression of its rulers, but to guard one part of the society against the injustice of the other part." (Same left-out line as above.)
  - Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1801: "All, too, will bear in mind this sacred principle, that though the will of the majority is in all cases to prevail, that will to be rightful must be reasonable; that the minority possess their equal rights, which equal law must protect, and to violate would be oppression." Left out: "Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people over his life. In 1801 women, enslaved people and, in most states, men without property could not vote."
  - Mill, On Liberty, 1859: "'the tyranny of the majority' is now generally included among the evils against which society requires to be on its guard." (shown with the source's double quotation marks).

Expert table (composite experts; each card says "A composite role written for Curio Class, summing up views found in published work. Not a real person."). Facts are checked in Sources; values are positions, not facts. Keys: F fact, V value; A agrees, D disagrees, N no view.
- Voting. Experts: a political scientist, a civil liberties lawyer, an election official, a historian. Claims: F1 "Australia has required voting in federal elections since a 1924 law, and turnout there is usually around 90 percent." (A, A, A, A). F2 "About 67 percent of voting-age US citizens voted in 2020." (A, A, A, N). V1 "A duty to vote is a fair price for a shared government." (A, D, N, A). V2 "Choosing not to vote is itself a kind of speech." (D, A, N, D). Reading: agree on 2 of 2 facts, split on 2 of 2 values.
- Phones. Experts: an education economist, a school counselor, a student-rights advocate, a principal. F1 "A 2016 study of schools in four English cities linked phone bans to higher test scores, most for lower-achieving students." (A, A, A, A). F2 "A 2025 study of English secondary schools found no difference in students' wellbeing between strict and relaxed phone rules." (A, A, A, A). V1 "Fewer distractions are worth less freedom at school." (A, N, D, A). V2 "Students learn self-control by practicing it, not by having the choice removed." (N, A, A, D). Reading: agree on 2 of 2 facts, split on 2 of 2 values.
- Cameras. Experts: a criminologist, a privacy lawyer, a shop owner, a police chief. F1 "A review of 40 years of studies found public cameras linked to a modest drop in crime, largest in parking lots." (A, A, N, A). F2 "In 1928 the Supreme Court allowed wiretaps without a warrant; in 1967 it overruled that decision." (N, A, N, A). V1 "Being recorded in public is a fair trade for safety." (N, D, A, A). V2 "Knowing you are watched changes how freely you act." (A, A, D, N). Reading: agree on 2 of 2 facts, split on 2 of 2 values.

Supporting questions (eight per wonder; discipline; F fact-leaning, V value-leaning, X decoy):
- Voting: "How did Australia come to require voting, and what changed after?" History F; "Who votes and who doesn't in the United States?" - Phones: "What did schools that collected phones find?" Data F; "How have schools handled new technology before, like calculators?" Geography F; "Who should decide school rules, and how?" Civics V; "Do most students own a phone?" X (one fact); "Which phone is fastest?" X (off).
- Cameras: "What happened to crime where towns added cameras?" Data F; "How have courts ruled on watching people in public?" History F; "What do shop owners and walkers each want most downtown?" Civics V; "What would cameras cost to buy and run each year?" Economics F; "Where on Main Street do problems actually happen?" Geography F; "Who should be allowed to see the video, and for how long?" ## Interactions in detail
- Wonder: choosing a different wonder later asks "Start this wonder fresh? Your work on the other one is kept." Each wonder keeps its own saved work.
- Beams: one or two beams can be chosen (a third replaces the oldest). In step 3, each Then and now answer tips the beam 2 degrees toward that side (each tension has 3 voices, so at most 6 degrees; the cap is 8). Show the usual reading never removes the student's choice; it adds a second marker and the sentence.
- Then and now: two buttons per voice (leans to the left word or the right word); there is no key, and the beam's tilt is the student's own reading.
- Map reading: a claim counts as agreed when no expert disagrees and at least two agree; as split when at least one agrees and one disagrees. "They agree on [n] of [facts] facts and split on [n] of [values] values" counts facts agreed and values split by that rule.
- Tags: Fact and Value per claim; Show the map turns on when every claim is tagged (until then the hint counts the claims left: "Two claims still need a tag.") and then shows the key beside the student's tags, with one line for each difference ("You tagged this a fact. It's about what matters most, so evidence alone can't settle it.").
- Set check lines turn from open circles to filled ones as each rule passes; text always says pass or not yet.
- Store: the chosen wonder, my-own text, beams, question, reason, voice leanings, tags and picks save to localStorage `eq-work` (try/catch). Start over (after Yes) clears all of it. Nothing is sent anywhere.
- Keyboard: lantern path, then the step's content in reading order; beams and chips are buttons; the expert table seats are a list. Visible focus everywhere.
- Sizes: at 1366x768 and 1280x720 the beams sit in a 2 by 2 grid and the voice thread runs left to right. At 680x425 the beams stack in one column with short beams, and the thread scrolls sideways inside its own box. At 760 and 375 wide the lantern path becomes a numbered strip, the thread runs top to bottom, and the expert table becomes four stacked cards with the map as a table below.
