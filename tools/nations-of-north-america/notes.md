## A 5-minute start

1. Read the welcome together. Ask: what would you need to live on this land long ago: food, shelter, a way to travel?
2. Tap the **Northwest Coast**, then the **Great Basin**. Compare: how did the land change food and homes?
3. Switch to **Which region?** and match the clues to regions on the map.

## The big idea

Long before Europeans arrived, hundreds of Native nations lived across North America. Each nation's way of life fit its land: what grew there, which animals lived there, how cold or dry it was. These nations are still here today, with their own governments, languages and cultures.

## About the regions

Ten of the regions are the "culture areas" scholars often use to compare ways of life in the United States and Canada. The eleventh, Northern Mexico, covers the deserts and the Sierra Madre south to about where Mesoamerica begins (scholars group it with the Greater Southwest and Aridoamerica). They are a tool for comparing, not boxes nations fit into: borders are approximate, homelands overlapped, and nations moved and traded across them. Southern Mexico and Central America, home to many more nations, are outside this tool.

## Talk about it

- Pick two regions. How did the land shape what people ate and how they built homes?
- Why did people in some regions move with the seasons, while others built towns that lasted for centuries?
- Which nations' homelands are where our school is today? (A tribal nation's own website is the best source.)
- What does "these nations are still here" mean? What might a student in one of these nations do today that is the same as you?

## Good to know

- Tap any nation's name to open a few more sentences about it: its language, something it is known for, and its life today.
- Nations are named the way they name themselves where that is common, with the more familiar English name too, for example Diné (Navajo) and Nimiipuu (Nez Perce).
- The tool describes ways of life before European contact in the past tense and the nations in the present tense. Avoid "the Native Americans used to..." as if they are gone.
- The 1830s removals of Southeast nations are mentioned briefly. They are hard history: teach them with care and with sources from the nations themselves.
- The paintings show the land and animals only, never people. Pictures of Native people should come from the nations themselves.
- This content has not yet been reviewed by tribal educators. Good sources for your own lessons: the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian (Native Knowledge 360°) and the education pages of individual tribal nations.
