# Research packet: town-council

Concept: Sit on the town council as the empty lot becomes a park or a parking lot; hear neighbors who disagree, speak in turn, learn what the mayor and council each decide, and vote on a compromise.
Grade: 2
Standards: D2.Civ.1.K-2, D2.Civ.5.K-2, D2.Civ.9.K-2, D2.Civ.10.K-2

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Civ.1.K-2 (Describe roles and responsibilities of people in authority): Second graders learn that the mayor leads town departments and carries out laws, while the town council listens to neighbors, debates ideas, and votes on plans.
- D2.Civ.5.K-2 (Explain what governments are and some of their functions): Second graders understand that local government makes decisions about shared community spaces, budgets, and neighborhood rules.
- D2.Civ.9.K-2 (Follow agreed-upon rules for discussions while responding attentively to others when addressing ideas and making decisions as a group): Second graders follow meeting rules: taking turns speaking, listening attentively to differing viewpoints, and not interrupting.
- D2.Civ.10.K-2 (Compare their own point of view with others' perspectives): Second graders compare different neighbor viewpoints (like wanting a quiet green park versus wanting parking for downtown shops) and craft a compromise.

## Real data

Local government decision roles:

| Role | Position type | Main civic responsibility | What they do at the meeting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mayor | Executive leader | Leads municipal government and carries out council laws | Presides over meeting, introduces the lot problem, explains city budget limits |
| Town Council Member | Legislative representative | Represents neighborhood residents and votes on ordinances | Listens to testimony, asks questions, proposes compromises, and casts a vote |
| Neighbor A (Mr. Silva, parent) | Resident / Witness | Advocates for children's outdoor play and neighborhood safety | Speaks for 2 minutes explaining why nearby children need grass and playground swings |
| Neighbor B (Ms. Chen, shop owner) | Local business owner | Advocates for downtown business vitality and accessibility | Speaks for 2 minutes explaining that customers cannot find parking to shop locally |
| Town Planner | Municipal staff advisor | Provides technical land facts and site dimensions | Presents lot map showing the parcel is 100 feet wide by 150 feet long |

Hearing debate and compromise options:
- Option 1 (All Park): Full lawn, trees, playground. Fails business parking need (0 spaces).
- Option 2 (All Parking): Paved asphalt, 40 parking spaces. Fails recreation need (no play space, noisy).
- Option 3 (Compromise: Pocket Park & Perimeter Parking): Front half becomes a fenced community pocket park with swings, trees, and benches; rear half becomes a 15-space permeable parking lot with a planted hedgerow barrier.

Council meeting procedure for Grade 2:
1. Gavel: Mayor calls the town meeting to order.
2. Testimony: Neighbor A and Neighbor B present their viewpoints in turn.
3. Discussion: Council members ask questions and discuss trade-offs.
4. Compromise: Council introduces the combined park-plus-parking proposal.
5. Roll call vote: Each council member votes "Aye" or "Nay".

## Common mistakes

1. Assuming the mayor decides everything alone like a king, overlooking the town council's legislative and voting power.
2. Believing one side in a neighborhood debate must be all bad or evil, rather than recognizing both sides have valid needs.
3. Thinking compromise means nobody gets anything, rather than both sides getting part of what they need to live well together.
4. Forgetting meeting rules and trying to speak out of turn or shout over disagreement.
5. Confusing town council decisions (local zoning, parks, streets) with national or state laws.

## Sensitivity

- Keep neighborhood disputes focused on shared civic amenities (parks, parking, sidewalks, streetlights) rather than divisive political topics.
- Model respectful listening where differing opinions are treated with dignity and fairness.
- Avoid villainizing small business owners or dismissive caricatures of neighborhood activists.
