# Research packet: the-last-carousel-ride
Concept: The town carousel stopped running in 1975; a rider and the owner's grandson remember why in different ways, and an old newspaper adds a clue. The class compares the tellings, lists possible reasons and picks the most likely.
Grade: 2   Standards: D2.His.6.K-2, D2.His.14.K-2, D2.His.16.K-2

## Standards in plain words
- D2.His.6.K-2: "Compare different accounts of the same historical event." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Listen to two people describe why the carousel closed and identify where their memories match and where they differ.
- D2.His.14.K-2: "Generate possible reasons for an event or development in the past." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Brainstorm several different explanations for why the carousel stopped operating, including mechanical breakdowns, repair costs, and fewer riders.
- D2.His.16.K-2: "Select which reasons might be more likely than others to explain a historical event or development." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Use evidence from an old newspaper article alongside the witness stories to decide which reason is the most supported by facts.

## Common mistakes
- Believing differing memories mean someone is lying: Second graders frequently assume that if a rider and the owner's grandson give different reasons, one of them is intentionally dishonest, rather than recognizing people experience events from different angles (Source: Library of Congress Primary Source Teacher Guides).
- Thinking printed newspapers are always 100 percent accurate: Children often assume anything in print is absolute truth, without realizing reporters can make mistakes or lack full insider information (Source: National Archives Document Analysis Framework).
- Searching for a single simple cause: Students often expect an event to have one isolated villain or reason, overlooking that mechanical wear, financial costs, and aging operators often combine (Source: C3 Framework History Indicators D2.His.14.K-2).
- Assuming past machines were made of modern plastics or electronics: Young children often imagine 1970s machines had microchips, not understanding wooden gears, leather drive belts, and cast-iron cranks (Source: National Carousel Association History).

## Sensitivity
- Honest treatment of community loss: Losing a beloved local ride or park can bring up wistful feelings; keep the mystery engaging and focused on problem-solving and historical discovery.
- Respect for elder oral histories: Treat both the rider's memory and the owner's family memories with dignity, showing that personal memories are vital pieces of historical evidence even when they need cross-checking.
