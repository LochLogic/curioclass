## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Read a label**. The potato chips label is up. Tap **Serving size** first, then **Servings per container**.
2. Press **3 servings (whole bag)**. The label grows a yellow column for what you really eat: 480 calories and 510 mg of sodium. Ask the class: "Who eats just 15 chips?"
3. Press **Show the 5/20 rule**. Every line now says LOW or HIGH. 5% DV or less is low, 20% or more is high.
4. Switch to **Cases** and solve case 1 together. Students pick the answer, then prove it by tapping the label line that cracks the case.

## The four modes

- **Read a label**: 24 snacks and drinks on the shelf. Tap any line of the label to learn what it means and whether it is something to get less of (saturated fat, sodium, added sugars) or more of (fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium). Change how much you eat to see the dual-column label that real packages use.
- **Compare**: two labels side by side. Pick a goal (less added sugar, less sodium, more fiber, more protein...) and the bars and verdict update. A warning appears when serving sizes differ, and **Compare whole packages** fixes it.
- **Sugar spotter**: guess the teaspoons of sugar in a snack, then watch the cubes drop. White cubes are added sugar and blue cubes are natural sugar from fruit or milk. A red line marks the most added sugar a kid should have in a whole day. **Sugar lineup** ranks all 24.
- **Cases**: ten case files about real choices (a drink with no added sugar, a low-salt bus snack, the serving-size trick, field trip rules). Three stars for the right answer and the right clue on the first try.

## What is real

- The label follows the FDA Nutrition Facts format (2016 rule), with the FDA Daily Values and rounding rules. Calcium in milk shows 25% DV, not 23%, because labels round vitamins and minerals to the nearest 5% in that range.
- Values are typical amounts for generic foods from USDA FoodData Central. Brands vary, so students should check the real package.
- Fresh fruit doesn't need a label. The apple and banana labels show what one would say.
- Added sugar: the American Heart Association says children 2 to 18 should have less than 25 grams a day. The %DV on labels uses 50 grams, the adult value.
- One teaspoon of sugar is about 4 grams.

## Talk about it

- Why does the label put serving size at the very top?
- The apple juice and the fruit drink have about the same total sugar. Why is one better?
- Plain Greek yogurt has 0 g added sugar but 4 g of sugar. Where does that sugar come from?
- No snack wins every line. How do you decide which line matters most?
- Find a real label at home and use the 5/20 rule on it.

## Good to know

- Keys 1 to 4 switch modes.
- Case stars are saved on this device only.
- The tool avoids "good food" and "bad food" language on purpose: it teaches reading and comparing, not shaming. Sports drinks, chips and soda are on the shelf because students know them.
