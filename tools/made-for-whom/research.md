# Research packet: made-for-whom
Concept: Three sources from the state's past open one at a time (a settler's diary, a railroad poster selling land, a newspaper editorial); students infer each one's audience and purpose, spot how the maker's view shaped it, and rank which helps most.
Grade: 4   Standards: D2.His.6.3-5, D2.His.11.3-5, D2.His.13.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.His.6.3-5: "Describe how people's perspectives shaped the historical sources they created." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to describe how an author's background, motives, and viewpoint influenced what they wrote and how they presented events.
- D2.His.11.3-5: "Infer the intended audience and purpose of a historical source from information within the source itself." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to examine clues within a document or image to figure out who it was created for and why it was made.
- D2.His.13.3-5: "Use information about a historical source, including the maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose to judge the extent to which the source is useful for studying a particular topic." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to judge which source provides the best evidence to answer a specific historical inquiry.

## Real data

- Library of Congress Printed Ephemera Collection: Burlington & Missouri River Railroad land sale poster (1872), https://www.loc.gov/item/rbpe.13401300/ (Public domain). Shows promotional advertising language and land grant financing.
- Library of Congress California and Western First-Person Narratives: https://www.loc.gov/collections/california-first-person-narratives/ (Public domain). Transcripts of 19th-century settler diaries, travel journals, and letters.
- Library of Congress Chronicling America: Historic American Newspapers: https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/ (Public domain). Searchable full-page newspaper scans featuring 19th-century editorials and community letters.

## Common mistakes

- Dismissing promotional sources as useless: Fourth graders frequently discard an advertising poster because it is biased, missing that it provides prime evidence of marketing strategies and corporate goals (NCSS C3 Framework).
- Assuming diaries were meant for the public: Students often assume personal diaries were written to teach future readers, rather than as private logs for daily memory or family correspondence (Library of Congress Primary Source Teacher Guides).
- Confusing the author with the printing press: Students often identify the newspaper printer or town delivery agent as the author of an editorial rather than the editor or essayist (National Archives Document Analysis Worksheets).
- Believing one single source contains the entire truth: Students often search for one definitive source that answers everything, rather than corroborating multiple sources (Stanford History Education Group).

## Sensitivity

- Promotional erasure of Indigenous homelands: Railroad advertisements routinely described lands as vacant or unpopulated, ignoring the Indigenous nations who lived there and had been forcibly displaced. Curio Class tools should prompt students to notice who is missing from promotional documents.
- Frontier hardship and grief: Settler diaries often describe extreme grief, illness, and child loss. Select age-appropriate excerpts emphasizing daily chores, weather, and settlement decisions.
- Living culture: Reference Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian (NK360) materials when discussing tribal displacement.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Stanford History Education Group / Digital Inquiry Group Sourcing Lessons (https://sheg.stanford.edu): Structured exercises training students to identify author, audience, and purpose before reading.
- DocsTeach by the National Archives (https://www.docsteach.org): Interactive activities including "Weighing the Evidence" where students rank primary source usefulness for specific questions.
- Library of Congress Primary Source Analysis Tool (https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/primary-source-analysis-tool/): Guided framework helping students observe, reflect, and question diverse printed materials.

## Gaps

- State-specific source pairing: Because Curio Class serves students across all 50 states, spec writers can either provide a universal Western expansion trio (Nebraska/Iowa railroad land broadside, prairie settler diary, local county newspaper editorial) or allow state selection from curated public-domain archives.
