## What it teaches
Children speak and respond in role as a moon-base crew. They use moon facts to steer a story, revise a choice, and explain how their revision changes the greenhouse ending.

## Key skills
- Speak as someone with a job in an imagined place.
- Listen and respond to another character.
- Mime a prop to make a role clear.
- Use a science fact to make a dramatic choice.
- Revise a scene and explain what changed.

## Support at home
- Pretend a chair is a moon buggy. Take turns being its driver and the radio operator, speaking to each other in role.
- Mime a watering can or a wrench. Ask someone to guess your job from your actions.
- Retell a familiar story, change one choice, and talk about how its ending changes.

## A five-minute start
The teacher is Commander Ada. Read Ada's cream script card aloud in role. Mission Control is the narrator, a separate character. Choose one or more crew jobs and let children mime their props. No real props are needed; a scarf or clipboard can help the teacher enter the role.

Tap Start mission. After each radio message, read Ada's card and allow time for the class to answer. The story never advances itself. At a choice, listen to the crew's spoken answer and tap its button.

## Modes
**Crew:** Six jobs can be shared by table groups or the whole class. Tapping a checked badge repeats its job line. A check assigns a job; it is not a score.

**Mission:** Eight story beats cover the long night, the greenhouse, batteries, ice, lifting, warmth, dust and the ending. The three meters count story resources, not physical measurements. Each starts at one dot, and three dots means full. Air is part of the dialogue and remains inside the base; it has no separate meter.

Rewind opens the story map. Tap Batteries, Water or Warmth after that choice has been visited. The resources return to their earlier state, and later nodes fade as the old story. Try another line and compare the ending. Back also removes a choice when returning to it. New mission keeps the crew jobs.

## Talk about it
- How can your voice and your pretend prop show your job?
- What did the crew say to Ada? How did she respond?
- Which moon fact helped you choose?
- Rewind one choice. What changed in our story? Why?

## Good to know
This is a 20 to 30 minute, teacher-led K-2 drama. Pause for spoken answers and group mime. Children may mime seated. The standards are TH:Cr3 (revise a choice), TH:Re9 (explain its effect), and TH:Cn11 (use science in drama). NGSS 1-ESS1-1 is context, not a full lesson on observing sky patterns.

Read speaks Mission Control's current message, never Ada's script. Radio again replays the current radio message. Sound controls narration and effects; Read remains available. Motion off removes rover travel, floating ice and transitions without advancing the story. Keyboard: Right for Next, Left for Back, R for Rewind, A for Ada's card. Tab and Enter operate every button.

Story progress stays only in memory. The shared kit remembers its Motion preference on this device. No class information is collected or sent. No microphone is used.

The roughly 29.5 Earth-day lunar sunrise cycle gives about 14.75 days each of daylight and night, away from polar exceptions. Lunar gravity is about one sixth of Earth's; mass does not change. Ice exists in permanently shadowed polar craters, and there are no liquid rivers. The Moon has an extremely thin exosphere, not breathable air. A blanket conserves existing warmth; it is not a heat source. The simplified greenhouse presumes a sealed base with heating and air.

The supplied radio script says moon nights are colder than any freezer on Earth. Read this as an everyday home-freezer comparison, not a claim about laboratory cryogenic equipment. Near-equatorial night temperatures around minus 130 Celsius are far below a home freezer around minus 18 Celsius.
