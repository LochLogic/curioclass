## A 5-minute start

1. In **Explore**, the ticket price starts at $60. Read the scene: a line out the door, a reseller with a sign, a sold-out seat map. Then read the graph: where does the gold price line cross demand?
2. Drag the price up until the line disappears and seats start going empty. Ask: "What price makes the number of fans who want in match the seats?"
3. Try the scenarios one at a time. **Song goes viral** and **Bad reviews** move demand. **Second night** moves supply. **Price cap $75** makes a legal ceiling.

## The big idea

For one night the arena has a fixed number of seats, so supply is a vertical line. Demand slopes down: fewer fans want in as the price rises. Below the market-clearing price there's a shortage, and the gap between the face price and what fans will pay is money someone can capture, often a reseller. Above it, seats sit empty.

## Talk about it

- Who gains and who loses when tickets are priced below the market-clearing price? Is that ever a good choice for an artist?
- With a second night, the most money comes from a price that leaves some seats empty. Why?
- A price cap is meant to help fans. What actually happens to the line and to resale prices?

## Tour mode

Demand is hidden. Each city has three nights. Students set a price, open sales and read the result: a line means the price was too low, empty seats mean too high. Each night adds a point on the demand curve. After night three, demand is revealed with a score: what share of the best possible revenue they earned.

## Good to know

- Demand is a straight-line teaching model, and the band is fictional. The numbers are for discussion, not about any real artist.
- Keyboard: focus the graph and use the arrow keys to move the price.
