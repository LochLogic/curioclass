## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Workshop**. Leave **Simple random**, **Neutral** and n = 400, and press **Run poll**. Read the headline card out loud, then point at the map: the filled dots with a check said yes, the hollow dots with an x said no.
2. Press **Run 50 more like this**. Ask: where do you think the true answer is? Then press **Reveal the truth**. The dots scatter around the white truth line, and the truth panel shows that the plus or minus caught the truth in most of them (about 45 to 49 of 51, roughly 95 percent).
3. Switch to **Mail to car and phone owners**, slide n up to 2000, and run 50 more. The new pile lands about 15 points below the truth, its printed plus or minus is about 2 points, and almost none of its bars reach the line. More people did not fix it.

## The three modes

- **Workshop** (explore, no score): Millbrook is an invented town of 20,000 residents in four neighborhoods. Pick **who you ask** (eight methods, each with its one-line description), **how you ask** (three wordings, the full question shows), and **how many** (20 to 2,000). **Run poll** asks one sample, rings the people asked on the map and adds one dot to the **Headline wall**. **Run 50 more like this** repeats the same design 50 times. **Reveal the truth** draws the true support (always for the neutral question) and says how far the newest poll is off, whether its plus or minus covers the truth, and how many of the polls shown caught it. Each design (who you ask, how you ask, how many) gets its own row on the wall, labeled with its method, wording and n, and each method has its own dot shape and color. Once the truth is shown, each row says how many of its polls caught the truth. **Show** filters the wall by method, and **Normal curve** fits a normal curve to each row with at least 10 polls and shades its middle 95 percent. When there are more designs than rows fit, the most recently used ones show. **New town** draws a new hidden town; **Clear wall** starts the wall over.
- **Case file** (1936): the Literary Digest poll, with two buttons. One runs the Digest's method in Millbrook (mail to car and phone owners, 2,000 people, 20 times), the other a fair method (stratified, 2,000 people, 20 times). The truth appears once both have run. Three discussion prompts, nothing to answer.
- **Budget desk** (optional challenge, no score): the Millbrook Courier gives you $10,000 per round. The panel shows what a design costs (dollars per response times n), and **Publish** is off while you are over budget. One poll per round; the truth stays hidden until you publish. Then two plain checks: is the truth inside your plus or minus, and is the headline's verdict right. A "backs" headline is right when the truth is above 50 percent, "says no" when it is below 50, and "split" (its plus or minus covers 50) when the truth is inside that plus or minus, so an honest "too close to call" from a fair poll counts as right. Round 3 is a close race: its truth is always between 48 and 52 percent. The round log keeps all three.

## Talk about it

- The mail poll asked 2,000 people and printed "plus or minus 2.0 points". What does that number promise, and what doesn't it cover?
- Why does Cluster, with the same n as Simple random, spread about twice as wide and catch the truth only about two times in three?
- Move n from 100 to 2000 on a biased method. What changes on the wall, and what stays put?
- Leans yes and Leans no move the result about 8 points up and 9 points down. Write a neutral version of a question you have seen in an ad or a survey.
- In the Budget desk close race, why can't a $10,000 poll say "backs" or "says no" with confidence? What would it take?
- The Digest had about 2.4 million answers. Gallup had far fewer. Why did the smaller poll win?

## Good to know

- The headline follows three rules. If the percent minus the plus or minus is above 50: "Poll: Millbrook backs skate park, 58 to 42". If the percent plus the plus or minus is below 50: "says no", with its numbers. Otherwise: "split". The rules use the numbers as printed (one decimal), and the two whole percents always add to 100.
- Every method's plus or minus uses the simple random formula, 1.96 times the square root of p(1 - p) / n, because that is what published polls print. That is why cluster samples catch the truth less often than 95 percent of the time: their real spread is wider than the formula says.
- Budget desk verdicts, simulated over 300 random towns at the most a $10,000 budget allows: Simple random, Stratified and Systematic (n = 400) are right 97 to 99 percent of the time, Cluster (n = 1000) 89 percent, and the biased methods 32 to 68 percent (they are right only when the truth happens to sit on the side they lean to). In close races fair designs stay at 99 percent, biased ones near 50.
- Systematic samples spread a little less than simple random ones here, because the street list runs neighborhood by neighborhood and block by block, so every k-th house works like a sample spread over all blocks.
- Checked numbers (default town, seed 1936): true support 44.75 percent, shown as 44.8. Fifty-one polls of n = 1000 on a fresh load: Simple random averages 44.8 (spread 1.7 points, 48 of 51 catch the truth); Cluster averages 43.6 (spread 3.1, 35 of 51); Mail to car and phone owners at n = 2000 averages 29.6, with a printed plus or minus of 2.0 and 0 of 51 catching the truth. In longer simulations: Mall on Saturday about 54 percent, Online poll about 74, Daytime phone calls about 33.5, Mail about 29.5; Leans yes adds about 8.4 points, Leans no subtracts about 8.5 to 9; Cluster catches the truth 67 to 72 percent of the time.
- The town and every poll come from a seeded random generator, so the default town and the same sequence of clicks give the same results on every computer. New towns and Budget desk towns follow a fixed sequence too, so a class that reloads together sees the same towns.
- Millbrook's numbers are invented to show how polls behave (the tool says so on screen). Real bias sizes vary.
- 1936 figures, checked 2026-10-25: about 10 million ballots mailed and about 2.4 million returned, names largely from telephone directories and car registration lists plus the Digest's subscribers (Squire 1988; Lohr and Brick 2017). The Digest's final count was Landon 1,293,669 to Roosevelt 972,897, which is 57 to 43 between the two of them; counting votes for minor candidates too, Landon's share was about 54 percent. Roosevelt won 60.8 percent of the popular vote to Landon's 36.5, and 523 electoral votes to 8. Gallup's much smaller poll called a Roosevelt win. Squire's study of a 1937 Gallup survey found that both the sample and who chose to mail ballots back were biased, and that with everyone answering, the Digest would have picked Roosevelt.
- Keyboard: Tab reaches the wall; Left and Right arrows move from dot to dot (Home and End jump to the ends), and each dot's method, wording, n, percent and plus or minus are read out. The wall's values are also listed as text for screen readers. With Motion off, rings and dots appear at once.
- Sources: Squire (1988), Public Opinion Quarterly 52(1): 125-133; Lohr and Brick (2017), Statistics, Politics and Policy 8(1): 65-84; Office of the Clerk, US House, 1936 election statistics; National Archives, 1936 electoral votes; Pew Research Center on the margin of error and on writing survey questions; OpenIntro Statistics, 4th ed., sections 1.3 and 1.4 (CC BY-SA).
