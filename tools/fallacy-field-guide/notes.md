## What it teaches

Students distinguish eight common fallacies from similar moves that use evidence fairly. They identify the exact words carrying an argument and explain why those words support their call.

## Key skills

- Mark the words that carry the reasoning.
- Distinguish a relevant criticism from a personal attack.
- Check samples, expertise, choices and causal comparisons.
- Explain why a similar argument can be fair.
- Write a fallacy and a fair twin on the same topic.

## Support at home

- Pick an everyday claim together. Ask which words give its reasons and whether those reasons support the claim.
- When someone says everyone likes something, discuss when popularity matters and when it does not.
- Invent two versions of a harmless argument about a movie or lunch choice. Make one version fairer and explain what changed.

## A five-minute start

Open Field and choose F2. Tap the first and last word of a field mark, choose a species, then Log sighting. Compare F3: choose Legit and the species it resembles. Open Guide to see how a fallacy and a correctly released twin confirm a card.

## Modes

Field contains four habitats of six fictional specimens. Field test examines the selected species without reading the key. After two misses a specimen is practice-only, so cycling through calls cannot earn a card. Show me reveals a study key without counting it. Practice-only successes say "Studied, not counted" both on screen and aloud.

Guide contains eight watercolor plates. Unseen cards are silhouettes; a caught fallacy is Spotted; a caught fallacy plus its correctly released twin is Confirmed. Confirmation persists until Reset field guide. Editing a logged call removes its current sighting from counts. Opening a card shows each saved specimen with the student's marked passage, so the fair twin and fallacy can be compared directly. Reselecting an unchanged call keeps its sighting.

Make one asks for two arguments of at least eight words, each with a marked passage. The field test is a self-check based on the student's answers. It does not judge free writing and earns no guide cards. Review both slips with a partner.

## Talk questions

- Could a conclusion be right even when its argument is poor?
- What makes expertise relevant to this exact question?
- When does a popular choice provide relevant evidence?
- What additional evidence would strengthen a causal claim?

## Good to know

Grade 10 ELA, RI.9-10.8: students evaluate validity and the relevance and sufficiency of evidence in Field and construct contrasting arguments in Make one. All people, brands, institutions, quantities and research results in specimens are fictional. The examples are reasoning exercises, not health advice or real research findings.

Definitions and fair-lookalike distinctions are paraphrased from Bradley Dowden, [Fallacies, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/fallacy/). Informal arguments can have overlapping faults, and the short specimens are deliberately simplified. An expert's relevant field supports considering their evidence; it does not make every claim certain. [CDC, About Dental Sealants](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/prevention/about-dental-sealants.html) supports the back-teeth sealant example; its 4,000 patients are fictional.

The F10 comment's no-staff detail is included in the fictional story context so students can judge the summary fairly. F13 assumes the transit office has checked its timetable; a distance alone cannot establish a missed connection. F23 treats a dentist's title as the offered reason for accepting a movie judgment; an ordinary personal preference need not be a fallacy. F15's sales statement does not explicitly make the health claim mentioned in its supplied field note; discuss that ambiguity rather than treating the inferred claim as a quoted fact.
