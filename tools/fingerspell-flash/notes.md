## A 5-minute start

1. Open **Alphabet**. Tap **A**, read how to make it, and have everyone copy it. Tap through a few more letters, or press letter keys.
2. Tap a letter in a look-alike group (like **M**) and compare it with the letters outlined in orange.
3. Switch to **Spell it**, type a student's name, and press **Fingerspell it**. Everyone spells along.
4. Finish with **Read it**: the class watches a word, then calls out or types what they saw.

## The modes

- **Alphabet:** all 26 handshapes, with how to make each one and the letters that are easy to mix up (the fists A, E, M, N, S and T; the two-finger letters U, V, R, K and H; and more). J and Z move, and their drawings show the path.
- **Spell it:** type any word or name and watch it fingerspelled at Slow, Steady, Quick or Fluent speed. **Hide the letters** turns it into a quick reading check, **Keep repeating** loops it, and the **Letter** buttons step through one shape at a time with how to make it.
- **Read it:** a word is fingerspelled with the letters hidden. Type what you saw. Each word read the first time speeds up the next one by a quarter of a letter per second; a miss slows it down. Two misses show the word slowly with its letters. Choose **One letter** (pick the letter from four that look alike), **Short words**, **Names**, **Places** or **Longer words**. The best speed is saved on this device.

## The big idea

Fingerspelling uses one handshape for each letter of the English alphabet. ASL signers use it for names, places, brands and words that don't have a sign yet, and a few short fingerspelled words have become signs of their own (like #JOB and #BUS). It is part of ASL, not a replacement for it: most words have their own sign.

Good fingerspelling is smooth and steady. The hand stays in one place near the shoulder, the rhythm is even, double letters slide or bounce once, and readers learn to catch the shape of the whole word instead of each letter.

## Talk about it

- Which letters are made from a fist? How do you tell them apart?
- Fingerspell your name to a partner at Steady speed. Can they read it?
- Why might a fluent signer's fingerspelling look different from the drawings?
- When would a signer fingerspell instead of using a sign?

## Good to know

- The drawings show a right-handed signer as you see them from the front. **Left-handed** flips every hand.
- They are line drawings, not a signing model. Real fingerspelling flows from one shape into the next and changes a little with the letters around it. Watch Deaf signers and your ASL teacher, and practice receptive skills with video of real signers too.
- Speeds are letters per second. Fluent signers fingerspell at about five or more.
- The drawings are public domain line art from wpclipart.com, via Wikimedia Commons.
