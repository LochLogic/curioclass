# Resilience Map: teacher notes

Resilience Map is a calm explorer for grades 9-12. Students look at stress as a map: an island of everyday ways people
cope, a picture of what stress does in the body, and a small plan they can build from chips. It fits an advisory or
health period of 15 to 30 minutes, with students on their own laptops.

## A 5-minute start

1. Open The map. Pick a situation on the right, for example a big test coming up.
2. Use the switch: "The part they can change" lights a solid, numbered route (work on the problem), "The part they
   can't" lights a dashed one (handle how it feels). Ask the class what changed and why.
3. Click a few landmarks along the route. Each card has a small thing to try and when it fits best.
4. Finish in My plan: students click a few chips, then Print if they want a copy.

## The three modes

- **The map.** Twenty landmarks in five areas, plus the Lighthouse (getting help), which is never dimmed. Eight
  fictional, ordinary situations each have two halves, what you can change and what you can't, following Lazarus and
  Folkman's idea of problem-focused and emotion-focused coping. The route shape (solid with a wrench, dashed with a
  wave) tells the halves apart, not only color.
- **Stress in the body.** Click a spot on the figure to read how stress can show up there (CDC, MedlinePlus). The chart
  compares a short burst, which settles, with stress that keeps going. It is a picture of an idea, not data about
  anyone.
- **My plan.** Four boxes: signs I might notice, when I can change it, when I can't, and people I can go to. Each box
  holds up to four chips. The Any time box with the help lines is always on the plan and on the printout.

## Talk about it

- Which situation felt most like one you have seen? Which half did it fall in, or was it both?
- Why might the same tool (a walk, a talk with a friend) work for one half and not the other?
- Which landmark surprised you? Which one would you actually try this week?
- Who counts as a trusted adult at school? Where would you find them?

Keep the talk on the fictional situations and on ideas. Students never need to say what is stressing them.

## Good to know

- **If a student is upset, your school counselor is the right next step.** The tool is not a screening or a
  counseling tool, and it does not label anyone or score feelings.
- A calm footer on every screen names a trusted adult at school and the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text
  988 in the US, or chat at 988lifeline.org). More help adds 911 for an emergency.
- There is no text box anywhere. The plan is chips only, so nobody types anything personal.
- The plan stays on the device. Nothing is saved until the student presses Save, and Clear erases it. Nothing is sent
  anywhere. On a shared computer, remind students to press Clear when they are done, or just Print.
- Facts on screen come from the 988 Lifeline, CDC, NIMH, MedlinePlus, Lazarus and Folkman, Folkman and Moskowitz, and
  CASEL. For you: the Harvard Center on the Developing Child notes that the "capabilities that underlie resilience can
  be strengthened at any age."
- Motion turns off the lighthouse beam and route fades. Sound and Read use a soft narrator voice for the welcomes and
  hints; everything is also on screen. The More help dialog is never read aloud by itself (classroom speakers would
  tell the room); it has its own Read this button.
- Situations and people in the tool are fictional. All art is drawn in code.
