# Research packet: wonder-kite

Concept: Pick a big question you care about (Why does our town have a library?) and tie small questions to the kite's tail as map, clock, rule or coin bows; each bow that helps holds a fact and lifts the kite.
Grade: 1
Standards: D1.1.K-2, D1.2.K-2, D1.3.K-2, D1.4.K-2

## Standards in plain words

- D1.1.K-2 (Explain why the compelling question is important to the student): First graders explain why a big community question matters to their daily life, learning, and neighborhood.
- D1.2.K-2 (Identify disciplinary ideas associated with a compelling question): First graders recognize whether a smaller question investigates geography (maps), history (clocks/time), civics (rules), or economics (coins/costs).
- D1.3.K-2 (Identify facts and concepts associated with a supporting question): First graders discover that each specific supporting question uncovers concrete facts and clear answers.
- D1.4.K-2 (Make connections between supporting questions and compelling questions): First graders see how multiple supporting questions fit together like bows on a tail to lift and answer the big compelling question.

## Real data

Inquiry structure for the compelling question: "Why does our town have a library?"

| Bow type (Disciplinary lens) | Supporting question (Small question) | Fact uncovered by the bow | Relevance to compelling question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Map bow (Geography) | Where is the library located in our town? | Located on Main Street in the town center near schools and bus stops | Explains accessibility: a central location lets all neighbors visit easily |
| Clock bow (History) | When was our library built and how did people read before? | Built 75 years ago; before that, a traveling book wagon visited monthly | Shows development over time and why a permanent home was needed |
| Rule bow (Civics) | What rules help everyone use the library fairly? | Rules require quiet voices, library cards, and returning books on time | Explains governance: shared rules protect materials for the whole town |
| Coin bow (Economics) | How does the town pay for books and staff? | Town taxes fund building upkeep, book purchases, and librarian salaries | Shows public goods: collective payment allows free borrowing for all families |

Irrelevant or distracting questions (weigh down the kite tail):
- "What color shoes does the librarian wear?" (Irrelevant detail; does not help explain the community function of a library.)
- "Does the library sell hot dogs?" (Factually incorrect premise; confuses a commercial restaurant with a public library.)
- "How many pages are in the thickest book?" (Trivia; does not answer why the town created and supports the library.)

Kite lift progression in Curio Class:
1. Ground: The kite sits on grass with only the compelling question displayed on its sail.
2. Low hover (1-2 bows): Adding 1 or 2 relevant disciplinary bows gives the kite stability.
3. High flight (4 bows): Attaching all 4 disciplinary bows (map, clock, rule, coin) supplies a complete set of facts, lifting the kite high into the sky.

## Common mistakes

1. Confusing a big compelling question (open-ended, arguable) with a simple factual quiz question that has a one-word answer.
2. Believing any question that mentions the topic is equally helpful, without evaluating whether it advances the core inquiry.
3. Thinking supporting questions are just random trivia rather than targeted steps toward answering a compelling question.
4. Struggling to classify questions into social studies disciplines (e.g., confusing an economic cost question with a history question).
5. Viewing public services like libraries or parks as commercial businesses where individual customers buy goods.

## Sensitivity

- Keep question topics centered on positive, inclusive community features (libraries, parks, community gardens, school playgrounds).
- Ensure economic questions explain tax-funded public goods as community cooperation rather than financial burden.
- Support beginning readers with clear visual iconography on the bows (compass rose for map, hourglass for clock, gavel for rule, coin for economics).
