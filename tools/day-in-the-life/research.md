# Research packet: day-in-the-life
Concept: Spend a school day with kids in Kenya, Peru, Japan and Norway, seeing how homes, food and travel fit the land and weather, then find on a population map where most people live in each country and why.
Grade: 3   Standards: D2.Geo.4.3-5, D2.Geo.6.3-5

## Standards in plain words
- D2.Geo.4.3-5: "Explain how culture influences the way people modify and adapt to their environments." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Explain how children in Kenya, Peru, Japan, and Norway adapt their homes, clothes, food, and school travel to fit their local climate and landscape.
- D2.Geo.6.3-5: "Describe how environmental and cultural characteristics influence population distribution in specific places or regions." (National Council for the Social Studies, C3 Framework, https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf)
  What a student must be able to do: Look at population density maps of each country and explain why people cluster in fertile plains or along coasts while mountains and deserts have few people.

## Common mistakes
- Imagining people are spread evenly across a country: Third graders frequently assume population density is uniform, failing to understand that steep mountains, arid zones, or dense rainforests leave huge areas nearly empty while fertile valleys and coastlines host millions (Source: C3 Framework Geography Indicators D2.Geo.6.3-5).
- Viewing global customs as costumes rather than functional adaptations: Students often think cultural habits (like layered wool in the Andes or steep roofs in snowy Norway) are merely decorative, missing how they directly solve climate challenges (Source: National Geographic Education Human-Environment Interaction).
- Assuming all schools worldwide use identical cafeterias: Children often expect hot cafeteria lines like their own, missing distinctive global traditions like Japan's classroom-based *kyushoku* or Norway's *matpakke* (Source: Japanese Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology).
- Confusing weather with long-term climate: Elementary students frequently mix up a single rainy day with regional climate zones that determine seasonal agricultural cycles (Source: C3 Framework Geography Indicators D2.Geo.4.3-5).

## Sensitivity
- Modern balanced cultural depiction: Portray Kenya, Peru, Japan, and Norway as modern 21st-century nations with vibrant cities, digital technology, and high educational standards, avoiding stereotypical safari tropes for Kenya, ancient-only depictions of Peru, or exotic caricatures of Japan.
- Dignity in resource differences: Highlight diverse school travel (walking footpaths, riding matatus, taking commuter trains, crossing fjords on ferries) with respect, showing how each mode fits the terrain rather than implying poverty or inferiority.
- Accurate cultural terminology: Use authentic names with care: *kyushoku* and *shokuiku* for Japan, *matpakke* for Norway, *ugali* and *matatus* for Kenya, *choclo* and *quinoa* for Peru.
