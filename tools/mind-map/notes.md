## Before you start

This tool covers depression, eating disorders, trauma and suicide. Preview it first, tell students what's coming, and let them know who they can talk to at school. The **Get help now** button at the top is always one tap away. The second practice conversation ("Sam sends a goodbye") talks about suicide and is labeled so you can choose whether to use it.

## A 5-minute start

1. Open **The map**. Start with **Everyday stress** in the middle, then choose one condition and read it together.
2. Tap the zones on the continuum along the bottom. Ask: "Can someone with a diagnosis be thriving? Can someone without one be struggling?" (Yes to both.)
3. Play a few rounds of **Myth or fact** as a class vote.

## The four modes

- **The map**: everyday stress and eight conditions (anxiety disorders, depression, ADHD, eating disorders, PTSD and trauma, bipolar disorder, OCD, substance use disorder). Each one covers what it is, what it can look like, what it is not, how common it is (with the source), and what helps. The continuum shows that everyone's mental health moves between thriving, coping, struggling and unwell.
- **Myth or fact**: twelve statements about mental health, each with the evidence and its source. The last screen connects myths to stigma.
- **Help a friend**: two made-up text conversations. Students choose a reply at each step; every choice gets feedback, and only the most helpful reply moves the conversation on. The steps are notice, ask, listen and connect.
  - "Jordan has been quiet" is about a friend who seems depressed.
  - "Sam sends a goodbye" is about a friend at risk of suicide. It shows asking directly, refusing to keep the secret, and calling 988 together.
- **Stress bucket**: stressors fill the bucket. Healthy coping (sleep, movement, talking to someone, counseling...) opens taps that drain it. Some habits (vaping or drinking, shutting people out, late-night scrolling) clog the taps and add stress. When the bucket overflows, it's time to reach out. Includes a box-breathing guide.

## How it was written

- The suicide conversation follows safe messaging guidance: no methods, help is available, recovery is common.
- Statistics come from NIMH (National Comorbidity Survey Adolescent Supplement), SAMHSA's National Survey on Drug Use and Health 2024 and the CDC. The crisis lines were checked in October 2026; please check them again if you print them.
- The tool teaches; it does not diagnose. It says so on every condition card.

## Talk about it

- What makes it hard for teens to ask for help? What would make it easier?
- Which myth surprised you? Where do you think it comes from?
- In the practice conversations, why was "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" the best reply?
- What are two taps you could open this week?

## Good to know

- Crisis lines: call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), text HOME to 741741 (Crisis Text Line), The Trevor Project for LGBTQ+ youth at 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678-678. In an emergency, call 911.
- Nothing typed or chosen in the tool is saved or sent anywhere.
