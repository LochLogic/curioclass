# Planet Builder (idea card)

- Module: science-6-gravity-and-the-solar-system, grade 6. Codes: MS-ESS1-2, MS-ESS1-3, MS-PS2-4
- Format / family: sandbox / none. Fit: good
- One idea: Drop a star, planets and moons into empty space and flick them into motion; gravity, which always pulls and pulls harder between bigger masses, decides whether they settle into orbits, crash or fly off, and the same pull holds a whole galaxy together.
- How it plays (solo student by default):
  - First screen: dark space with one star, a tray of bodies with mass sliders, and a drag arrow for launch speed and direction.
  - Core loop: place, set mass, flick; trails draw the paths. A pull arrow between any two bodies grows when either mass grows and shrinks with distance. Optional goals sit in a side drawer ("a moon that circles its planet", "two planets that never collide") and never block free play.
  - Scale tray: load "Our solar system" with real data cards (diameter, distance, mass); toggle true sizes or true distances to see why both never fit on one screen, then scroll a long strip from the Sun to Neptune at one scale.
  - The aha or the end state: zoom far out to a spinning disk of stars; turn gravity down and it flies apart, turn it back up and it holds. An orbit is falling and missing.
- Standards, one line per code:
  - MS-ESS1-2: the sandbox orbits and the galaxy zoom model gravity's role in motions in the solar system and galaxies.
  - MS-ESS1-3: the real data cards and the size and distance toggles have the student analyze scale (Jupiter is about 11 Earths across).
  - MS-PS2-4: the pull arrows and mass sliders give the evidence for a short written claim that gravity attracts and depends on the masses.
- Not like: orbit-lab (grade 9, Kepler's laws with numbers); moon-phase-model (lighting and phases); field-lines (grade 11 field maps).
- Art: orbits and trails drawn in code; planet cards from public domain NASA imagery (checked) or generated art.
- Proposed title: keep
- Proposed concept line: Place stars, planets and moons and flick them into motion; gravity grows with mass and decides orbits or chaos, then load our real solar system to scale and zoom out to a galaxy.
- Flags: needs a data source (NASA planetary fact sheet, checked); overlap risk (orbit-lab; keep this one qualitative and scale-focused)
