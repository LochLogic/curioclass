## What it teaches

Students can describe, analyze, interpret and evaluate a painting while pointing to visible evidence.
They distinguish an interpretation from an observation, a sourced fact from a visual inference, and a judgment from a generalization.

## Key skills

- Find details that support a sentence about a painting.
- Explain how visual elements work together.
- Connect a possible meaning to evidence.
- Judge a work through a stated criterion.
- Revise a short critique or museum label.

## Support at home

- Look at a picture together. Each person says one thing they can see and points to it.
- Try two readings of the same picture. Ask what visible detail makes each reading possible.
- Compare judging a picture for realism with judging it for feeling. Explain why the conclusions may differ.

## A 5-minute start

1. Choose a painting and a seat, then Take your seat.
2. Tap Pin on a descriptive claim. Select its evidence in the painting, then Done.
3. Try the claim that interprets too early. Challenge it for Wrong round.
4. Build a claim of your own with the menus and Pin it.
5. Open Record to see how pinned claims become a piece you can edit.

## Screens and rounds

Choose offers three authentic museum paintings and the Critic, Curator and Artist seats.
Panel moves forward through Describe, Analyze, Interpret and Judge. Each round needs all three floor claims resolved
and at least one student claim pinned. Students may make up to three claims per round.
Judge adds four criteria: Looks real, Well designed, Strong feeling and Does a job. Some panel claims need different lenses.

Record lists pinned and challenged claims, grouped by round. The Critic edits a review, the Curator edits a wall label,
and the Artist edits notes about how the work was made. Print is available even when a writing check suggests trimming.
Finish brings the editable piece into view. Scroll upward to revisit its supporting transcript.
The Curator checks 100 words and 25 words per sentence; the Critic checks 150 words. Artist notes have no word limit.

## Talk about it

- Can two interpretations be supported by different details in the same painting?
- What extra source would be needed to claim what an artist personally believed?
- How does changing the judgment lens change your case?
- Does pointing at an object fully support an argument about relationships? Explain the connection aloud.

## Good to know

For solo use or groups of three taking turns. There are unlimited evidence attempts and no score or timer.
Pin reveals numbered evidence locations; up to two can be selected. A matching listed spot supports a panel claim.
Analyze claims need an element carried by their first spot and a different second spot. These support tables are
deliberately limited teaching models, not an exhaustive account of reasonable art criticism.

Keyboard: Tab through controls. After Pin, arrow keys move among evidence spots and Enter selects.
Escape cancels a pin or closes a zoom. Look closer enlarges the painting; Whole picture restores it.
Challenge opens a dialog that keeps the sentence visible beside the reasons. Escape closes it without changing the claim.
Short screens show one floor claim at a time with Previous and Next. No student work is saved or transmitted.
Motion uses the shared device preference. Sound and Read use the kit; unrecorded narration uses the browser voice.

The museum originals contain people. All five newly generated illustrations depict only places or objects.
The Gulf Stream depicts a Black sailor in danger. The supplied claims stay with visible evidence; a teacher may
discuss broader museum-supported interpretations separately. No additional race-related historical claim was added.
NCAS VA:Re7 is practiced through observation and analysis, VA:Re8 through interpretation, and VA:Re9 through judging by criteria.
