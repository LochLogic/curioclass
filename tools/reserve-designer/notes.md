## What it teaches
Students design a reserve, evaluate its habitat connections and revise it after testing single-cell losses.
They use simple models to explain how herd vigilance and colony alarms can help animals survive.

## Key skills
- Join habitat patches while staying inside a budget.
- Compare species with different habitat and space needs.
- Identify fragile corridors and test an alternative route.
- Keep working farms in a conservation plan.
- Distinguish a group model from evidence about real animals.

## Support at home
- On a walk, look for a road or fence that might interrupt an animal's route. Discuss a possible safe connection.
- Look for birds in a group and compare how many are feeding and watching at one moment.
- Sketch two green spaces near home and a possible connection between them. Nothing needs to be purchased.

## Five-minute start
Open Plan. Protect one forest cell, then a neighboring wetland. Read the changing frog row.
Try a farm and compare keeping it in production with restoring it. Enter Stress test and run the current design.
Return to Plan and explain one change that would make a particular species less vulnerable.

## Modes
**Plan:** $4,000k buys forest ($250k), meadow ($200k), wetland ($150k), farm ($300k), or a highway crossing ($500k).
Any buyable land cell touching town in eight directions costs another $100k, except crossings, which stay $500k.
Restoring a farm costs another $100k. At least 16 of 20 farms must keep farming.
Click ordinary land to protect or sell. Enter opens its action menu. Arrow keys move through the map.
Working farms count toward connected protected land and bear area. Restored farms become meadow.
Town and water cannot be bought. A river cell counts if its left or right bank is protected.
The road bridge links the river north and south only when both neighboring river cells count.

**Stress test:** locks the plan. Run 50 years tests one possible loss for each purchased cell, never fifty sequential losses.
The title is a planning horizon, not a probability forecast. Every possible future removes exactly one purchase.
The outcome is computed before playback. Weakest cells, future thumbnails and Back to plan respond during playback.
Selecting a future names the lost cell and newly broken needs. Changing the plan clears the old test summary.
The loss label is illustrative; the rule calculation removes protection from that cell. It does not simulate fire spread or population dynamics.

**Groups:** one to twenty elk independently watch with a model probability of 20 percent per elk.
The chance at least one watches is 1 minus 0.8 raised to herd size. At ten elk this rounds to 89 percent.
The real finding is reduced individual vigilance in larger herds, unlike this deliberately fixed individual model.
The eight prairie dogs shelter together for three seconds. Another Alarm call restarts the three seconds.
Motion off fixes the number watching to rounded 20 percent and removes sliding; the dog timer stays three seconds.

## Talk questions
- Which species can use a purchased working farm for connection? Which still needs meadow?
- Why can a plan meet every need today yet fail every single-loss future?
- Which one purchase would you sell to fund a second route, and what might that sacrifice?
- What does the elk model assume that the real study says can change?
- Compare herd sizes 1 and 20. Use the displayed watcher chances as evidence, then explain why the 20 percent assumption matters.

## Good to know
The valley, prices, budget and thresholds are simplified model values. Real reserves need many more species,
water rights, willing sellers and fire plans. The plan saves locally and never leaves this device.
No account, export code, analytics, network requests or recorded audio are included.
