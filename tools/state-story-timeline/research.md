# Research packet: state-story-timeline
Concept: Pick your state and walk its story from the Native nations who lived there first to statehood and today; ask questions about who drove each change, link causes to effects, and compare a kid's life then and now.
Grade: 4   Standards: D2.His.2.3-5, D2.His.3.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.His.2.3-5: "Compare life in specific historical time periods to life today." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to compare how daily chores, schooling, and play for children in past eras compare with daily life now.
- D2.His.3.3-5: "Generate questions about individuals and groups who have shaped significant historical changes and continuities." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to ask questions about the leaders, Native nations, settlers, and everyday workers who drove major state events.
- D2.His.14.3-5: "Explain probable causes and effects of events and developments." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to link cause-and-effect chains in state history, such as new transport lines leading to town booms.

## Real data

- National Archives Milestone Documents: Northwest Ordinance of 1787, https://www.archives.gov/milestones/northwest-ordinance (Public domain). Three-stage territorial admission blueprint.
- National Archives Milestone Documents: Statehood enabling acts and admission proclamations, https://www.archives.gov/milestones/ (Public domain).
- Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian Native Knowledge 360: https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360 (Educational open resources). Interactive lessons on homelands, treaties, and tribal sovereignty.
- Library of Congress National Child Labor Committee Collection: https://www.loc.gov/collections/national-child-labor-committee/ (Public domain). Lewis Hine photographs of working children in early 20th-century America.

## Common mistakes

- Treating state history as beginning only when white settlers arrived: Fourth graders often assume history began at statehood, ignoring thousands of years of Indigenous governance and land stewardship (Smithsonian NMAI NK360).
- Imagining state boundaries were always fixed: Students often view state lines as natural geographic borders rather than human political agreements negotiated through treaties and congressional acts (National Archives).
- Equating historical childhood differences purely to technology: Students often focus only on the lack of screens, missing deeper differences in hard manual chores, child labor, and seasonal school attendance (Library of Congress Classroom Materials).
- Referring to Native Americans only in the past tense: Students frequently speak of Indigenous peoples as historical figures rather than contemporary citizens and active sovereign tribal governments (Smithsonian NMAI NK360).

## Sensitivity

- Indigenous sovereignty and displacement: Treat Native history with respect, naming specific tribal nations. Frame treaties as nation-to-nation agreements and accurately recount forced removals and land loss without graphic trauma. Rely on tribal education sources.
- Enslavement and compromises: Acknowledge that the admission of several states involved fierce national debates and compromises over the expansion of chattel slavery.
- Presenting child labor: Depict historical child labor respectfully to illustrate why child labor laws and universal public education were created.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Smithsonian NMAI NK360 Interactive Timelines (https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360): Multi-perspective timeline exploring Indigenous homelands, treaties, and cultural survival.
- Library of Congress US History Primary Source Timeline (https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/united-states-history-primary-source-timeline/): Era-by-era primary source explorer.
- PBS LearningMedia State Studies: Digital timeline modules exploring state settlement, statehood acts, and economic transformations.
