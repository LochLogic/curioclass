## A 5-minute start

1. Leave the settings as they are ($10,000, 60% stocks and 40% bonds, starting in 1990) and press **Launch**. Thirty years of real history play in about five seconds, with events along the way.
2. Look at the faint lines: each asset on its own. Which grew most? Which was the bumpiest?
3. Open **Missions** and try **Bad timing**: predict, then launch.

## The big idea

Different investments carry different risks and rewards. Stocks have grown the most over long periods but can fall hard in a single year. Bonds and cash are steadier but grow less, and cash can lose buying power to inflation. Spreading money out (diversifying) and staying invested longer have narrowed the range of outcomes in the past. Fees, even small ones, compound against you. None of this is a promise: history is not a forecast.

## The three views

- **Time machine:** pick a start year, how long to stay, a starting amount, yearly additions, a mix and a fund fee. **Buying power** shows the result in start-year dollars, after inflation.
- **Every start year:** the same mix held for 1, 5, 10, 20 or 30 years, starting in every possible year since 1928. Each bar is one stay's average yearly return. Watch the range shrink as the stay gets longer. Tap a bar to ride it.
- **Missions:** seven predict-then-check questions: a crash, cash and inflation, fees, steady saving, 2022, gold in the 1970s, and time in the market.

## Talk about it

- Why might someone choose bonds or cash even though stocks grew more?
- In **Every start year**, why does a 20-year stay look so different from a 1-year stay?
- The 1% fee left about a third less after 40 years. Why so much more than 1%?
- What is a reason these past results might not repeat?

## Good to know

- **Educational only. This is not investment advice**, and it names no funds or companies to buy.
- Data: annual total returns, 1928 to 2025, for the S&P 500 with dividends, 10-year US Treasury bonds and 3-month Treasury bills, and gold, from Aswath Damodaran at NYU Stern (January 2026 update). Inflation is the December-to-December change in the US Consumer Price Index (CPI-U).
- Gold's price was fixed by law until 1971, so its line is nearly flat before then.
- Simplifications: one return per year, no taxes, the mix is rebalanced to its percentages every year, yearly additions go in at the start of each year, and the fee is taken once a year. Real funds track an index closely but not exactly.
- Biggest drop is measured from year-end to year-end, so drops within a year (like most of 1987's crash) look smaller than they felt.
