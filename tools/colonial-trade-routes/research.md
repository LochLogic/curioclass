# Research packet: colonial-trade-routes
Concept: Follow real cargoes across the 1700s Atlantic from New England, the Middle Colonies, the South, the Caribbean and Britain; see who made each, including enslaved Africans, and how the trade changed the colonies over time.
Grade: 5   Standards: D2.Eco.3.3-5, D2.His.2.3-5, D2.Geo.11.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Eco.3.3-5: "Identify examples of the variety of resources (human capital, physical capital, and natural resources) that are used to produce goods and services." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to categorize the natural resources, physical tools, and human labor (including enslaved labor) used to produce colonial goods.
- D2.His.2.3-5: "Compare life in specific historical time periods to life today." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to compare eighteenth-century transatlantic maritime commerce and labor with modern trade and daily life.
- D2.Geo.11.3-5: "Describe how the spatial patterns of economic activities in a place change over time because of interactions with nearby and distant places." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to describe how Atlantic trade routes connected distant continents and reshaped colonial seaports and plantation regions.

## Real data

- Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture Searchable Museum: https://www.searchablemuseum.com (Smithsonian Institution). Primary source artifacts, slave ship logs, and Transatlantic Slave Trade records.
- Slave Voyages: The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database: https://www.slavevoyages.org (Emory University / Hutchins Center / open access). Records of over 36,000 historical voyages, cargo manifests, and port trajectories.
- Smithsonian National Museum of American History: On the Water: Stories from Maritime America: https://americanhistory.si.edu/onthewater (Smithsonian Institution). Colonial shipping ledgers, maritime artifacts, and trade route maps.

## Common mistakes

- Believing Northern colonies had no connection to slavery: Fifth graders frequently assume slavery only existed in the South, overlooking that Northern shipbuilders, merchants, and rum distillers profited extensively from the trade (National Park Service / NMAAHC).
- Imagining trade as a rigid, simple three-point triangle: Students often picture ships sailing only in a neat triangle, whereas Atlantic commerce involved diverse bilateral and complex coastal feeder voyages (Smithsonian NMAH On the Water).
- Viewing trade goods purely as inanimate cargo: Students often treat trade as barrels and bales without confronting that millions of human beings were commodified and trafficked (NMAAHC).
- Overlooking British mercantilist restrictions: Students assume colonists enjoyed free trade with any European country, forgetting that British Navigation Acts restricted legal destinations (National Archives).

## Sensitivity

- Solemn treatment of enslavement: The Transatlantic Slave Trade and the Middle Passage must be treated with moral gravity. Do not create gameplay where students play as slave traders or purchase human beings. Focus on historical trade routes, cargo types, labor realities, and resistance.
- Indigenous displacement: Recognize that expanding tobacco and rice plantations dispossessed coastal Native American nations of their ancestral homelands.
- Reference living institutions: Cite the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture (NMAAHC).

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Smithsonian NMAH On the Water Maritime Trade Explorer (https://americanhistory.si.edu/onthewater): Digital maps and artifact explorer for colonial Atlantic commerce.
- Slave Voyages Interactive Transit Maps (https://www.slavevoyages.org): Animated map tracking historical voyages across the Atlantic.
- Brown University Choices Program Transatlantic Trade Modules: High-quality secondary curriculum mapping Atlantic commercial and labor flows.

## Gaps

- Cargo measurement conversions: Historical colonial manifests record quantities in hogsheads, tierces, and quintals; Curio Class tools should standardize these into clear unit counts (e.g. 100 barrels of flour, 50 casks of rum) so fifth graders can compare regional outputs easily.
