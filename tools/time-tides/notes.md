Time Tides is a flat world map of the last 12,000 years, not a contest: there is no score and nothing to win. Kids slide a timeline from the end of the Ice Age to 2010 and watch borders rise, spread, split and fade across 86 moments in time. Each moment has a short story and pins on the map where borders shifted; twelve guided story arcs (Rome, the Mongols, African kingdoms, the Americas, decolonization and more) fly the map from chapter to chapter. Click any state to see how big it grew over time and follow it through the centuries.

**Things to notice together.** Change over time is the big idea: pick one place, say the Nile valley or the Andes, and step through the eras to see who held it and for how long. Borders are human choices, not lines on the land: compare a border that follows a river or mountains with one drawn straight by a treaty (the 1885 and 1920 stories show both). Solid lines mean a firm border and dashed lines a loose or uncertain frontier, so ask how anyone could know where a border was 3,000 years ago. The map shows states and empires first; turn on Non-state peoples in Layers and ask who lived in the "empty" places. Every map is a source made by someone, with choices about what to show and what to leave out, so it is worth asking what this one leaves out.

**How to play.** Drag the timeline at the bottom, or press Play to watch history roll by; the arrows (or the arrow keys) step one moment at a time. Drag the map to move, scroll or pinch to zoom, and click a state to open its notes. Stories opens the guided arcs, Search (or the / key) finds any empire, year or story, and Layers turns on rivers, peoples, grid lines and colors for color blindness.

**Good to know.** It works best with a mouse or trackpad on a laptop or larger screen; on a phone fingers can drag, pinch and tap, but the side panel covers much of the map. Dates use BC and AD. The borders are simplified drawings, sometimes centuries wide, and a few labels are anachronisms (Canada appears in 1815, for instance), which makes them good material for "is this map right?" questions. Nothing is saved and nothing is sent anywhere; the download button saves a picture of the current map to the device. Reduced motion settings make the map jump instead of glide.
