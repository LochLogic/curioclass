# Research packet: history-article-review
Concept: Students read short kid-magazine articles about the Revolution, write each one's main claim in a sentence, then check its argument and explanation (enough evidence? steps in order? voices missing?) and give a reviewer's rating with reasons.
Grade: 5   Standards: D2.His.17.3-5, D4.4.3-5, D4.5.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.His.17.3-5: "Summarize the central claim in a secondary work of history." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to read a secondary history article and summarize its main argument in one clear sentence.
- D4.4.3-5: "Critique arguments." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to judge whether an article backs its claims with factual evidence and addresses competing points of view.
- D4.5.3-5: "Critique explanations." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to check whether historical events are explained in logical chronological order with clear cause-and-effect steps.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the article topic with its central claim: Fifth graders commonly write "the article is about George Washington" rather than stating the thesis (such as "the author argues Washington's retreat saved the army") (NCSS C3 Framework).
- Assuming published articles are automatically complete: Students frequently assume that if an article appears in a printed magazine, it contains every relevant fact and has no bias (Stanford History Education Group).
- Overlooking omitted voices: Students often miss that standard accounts focus exclusively on famous military officers while leaving out women, Loyalists, enslaved people, and Native nations (Museum of the American Revolution).
- Accepting assertions without evidence: Students frequently accept author claims as true without checking whether dates, quotes, or records back them up (NCSS C3 Framework Dimension 4).

## Sensitivity

- Complex civil war dynamics: Present the American Revolution not as a simple good-versus-evil contest, but as a complex civil struggle where Patriots, Loyalists, enslaved people seeking freedom, and Native nations protecting their homelands held differing, compelling viewpoints.
- Contradictions of liberty and slavery: Acknowledge the deep paradox of Patriot leaders fighting for political liberty while holding men, women, and children in lifelong chattel bondage.
- Living institutions: Reference the Museum of the American Revolution and Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- Stanford History Education Group Reading Like a Historian (https://sheg.stanford.edu): Curriculum teaching students to evaluate claims, evidence, and reliability in historical writing.
- Newsela Article Analysis Tools: Digital reading platform prompting students to identify central claims and evaluate supporting evidence.
- ReadWorks Grade 5 Paired Text Review: Argument analysis modules comparing competing historical perspectives.

## Gaps

- Kid-friendly review rubric: Professional historical peer review standards are overly technical for fifth graders; Curio Class tools should provide a 3-part reviewer scorecard (1: Central Claim, 2: Evidence & Order, 3: Missing Voices) with a 1-to-5 star rating and comment bubbles.
