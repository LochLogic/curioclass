## What it teaches

Students can arrange an existing tune for an occasion by choosing players, roles, dynamics, octaves and a section plan.
They can revise an arrangement using range, balance, duration and occasion feedback, then explain a musical decision.

## Key skills

- Give the melody and accompaniment distinct jobs.
- Match a part to a player's comfortable sounding range.
- Compare tempo, number of bars and total duration.
- Revise after listening and checking, and explain why a change helps.
- Use variety and an ending to shape a performance.

## Support at home

- Hum a familiar tune softly, then brightly. Ask what occasion each version might suit.
- Tap a steady pulse while someone hums. Try a quieter pulse and discuss whether the tune is easier to hear.
- Listen for an introduction or ending in a song you already know. Describe what tells you it is beginning or finishing.

## A five-minute start

1. Press Play. The opening is Chords only, so the guitar prepares the entrance before the fiddle brings in the tune.
2. Press Stop and tap Length. The opening plan has 21 bars at 72 BPM: 21 × 240 ÷ 72 = 70 seconds.
3. Tap Add section, then A: The tune. It adds eight bars before the ending. Length now passes at about 97 seconds.
4. Look at the other checks. Try a New section and compare the musical effect with simply repeating A.
5. After looking at all four checks, make another revision. Choose a reason chip or type a short reason, then open Concert card.

## The modes

**Arrange:** tap a role block for its role, dynamic and octave. Tap an instrument for Solo or Remove player.
Tap a section heading for Move left, Move right, Remove or Play from here. Edits remain live during playback.
The grid is the score plan, not note-by-note composition. At phone width it turns so sections are rows.

**Concert card:** the program gathers the occasion, band, plan and last eight explained revisions. Amber checks appear
under Still to solve. Play follows the program's sections. Back to Arrange returns to the desk.

## Talk about it

- What changed when you made the backing softer rather than making the melody louder?
- Which revision helped the occasion most? Which revision helped the music most?
- What did a new section do that another repeat of A did not?
- Which check cannot tell you whether the arrangement is expressive? Listen and defend your answer.

## Good to know

- Five players and fourteen sections are the limits. The Add controls disable at those limits.
- Changes save on this device only. Storage failures leave the current plan usable. Reset asks Clear or Keep.
- A new occasion initially carries the current arrangement. Returning to a previously saved occasion restores that occasion's plan.
- Optional reasons begin after all four lights have been tapped, or after a Play finishes. Undo reverses the newest change, including after reloading.
- A repeated instrument can be added as a separate player. Solo is a listening aid and does not change the printed arrangement or checks.
- The full instrument ranges are references. The smaller school ranges, dynamics multipliers, loudness values, balance threshold
  and occasion windows are Curio Class teaching models. They are not universal performance limits.
- Balance compares backing loudness with twice the melody loudness. It skips Intro and Outro, as the spec requires.
- Duration is nominal: bars × 240 ÷ BPM. Last line twice slows during its final bar, so actual playback is slightly longer.
- Intro presets show only the roles they sound. Incompatible saved roles appear as Rest. Walk-up and Drum call use their named part for the first two bars, then Chords for the last two. Hold ends on F: a Melody player sustains one F and chord players sustain the chord.
- Call and answer uses two Melody players. The first calls; the other answers an octave higher. Balance flags a Call and answer section with fewer than two Melody players. Counter-line uses the first
  Melody player for A and the others for the slower line. With one Melody player, it plays the slower line alone.
  The editor explains this when those sections are selected. In compact views, revision reasons open together in a dialog.
- Narration falls back to the browser voice. The specified Kore recordings were not made in this Build-only run.
- Motion off preserves tempo and duration, stops the lamp shimmer and moves the playhead by beats.

## Standards through student action

- **MU:Cr2:** organize a band and a section plan for a specific occasion.
- **MU:Cr3:** listen, inspect feedback, revise, record a reason and compare with Undo.
- **MU:Pr4:** choose tempo, roles, dynamics and an ending for the intended performance.

## Content sources and build limitation

The tune is the American traditional, public-domain play-party song Skip to My Lou. The spec dates it to the 1840s.
The chorus was corrected during cold review from [Zebra Keys, Skip To My Lou, p. 1 (2007 engraving)](https://www.zebrakeys.com/pdf/skip_to_my_lou.pdf).
The traditional melody is transposed from C to F without changing rhythm: eight 4/4 bars, 32 beats, E4 to C5.
The same score's chord symbols C, C, G, G, C, C, G, C transpose exactly to F, F, C, C, F, F, C, F.
Only the traditional melody and harmonic symbols are used; the printed piano accompaniment and engraving are not reproduced.
The [publisher's public-domain sheet music page](https://www.zebrakeys.com/resources/sheetmusic/publicdomainsheetmusic/)
identifies the collection as public domain and permits redistribution. The PDF itself carries a 2007 engraving copyright.
This is an identified modern printing of public-domain musical material, not a verified historical public-domain printing.
The spec's stricter historical-printing provenance requirement remains open. Do not describe it as satisfied.

Earlier primary sources inspected, Leah Jackson Wolford, *The Play-Party in Indiana* (1916), p. 89, and
L. D. Ames, *The Missouri Play-Party* (1911), p. 304, print different verse variants.
They do not authenticate this chorus. [Wolford scan](https://archive.org/details/playpartyinindia00wolfiala/page/89/mode/1up).
The traditional tune has multiple variants. This tool presents one chorus instrumentally and does not reproduce historic lyrics.

[CALLERLAB, Music As Used By Callers, 1994](https://knowledge.callerlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/2023-09-30-MusicAsUsedByCallers-1994.pdf)
describes a comfortable dance tempo as 128 to 132 BPM. This does not validate the spec's 112 to 128 BPM classroom window.
The implemented window stays the spec's own teaching choice, identified on screen as a Curio Class model.

Other references supplied by the spec: [National Core Arts Standards](https://www.nationalartsstandards.org/), music MU:Cr2,
MU:Cr3 and MU:Pr4; Samuel Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*; [Vienna Symphonic Library Academy](https://www.vsl.co.at/en/Academy);
[Britannica, Banjo](https://www.britannica.com/art/banjo). Instrument range table and open-G tuning follow the spec;
those edition-level range assertions were not independently reconciled to Adler during this run.

New strains, occasion windows, school ranges and model rules are written for Curio Class. Illustrations generated with
OpenAI gpt-image, edited for Curio Class. The tool includes no historic lyrics, downloaded media or recorded audio.
