## What it teaches

Students compare how texture, rhythm, harmony, instruments and circulation change the sound of one melody.
They use audible evidence to place arrangements in historical context and test combinations of musical rules.

## Key skills

- Hear one melodic contour through different arrangements.
- Distinguish a free rhythm, syncopation, swing and a backbeat.
- Compare musical layers and the instruments carrying them.
- Connect a sound to its cultural setting and means of circulation.
- Make a historical estimate, then revise it using revealed evidence.

## Support at home

- Hum a short familiar tune twice, once freely and once over a steady tapped pulse. Talk about what changed.
- Ask someone how music reached them when they were younger: live, radio, records or another route.
- Listen to a piece together and describe the layers without needing instrument names.

## A five-minute start

Press Power. Hear Chant, then use Station to hear the same tune with a held organum voice beneath it.
Pin one station with Compare, tune another and press Flip. The switch preserves the place in the melody.
Ask students to name one audible change before reading the feature rows. Open Mystery station and make one estimate.

## The modes

**Dial:** twelve equally spaced tuning positions, with a separate true-time strip showing years to scale.
Drag the tuning knob or needle, or use Station. Between marks, the nearest station fades into static.
Compare pins A; a different locked station becomes B. Flip switches arrangements at a bar boundary.

**Mixing desk:** Beat controls timing and drums, Harmony controls accompaniment, Sound controls timbre and texture.
The starting mix is Baroque for all three. Knob choices appear at once and take effect at a bar boundary.
Each row identifies its source era. Twelve choices in three controls give 1,728 combinations.

**Mystery station:** six different randomly selected stations. Listen, place the year pin and press Tune in.
The reveal shows the absolute gap in years and highlights two useful clues. The final count uses a 50-year tolerance.
Nothing about the set is saved. Dates are representative listening anchors, not boundaries of eras.
The live counter follows the tune's eight phrases. Organum expands them into fourteen measures of three beats;
the counter is a shared melody position, not a claim that every arrangement has eight notated measures.

## Talk questions

- Which change was easiest to hear when the melody stayed fixed? Which required a second listen?
- How did printing, home instruments, radio, records, clubs or sampling shape musical practice?
- What does an equally spaced radio dial hide that the true-time strip makes visible?
- Why does a date of international encounter tell us little about the age of a living tradition?

## Good to know

All sound is synthesized locally. These are original arrangements of **The Traveler**, not historical recordings.
The controls include kit Sound, Motion and Read. Read speaks the current station's features in order, or the hint
while a mystery remains sealed. Speech uses the browser's available voice because this build creates no recordings.
The requested bank is news-reader; browser fallback cannot guarantee its recorded Rasalgethi voice.

Keyboard: Left / Right changes stations, Space plays or pauses when focus is outside a button,
C pins Compare, F flips, M opens Mixing desk. The tuning, volume, mixing and mystery inputs accept arrow keys.
The mystery pin moves in ten-year steps. Tab reaches every control; native buttons use Space or Enter.

The European concert-music sequence and American popular styles are a selected path, not a complete world history.
African American roots of ragtime, jazz and rock and roll, and Black and Latino creation of hip hop, are explicit.
Disco's Black, Latino and gay club contexts are included. These are musical-history contexts, not scored identities.
MU:Re7 is practiced through listening comparisons and predictions; MU:Cn11 through the context rows and date reveals.

## Owner-added station: Javanese gamelan

The twelfth station is a parallel Indonesian tradition. Its 1889 anchor marks a documented Paris encounter,
not the invention of gamelan, and does not make gamelan a stage between Romantic music and ragtime.
Sundanese and Javanese artists took part in the Paris fair. The selected Javanese-inspired model here is
not a reconstruction of the fair's mainly Sundanese musical repertoire.
The original tune is kept in the same equal-tempered pentatonic pitch collection so its contour can be compared.
It is labeled a simplified original arrangement, not traditional repertoire or authentic slendro or pelog tuning.
Related melodic layers, bronze-like metallophones, gong punctuation and a drum pulse are modeled.
The postcard depicts instruments in an empty pavilion. The fresh cultural-content check is in refine/sensitivity.md.

The added station uses these sources, checked during the build:

- [Smithsonian Folkways, Music of Indonesia, volume 14, revised note supplement](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40441.pdf),
  PDF pages 16-17: related simultaneous melodic lines and recurring gong punctuation; metallophones and hanging gongs.
- [UNESCO, Gamelan](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gamelan-01607), instruments, interlocking layers,
  centuries of practice and transmission through formal and informal education. Verified during the cold review.
- [Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, Sumarsam: Dualisms in the Formative and Transformational
  Processes of Javanese Performing Arts](https://asia.si.edu/research/essays/sumarsam/), the Sundanese and
  Javanese performers at Paris in 1889 and the distinction between their repertoire and court gamelan.
- [Philharmonie de Paris, L’orchestre au cœur d’un musée vivant](https://philharmoniedeparis.fr/fr/magazine/perspectives/lorchestre-au-coeur-dun-musee-vivant),
  Vincent Agrech, 4 June 2025: Javanese gamelan heard by Debussy in 1889, within a discussion of colonial encounters.
