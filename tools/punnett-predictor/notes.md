## What it teaches
Students connect a Punnett square to chromosome segregation in meiosis. They simulate offspring, compare observed and predicted proportions with chi-square, and investigate linkage.

## Key skills
- Combine one allele from each parent to find a possible offspring genotype.
- Trace chromosome pairs through two divisions to four haploid products.
- Compare a small sample with a large sample using counts and fractions.
- Check whether a statistical test has enough expected observations.
- Explain why linked genes make parental combinations more common.

## Support at home
- Flip two coins twenty times. Count the pairs, then discuss why four possible outcomes do not guarantee equal counts.
- Ask your child to explain why four children need not include one of each possible inherited combination. Avoid guessing anyone's genotype from appearance.
- Compare a small handful of mixed objects with a larger handful. Discuss why proportions can change.

## Talk about it
- Why is a square a list of chances rather than a schedule for four offspring?
- How can a correct model give a result above the 5% chi-square cutoff?
- What evidence distinguishes linkage from ordinary sample variation?
- Why does a 50% recombinant fraction look like independent assortment?

## Good to know
Chi-square uses positive-probability phenotype classes, with df equal to their number minus one. A test requires at least ten offspring and every expected count at least five. A deterministic cross has nothing to test. A fit supports compatibility, not proof of the model. Clear restarts the same seeded random stream, not a fresh random seed.

The tail probability describes a discrepancy at least as large as the observed one, assuming the prediction. It is not the probability that the model is true. Choosing a new seed until a result fits is not independent evidence. The animated tally reveals actual sampled prefixes, including their counts and fractions.

The chart uses a logarithmic horizontal axis and follows the first listed dominant phenotype (round yellow in the two-gene model, gray normal in flies). Blue and pink distinguish inherited homologs, not different genes. One meiosis produces four haploid products that may share genotypes. For peas these are meiotic products; plant gametes form later through the haploid generation.

Real traits often depend on many genes and on the environment. Real crossing over can happen more than once between distant genes; this model counts one. The slider represents the recombinant gamete fraction, not the fraction of all meioses with a crossover.

B/b and V/v are simplified classroom symbols for the fly traits, not official Drosophila allele nomenclature.
