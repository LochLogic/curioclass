## What it teaches

Students build source credits by finding details in books, web pages and photo pages. They test whether a reader can find the original and thank its creator.

## Key skills

- Find an author, title and publication year on different book pages.
- Tell an author from an illustrator and a photographer from an uploader.
- Record a page title, website, date and full web address.
- Explain how credit helps both creators and readers.
- Revise a credit after seeing where its trail breaks.

## Support at home

- Open a book together. Find who wrote it, who illustrated it and its publication year.
- Look at a family photo. Ask who took it and how you would thank that person.
- Notice a credit on a recipe, library book or newspaper picture. Talk about how a reader could find the source.

## A five-minute start

Open Owl Poster. Tap the first card slot, then Ana Ruiz on the cover. Find the title and turn to the copyright page for the year. Open the web and photo pieces to make their cards. Try the test before everything is complete, then watch where the reader gets stuck. Correct one card and test again.

## Modes

**Build:** Three projects each contain a book fact, a web fact and a photo. Select a card slot and then any source detail. Nothing is judged until Test my credits. All three pieces need an opened or edited card, but their slots may still be empty. Every test follows the book, web page and photo in that order. A source's first successful test earns one postcard; repeated tests never duplicate it.

Each trail ending stays long enough to read its message aloud. Motion off keeps the same timing. Tap a piece tag to jump to its ending, or Back to my cards to stop and revise. Tapping a card slot during a trail also returns to the source so it can be edited.

**Creator's Corner:** Nine named creators have painted mailboxes. Two writing teams have separate group cards. Open a story and switch No credit on and off to see how an unnamed piece loses its trail back. Illustrators' stories are included even when the borrowed piece uses the book's words.

## Talk questions

- Two books share a title and an author. What else helps the reader choose your edition?
- Who wrote Our Moon? What did Ana Ruiz make for that book?
- What happens when a photo uploader gets the thank-you instead of the photographer?
- Why might you include the date you looked at an undated page?
- How does a credit help someone check a fact?

## Good to know

The documents, people, websites and creator stories are made up. The .example addresses are reserved examples and do not open websites. All photos are generated illustrations of fictional borrowed sources. Download on a photo page deliberately says that downloading is not needed for a credit.

This is a classroom credit format built around who, what, when and where. It does not claim to teach a complete MLA or other formal citation style. The web trail can find a correctly identified page without a date, then explains why dates help identify versions. An undated page offers the current date in America/Denver. The source book's claim that the Moon has no air to breathe means no breathable air; NASA describes its extremely thin exosphere.

The tool teaches acknowledging authorship and including information that lets others credit content, supporting AASL Engage VI.B.2 and VI.B.3. It does not decide whether a use is permitted, evaluate source credibility or teach licensing.

Cards and postcards save only in this browser on this device. Start over asks before clearing all three projects. Storage failures do not stop play. Sound and Motion are optional; Read speaks the current hint. Fixed voice lines use the kit's browser narration fallback until recordings are supplied. Keyboard users select a slot and activate a source detail with Enter. Panel controls come before source chips and piece tags in the Tab order.
