# Shakespeare Remix

## One idea
Shakespeare was a remixer: lay one of his scenes beside the older book he borrowed from, link the pieces, and mark each link kept, cut, added, changed or flipped with a reason, until the change log shows what he did to his source and why; then remix one of his lines yourself and log your own changes the same way.

## Not like
- globe-theatre-stage (ela 12, this group, same family): the text is fixed and productions differ. Here the text itself is the thing that changed, from an older source into Shakespeare.
- remix-lab (ela 8, not specced): plans a modern retelling of a myth with keep, change and flip. Here the main work is analysis of a real transformation (Ovid, Montaigne and Plutarch into Shakespeare), with proof from both texts; the student's own remix is a short last step that uses the same log.
- world-lit-atlas (ela 10, this group): what readers of a place already knew. Here the question is what one writer took from another.
- satire-decoder (ela 11, wave 4 earlier group): said versus meant. Here it is source versus result.
- American Lit Timeline (ela 11, this group): works placed beside their moment. Here two texts are placed beside each other, segment by segment.

## Who and how
- Grade 9 ELA, module Shakespeare and His Sources: "How an author draws on and transforms source material".
- Standards: RL.9-10.9.
- Driver: students in pairs on laptops, 25 to 40 minutes for one remix set; a teacher can project one set and take the class's marks.
- tool.json: driver "students", minutes "25-40", modes ["Log", "Your remix"], grades_label "Grade 9". Kit starter with `kit:theme-912` (dark) and an accent override.

## Screens and modes
Kit frame. Header: "Shakespeare Remix", small line naming the set ("Ovid into A Midsummer Night's Dream"); Motion, Sound, Read. A set picker of three cards opens first, each with the two titles, the source's date and a one-line "what to watch for".

**Log mode.** Three columns set by Bard.Align: **Source** (left), **Shakespeare** (middle), **Ours** (right, collapsed by default to a narrow tab labeled "A modern retelling, written for Curio Class"). Each text is split into numbered segments (Content). Thin link lines join matching segments; a source segment with no partner shows a "nothing here" stub on the other side, and the reverse.
- Tap a link (or a stub) to open the **log card**: a mark (**Kept**, **Cut**, **Added**, **Changed**, **Flipped**), a **Why** chip row (**For laughs**, **For the stage**, **For the verse**, **To change who we side with**, **To fit the play's world**, **Other**, with a short note box up to 140 characters), and **Quote it**: tap a few words in each column to attach them as evidence (highlighted in both).
- The **change log** strip under the columns lists every link in order, with its mark word, why chip and quoted words. **Compare with ours** shows the tool's log beside the student's, row by row, and never scores; a row that differs reads "Ours says Flipped. Which words would change your mind?".
- When every link has a mark, the **pattern bar** appears: counts per mark, and one sentence the student completes from a frame, "Shakespeare mostly ___ his source, especially when ___, which makes ___." (word chips plus free text).

**Your remix.** Pick one Shakespeare segment from the set (three are offered per set). Choose a new setting from four cards (a school hallway, a group chat, a night bus, a spaceship). Write your version (up to 400 characters) in a box under the original. Then log your own changes against Shakespeare's lines with the same marks and why chips. **Read my remix** shows the original, the student's version and their log, with **Copy**.

**Afterlives** card (in the panel, per set): later works that reused the same story, as title, author or maker, year and one line, never quoted.

Panel (right): `#hint`, **Marked** count (links marked, of total), **Afterlives**, and **Clear my work** (asks Yes / No).

## Content
Sources and editions are in Sources; every quotation below was checked against them. Shakespeare lines are cited by act and scene and numbered within each excerpt. The "Ours" retellings are written for Curio Class and say so.

**Set 1. Ovid, "Pyramus and Thisbe" (Metamorphoses, Book IV), into A Midsummer Night's Dream, 5.1** (the workmen's play).
What to watch for: "Shakespeare kept the plot and flipped the feeling." Context line: Shakespeare used the same Ovid story for tragedy in Romeo and Juliet; here his amateur actors turn it into comedy before a laughing audience.
Segments and the tool's log:
1. The wall. Source: "Envious wall, why dost thou stand in the way of lovers? ... that a passage has been given for our words to our loving ears." Shakespeare: "Thou wall, O wall, O sweet and lovely wall, / Show me thy chink, to blink through with mine eyne." and "O wicked wall, through whom I see no bliss, / Curs'd be thy stones for thus deceiving me!" Log: **Changed**, for the stage: the wall is now an actor (Snout) holding up his fingers as the chink. Quote "Envious wall" / "O wicked wall".
2. The kisses. Source: "or if that is too much, that, at least, thou shouldst open, for the exchange of kisses." Shakespeare (Thisbe): "My cherry lips have often kiss'd thy stones," Log: **Flipped**, for laughs: kissing the wall itself.
3. The meeting place. Source: "to meet at the tomb of Ninus, and to conceal themselves beneath the shade of a tree. There was there a lofty mulberry tree, very full of snow-white fruit, quite close to a cold spring." Shakespeare: "Wilt thou at Ninny's tomb meet me straightway?" (Ninny is a slip for Ninus; omit Riley's footnote mark). Log: **Changed**, for laughs.
4. The lion. Source: "Lo! a lioness approaches, having her foaming jaws besmeared with the recent slaughter of oxen, about to quench her thirst with the water of the neighboring spring." Shakespeare: "You, ladies, you, whose gentle hearts do fear / The smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on floor, ... Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am / A lion fell, nor else no lion's dam;" Log: **Added**, for laughs and for the stage: the lion stops to tell the audience he is not a real lion.
5. Pyramus finds the veil. Source: "One night will be the ruin of two lovers, of whom she was the most deserving of a long life. My soul is guilty; 'tis I that have destroyed thee," Shakespeare: "O grim-look'd night! O night with hue so black!" (from his first entrance) and his death, "Thus die I, thus, thus, thus. / Now am I dead, / Now am I fled; / My soul is in the sky. / Tongue, lose thy light! / Moon, take thy flight! / Now die, die, die, die, die." Log: **Flipped**, for laughs and for the verse: the grief becomes jingling rhyme.
6. The mulberry. Source: "For the color of the fruit, when it has fully ripened, is black;" (the story explains why mulberries are dark). Shakespeare: no partner. Log: **Cut**, to fit the play's world: the workmen's play needs no origin story.
7. The onstage audience. Source: no partner. Shakespeare: the watching nobles joke through the play, for example Theseus: "I wonder if the lion be to speak." Demetrius: "No wonder, my lord. One lion may, when many asses do." Log: **Added**, to change who we side with: we laugh with the audience, not cry with the lovers.
Ours: "Wall Vent", two neighbors in an apartment building talk through a heating vent, plan to meet at the corner store, and the story ends with a misunderstanding over a dropped phone and both of them laughing on the sidewalk (about 120 words, five segments, written for Curio Class; no deaths).
Afterlives: Romeo and Juliet, William Shakespeare (about 1595), the same story as tragedy; West Side Story, Arthur Laurents, Leonard Bernstein and Stephen Sondheim (1957), Romeo and Juliet moved to rival New York gangs.
Content note shown on the set card: "Both versions end with the lovers' deaths; Shakespeare's is played for laughs."

**Set 2. Montaigne, "Of the Caniballes" (John Florio's translation, 1603), into The Tempest, 2.1** (Gonzalo's commonwealth).
What to watch for: "Shakespeare kept the words almost exactly, then let two listeners mock them."
1. The list of absences. Source: "It is a nation, would I answer Plato, that hath no kinde of traffike, no knowledge of Letters, no intelligence of numbers, no name of magistrate, nor of politike superioritie; no use of service, of riches or of povertie; no contracts, no successions, no partitions, no occupation but idle; no respect of kindred, but common, no apparell but naturall, no manuring of lands, no use of wine, corne, or mettle." Shakespeare: "I' th' commonwealth I would by contraries / Execute all things; for no kind of traffic / Would I admit; no name of magistrate; / Letters should not be known; riches, poverty, / And use of service, none; contract, succession, / Bourn, bound of land, tilth, vineyard, none; / No use of metal, corn, or wine, or oil; / No occupation; all men idle, all;" Log: **Kept**, for the verse: the same items, set into blank verse. Quote "no kinde of traffike" / "no kind of traffic".
2. Who is speaking. Source: Montaigne describes a real people reported to him from Brazil. Shakespeare: an old courtier daydreams about ruling the island. Log: **Changed**, to fit the play's world.
3. "No sovereignty". Source: "nor of politike superioritie". Shakespeare: "No sovereignty,--" then Sebastian: "Yet he would be King on't." and Antonio: "The latter end of his commonwealth forgets the beginning." Log: **Added**, to change who we side with: the mockers point out that Gonzalo would be king of a land with no kings.
4. Virtue. Source: "The very words that import lying, falshood, treason, dissimulations, covetousnes, envie, detraction, and pardon, were never heard of amongst them." Shakespeare: "All things in common nature should produce / Without sweat or endeavour; treason, felony, / Sword, pike, knife, gun, or need of any engine, / Would I not have; but nature should bring forth, / Of it own kind, all foison, all abundance, / To feed my innocent people." Log: **Changed**: from a people who do not know the words for vice to a dream of nature doing the work.
5. The Golden Age. Source: "what in those nations we see by experience, doth not only exceed all the pictures wherewith licentious Poesie hath proudly imbellished the golden age," Shakespeare: "I would with such perfection govern, sir, / T' excel the Golden Age." Log: **Kept**.
Skip Antonio's "whores and knaves" line (the excerpt cuts around it with "[...]").
Ours: "Group Project", a student's speech planning a class with no grades, no deadlines and no group leaders, while two classmates whisper that she has just appointed herself leader (about 120 words, five segments, written for Curio Class).
Afterlives: Forbidden Planet, directed by Fred M. Wilcox (1956 film), The Tempest moved to a distant planet; Une Tempête, Aimé Césaire (1969 play), the story retold from Caliban's side.
Context card: Montaigne's essay was written in the 1570s to 1580s from reports of the Tupinambá of Brazil; it praises them against Europe, and it also speaks for them. Both points are in the card.

**Set 3. Plutarch, Life of Antonius (Sir Thomas North's translation, 1579), into Antony and Cleopatra, 2.2** (Enobarbus describes the barge).
What to watch for: "Shakespeare kept the facts and turned them into a spell."
1. The barge. Source: "to take her barge in the river of Cydnus; the poop whereof was of gold, the sails of purple, and the oars of silver, which kept stroke in rowing after the sound of the music of flutes, howboys, cithernes, viols, and such other instruments as they played upon in the barge." Shakespeare: "The barge she sat in, like a burnished throne, / Burned on the water. The poop was beaten gold; / Purple the sails, and so perfumed that / The winds were love-sick with them; the oars were silver, / Which to the tune of flutes kept stroke, and made / The water which they beat to follow faster, / As amorous of their strokes." Log: **Kept** (gold, purple, silver, flutes, kept stroke) and **Changed**, for the verse: "burnished throne", "Burned on the water", the love-sick winds and amorous water are new.
2. The instruments. Source: "flutes, howboys, cithernes, viols". Shakespeare: "flutes" only. Log: **Cut**, for the verse.
3. Cleopatra herself. Source: "And now for the person of her self, she was laid under a pavilion of cloth of gold of tissue, apparelled and attired like the goddess Venus, commonly drawn in picture:" Shakespeare: "For her own person, / It beggared all description:" Log: **Flipped**: Plutarch describes her, Shakespeare says she cannot be described.
4. Who tells it. Source: the historian narrates. Shakespeare: Enobarbus, a hard-bitten soldier, tells it to Roman officers. Log: **Changed**, to change who we side with: even a cynic is dazzled.
Ours: "Arrival", a pop star's arrival at an awards show, told first as a news brief and then by a bored security guard who cannot stop describing it (about 120 words, written for Curio Class).
Afterlives: All for Love, John Dryden (1677), the same story as a tighter tragedy; Cleopatra, directed by Joseph L. Mankiewicz (1963 film).

Your remix segments offered: Set 1 segments 1 and 5, Set 2 segment 3, Set 3 segment 1.

## Interactions in detail
- Links: tap a link line, its stub, or either segment (then pick the partner); arrow keys move between links, Enter opens the log card. One mark per link; the student can change it any time.
- Quote it: tapping words in a column toggles them as evidence (whole words only, up to 15 words per side); they highlight in the accent with an underline.
- Compare with ours: a second, thinner log row under each student row; marks that match show "Same", others show both marks and the prompt above. Never a score.
- Pattern bar: counts computed from the student's marks; the frame sentence keeps its chips and text in the store.
- Your remix: the 400-character box shows a live count; the log for the remix uses the same card; Copy puts plain text on the clipboard.
- Store: localStorage `remix-work` per set (marks, why, notes, quotes, remix text), try/catch. Clear my work (after Yes) empties it.
- Sizes: at 1366x768 and 1280x720 Source and Shakespeare sit side by side with Ours as a tab; the change log strip shows six rows. At 680x425 the columns become two tabs with the link list between them. At 760 and 375 wide the columns stack, each segment shows its partner's number, and links open from the change log list instead of lines.

## Voice
Bank voice `casual-peer` (Zubenelgenubi), style "Say casually and naturally, like a friendly teenager explaining something". `Curio.voice`, scope the set.
- welcome (how, first open): "Shakespeare borrowed a lot. Pick a set and line up his scene with the book he took it from."
- link (how, first link opened): "Mark what he did with this part: kept it, cut it, added something, changed it, or flipped it. Then pick why."
- quote (how, first mark without quoted words): "Tap a few words on each side to prove it."
- differ (event, each Compare row that differs; stale when that mark changes): "We marked that one differently. Which words would change your mind?"
- pattern (event, all links in a set marked; stale when any mark changes): "That's the whole log. Finish the sentence about what he mostly did."
- remix (how, first Your remix): "Your turn. Pick a setting, rewrite the line, then log your own changes."
