# Is the Historian Right?
Renamed from: Historian's Toolkit

## One idea
Every historian's claim is a case to test: open the case file, put each clue on the scale as supports, weakens, complicates or doesn't decide it, and the verdict stamps you are allowed to use (convincing, partly convincing, not convincing) follow from your own scale, so you cannot call a claim convincing while a clue on your scale weakens it; then you write the verdict with the language of cause and evaluation and, where the claim says "mainly", rewrite it as strongly as the evidence allows.

## Not like
- panel-of-experts (social studies 7, specced): four experts weigh one big question. Here one historian's claim at a time, tested clue by clue, and the language of cause and evaluation is the point.
- perspective-frames (eld 6-8, not built): sorts statements by speaker and compares views. Here the student judges one argument against evidence, with verdict language.
- cause-and-effect-rewind (eld 3-5, not built): links steps with because and so. Here causes are weighed ("the main cause" against "one cause"), and a claim can be too strong.
- premise-inspector (ela 11): lays later speeches over the Declaration's premises. Here historians' causal claims and evidence, with ELD frames.
- source-mixer (library 11): mixes sources into an answer about the Dust Bowl. Here the claim is given and the student evaluates it.
- definition-duel, chart-narrator, inbox-simulator, argument-frames-pro (eld 9-12, this group): this is the group's history tool and its only mystery.

## Who and how
- ELD grades 9 to 12. Module: Analyzing Historical Arguments (Language for Social Studies).
- Driver: a student alone; pairs can argue the scale before checking. 25 to 40 minutes (one case about 12).
- Standards: WIDA.ELD-5 (Language for Social Studies) through three historical claims and primary sources; WIDA.Argue taught in full: identify a claim and its causal language, weigh evidence for and against, state a verdict, and support it with evidence and reasoning, including a counter-point ("However, ___ complicates this").
- Help setting: **Lots of help**, **Some help**, **Little help** (WIDA levels 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 in teacher notes). Default Some help.
- Topic line: "the language of cause, evidence and evaluation".
- Content notes (tool page and teacher notes): the Titanic and Chicago fire cases mention deaths; the frontier case names the forced removal of Native nations and laws used to stop Black men from voting. Facts are plain and brief.
- tool.json: driver "students", minutes "25-40", modes ["Cases", "Language tools"], grades_label "Grades 9-12". Kit starter only.

## Screens and modes
Header: title "Is the Historian Right?"; mode buttons **Cases** (opens first), **Language tools**; Motion, Sound, Read. Panel: Help setting, hint, tip.

### Cases (opens first)
- Three case folders on a desk: **The Titanic, 1912**, **The Great Chicago Fire, 1871**, **The Frontier, 1893**. Each shows "Not opened", "In progress" or "Verdict filed".
- Inside a case, four steps across the top: **1 The claim**, **2 The clues**, **3 The verdict**, **4 Write it**. Steps open in order; any done step can be revisited.
- 1 The claim: the claim card. Causal words are underlined (mainly because, explain, the most important effect). At Lots and Some help a **Plain English** layer sits under the claim. The student taps the cause and the effect in the claim (two taps); the tool shows "Cause: ___ → Effect: ___". A wrong tap gets "That part says when, not why. Tap the cause."
- 2 The clues: six clue cards on the left (text, table or picture, each with its source line). The scale on the right has four trays, each a word plus an icon: **Supports** (▲), **Weakens** (▼), **Complicates** (↔, another cause or a limit), **Doesn't decide it** (○). Drag or tap a card then a tray. **Check my scale** shows the case key: each card ✓ or "We put it in ___", with the one-line reason; cards can then be moved. Checking is optional before the verdict.
- 3 The verdict: three stamps, **Convincing**, **Partly convincing**, **Not convincing**. A stamp is available only when the student's scale allows it (rules below); an unavailable stamp shows why ("A clue on your scale weakens the claim."). For a claim with "mainly" or "explain", a **Fix the claim** strip follows: choose the strongest wording the scale allows (was the main cause of, was one cause of, contributed to, did not cause) and see the rewritten claim.
- 4 Write it: a short verdict paragraph from frames (see Content), with the student's own clue choices filled in as chips. **Read it** reads it aloud. **Copy** copies plain text. Filing the verdict marks the folder.

### Language tools
- Four drawers: **Cause words** (caused, led to, as a result, because of, contributed to, was one reason for, was the main cause of), ordered on a strength strip from weakest to strongest; **Evidence words** (shows, suggests, supports, according to, this is clear from); **Evaluation words** (convincing, partly convincing, however, although, overlooks, ignores, leaves out, goes too far); **Hedges** (may, might, probably, it is likely that). Each word card has a plain meaning, an example from the cases and **Hear it**.
- **Stronger or weaker?**: eight pairs of sentences about the cases; tap the one that claims more ("The lifeboats caused the deaths." / "Too few lifeboats contributed to the deaths."). Feedback names the word that changes the strength.

## Content
### Case 1: The Titanic, 1912
- Claim (written for Curio Class, the kind of claim many history books make): "The Titanic disaster was so deadly mainly because the ship did not carry enough lifeboats." Plain English: "Many people died. The main reason: too few lifeboats." Cause: not enough lifeboats. Effect: so deadly.
- T1 (table, US Senate report, 1912): "People on board: 2,223. Lifeboat seats: 1,176." Key: Supports. Reason: there were seats for only about half the people.
- T2 (US Senate report): the ship was "fulfilling all the requirements of the British Board of Trade." Key: Supports. Reason: the rules allowed too few boats, so the shortage was legal; it points to a deeper cause, the rules.
- T3 (table, US Senate report): "Saved: 706. Lost: 1,517." Key: Complicates. Reason: 706 is fewer than the 1,176 seats, so boats left with empty seats; how the boats were filled mattered too.
- T4 (US Senate report): "On the third day out ice warnings were received by the wireless operators on the Titanic." Key: Complicates. Reason: another cause, why the ship hit the ice at all.
- T5 (US Senate report): the ship was going "not less than 21 knots" and "the ship's speed was not reduced." Key: Complicates. Reason: another cause of the collision.
- T6 (US Senate report, recommendations): "There should be enough lifeboats for all on board." Key: Supports. Reason: the investigators saw lifeboats as a key problem to fix.
- Key verdict: Partly convincing. Fix the claim: "was one cause of" or "contributed to" (allowed); "was the main cause of" is not allowed while Complicates holds three cards.

### Case 2: The Great Chicago Fire, 1871
- Claim (written for Curio Class, the view most historians hold today): "Mrs. O'Leary's cow did not start the Great Chicago Fire. The cow story came from a newspaper, and the real cause is unknown." Plain English: "The famous cow story is not true. Nobody knows how the fire started." Cause: a newspaper story. Effect: people believed the cow story.
- G1 (Board of Police and Fire Commissioners, 1871 inquiry): "we are unable to determine" whether a spark from a chimney or a person started it. Key: Supports. Reason: officials at the time could not find the cause.
- G2 (later accounts): "A reporter, Michael Ahern, later said that he and other reporters made up the cow story. Accounts disagree about the year he said it." Key: Supports. Reason: it supports the claim, but it is one person's statement made many years later, so it is weaker than G1.
- G3: "The fire started in or near the O'Leary family's barn on DeKoven Street." Key: Doesn't decide it. Reason: it explains why people blamed the cow, but it fits both stories.
- G4 (photograph, AIC 59281): "Chicago after the fire, 1871." Key: Doesn't decide it. Reason: it shows the damage, not how the fire began.
- G5: "In 1997, the Chicago City Council passed a resolution clearing Mrs. O'Leary and her cow." Key: Doesn't decide it. Reason: a vote 126 years later is an opinion, not new evidence.
- G6: "About 300 people died, and about 100,000 people lost their homes." Key: Doesn't decide it. Reason: it shows how serious the fire was, not its cause.
- Key verdict: Convincing.

### Case 3: The Frontier, 1893
- Claim (Frederick Jackson Turner, "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," 1893): "The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development." Second line from the same paper: "the most important effect of the frontier has been in the promotion of democracy" (confirm against the printed text at build). Plain English: "Turner says: there was open land in the West, and settlers kept moving west. This is why America grew the way it did, and why it became more democratic." Cause: free land and settlers moving west. Effect: American development and democracy.
- F1 (1890 Census, quoted by Turner): "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." Key: Doesn't decide it. Reason: it tells why Turner wrote in 1893, not whether he was right.
- F2 (Indian Removal Act, 1830, its title): "An Act to provide for an exchange of lands with the Indians residing in any of the states or territories, and for their removal west of the river Mississippi." Key: Weakens. Reason: the land was not free; Native nations lived there, and the US government removed many of them by law and by force.
- F3 (Homestead Act, 1862): "A settler could claim 160 acres of public land by living on it and farming it for five years." Key: Supports. Reason: settlement westward was real, and the government pushed it.
- F4: "In 1869, Wyoming Territory gave women the right to vote, before any state did." Key: Supports. Reason: a western place widened democracy.
- F5: "In 1890, Mississippi's new constitution added a poll tax and a literacy test. They were used to stop Black men from voting." Key: Complicates. Reason: in Turner's time, democracy was shrinking for many Americans, so the frontier was not the whole story.
- F6: "In 1619, colonists in Virginia met as an elected assembly, the House of Burgesses." Key: Complicates. Reason: self-government in America had older roots in the East.
- Key verdict: Partly convincing. Fix the claim: "contributed to" (allowed); "explain" (the strongest) is not allowed while Weakens holds a card.

### Write it frames
- "The historian argues that ___ [cause word] ___."
- "[Clue] supports this, because it shows that ___."
- "However, [clue] complicates this, because it suggests that ___."
- "[Clue] weakens the claim, because ___." (offered only when Weakens has a card)
- "Overall, the claim is [stamp], because ___."
At Lots of help each blank has chips (the clue cards' short names and the reasons from the student's own scale); at Some help the clue slots have chips and the reasons are typed; at Little help one text box with the frames shown as faint starters that disappear as the student types.

### Stronger or weaker? pairs
1. "Too few lifeboats caused the deaths." / "Too few lifeboats contributed to the deaths." 2. "The cow started the fire." / "The cow may have started the fire." 3. "Free land explains American democracy." / "Free land was one reason for American democracy." 4. "The ice warnings show the crew was careless." / "The ice warnings suggest the crew could have slowed down." 5. "The speed was the main cause of the collision." / "The speed was one cause of the collision." 6. "This photo proves the cause of the fire." / "This photo shows the damage from the fire." 7. "The Homestead Act led to settlement in the West." / "The Homestead Act helped lead to settlement in the West." 8. "Turner's claim is wrong." / "Turner's claim goes too far." In each pair the first claims more.

## Interactions in detail
- Stamp rules, from the student's own scale (S, W, C are the numbers of cards in Supports, Weakens, Complicates): **Convincing** when W = 0, C = 0 and S ≥ 2; **Partly convincing** when S ≥ 1 and W + C ≥ 1; **Not convincing** when W ≥ 1 and W ≥ S. With the keys: Titanic S3 C3, Partly only; Chicago S2, Convincing only; Frontier S2 W1 C2, Partly only. A scale with no cards placed allows nothing ("Put clues on the scale first.").
- Fix the claim: allowed wordings follow the scale: "was the main cause of" (or the claim's own "mainly" or "explain") only when Convincing is allowed; "was one cause of" and "contributed to" when Partly is allowed; "did not cause" when Not convincing is allowed.
- Claim taps: the cause and effect spans are fixed per case; a tap on any other words gets the "when, not why" or "that is the effect" note.
- Check my scale shows the key but never moves a card; the stamps follow the student's scale, so a student may disagree with the key and still write a consistent verdict. The tip says: "Historians disagree too. Your verdict must match your scale."
- Write it check (Little help): the paragraph needs a cause word, an evidence word, an evaluation word, the chosen stamp, and at least two clue references (a clue's short name or two of its keywords). Each missing part is named once. Lots and Some help: a frame with an empty slot is pointed to.
- Mode change calls `V.hush()`. Storage `historian-right:v1` (try/catch): scales, stamps, paragraphs, help setting; **Clear** asks first.
- Sizes: at 1366x768 the clue cards and the four trays fit side by side; at 1000x625 the trays go in a 2 by 2 grid under the cards; at 760 cards above trays; at 375 cards in a sideways strip and trays as four buttons (tap a card, then a tray). The step bar stays visible. No horizontal page scroll.
