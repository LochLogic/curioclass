## What it teaches

Students identify a historical claim's cause and effect, weigh evidence for and against it, and write a verdict that matches their own scale. They practice the language of cause, evidence and evaluation, including a counter-point.

## Key skills

- Separate a claimed cause from its effect.
- Distinguish support, weakening evidence, another cause or limit, and evidence that does not decide a claim.
- Match a verdict and the strength of causal wording to the evidence.
- Refer to specific clues and explain why they matter.
- Qualify an argument with however, although and hedges.

## Support at home

- When someone says one thing caused an everyday event, ask what else might have contributed.
- Compare “caused” with “contributed to” in a conversation. Ask which needs stronger evidence.
- Ask your student to explain one clue that supports a claim and one that limits it. No screen is needed.

## Five-minute start

Open the Titanic folder. Tap the cause, then the effect. Select People and seats and put it in Supports. Select Saved and lost and discuss whether having more seats than survivors complicates the claim that the shortage was the main cause. Continue placing all six clues before opening The verdict.

## Modes

Cases contains three independent files with four steps each. Revisit any unlocked step. Check my scale reveals the interpretive key without moving a card. The student's placements control the stamps, even when they disagree with the key. There are no points, timers or win states.

Language tools contains four drawers and eight strength comparisons. The comparison sentences are claims to evaluate, not statements of established historical fact. A stronger sentence is not necessarily truer.

Lots of help offers clue and reason choices, suitable for WIDA levels 1-2. Some help offers clue choices with typed reasons, suitable for levels 3-4. Little help offers a free paragraph with faint frame starters, suitable for levels 5-6. These are supports, not assessments of a student's language level.

## Talk questions

- Does a clue describe the cause, the consequences, or a different question?
- What does “mainly” require that “contributed to” does not?
- Can evidence support a part of an argument while exposing a limit in the whole argument?
- Whose experiences does Turner's explanation leave out?
- Why does a later vote about responsibility differ from new evidence about an event?

## Good to know

Content notes: the Titanic and Chicago fire cases mention deaths; the frontier case names the forced removal of Native nations and laws used to stop Black men from voting. These facts are plain and brief. WIDA.ELD-5 is practiced through historical sources; WIDA.Argue through claim identification, weighing evidence, causal revision and a supported evaluation with a counter-point. This tool does not simulate tragedy or reward historical harm.

“Free land” is Turner's wording, not the tool's description of an unoccupied place. Native nations lived on the land. F2 names removal by law and force. The Frontier picture shows survey objects rather than an empty landscape.

The key is an interpretation to discuss. In particular, the Chicago claim's categorical denial is stronger than the cited museum account, which says the fire's cause is uncertain. This tension is retained from the specification and flagged for cold review in handoff.md. The plain-English layer is also the specification's wording.

The grammatical form changes “was” to “were” for “Too few lifeboats.” The wording choices retain their dictionary form. If every clue supports a claim, the writing frame asks for a limitation of a second clue rather than falsely labeling a supporting clue as complicating. This keeps a counter-point possible on unusual student scales.

The scale is a consistency aid, not an automatic historical truth detector. Counting clues does not measure their reliability or historical importance. One supporting clue is thin evidence and permits Partly convincing. Every clue in Doesn't decide it permits no stamp.
