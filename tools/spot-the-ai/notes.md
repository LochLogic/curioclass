## What it teaches

Children tell apart a machine following steps a person wrote and a machine using patterns learned from many examples.
They see how machines can help in everyday life and why an AI guess can be wrong.

## Key skills

- Watch what a machine does before making a prediction.
- Compare a written rule with a pattern learned from examples.
- Use evidence from the inside of a machine to explain a guess.
- Notice when a new example leads to a mistaken guess.

## Support at home

- Take turns giving a simple rule, such as clap twice when someone taps the table. Follow it exactly.
- Name a familiar machine and ask how it might decide what to do. It is fine to say you do not know.
- Look for a shared pattern among three household objects. Find something else that almost fits but does not.

## A 5-minute start

1. Tap the blue door on the shelf. Watch the number sequence, then tap Open it up.
2. Find the person sticker, pencil and picture rule. Tap Press it again.
3. Tap the face phone. Open it, then use Try a new one for a hat and a cat.
4. Compare the door's written steps with the phone's examples. Explain that the pictures show the idea of the inside.
5. Try the photo finder twice. Its new puppy works, but its fluffy mop guess is wrong.

## The modes

Machine Shop starts first. Machines are paired on the shelves. Tap one to see its job, open it to inspect its decision,
then try a new example or repeat its rule. Back to shelf returns to the full collection.
Tapping the already selected shelf tile keeps its job and voice running. Use the repeat control to replay the job;
selecting a different machine or returning to the shelf still stops the current one immediately.

AI or Not? presents ten machines in a fixed mixed order. Sparkle hands mean AI and fists mean a rule machine.
Tap the class vote, change it if needed, then Open it up. A matching guess lights a bulb with a check mark.
A missed guess leaves a plain bulb. Next advances; the final display compares all five pairs. There are no teams.

## Talk questions

- Does talking make the teddy AI? What did its picture rule show?
- What did the phone's many examples share? What happened with a hat? With a cat?
- Why did the photo finder choose the mop? What did it look like?
- Which machine could help you find something or get somewhere? What would you check if it guessed wrong?

## Good to know

AI here means a computer that learned patterns from many examples and uses them to guess. A rule is a sequence of
steps written by a person. Real products can combine both. These are simplified, original scenes, not descriptions
of every elevator, camera or map app. The written rule may take a number as input, and a talking toy cycles through
the same three phrases. The same input and rule produce the same behavior.

The face pile is a conceptual illustration, not a claim that a personal phone stores these people's portraits.
Face geometry and the dog features are introductory picture clues, not complete accounts of how neural networks work.
The lesson trains no model. It requests no camera, microphone or personal data and makes no network requests.
Progress lasts only until the page closes. Only the kit's Motion preference is stored on this device.

The bulbs record matching class guesses, not independently demonstrated understanding. The required fixed order
can be remembered on replay. Ask children to point to the rule card or learned examples and explain their evidence;
do not use the bulb total as a mastery score. A different sound must never identify AI before the reveal.

For the teddy, watch the highlighted saying move around the three-part picture rule. Its rule repeats the same
cycle, not the same sentence each time. The speaker's question bubble follows the narrated question and answer,
so a class can wait and listen without a question being spoken over a different answer.

This lesson uses familiar examples of machine learning. It is not a definition of every approach called AI.
People build both kinds of machine and choose many of the AI training examples. The contrast is how a decision
is made, not whether people were involved.
The face cards illustrate learning from examples; a real phone matches the enrolled owner's facial representation,
not just the presence of eyes, a nose and a mouth. A cat also has those features and still does not match the owner.

## Standards and sources

E1-ALG-ML-02: children compare learned examples with written rules and inspect the pattern and a new example.
E1-SOC-HU-15: children act on familiar applications and compare their helpful results and a mistaken result.
These identifiers are listed in data/standards/csta-2026-ids.txt. AI4K12 Big Idea 3 supports the learning-from-data idea.
The 2017 connection is 1A-IC-16, how computing changes everyday life; it is not a 2017 AI standard.

- [AI4K12, Big Idea 3](https://ai4k12.org/big-idea-3-overview/): computers can learn from data.
- [Apple Platform Security, Facial matching security](https://support.apple.com/en-om/guide/security/sece151358d1/web):
  neural-network training, matching enrolled facial representations, and recognition with a hat. Official regional
  copy checked on 2026-11-07 after the unregionalized route returned 429.
- [Google Maps 101](https://blog.google/products/maps/google-maps-101-how-ai-helps-predict-traffic-and-determine-routes/):
  historical traffic and machine learning for traffic prediction and route choice.
- [Hinton et al., IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 29(6), 2012](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/HintonDengYuEtAl-SPM2012.pdf):
  speech models learned from recorded speech.
- [Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, NeurIPS 2012](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html):
  object recognition learned from labeled pictures.
- [Covington, Adams and Sargin, RecSys 2016](https://research.google/pubs/deep-neural-networks-for-youtube-recommendations/):
  learned video recommendations.

## Accessibility

All actions have icons and words and targets of at least 60 pixels. Tab and Enter operate the tool; A and N vote in
the showdown. Read speaks the current next step. Sound starts on and can be turned off; Read still works.
Motion off keeps the same job timing and draws a still fan of examples. Narration uses the best available browser
voice. The curious-librarian/Aoede recording manifest is prepared; no voice recordings were made in this build.
