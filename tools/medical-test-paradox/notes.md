## What it teaches
Students read a conditional probability by choosing the correct group to divide by. They connect natural frequencies,
two-way tables and a two-test tree, and explain how prevalence changes what a positive result means.

## Key skills
- Distinguish having a condition from testing positive.
- Divide by the group named after "if" in a question.
- Subtract overlap when finding the chance of either event.
- Multiply along a probability tree under a stated independence assumption.
- Compare expected counts with repeatable random outcomes.

## Support at home
- Ask "out of which group?" when you hear a percentage in everyday conversation.
- Sort familiar objects by two traits, such as red and round. Count each overlap before making a fraction.
- Discuss why the same clue can mean different things when the starting situation changes.

## A 5-minute start
1. Leave Town at its defaults and press Test everyone.
2. Make a prediction before locking it. A high Catches value is not the answer to this question.
3. Press Positives only. Compare 90 true positives with 891 false positives.
4. Open Table. Compare the Positive column with the Has it row: 90/981 versus 90/100.
5. Raise How common and repeat. Ask which denominator changed.

## Modes
Town shows all four groups. Filled icons have the condition; plus badges identify positive tests. At 100,000 people,
one square represents ten people; partial icons represent remaining people or fractional expected counts.
On average uses expected values. Random run makes two seeded draws per person. Run 100 towns pools true and false
positives across towns, rather than averaging individual percentages. The horizontal axis is logarithmic.

Table supports dragging a question onto a fraction, or selecting a question and tapping a fraction with a keyboard.
Wrong matches give the relevant fraction and can be tried again. This is guided practice with no score.
After an answer-giving hint, a later match is labeled as the worked example. It does not receive the feedback
for an unassisted match. Trying every target therefore cannot create an unassisted result for that question.

Second test applies the same test only to first positives. The tests are assumed independent conditional on whether
someone has the condition. This assumption is educational, not a claim about real repeat tests. In Chances, multiply
along branches. The two double-positive leaves also show their products as shares of the whole town, distinct from
the conditional chance of the second result. Solid and dashed routes distinguish the two positive paths.
First negatives stop and are labeled separately. The first-round chances come from the displayed
town's counts; the second-round chances use the chosen test rates. An empty condition group keeps its assumed
test rate on its unused branch, which carries no probability mass.

Cases has seven examples. The perfect-test case deliberately uses 100% Clears beyond the usual 99.9% slider maximum.
The coin case shows independence: a positive leaves the condition's probability unchanged.
In the Test twice case, a plus means positive twice. Its whole-town grid counts 81 caught, 19 missed, 80.2 false
alarms and 9,819.8 well people without two positives, on average. Table continues to describe a single test.
Glossary opens the five term chips. Tap a term, then Close glossary or Escape to return to the simulation.

## Talk questions
- Why does "catches 90%" not mean that 90% of positive tests are true positives?
- Which group belongs in the denominator for "if I test positive"?
- Why must the overlap be subtracted once in the addition rule?
- What would fail in the second-test calculation if the two tests tended to make the same errors?

## Good to know
Glimmer fever is fictional. This is a probability model, not guidance for interpreting a real medical test. Ask a doctor
about personal results. No personal information is requested and no work is saved. Settings last for this page session;
the kit remembers Motion on this device.

The tool serves S-CP.A.1 through A.5, B.6, B.7 and the branch multiplication idea in B.8 (+). It does not teach B.9 (+)
counting. The module's broad S-CP.A and S-CP.B catalog labels are retained; a catalog owner should place B.9 elsewhere.

The historical screening example uses fictional framing while retaining its teaching numbers. Many physicians in the
reported study struggled with percentage framing; most answered correctly with natural frequencies. This is not a
claim about all doctors or all tests.
