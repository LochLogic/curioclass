## What it teaches
Students balance chemical equations by changing whole-molecule counts while keeping formulas fixed. They use atom and mass ledgers to justify conservation and use periodic group charges to predict simple products.

## Key skills
- Count every atom, including atoms inside parentheses.
- Choose coefficients without changing a substance's formula.
- Reduce a balance to the smallest whole numbers.
- Track a polyatomic group when it stays intact.
- Use usual ion charges to choose a product formula.

## Support at home
- Arrange small household objects in two equal collections. Double both collections and discuss why their ratio stays the same.
- Ask your student to explain why two water molecules differ from one hydrogen peroxide molecule using a sketch.
- Talk about a familiar reaction, such as bread rising, and ask where its atoms went. Do not perform chemical demonstrations.

## A five-minute start
Open Workbench on Water. Increase water to two and hydrogen to two. Ask which atoms match and why both masses become 36.03 g. Open the water formula card and compare a coefficient change with a subscript change.

## Modes
Workbench opens all 25 equations for exploration. The images connect symbolic equations with recognizable contexts.

Levels groups the same equations into five increasing challenges. A check means the current equation has a balanced lowest-term solution, not that the student completed a formal assessment. Equations already balanced at one are intentionally included.

Predict has ten cards. P1, P3 and P8 are worked examples. Others ask for a product before showing the product ledger. Wrong choices explain the charge pattern; there is no prediction score or saved mastery badge. Review a student's reasoning aloud rather than treating retries as assessment evidence.

## Talk questions
- Why can equal total masses alone fail to prove every element is conserved?
- What stays the same when every coefficient doubles?
- Why can nitrate stay together in one equation while hydroxide breaks apart in another?
- Which periodic-group pattern helped you predict calcium chloride?

## Good to know
Coefficients range from 1 to 30. Zero, fractional and out-of-range values are refused. Reset returns counts to one, including worked examples.

Mass assumes each coefficient is an amount in moles. Values use the Royal Society of Chemistry masses already in Elements Inspector, retained to their original precision. Summation happens before rounding to two decimals. Individual mismatches can cancel in the total mass, so every atom row matters.

Small molecules have ball-and-stick models. Ionic substances use atom inventories as small ion clusters. Other large structures are labeled atom inventories and do not assert molecular geometry. Drawings are counting models rather than literal scale or a simulation of reactions.

Count groups uses only groups that stay whole on both sides. Ledgers always check the underlying elements, even when a group is displayed as one row. Symbols, numbers and words accompany all colors.

This is symbolic chemistry, not laboratory instructions. The sodium and water example is a teacher demonstration behind a shield. Lead compounds, chlorine, carbon monoxide and burning substances are hazardous. The peroxide image illustrates decomposition on a cotton pad; it is not wound-care advice.

Progress is stored on this device only, using localStorage with an unavailable-storage fallback. Clear progress removes the saved equation checks. Sound and Motion use the shared controls; Read speaks the current hint. Narration falls back to the browser because this Build does not record audio.

## Sources and scope
- [OpenStax Chemistry 2e, 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations): coefficients, subscripts, atom balance, mole ratios and lowest terms.
- [OpenStax Chemistry 2e, 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-2-classifying-chemical-reactions): reaction classes and the equations' contexts.
- [OpenStax Chemistry 2e, 2.6](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-6-ionic-and-molecular-compounds): usual group ion charges and charge-neutral formulas.
- [RSC periodic table](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/): atomic masses, through the repository's Elements Inspector table.
- [PubChem magnesium peroxide, CID 61745](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/61745): MgO2 is a real compound, correcting the spec's unstable-substance lookup.
- PubChem hydrogen peroxide CID 784, ozone CID 24823 and carbon monoxide CID 281: identities and uses in formula cards.
- American Chemical Society, Guidelines for Chemical Laboratory Safety in Secondary Schools (2016): demonstration safety context.

## Display details
Atom dots stop at 24; the numbers remain exact. Counts above eight use a model multiplier. On narrow screens the individual gram details are hidden, while atom counts and both total masses remain visible. Secondary navigation and story pictures can scroll on phones.
