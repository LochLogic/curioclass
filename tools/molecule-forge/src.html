Bench manual

The spots rule

Tap a tray atom to add it. It joins an open spot on the piece you last touched. Each bond fills one spot at each end. A double bond fills two; a triple fills three. When a piece is full, the next atom starts a new piece. The well holds 40 atoms.

Drag a tray atom onto a ball or peg to choose its bond. Drop it in empty space to start a new piece. Drag two model atoms together to join them. Both need free spots. Pull an atom far away to break its bonds. Drop it on the tray to melt it. Tap a bond to change its order. H and Cl bonds stay single.

Shapes and readouts

The outer electrons set the spots. Groups of electrons spread out: two groups form a line, three a flat triangle, and four point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Lone pairs take up extra room. While you build, each lone pair takes 2.5 degrees off the four-group angle of 109.5 degrees.

A bond marked “estimate” uses the two atom radii added together. Double bonds use 0.87 times that sum; triple bonds use 0.78. A bond marked “model” shows the distance in the stored shape. These are drawing rules, not a way to measure an unknown real molecule.

The strip uses Hill order: C first, H next, then the other symbols in ABC order. With no C, all symbols use ABC order. Guide headings keep forms such as NH3 and HCl.

The guide names a piece only when all spots are full. It checks which atoms join, including every H. It treats both alternating drawings of benzene as one molecule. Benzene's six ring bonds all have the same length, about 139 pm. The two drawings do not mean long and short bonds. Vanillin and aspirin also have two ring drawings.

A named piece moves to a stored PubChem shape. Most are computed shapes, not direct measurements. Water's measured bend is 104.5 degrees; this shape rounds to about 104. Ammonia measures about 107; its shape here rounds to about 106. Methane rounds to about 109.

Hydrogen and silane use a textbook shape instead of a PubChem one: H2 has a 74 pm gap; silane uses a tetrahedron with estimated 142 pm bonds. Allene uses an ideal shape too, with the two end groups at right angles and estimated bond lengths. The readout names these cases.

Sticks shows the bonds. Spheres shows the relative space atoms take up. Ball colours follow Jmol CPK; each ball also has its element letters. “Not in this guide” means just that. It does not mean a new substance. The kit does not check mirror forms or other 3D forms with the same connections.

Crystal seed plate

Switch to Crystal. Add one or two seed atoms, then press Grow. C grows diamond; C=C grows graphite. Si grows silicon; Si with O grows silica. Na with Cl grows salt; Na alone grows sodium metal. Cu grows copper. Tap a seed bond to change it. An unsupported seed keeps the last specimen on the bench. Undo restores the seed. Clear seed removes both.

These are small pieces of much larger patterns. Diamond and silicon stop at 525 atoms, graphite at 600 in four sheets, salt at 392 ions, copper at 561 atoms, and sodium at 559. Silica has 147 Si and 220 O atoms. At an open edge there are fewer neighbours. “Inside” counts each atom or ion with all its neighbours.

Cut open shows the inside around the atom you inspect and keeps its neighbours in view. Tap a ball to count them. You can also use the ATOM number or the [ and ] keys.

Silica here has straight Si–O–Si links. It is based on a form called cristobalite, with simpler angles. Quartz links bend to about 144 degrees. Some O atoms are missing at this sample's edges, so the whole piece has less than two O for each Si. Deep inside, the ratio is two.

Push and current

Push shows salt crack, metal bend, graphite sheets slide, or a network hold. Bonds across a network's cut glow at the end. The count comes from this piece. Current shows a stream for metals and graphite, a few carriers for silicon, and none for solid salt, diamond or silica.

These are simple pictures of what materials do. They do not solve forces or circuits, or predict strength and current. Each test starts with the original pattern. Cracked salt loses contacts across its gap. Bent metal keeps the original neighbour count: this model does not work out its new contacts.

Tap or press any key to settle a moving test at once. A salt grain just one millimetre wide holds about 4.5 × 10¹⁹ ions. Our bench can show only a tiny part.

Keyboard and view

Keys 1 to 9 add H, C, N, O, S, Cl, Si, Na and Cu. Tab reaches controls, then atoms; Enter selects. Shift+Enter on another atom joins it. Shift and tap does the same. Delete melts the selected atom. Arrow keys turn 15 degrees. + and − zoom. 0 centers.

T switches the bench. V changes view. H starts Show. G grows. P pushes. E tests current. X cuts open. B opens the guide; M opens this Manual. Control+Z undoes up to 30 edits; Control+S saves on this device. Escape closes a drawer. Show turns 12 degrees each second. With Motion off, the turn keys move 30 degrees.

Things people have found

  • Lone pairs squeeze the angle: methane 109.5°, ammonia about 107°, water 104.5°. OpenStax 7.6.
  • Ethanol and dimethyl ether share the same atoms. One boils at about 78 °C; the other at −24.8 °C at normal air pressure. PubChem 702; 8254. The order you tap decides which isomer you get.
  • Benzene's double bonds can sit two ways. It is still one molecule, with six equal ring bonds. Vanillin and aspirin have two drawings too. Checked against the bundled PubChem graphs.
  • There are 92 legal structures with one to three C, N or O atoms filled with H. This guide names 36; 56 have no name here. Counted from this kit's spots rule, not a claim that they are new.
  • Diamond and graphite are both carbon. Graphite's sheets have a weak pull between them and can slide. Pencil lead is graphite with clay. OpenStax 10.5.
  • Salt cracks when like charges face each other. Copper bends as its atoms move past one another. OpenStax 10.5.
  • CO2 forms small molecules, but SiO2 forms a huge network. C and Si share a column in the periodic table. OpenStax 18.3.
  • Silicon is a semiconductor: it carries a little current, and more when warm. OpenStax 18.3.
  • In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea from non-living starting chemicals. ACS; Wöhler's paper.

Where the model ends

Spots leave out some bonds and charges. O=Si=O fits the spots rule, but ordinary bulk silica is a network. Three O atoms can form a ring here; real ozone is bent. It needs charge rules this kit leaves out. A Na–Cl pair stands for two ions, not a covalent molecule. Copper's electrons are shared through the metal. Matched molecules use their stored shape even when the simple lone-pair rule would give a different one.
