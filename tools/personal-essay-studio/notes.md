## What it teaches

Students turn a small true moment into a personal narrative with precise details and reflection. They revise openings and paragraph order while keeping earlier versions to compare.

## Key skills

- Narrow a broad topic to one place and a short stretch of time.
- Use dialogue, sensory details, actions and immediate thoughts.
- Connect a detail to a change in thinking.
- Try different openings and sequences for a purpose and audience.
- Compare versions and read aloud to choose what to revise.

## Support at home

- Ask your student to describe an ordinary moment using one sound and one action. Let them choose what to share.
- Listen to a paragraph read aloud and say what you could picture. Ask where your attention paused.
- Ask what changed between the start and the end of their story, without supplying the wording.

## A five-minute start

Open Spark and turn over a neutral prompt card. Capture a small moment, tick the two size checks if they apply, and choose Use this spark. Write freely in Draft, tap a general sentence, and open Zoom lens. Answer any question in notes, then place the notes under the sentence and rewrite in your own words.

## Modes

Spark keeps up to five possible moments. Draft supplies five close-up questions and a finder for stock phrases and abstract words. Then and Now connects a draft detail with a change in thinking. Revise keeps ten versions, compares added and cut words, previews openings, and tries paragraph sequences with self-assigned jobs and a chosen outcome.

Plan two or three sittings of 30 to 45 minutes. W.11-12.3a is practiced through Spark and Opening swap; 3b and 3d through the lens and the student's revision; 3c through the Sequence board; 3e through Then and Now. W.11-12.5 is practiced by revising and comparing actual versions.

## Talk questions

- Which specific detail does the most work in your essay?
- What does the reader need to know before the moment, and what can wait?
- Does the ending show a change in thinking that the moment supports?

## Good to know

You choose what to share. Prompts do not ask for trauma or private family matters. Students can choose an ordinary skill, routine or object. Read speaks only instructions, never student writing. Read-aloud checks mean students read aloud themselves; this tool does not record audio.

The meter is a transparent count of surface signals, not a score or an assessment of vividness. Capitalization can misidentify names, signals can overlap, and repeated numbers can inflate close-ups. A quoted span counts once. Stock phrases take precedence over abstract words inside them. Bars appear after 50 words and are capped visually at 25 signals per 100; printed counts remain exact. Both general statements and specific details can belong in an essay. There is no ideal ratio.

Autosave uses this browser's localStorage. Copy essay is available in Revise. Work is private to this browser profile, not password protected, and can be lost if browser data is cleared. A blocked-storage banner asks students to copy before closing. Clear everything requires two confirmations.

Named versions are protected. At ten versions the oldest unnamed version drops first; if all ten are named, clear a version name before saving another. Opening and sequence changes stop if a protective snapshot cannot be saved.

Lens answers stay with the selected sentence. Selecting a different sentence loads its own notes, and returning restores the earlier answers. Dropped notes remain editable draft text; remove the note block after using it, including before Copy essay.

An ending preview follows changes to Then, The turn and Now. Editing the draft clears an old opening preview, so try the opening again before applying it. Paragraph jobs follow unchanged paragraphs when new writing is inserted; assign a job again after rewriting a paragraph.
