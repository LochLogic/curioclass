## What it teaches

Students design a lunchbox that slows thermal energy transfer and test whether it meets limits on cost, wall thickness and time.
They then plan fair comparisons and use temperature graphs to explain the effects of mass, material and heating.

## Key skills

- Trace heat through walls, moving air and sunlight.
- Compare one design change at a time using a test log.
- Recognize melting from a flat temperature line.
- Change one variable while holding the others constant.
- Tie a claim to the settings and the graph that support it.

## Support at home

- Ask your child why a cold bottle eventually warms up. Which way does heat move?
- Look at a lunchbox you already own. Find its wall, lid and outer surface, and discuss how each might slow heat transfer.
- Recall two runs together. Ask what changed and which evidence showed that it helped. No experiment in a car is needed.

## A five-minute start

Open Build and tap Run to test the bare box. Watch the red lunch line leave the blue cold zone.
Add a wall or a cold pack, test again, and compare the two entries in the field log.
Have students name which heat path the change affects before running a third test.

## Build

The lunch starts at 3 °C. The job is to keep it at 4 °C or colder through noon on an $8 game budget.
Each wall layer takes 10 mm. Thicker walls leave fewer pack slots. Covers and the seal affect different heat paths.
Tap a layer label or its band to remove it; tap a pack in the box to remove it. Skip to noon completes a running test.
Changing a part stops a running test and keeps its partial lunch line as a ghost for comparison.
The log keeps every completed run this session, four at a time. Clear log clears the visible history, not the design.

After a qualifying design, two one-attempt questions record understanding. A correction explains a wrong first answer.
The engineer's note is unscored, limited to 280 characters and stored only on this device. Reset preserves a saved note.
A first-pass success asks for evidence from that test; the comparison prompt appears after multiple logged tests.

## Bench

Choose Mass, Matter or Energy. Only that variable may differ between A and B; start temperature must match.
Check plan locks a fair plan, then students predict which sample changes temperature more and Run.
Change plan stops a run and unlocks its settings. A different question acts immediately too.
Mass and Matter receive equal heat; Energy deliberately compares Low and High heat.
The graph fixes its temperature range before each run. A freezer water sample can reach zero and melt without warming.
Each question records its first claim only. A wrong claim shows the supported correction and the mark "tested; review".
The final recap counts first claims supported by the graphs; displaying a correction never counts as fixing an answer.
Prediction is not a score; first-attempt fairness is shown beside the result. Repeating a question never upgrades its first claim.

## Talk questions

- Why can thicker walls help without keeping lunch cold forever?
- What is happening while the frozen bottle's temperature line is flat?
- Why might steel start cold and still do a poor job here?
- Which settings must match for a fair test of mass? What changes for a test of heating?

## Good to know

This is a model, not a guarantee that real food is safe in a parked car. A parked car heats fast even on a mild day.
Never leave a person or pet inside. The illustrated car is an empty test chamber.
USDA recommends at least two cold sources in an insulated lunchbox, keeping perishables at 40 °F or below.
The model uses a conservative 4 °C threshold; 40 °F is about 4.4 °C, not exactly 4 °C.
Use a refrigerator when available rather than leaving real lunches in a car.

All prices, resistance values, film coefficients, leak rates, sunlight exposure, contact rates and lunch heat capacity
are model or game assumptions. Packs are each 200 g; the lunch heat capacity is 1,050 joules per degree.
The car curve starts at exactly 80 °F (26.67 °C), displayed as approximately 27 °C, and rises by 41 °F in an hour.
Foil reduces the modeled solar path. The model's sealed air gap is thin; material rankings depend on thickness and construction.
The lunch uses one effective heat capacity without a food-freezing phase change. Its initial below-zero dip is a model limitation.
The Bench cups are idealized sealed systems with no other heat exchange. Water is treated as pure water at ordinary pressure.
Steel stays solid throughout the displayed range. The graph compares supplied heat; students do not calculate energy totals.
The temperature caption is a Grade 6 shorthand for average particle kinetic motion, not a claim that all particles move at one speed.

## Standards

MS-PS3-3: students construct, test and revise a device that minimizes thermal energy transfer.
MS-PS3-4: students plan and carry out comparisons of heating, mass and type of matter using temperature graphs.
MS-PS3-5 is not claimed. Bench displays the specified heat scale as context, with no energy calculation task.

## Accessibility and privacy

Use Tab and Enter for controls and pack removal, with visible focus. Line dashes and labels supplement color.
Motion off keeps both test durations. Sound off silences tones and narration; Read speaks the current hint.
Narration uses the shared voice queue and browser speech until the friendly-dad recordings are supplied.
The tool works offline, collects nothing and sends no network requests. The only student writing saved is the local note.
