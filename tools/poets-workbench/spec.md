# Poet's Workbench (Curiosity No. 10)

## The real thing
What a poet works with: the sounds of words. Poets, songwriters, editors and the scholars who study verse mark a line's
stressed and unstressed syllables above the words (scansion), count syllables, and keep a rhyming dictionary at hand.
This is that desk: a typewriter page where every line you type is scanned as you write it, from the pronunciations
of over 126,000 English words in the Carnegie Mellon University Pronouncing Dictionary, with a drawer of real
rhymes beside it and a shelf of public-domain poems to scan.

## The first two minutes
- On open: a sheet of typewriter paper on a dark walnut desk. Typed on it, already scanned, is one line from the shelf:
  "Whose woods these are I think I know." Red proof marks sit over each syllable, x and / alternating, with "8" in the
  left margin and "iambic tetrameter" in small type at the line's end. A caret blinks on the empty line below, and a
  quiet typed note in the margin says: "Type a line."
- First action, nothing read: type anything, such as "I like to eat my pizza hot". As each word ends, its marks drop in
  above it with a soft typebar clack (Sound on): x / x / x / x /. The margin counts 8. The line is iambic too.
- First reaction: tap "hot". The desk drawer slides out with its rhymes: lot, not, spot, forgot, apricot, grouped by
  syllables, and slant rhymes (hop, top, shock) in a second column. Tap "spot" and it can be dropped into the page.

## Things to do
1. Type your name and see where its stress falls; try a friend's name.
2. Write two lines that rhyme and watch the right margin give them the same letter.
3. Tap any word for its perfect rhymes and slant rhymes, and hunt for a word with no perfect rhyme at all.
4. Type "I will record a record" and see the two words stressed differently.
5. Flip a one-syllable word's stress by tapping its mark and watch the line's meter name change.
6. Press Play and hear the line's beat tapped out, strong and weak, then speed the tempo knob up.
7. Load a sonnet from the shelf and read its rhyme scheme down the margin.
8. Load The Raven and find the rhymes hiding inside its lines, not only at the ends.
9. Switch on sound repeats and see the alliteration light up in a line of Poe or Dickinson.
10. Put haiku paper in and write three lines to 5, 7 and 5 syllables.
11. Write a limerick on limerick paper and listen to its gallop.
12. Load Jabberwocky and see which words the dictionary has never heard of, then save your own draft for later.

## Controls
- The page: a plain text editor, any length; a blank line separates stanzas; paste works. Tap a word (Alt+R at the
  caret) to open its rhymes. Tap a stress mark (Alt+F) to flip a one-syllable word, or step a longer word through its
  dictionary pronunciations (record: three).
- Paper tabs (keys 1 to 5): plain; haiku (faint 5, 7, 5); limerick (A A B B A); common meter (8, 6, 8, 6); sonnet
  (14 lines, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG). Faint guides only; nothing is checked against them.
- Meter lens dial: auto, iamb, trochee, anapest, dactyl; draws that foot's pattern faintly beside the marks (arrows).
- Sounds key: underlines repeated first consonants (alliteration) and stressed vowels (assonance) in a line.
- Play key: taps the line (Shift: the poem) on a woodblock, loud on stressed syllables; tempo knob 60 to 160 beats
  per minute, step 5. Speak toggle: reads the line with a voice installed on this device; hidden when none exists.
- Rhyme drawer tabs: Perfect, Same vowel, Same ending (the last two are slant rhymes), grouped by syllable count;
  arrows move, Enter inserts at the caret, Escape closes. Shelf (thin books at the desk's left): load one of 16 poems
  as an editable copy. Drafts: 4 slots on this device ("Your drafts stay on this device."); print with the marks.
- Power panel, three small keys at top right: Motion, Sound, Read.

## Readouts
- Margins, in the typewriter face: syllables per line at the left; rhyme letter at the right (A, B, C...), coloured
  by rhyme family, with end words underlined in the same ink.
- Line end, small type: the closest meter and feet ("iambic tetrameter") and how many syllables match that pattern
  ("7 of 8"), shown for the caret's line, or all lines with the meter lens on.
- Top strip (the paper bail): stanza rhyme scheme ("AABA BBCB CCDC DDDD"), lines, words and syllables in the poem.
- Rhyme drawer head: the word, its dictionary sounds ("R EH1 K ER0 D"), syllables, stress ("/ x") and the number of
  perfect rhymes found ("0 perfect rhymes" is a real answer).
- A dotted underline marks a word the dictionary does not have; its count and marks are a guess.

## The Manual
Lower-left drawer: reading the marks (/ stressed, x not); feet and line lengths; perfect and slant rhyme; how the
dictionary hears words; honesty notes; sources and the CMU licence notice. "Things people have found":
- "Orange" and "month" have no perfect rhyme anywhere among the dictionary's 126,052 words (CMU Pronouncing
  Dictionary 0.7b, checked 2026-11-05).
- "Record" has three listed pronunciations: stress first for the noun, second for the verb (CMU Pronouncing
  Dictionary).
- Alexander Pope rhymed "obey" with "tea", and "away" with "tea", in The Rape of the Lock (1714); today's dictionary
  does not rhyme them, a clue that the word was said differently then (Project Gutenberg ebook 9800).
- Frost's "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" links its stanzas: AABA BBCB CCDC DDDD, and its last two lines are
  the same (New Hampshire, 1923).
- Dickinson's "Because I could not stop for Death" alternates lines of 8 and 6 syllables, the common meter of hymns
  (Poems, 1890; Poetry Foundation glossary, "Common measure").
- Poe named The Raven's rhythm himself: trochaic, in lines of eight feet (Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition", 1846).
- Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 is rhymed ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, yet the workbench hears "temperate" and "date" only as a slant
  rhyme: the rhyme lands on an unstressed syllable (The Golden Treasury, 1861 text; CMU Pronouncing Dictionary).
- Carroll's "brillig", "slithy" and "toves" are not in the dictionary, so their beats are guessed from spelling
  (Through the Looking-Glass, 1871; CMU Pronouncing Dictionary).
- Japanese haiku count sound units called on, which are shorter than most English syllables, so many English haiku
  are shorter than 17 syllables (Haiku Society of America, "Definitions").

## Real material and sources
- CMU Pronouncing Dictionary 0.7b (Carnegie Mellon University; BSD-style licence, verified 2026-11-05 at
  github.com/cmusphinx/cmudict: redistribution allowed with the copyright notice kept, so the notice goes in the
  Manual). 126,052 words, 135,166 pronunciations, 3,618,488 bytes. No download in the build: copy it from the copy
  staged at _scratch/poets-workbench/cmudict.dict (from the cmudict Python package, 2026-11-05; identical in
  size to the GitHub master); if it is missing, stop and report. Pack it with a small build script: the 69 phone
  symbols to one character each, one "word pronunciation|pronunciation" line per word, gzip, base64. Measured
  2026-11-05: 2,068,281 bytes packed, 796,387 gzipped, 1,061,852 as base64. Unpack with DecompressionStream at load,
  then build the rhyme index in idle chunks; the page and the first line are usable before it finishes (under 1 s).
- Rhyme rules: perfect rhyme = same sounds from the last stressed vowel to the end; slant = same stressed vowel with a
  different ending, or same final consonants with a different vowel. Entries with digits or periods are dropped; the
  many names in the dictionary stay (it has no way to tell them apart). Unknown words rhyme by spelling (same last
  vowel letters and ending), shown with a dotted letter.
- Scansion rules: words of two or more syllables take the dictionary's stress (1 and 2 stressed, 0 not); one-syllable
  content words lean stressed; one-syllable function words (a built-in list of about 150 articles, pronouns,
  prepositions, conjunctions and helping verbs) are free. The line takes the foot and starting offset that matches
  most syllables; free syllables follow it and show grey. Words with several pronunciations take the one that fits
  the line best ("ourselves" has two and three syllable forms).
- Poems: 16 public-domain texts already in the Reading Room, read from library/<id>/text.md (published before 1930):
  golden-sonnet18, golden-tiger, golden-ozymandias, poe-raven, poe-annabel, dickinson-death, dickinson-hope,
  frost-woods, frost-road, hughes-dream-variations, dunbar-sympathy, mckay-if-we-must, owl-pussycat,
  longfellow-revere, mccrae-flanders, jabberwocky. Sources and Gutenberg ids: data/library/sources.csv. About 26 KB.
- Budget: dictionary about 1.07 MB, poems 26 KB, code about 250 KB. About 1.35 MB, under the 2 MB goal.

## Honesty notes
In the Manual: the dictionary is one American English set, so other accents may stress or rhyme words differently,
and it includes many names; one-syllable words are marked by guess and can be flipped; marks sit over the written
vowels by estimate (the dictionary gives sounds, not spelling breaks); unknown words are guessed from vowel groups;
the meter name is the closest pattern, and poets vary meter on purpose; the beat is a tap, not a reading.

## Voice
Bank voice smooth-narrator (an audiobook reader at a writer's desk), unlike Span and Spire (firm-captain) and Pocket
Universe (casual-peer). First-run hint, spoken once: "Type a line." Read speaks the current hint (recorded lines
only); readouts reach screen readers through a polite live region. Read never speaks the student's words; only the
Speak toggle does, with a voice installed on this device (localService true), so the text never leaves it.
