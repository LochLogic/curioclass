# Research packet: tools-for-change
Concept: Cards show ways people have changed things, from petitions and boycotts to letters and voting, each with a real past and present example; students pick strategies for a local problem, predict results, and turn the plan into a letter or poster.
Grade: 5   Standards: D2.Civ.14.3-5, D4.6.3-5, D4.7.3-5, D4.3.3-5

## Standards in plain words

- D2.Civ.14.3-5: "Illustrate historical and contemporary means of changing society." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to describe how citizens use petitions, boycotts, letters, and voting to solve community problems today and in the past.
- D4.3.3-5: "Present a summary of arguments and explanations to others outside the classroom using print and oral technologies (e.g., posters, essays, letters, debates, speeches, and reports) and digital technologies (e.g., Internet, social media, and digital documentary)." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to turn a proposed solution into a clear civic letter or awareness poster for a real audience.
- D4.6.3-5: "Draw on disciplinary concepts to explain the challenges people have faced and opportunities they have created, in addressing local, regional, and global problems at various times and places." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to explain historical obstacles faced by changemakers and the opportunities created by collective action.
- D4.7.3-5: "Explain different strategies and approaches students and others could take in working alone and together to address local, regional, and global problems, and predict possible results of their actions." (https://www.socialstudies.org/system/files/2022/c3-framework-for-social-studies-rev0617.2.pdf). The student must be able to choose specific civic strategies for a local problem and predict the likely outcomes.

## Real data

- National Archives Center for Legislative Archives: Petitions to Congress (1789-present): https://www.archives.gov/legislative (Public domain). Anti-slavery petitions, women's suffrage memorials, and citizen petitions.
- Library of Congress American Suffrage and Civil Rights Collections: https://www.loc.gov/collections/ (Public domain). Historic protest broadsides, petition drives, and boycott flyers.
- National Park Service Montgomery Bus Boycott Archives: https://www.nps.gov (Public domain). Firsthand leaflets, carpool organization maps, and legal filings.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming only voting adults can influence society: Fifth graders often think that because they cannot vote, they have no civic voice, overlooking that petitions, letters to the mayor, and awareness posters are powerful tools for young citizens (National Archives).
- Believing a single tactic always solves complex problems: Students frequently imagine a petition alone automatically forces change, missing that successful campaigns combine community meetings, letters to leaders, and public posters (NCSS C3 Framework).
- Overlooking likely resistance and delays: Students often predict immediate unanimous victory, forgetting that elected officials face budget limits and community members may hold opposing viewpoints (NCSS C3 Framework Dimension 4).
- Confusing a boycott with a street riot: Students sometimes confuse peaceful economic boycotts (refusing to spend money) with violent unrest (Civil Rights Education).

## Sensitivity

- Constructive democratic participation: Frame civic tools around peaceful, legal, constitutionally protected rights (First Amendment rights of speech, press, petition, and assembly).
- Civil rights movement dignity: Treat the Montgomery Bus Boycott and historical civil rights struggles with deep respect, acknowledging the bravery and risks undertaken by everyday citizens.
- Non-partisan local problems: Focus practice exercises on relatable community improvements (e.g. installing a safe school crosswalk, planting park shade trees, cleaning a local park) rather than divisive political battles.

## Similar tools elsewhere

- iCivics: Activate (https://www.icivics.org/games/activate): Interactive civic simulation where players select a local cause, recruit volunteers, organize petitions, and influence town leaders.
- Generation Citizen Action Civics Curriculum: Structured curriculum guiding students to research local issues and execute civic action plans.
- DocsTeach "Taking Action: Citizen Petitions" (National Archives): Interactive activities analyzing how citizens used petitions to change national policy.

## Gaps

- Scaffolding local problem choices for fifth graders: To prevent student decision paralysis, Curio Class tools should provide 3 well-defined starter community scenarios (1: Installing a pedestrian crosswalk by a school park, 2: Cleaning up park litter and adding recycling bins, 3: Saving an old library reading room) along with modular strategy cards (Petition, Letter to Council, Awareness Poster, Public Survey).
