# Tools for Change (idea card)

- Module: social-studies-5-tools-for-change, grade 5. Codes: D2.Civ.14.3-5, D4.6.3-5, D4.7.3-5, D4.3.3-5
- Format / family: cards / none. Fit: good
- One idea: A local problem (the library is closing on Saturdays) meets a hand of strategy cards (petition, boycott, letter, speaking at a meeting, voting, volunteering), each with one real past and one real present example; the student plays a plan, predicts what each card will do, sees a plausible result, and turns the plan into a letter or poster.
- How it plays (solo student by default):
  - First screen: a town map with the problem pinned and a fan of face-down strategy cards.
  - Core loop: flip a card to read its two real examples (what the people faced, what they did, what changed); play up to three cards on the problem and predict a result for each; the town responds with an honest, modeled outcome (some tools need many people or a long time).
  - The aha or the end state: plans that match the tool to the decision maker work best (a letter to the library board, not a boycott of a free library); the student prints or saves the letter or poster.
  - Class mode or extras (optional): two more local problems (a park with broken swings, a noisy truck route).
- Standards, one line per code:
  - D2.Civ.14.3-5: the strategy cards, each with a real past and present example of changing society.
  - D4.6.3-5: each example names the challenge people faced and the opening they used.
  - D4.7.3-5: the student picks strategies and predicts results before the town responds.
  - D4.3.3-5: the finished letter or poster, made for someone outside the classroom.
- Not like: changemaker-cards (grade 2, collects people, not strategies); levers-of-power (grade 8, roles and levels of government); bill-to-law-board-game (one path, the legislature).
- Art: people (illustrated) for the examples, so real people look real and respected; generated town scene.
- Proposed title: keep
- Proposed concept line: keep
- Flags: needs a data source (paired past and present examples, each checked); overlap risk (with levers-of-power, kept apart by focus: strategies here, levels and roles there)
