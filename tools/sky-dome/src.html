Sky Dome / Manual

OBSERVATORY FIELD NOTES · NO. 05

Turn the sky

Turn the time ring with a finger or mouse. A full turn is a day. Turn the date wheel to keep the same clock time on another night. Play runs the projector at the rate beneath it. The reverse switch runs backward. Now returns to your device's time.

Drag on the dome to look around. Scroll or pinch to zoom; Dome returns to the whole sky. Tap any star, planet, Sun, Moon or square deep-sky marker for its measurements.

Move the observatory

Choose a place in the search field, or type latitude and longitude. North and east are positive; south and west are negative. On a narrow screen, Place opens the setting circle and rim levers.

Your home is an estimate from the device time zone, without a location request. At home, the clock uses the device's legal time zone. Elsewhere the clock follows the Sun at that longitude, rather than the town's legal time. This is called local mean solar time: longitude divided by 15 gives its offset from UTC in hours.

Keys at the instrument

Space play / pause. [ and ] change rate. N now. L latitude; O longitude. On the sky, arrows pan 5°, Shift arrows 20°; + and − zoom. Dome also puts keyboard focus on the sky. Tab from there visits 30 bright objects above the horizon, then leaves the sky. Enter selects the announced object. The ordinary Tab order reaches the instrument controls before this long object list. J visits the 20 deep-sky markers; Shift J visits them backward, including those below your horizon.

On the time ring, Left / Right moves ten minutes, or an hour with Shift. On the date wheel, Left / Right moves a day, or a month with Shift. Up / Down on Year changes a year, or a century with Shift. Type a year and choose BC or AD, or type “2800 BC” directly. The same modern calendar rules run backward into ancient dates, called the proleptic Gregorian calendar. Internally, astronomical year 0 means 1 BC and −2799 means 2800 BC; the year window shows the plain BC year.

Read the glass

ALT is height above the horizon. AZ is the direction measured from north through east. RA (right ascension) and DEC (declination) are the sky's longitude and latitude. They follow the slow change in Earth's axis, called precession, to the chosen date. A star below the horizon is marked below, even with Ground off.

Magnitude runs backward: a smaller number means a brighter object. Five magnitudes means a factor of 100 in received light. Dot size and glow compress that enormous range so faint stars remain visible. The Sun, Moon and planet disks are enlarged to find them.

B−V compares a star's blue and visual brightness. Higher values generally mean cooler, redder stars. The dome uses a continuous display-colour ramp from Yale's measured colours. When colour is missing, the star's spectral type supplies an approximate colour. This is a screen representation, not a spectrum. Temperature is a rough colour estimate, especially uncertain for supergiants, reddened stars and multiple systems; it is omitted outside B−V −0.4 to +2.0. A colon marks an uncertain catalogue value; a question mark marks a questionable one. Brightness is the catalogue value, not a live measurement of a variable star.

Planet apparent magnitudes are not modelled. Their MAG readout says “not available”; their navigation entries come before the stars ranked by catalogue magnitude. Sun, Moon and planet disks are enlarged symbols, not a photometric simulation.

The Sun's MAG is the nominal visual value −26.74 from NASA's Sun Fact Sheet, not a measurement that varies with the selected date.

Distances are approximate NASA fact-page values for 16 stars. Nearby distances below 20 light years show one decimal; the others show whole light years. Other distances say “no checked value”. No distance is inferred from brightness. An absent colour says “not measured”.

The 20 square Messier markers are catalogue centres, not pictures or apparent sizes. Names switches the markers and their labels. Their extended-light magnitudes depend on the measurement aperture; where HEASARC and SEDS disagree, the readout shows both estimates as a range. M41's published centres differ by 14 arcminutes, within the cluster's extent. The marker uses the HEASARC centre.

DAYLIGHT is the interval between standard sunrise and sunset, including average atmospheric refraction and the Sun's radius, over a flat sea horizon. Mountains, weather and elevation change what you actually see. “Above all day” and “Below all day” replace events at the poles.

Moon AGE counts days since the previous computed new Moon. LIT is the fraction of the Moon's visible disk illuminated. Within the displayed age's 0.1-day resolution of a conjunction, AGE shows approximately zero; minute-level model errors can change which cycle it falls in. The bright side faces the Sun on this sky, including in southern skies. Rise and set are events on the displayed local date; occasionally one does not occur that day. At home, daylight-saving dates can contain 23 or 25 clock hours; event searches use those local date boundaries.

Why it changes

Earth's daily spin turns the sky. Earth's trip around the Sun changes which direction the night side faces during the year. The Moon goes around Earth, so we see changing portions of its sunlit half. The planets move against the distant stars; our moving viewpoint makes their trails sometimes loop backward.

Earth's axis is tilted. During your hemisphere's summer, it leans toward the Sun: the Sun takes a higher, longer path across the sky, so daylight lasts longer. In winter the path is lower and shorter. The two hemispheres have opposite seasons.

CONST names the constellation's sky area. With Lines and Names on, faint names mark the conventional figures. The Big Dipper is part of Ursa Major; the Southern Cross is Crux. At the North Pole every direction along the ground is south; at the South Pole every such direction is north. The compass letters keep the dome's orientation.

Trails show the preceding year, sampled every three days. They are recalculated from orbits, then projected against today's stars. The thin dashed amber path is the ecliptic, the plane of Earth's orbit. Grid cycles from off, to altitude / azimuth, to RA / declination.

Things people have found

A longer Sun path

Over Denver on June 21, 2026 the instrument shows about 15 hours of daylight; on December 21 it shows about 9 hours 21 minutes. USNO's rounded rise and set times give 14 hours 59 minutes and 9 hours 21 minutes, within a minute of the model. Latitude and the Sun's changing path explain the difference.

A star almost still

Polaris is now within a degree of the north celestial pole. Its height above the horizon is therefore close to northern latitude. A star day is about 23 h 56 min 4 s, so a star rises about four minutes earlier the next night.

The pole had another neighbour

In about 2800 BC, Thuban was near the north celestial pole. Earth's axis slowly changes direction. The year wheel includes precession and catalogue proper motion, so the pole's neighbourhood changes. The rim names the closest named catalogue star to the pole in your hemisphere. It may still be many degrees away.

Two very different Orion stars

Betelgeuse's B−V is about +1.85; Rigel's is about −0.03. One is red and cool, the other blue-white and hot. Brightness alone does not tell us distance: Sirius is only about 8.6 light years away.

Wandering lights

Venus stays within about 47° of the Sun. Mars can trace a backward loop as Earth overtakes it. The Moon rises about 50 minutes later per day on average; individual days differ substantially.

Honesty at the eyepiece

This is an approximate planetarium for exploration, not telescope pointing or eclipse prediction. JPL's short-interval elements are used for 1800–2050 and its long-interval elements otherwise, within 3000 BC–AD 3000. The Moon is computed with a shortened series of repeating orbital terms, called a truncated periodic expansion; lunar errors and uncertain ancient Earth rotation grow far from today. No eclipse shadows are modelled.

The 180° dome is a round, equal-angle fisheye map with the same scale across and up. Angles from the centre keep their scale; like every flat sky map, it still distorts shapes away from the centre. The ground is an ideal geometric horizon with a small generic silhouette, not a surveyed landscape. Refraction uses a standard average atmosphere. The Milky Way is a drawn galactic band with a broad brightness profile, not a photograph or dust map. It fades with daylight.

Sources and checks

9096 stellar rows are bundled from the 9110-entry Yale catalogue; 14 blank or non-stellar entries are excluded. All 8,786 measured B−V values are retained; the 310 missing colours use a spectral-class estimate. Star dots are batched into display-colour and opacity bins for drawing; every numerical B−V and magnitude readout keeps the catalogue value. Names use a 2022 IAU snapshot (333 matched HR entries). The place search has 1,238 Natural Earth entries, checked against Wikidata city coordinates; 13 missing or conflicting entries were omitted. Coordinates are approximate city centres, not your location. Country names disambiguate search results.

Natural Earth's Maradi entry was misnamed Niamey. Its NAMEASCII field and Wikidata Q664550 identify Maradi; that name is corrected here. Distinct cities with the same name and country include coordinates in the search list.

Home-zone associations use Natural Earth's time-zone fields; an exact city-name match is also tried. An unmatched zone is explicitly labelled as an equatorial longitude estimate from the UTC offset. Enter latitude and longitude for a precise site.

Lines cover all 88 constellations, using d3-celestial's modern convention; Serpens has two parts. Every endpoint was matched to a Yale star within 0.003°. The IAU standardises sky areas, not stick figures; these are not boundaries. Cultural sky stories remain omitted as the specification advises.
