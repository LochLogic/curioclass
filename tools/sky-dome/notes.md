## What it teaches

Students can compare how the Sun, Moon and stars move through a day and across seasons, and connect those patterns to time and place on Earth. The teacher drives for grade 1; older students can operate the observatory independently.

## Key skills

- Change one observation setting and compare the sky.
- Read dates, directions, angles and daylight duration.
- Connect Moon phase to the illuminated part of its visible face.
- Compare a star's apparent brightness with available distance information.
- Distinguish a measured catalogue value from a model or an unknown value.

## Support at home

- Notice the Moon together on several evenings and describe how its shape changes.
- Compare where a familiar star appears on two evenings at the same time.
- Notice how daylight changes through the year. Never look directly at the Sun.

## Five-minute start

Open the dome and turn the time ring. Leave room for students' observations. Step the date wheel, then change latitude. Read is available throughout; the Manual explains the measurements after students have something they want to understand.

Read pauses the projector while it describes the displayed sky. The opening hint speaks once after a first non-time gesture when Sound is on, while its on-screen line remains. A first time-dial, date-wheel, Play or rate-knob gesture stays silent. Home and End on the date wheel reach January 1 and December 31 while preserving the clock; on the time wheel they reach 00:00 and 23:59.

## Instrument

One continuous observatory, with no lesson sequence. The main time, date and transport targets are at least 60 px. Motion off stops automatic time and makes Play step the sky. On phones, Place opens the secondary controls. A teacher plan is not supplied by this Build, so do not replace an anchor lesson card on the strength of this file alone.

## Talk questions

- What changed when you moved time, and what changed when you moved place?
- What stays near the celestial pole?
- Does a brighter-looking star have to be nearer?
- How does daylight differ between these two dates at the same latitude?

## Good to know

The sky uses 9,096 Yale stars, including all 8,786 measured B-V values, and JPL orbital elements. Read the Manual's honesty notes before using ancient dates quantitatively. There are 88 conventional figures, 20 marked Messier objects, 1,238 checked Natural Earth places and checked distances for 16 stars. The source catalogue has no B-V for 310 stars; these use spectral colours. A missing distance says “no checked value”. Nearby distances below 20 light years show one decimal. Sun DIST shows au and light minutes. J and Shift J visit deep-sky objects by keyboard. Place search accepts the city alone when unique or its country-qualified label. The tool makes no automatic network requests or location request. Shared settings stay on this device.

The pole readout names the closest named catalogue star in the observer's hemisphere, with its measured angular offset. It does not imply a close pole star exists at every date. Repeated city-and-country labels include coordinates to distinguish their entries. Natural Earth's mislabeled Maradi entry is corrected against its alternate name and Wikidata record; Niamey retains its separate coordinates. Cultural sky stories are omitted as the specification advises; star names are credited identifiers, without cultural origin claims.

## Refine changes

The whole-dome sky and brass ring are round, with one scale in both directions. Lines plus Names labels the figures;
CONST in each object card names its sky area. The Big Dipper belongs to Ursa Major; the Southern Cross is Crux.
Year accepts 2800 BC directly and its BC/AD flag switches eras. The brass rate knob has keyboard detents.
Read says the clock as a time of day. Date/time marks use 9-unit dark engraving on gold, outside the central value window.
The Manual explains seasons and preserves all data checks under Sources and checks.
