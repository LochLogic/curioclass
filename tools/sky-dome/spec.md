# Sky Dome (Curiosity No. 05)

## The real thing
A planetarium: the real sky from any place on Earth at any moment, with a projector that can run time fast, slow or
backward. Planetarium presenters use one to show a room the night sky; astronomers and observers use planetarium
software to plan a night; navigators once learned the stars on one. Sky Dome is that dome, with about 9,000
naked-eye stars, the Sun, the Moon and the five bright planets in their computed places, and a brass date wheel and
setting circle to move through time and around the world.

## Map
- Anchor: science-1-sun-moon-and-stars (1-ESS1-1, 1-ESS1-2).
- Also reaches: science-5-sun-stars-and-shadows, science-5-star-brightness, science-6-earth-sun-moon-system,

- Grade band: grades 1 to 9. Grade 1 meets it on a projector with a teacher; grades 5 and up drive it alone. Main
  controls (time dial, date wheel, play) have 60 px targets for the youngest users.
- Teacher plan: would teach 1-ESS1-1 (patterns of Sun, Moon and stars) and 1-ESS1-2 (daylight across the seasons,
  from the DAYLIGHT readout) in full, so it can take the module's lesson card if its plan.md does.

## The first two minutes
- Open: tonight's sky over the student's area at the current time, guessed from the device's time zone (America/
  Denver opens over Denver), no permission asked. A dark dome, a brass horizon ring with N E S W, a faint ground
  silhouette, stars at their true brightness and colour. One quiet line on the dome: "Drag the time dial."
- First action with nothing read: drag the time dial (or press play).
- First reaction: the whole sky wheels around one still point, the Pole Star, stars rising in the east and setting in
  the west; the Moon climbs and, as days pass on the date wheel, changes shape.

## Things to do
1. Run time through one night and find the one star that barely moves.
2. Step the same clock time night after night with the date wheel and watch the whole sky slide west through the
   seasons.
3. Find tonight's Moon, then step a day at a time and watch it fatten, rise later, and wane.
4. Turn the daylight off and see the stars behind the Sun at noon; find which constellation the Sun is in.
5. Read DAYLIGHT on the shortest and longest days of the year, then move north and do it again.
6. Set your place to the equator and watch every star rise straight up; then the North Pole, where none set.
7. Set the latitude far south and find the Southern Cross, which most of the United States never sees.
8. Leave planet trails on for a year and catch Mars looping backward against the stars.
9. Follow Venus for months and see that it never wanders far from the Sun.
10. Tap Betelgeuse and Rigel and compare their colour readouts and temperatures.
11. Tap the brightest stars and compare magnitude with distance: some are bright because they are close.
12. Set the year to 2800 BC and find which star stood near the pole when the pyramids were built.
13. Find the Big Dipper on a spring evening and an autumn evening: the same stars, turned.

## Controls
- Sky: drag to look around (azimuth and altitude); wheel or pinch to zoom the field of view from 20 to 180 degrees
  (180 is the whole dome as a fisheye). Arrow keys pan 5 degrees, Shift 20; + and - zoom.
- Time dial: a brass ring under the dome, 24 hours around, drag or Left and Right (10 minutes), Shift (1 hour).
- Date wheel: a second ring, 365 days around with month marks; Left and Right one day, Shift one month.
- Year: a small odometer, 3000 BC to AD 3000 (the range the planet elements are valid for); Up and Down, Shift by
  100 years.
- Play knob: detents stop, real time, 1 min/s, 10 min/s, 1 h/s, 1 day/s and Same time each night (steps one day at
  a fixed clock time). Space plays and pauses; [ and ] step the knob.
- Now pad: returns to the device's current date and time. Key N.
- Setting circle: latitude dial 90S to 90N and longitude dial 180W to 180E, 0.1 degree steps; or a place search box
  over a bundled list of about 1,200 cities. Keys: L focuses latitude, O longitude.
- Toggles (brass levers on the dome's rim): Constellation lines (C), Names (M), Daylight (D), Ground (G), Grid
  alt-az or equatorial (R cycles), Planet trails (T), Milky Way (W).
- Tap or Enter on a star, planet, Sun or Moon: selects it and opens its readout card. Tab cycles the 30 brightest
  objects above the horizon.
- Power panel (Motion, Sound, Read): lower-left beside the plate, styled as brass push buttons.

## Readouts
Monospace, warm cream on the dark rim, in small engraved windows.
- DATE (YYYY-MM-DD), LOCAL TIME (HH:MM, the device's zone when home, otherwise local solar time), UTC.
- LAT and LON (one decimal), the place name when chosen from the list.
- SUN: altitude degrees (one decimal), RISE and SET (HH:MM), DAYLIGHT (h and min).
- MOON: phase name, LIT percent (whole), AGE days (one decimal), RISE and SET.
- Selected object: NAME, MAG (two decimals), B-V colour index (two decimals) and TEMP K (rounded to 100), DIST light
  years (whole, where known), ALT and AZ degrees (one decimal), RA and DEC.
- POLE STAR offset (degrees from the celestial pole, one decimal) when the pole is in view.

## The Manual
Contents: how to move through time and place; what magnitude, colour index and distance mean; why the sky turns;
seasons and the Sun's path; Moon phases; planets and their loops; the pole over the centuries; honesty notes; sources.

Things people have found:
- Polaris's height above the horizon is within about a degree of your latitude, because it sits within a degree of
  the celestial pole (US Naval Observatory; NASA "Polaris" fact page).
- The stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night, because a star day is 23 h 56 min 4 s (US Naval Observatory,
  sidereal time).
- The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day on average (NASA, Moon in motion).
- Betelgeuse has a colour index of about +1.85 and Rigel about -0.03: one is a cool red supergiant, the other a hot
  blue-white one (Yale Bright Star Catalogue, 5th ed.).
- Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky, magnitude -1.46, partly because it is only about 8.6 light years
  away (Yale Bright Star Catalogue; NASA).
- Mars appears to move backward against the stars for about two months around opposition, as Earth overtakes it
  (NASA, "Mars retrograde").
- Venus never gets more than about 47 degrees from the Sun, so it is only ever an evening or morning star (NASA
  Venus fact sheet, greatest elongation).
- Around 2800 BC the pole star was Thuban in Draco, because Earth's axis slowly circles over 26,000 years (Meeus,
  Astronomical Algorithms, precession; NASA).

## Real material and sources
- Stars: Yale Bright Star Catalogue, 5th revised edition (Hoffleit and Warren 1991), about 9,100 stars to magnitude
  6.5, via NASA HEASARC (listed under the US Government Works licence on data.nasa.gov; the catalogue's own
  documentation calls it public domain). RA, Dec, magnitude, B-V and proper motion, packed at about 8 bytes a star.
  About 95 KB as base64.
- Star names: IAU Catalog of Star Names (IAU WGSN), about 300 bright stars' proper names; IAU products are released
  under Creative Commons Attribution (exact version to confirm on iau.org/WG280). About 8 KB.
- Distances: Hipparcos new reduction parallaxes (van Leeuwen 2007) for the named stars only, as published numbers.
  The CDS distribution asks for acknowledgement in scientific use; a few hundred parallax values are facts, credited.
  Flagged; alternative is to show distance only for stars with a NASA or ESA fact page. About 3 KB.
- Constellation lines: traced as pairs of catalogue star numbers from the IAU and Sky & Telescope constellation charts
  (CC BY; copies show 3.0, the IAU OAE adaptations 4.0). About 4 KB. The d3-celestial line file (BSD-3-Clause) is a
  permissive fallback, flagged because it is not CC BY. Stellarium's modern lines (CC BY-SA) are not used.
- Planets: JPL "Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets" (Standish), valid 3000 BC to AD
  3000. Sun, Moon, rise and set, phases, eclipses and precession from the published methods in Meeus, Astronomical
  Algorithms (formulas, written in our own code). About 10 KB of code.
- Places: Natural Earth populated places (public domain, verified). About 40 KB.
- Milky Way: a band drawn in code along the galactic equator with a brightness profile by galactic longitude; no
  image data.
- Total goal under 600 KB. No network requests.

## Honesty notes
Said in the Manual:
- Planet and Moon places are good to a fraction of a degree, not telescope-exact, and drift more far from today.
- The Milky Way is drawn, not photographed.
- Air bending starlight near the horizon (refraction) is included as a standard average.
- Constellation lines are a modern convention; distances are known well only for nearer stars.
- Away from home, the clock shows local solar time, not the town's legal time zone.

## Done when
1. Opens on tonight's sky over the device's time-zone city with no welcome screen and no location permission prompt.
2. Ten star positions match HEASARC BSC5 coordinates precessed to the date within 0.1 degree; five planet positions
   match JPL Horizons for a date in 2026 within 1 degree (spot checks in the report).
3. Sunrise, sunset and daylight for Denver on the solstices match USNO tables within 2 minutes.
4. Moon phase and age match USNO for three dates; the Same time each night detent shows the seasonal drift.
5. Year 2800 BC puts Thuban within about 2 degrees of the pole.
6. Every control works by keyboard and announces its value; main targets are 60 px.
7. 60 frames a second at 1366x768 with all stars and lines on a school Chromebook profile.
8. Layout checked at 1366x768 and 375x812; plate and Manual drawer lower-left at both.
9. Motion off: time steps instead of gliding and the sky never turns on its own; contrast passes AA.
10. No network requests; every source and licence in the Manual and tool.draft.json `sources`.
11. Short test passed and written in the report, with a frame beside Elements Inspector.
12. No score, goal, level, sticker or finish screen anywhere.
